Good morning, Travis. It's Thursday, November fifth, 2026. On the list today: Sanofi drops a STAT-six degrader and takes forty percent off Nurix, CVS breaks itself in two, Boehringer pays three hundred fifty-eight million dollars for options on six preclinical Chinese cancer programs, AbbVie licenses an IBD drug another company had shelved, Novo's Wegovy pill beats estimates, and Spyre drops its TL1A antibody in arthritis. Plus what a Democratic Congress means for the F-D-A, a whistleblower suit at Madrigal, and two European financings. Here's what you need to know.

First, and this is today's spotlight. After Tuesday's close, Nurix said Sanofi is stopping the Phase 1 of S-A-R-four-four-eight-two-seven-two, an oral degrader of STAT-six, the transcription factor under the I-L-four and I-L-thirteen receptors, and ending the program. Sanofi told Leerink the reason was "emerging nonclinical findings," and that STAT-six is still "an important pathway in immunology." Nurix shares fell more than forty percent Wednesday morning. Nurix's platform, which it calls DEL-AI, found the molecule. It loses about four hundred fifty-five million dollars in potential STAT-six milestones. The spotlight covers why analysts call this a molecule problem rather than a target problem, why Sanofi keeping its other STAT-six drug, an inhibitor from Recludix, is the strongest evidence for that, and what it means for Kymera's Phase 2b due by year end.

Second, CVS Health is splitting into two public companies. One keeps Aetna, the Caremark pharmacy benefit manager, Oak Street Health, Signify and the specialty pharmacy business. The other, CVS Pharmacy, takes the roughly nine thousand retail stores, mail order and the walk-in clinics, and will be led by Prem Shah. The split should close in the first half of 2028. It reverses twenty years of vertical integration, from buying Caremark in 2007 to Aetna in 2018. Forrester's Arielle Trzcinski said CVS is "effectively acknowledging that bigger isn't always better." C-E-O David Joyner says regulation isn't the reason. Still, states like Arkansas and Tennessee have passed laws barring P-B-Ms from owning pharmacies, there's a bipartisan federal bill to do the same, and Democrats now control Congress. For drugmakers, the point is that the P-B-M stays with the insurer, so the company they negotiate rebates with keeps the same structure.

Third, Boehringer Ingelheim signed its second oncology deal with Sino Biopharmaceutical, now called S-B-P Group. That's three hundred fifty-eight million dollars upfront and near-term, plus up to seven billion in option and milestone payments and royalties up to the double digits. It covers six preclinical antibody-drug conjugate and T-cell engager programs: five from S-B-P's Shanghai antibody company LaNova Medicines and one from Beijing Tide. S-B-P runs early development, and Boehringer can opt in after agreed clinical milestones, taking ex-China rights to four programs and worldwide rights to two. Boehringer opened a Shanghai R and D center last month and did a one point two six billion dollar T-L-one-A by I-L-twenty-three bispecific deal with Simcere in January. Paying that much upfront for assets that haven't reached the clinic shows how far the China licensing market has moved, even as Endpoints' new Signal survey finds China rising on biotech executives' worry list.

Fourth, AbbVie paid Nxera a hundred million dollars upfront, with up to one point three seven billion in milestones, for N-X-E-oh-oh-three-three-seven-four-four. It's an oral E-P-four receptor agonist for inflammatory bowel disease, designed to act in the gut and keep systemic exposure low. E-P-four is a prostaglandin E-two receptor on the intestinal lining, and the idea is to promote epithelial repair rather than suppress the immune system. Nxera shelved the program in a restructuring last November, after it had finished Phase 1 in ulcerative colitis patients and in a cohort challenged with N-SAIDs. Jefferies' read: "Since the EP4 agonist acts on the intestinal epithelium, its mechanism of action differs from existing drugs that target the immune system, so we think it will most likely end up being used in combination." That points to adding it to Skyrizi or Rinvoq, and fits C-E-O Rob Michael's stated preference for bolt-ons over megadeals.

Fifth, Novo. We covered the write-offs and the C-F-O exit yesterday, so here's the rest. The Wegovy pill, oral semaglutide, brought in five point three nine billion Danish kroner in the third quarter, about eight hundred eleven million dollars, against a consensus of about four point nine six billion kroner. Novo now guides adjusted sales and operating profit to as much as two percent growth at constant exchange rates. In May the top of the range was a four percent decline. The shares rose more than six percent. For comparison, Lilly's oral Foundayo did a hundred eighty-four million dollars, forty-four million short of estimates. Novo is ahead in oral. The new C-F-O is Luigi La Corte from Galderma, and the outgoing C-F-O told Endpoints that CagriSema won't launch in Europe for "a couple of years."

Sixth, Spyre is out of rheumatology. Its long-acting anti-T-L-one-A antibody S-P-Y-oh-seventy-two missed its primary endpoints in the psoriatic arthritis and axial spondyloarthritis arms of the SKYWAY basket trial. The rheumatoid arthritis arm was positive in August, but the benefit wasn't big enough to advance. Spyre now goes to hidradenitis suppurativa: a pivotal monotherapy program in 2027, funded by more than four hundred million dollars raised in October, and a Phase 2 combining it with an I-L-seventeen A and F antibody. Its I-B-D combination readout is due in the third quarter of 2027. T-L-one-A keeps working in the gut and is struggling in the joints.

Seventh, what a Democratic Congress means for the F-D-A. STAT reports Bernie Sanders will likely chair the Senate health committee just as the user-fee law that funds F-D-A reviews comes up for renewal before it expires next September 30th. He's one of the few senators who opposes the premise of industry-paid user fees. Analysts are mostly calm, though. RBC's Brian Abrahams said the I-R-A is "too complicated to materially change." Leerink's David Risinger said Democrats have "bigger fish to fry" than the most-favored-nation deals. Things to watch: whether Heidi Overton's nomination as F-D-A commissioner gets a vote in the lame-duck session or expires, and more oversight of Secretary Kennedy's vaccine policy.

Eighth, Madrigal. STAT reports that Michael Charlton, its former head of clinical development, has sued in federal court in Illinois. He alleges he was forced out in January after raising internal concerns about the safety of Rezdiffra, the thyroid hormone receptor beta agonist approved for MASH, and he claims retaliation under federal whistleblower law. These are allegations in a complaint, and we don't have Madrigal's response. But Rezdiffra is the first approved MASH drug and the commercial benchmark for the field, so it's worth tracking.

Ninth, two European financings. Oxford spinout Alethio Therapeutics raised a ninety million dollar Series A, co-led by Sofinnova and RA Capital, for A-T-X-oh-eleven, an antibody against G-six-b. That's a receptor on platelets and megakaryocytes, the cells that drive myeloproliferative neoplasms and marrow scarring. The idea is to work regardless of whether a patient's driver mutation is in JAK-two, CALR or M-P-L, starting with essential thrombocythemia. It reaches the clinic in the first half of 2028. And Cambridge University spinout T-Therapeutics raised a hundred thirty-three million dollars for T-cell-receptor drugs in cancer and autoimmunity, with Johnson and Johnson, Merck K-G-a-A and Lilly joining Sanofi as backers. That's four pharma investors in one round.

Tenth, Amgen started a Phase 3 of a MariTide autoinjector, with primary completion in June 2027 and the first Phase 3 obesity data due next year. BMO calls MariTide's differentiation "still more of a show-me story" until there's Phase 3 tolerability data.

On AI in drug discovery: no new company launches or data today beyond the Nurix story. STAT's AI newsletter this week profiles startups that are choosing not to use AI for discovery and are putting it elsewhere in development.

Looking ahead: the first wave of ASH abstracts is out, with data from J-and-J, Servier and AstraZeneca. SITC runs through Sunday.
