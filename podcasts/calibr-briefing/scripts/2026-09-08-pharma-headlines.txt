Good morning Travis. Here are your biotech and pharma headlines for Tuesday, September eighth, twenty twenty-six. U-S markets reopen after Labor Day, the European Respiratory Society congress in Barcelona is in its final stretch, and the biggest number of the day has not printed yet.

First, the readout that lands while you are listening to this. At twelve fifteen Central European time — six fifteen Eastern — Marc Humbert of Université Paris-Saclay, who runs the French national reference center for pulmonary hypertension, presents topline Phase two results from PHocus, testing mosliciguat in pulmonary hypertension associated with interstitial lung disease. Roivant hosts an investor call at eight Eastern. The numbers are not public as I record, so I am not going to guess at them — what I can give you is the bar. Mosliciguat is an inhaled, once-daily soluble guanylate cyclase activator, and the word activator is doing real mechanistic work. Riociguat, the approved oral drug in this family, is a stimulator: it needs soluble guanylate cyclase in its reduced, heme-containing state to work. An activator does the opposite — it engages the oxidized, heme-free form of the enzyme, the form that accumulates precisely under the oxidative stress you find in a fibrotic lung. So the thesis is that in interstitial lung disease you have a pool of dysfunctional enzyme that the stimulator class cannot reach, and inhaled delivery gets you pulmonary vasodilation without the systemic hypotension that has sunk oral vasodilators in this population. PHocus randomized one hundred thirty-five adults, double-blind and placebo-controlled, twenty-four weeks, followed by an extension. The Phase one-b ATMOS study is what set expectations: a single inhaled dose produced a mean peak reduction in pulmonary vascular resistance of up to thirty-eight percent, which is among the largest acute effects ever reported in pulmonary hypertension. That number is also the trap. An acute hemodynamic response after one dose is not twenty-four weeks of function, and this indication has a long history of hemodynamics that did not convert. Remember the provenance and the price: Roivant created Pulmovant in September twenty twenty-four specifically to in-license this asset from Bayer, for about fourteen million dollars upfront, up to two hundred eighty million in milestones, and high-single-digit royalties. That is cheap optionality on a mechanism Bayer chose not to carry itself. The competitive frame is narrow and specific — the only therapy actually approved for pulmonary hypertension in interstitial lung disease is United Therapeutics' inhaled treprostinil, a prostacyclin analog, on the strength of the INCREASE trial's six-minute walk distance benefit. If mosliciguat has a comparable functional effect with once-daily dry powder dosing instead of four-times-daily nebulized prostacyclin, that is a real commercial wedge. If the six-minute walk did not move, the thirty-eight percent becomes a cautionary tale about acute pulmonary vascular resistance as a surrogate. Either way this is Roivant's most consequential single data point of the year.

Second, and this one is fully in hand. Novo Nordisk reported first results from STEP Young, the Phase three of once-weekly semaglutide in children aged six to under twelve with obesity. One hundred sixty-five children, randomized and placebo-controlled, sixty-eight weeks, with both arms on reduced-calorie diet and increased physical activity. The trial hit its primary endpoint of percent B-M-I change. The headline secondary is the one that will get repeated: forty point four percent of children on semaglutide were no longer classified as having obesity at week sixty-eight, versus zero percent on placebo. Weigh that against the baseline — more than eighty-five percent of these children had class two or class three obesity, which in adult terms corresponds to a body mass index at or above thirty-five and forty respectively. In pediatrics the classification is percentile-based, so obesity is a B-M-I at or above the ninety-fifth percentile for age and sex, and class two and three are one hundred twenty and one hundred forty percent of that percentile. Getting two in five severely obese six-to-eleven-year-olds below the obesity line inside a year, against a placebo arm that moved nobody, is a large effect. The safety line matters as much as the efficacy line here: Novo says tolerability was consistent with the adult and adolescent semaglutide programs, no new safety concerns, and specifically no signal on growth or pubertal development. That last clause is the one pediatric endocrinologists were waiting for, and it is the one regulators will interrogate. Ania Jastreboff at Yale, who directs their obesity research center and has run the pivotal adult and adolescent work in this class, framed the result around the strong predilection of childhood obesity to become adult obesity and the early emergence of complications. Two pieces of context. This trial exists because regulators required it — it is a post-marketing requirement attached to the semaglutide Phase three program, not a discretionary expansion. And Wegovy is already approved down to age twelve on the back of STEP TEENS, with liraglutide before it, so the commercial question is a label extension into the six-to-eleven band rather than a new market. Detailed results come at ObesityWeek in Washington, November fourteenth through seventeenth. Expect the argument about medicalizing childhood obesity to get considerably louder between now and then, and expect Lilly to be asked on every call about pediatric tirzepatide and pediatric orforglipron.

Third, the item today's spotlight takes apart. Insilico Medicine published a study in Nature Biotechnology, with collaborators at Harvard Medical School, Stanford, the Broad, R-W-T-H Aachen, Peking University and Westlake, applying six independently developed proteomic aging clocks to serum from the Phase two-a trial of rentosertib in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. Rentosertib is the flagship claim of the whole A-I drug discovery field: its target, the kinase T-NIK, was nominated by Insilico's target discovery platform, its chemical structure was generated by their generative chemistry engine, and it is now in a three hundred twenty-patient Phase three in China. The new paper reports that all six clocks — built by different groups on different methodologies, four trained on chronological age and two on mortality risk — independently registered a reduction in predicted biological age in treated patients while placebo drifted the other way. That is a genuinely interesting result and it is also a forty-two-patient exploratory analysis of a twelve-week trial. More in the spotlight, including the single finding in this paper that should bother anyone modeling the Phase three.

Fourth, a useful counterweight published the same day. BioSpace ran an opinion piece by a medicinal chemist and machine learning scientist at Avicenna arguing that foundation models will change drug design, but not in the way the field is selling. His two objections are worth holding onto because they are structural rather than rhetorical. One: the scientific literature these models ingest carries a well-documented reproducibility problem, and a reproducible model connection built on irreproducible science is not progress. Two: probability distributions in chemistry data shift with context — a substituent change that improves potency in one chemical series can be actively misleading for a model trained across series, because the cause-and-effect relationship in the data quietly moved. He takes direct aim at the claim, made in Anthropic's Claude Science launch demo, that A-I-enabled biology will deliver a hundred years of progress in ten, pointing out that would require more than four hundred seventy novel drug approvals every year for a decade. Where he does see near-term value is narrower and, I think, more credible: identifying novel biological networks as targets, and mining clinical data for patient selection and translational endpoints. Hold that next to the Insilico paper — a target-discovery win plus a biomarker story is almost exactly the shape of value he describes.

Fifth, from China's vaccine bench. Clover Biopharmaceuticals reported positive Phase two data on two combination respiratory vaccines in older adults — four hundred twenty participants aged sixty to eighty-five, run in Australia, placebo-controlled. S-C-B ten twenty-two is a bivalent shot against respiratory syncytial virus and human metapneumovirus; S-C-B ten thirty-three adds parainfluenza virus type three. Both are built on Clover's trimer-tag protein platform. The strategically interesting slot here is human metapneumovirus — there is no approved vaccine against it anywhere, and it is a meaningful driver of lower respiratory tract disease in exactly this age group. G-S-K, Pfizer and Moderna have all won the standalone R-S-V market and are now racing toward combinations, mostly pairing R-S-V with influenza or coronavirus. Clover is combining within the paramyxovirus and pneumovirus family instead, which is a different bet: less overlap with the flu franchises, more dependence on an endpoint nobody has yet validated with a regulator.

Sixth, two smaller items on inhaled delivery, which is quietly the theme of the day. Rezubio announced dual investigational new drug clearances from both the F-D-A and China's N-M-P-A for R-Z five-twenty and has dosed the first cohort in Phase one. It is an inhaled agent for chronic respiratory disease and the second candidate into the clinic from what the company calls membrane-anchored drug design — engineering the molecule to stay anchored and locally active in lung tissue while limiting systemic exposure. Simultaneous U-S and China I-N-Ds from a China-origin platform company is the part worth noting. Separately, Aptar Pharma and Aceso Therapeutics announced a collaboration on A-C-T one-oh-one, an antisense oligonucleotide for cystic fibrosis, with Aptar's Nanopharm unit leading formulation and device work to get it into the lung. No economics disclosed. The relevance is the roughly one in ten cystic fibrosis patients whose mutations are not addressable by C-F-T-R modulators — that population is the entire remaining commercial and clinical opportunity in the disease, and the bottleneck for nucleic acid approaches there has always been delivery, not chemistry.

And seventh, briefly logged: SynAct Pharma released a further analysis of its Phase two-b ADVANCE study of resomelagon in rheumatoid arthritis, arguing the reanalysis reinforces the earlier EXPAND results and supports Phase three at forty milligrams once daily, with a proposed design and toxicology package going to the F-D-A for a Type C meeting early in the fourth quarter and feedback expected around year end. Resomelagon is a melanocortin receptor agonist — the resolution-pharmacology thesis, pushing inflammation toward active resolution rather than blocking a cytokine. Read the framing carefully: the company's own language is about reducing the impact of baseline instability and prospectively powering the A-C-R twenty and A-C-R fifty comparisons. That is a post-hoc rescue of a trial that did not cleanly succeed, and the F-D-A meeting is the event, not this analysis.
