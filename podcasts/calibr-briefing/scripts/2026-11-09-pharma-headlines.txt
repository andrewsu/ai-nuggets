Good morning, Travis. It's Monday, November ninth, 2026. Three meetings ran through the weekend: the liver meeting, AASLD, in Denver, the American Heart Association's sessions in Chicago, and SITC, the cancer immunotherapy meeting in Phoenix. On the list: a pivotal win for Vir in hepatitis delta, a single shot of a genetics-derived s-i-R-N-A that cleared fat from the liver, an A-I discovery deal for Pierre Fabre, a two-antibody approach to prostate cancer from Johnson and Johnson, another PD-1 VEGF bispecific chasing ivonescimab, and a null trial on cardiac arrest. Here's what you need to know.

First, and this is today's spotlight. On Sunday at AASLD, Vir Biotechnology reported that ECLIPSE 1, the first of three Phase 3 trials of its hepatitis delta combination, met its primary endpoint. The regimen is two monthly injections given under the skin. One is elebsiran, an s-i-R-N-A licensed from Alnylam that silences hepatitis B's R-N-A and so shuts down the surface antigen that the delta virus needs as its coat. The other is tobevibart, an antibody against that same surface antigen. In a hundred twenty-four patients, more than half of them with cirrhosis, forty-four percent had undetectable virus plus a normal liver enzyme at week forty-eight, against zero in a delayed-treatment arm. Seventy-nine percent had undetectable virus, and there were no discontinuations. Vir plans to file in mid-twenty twenty-seven. Gilead's Hepcludex was approved in the U-S in May, and Mirum's brelovitug is racing Vir to a filing. The spotlight takes on how good these numbers really are, and who wins this market.

Second, a callback to yesterday's spotlight on genetic validation. Regeneron presented, and published in the New England Journal of Medicine, the first human data for its CIDEB s-i-R-N-A in fatty liver disease. CIDEB is a protein on the fat droplets inside liver cells. The Regeneron Genetics Center found that people born with broken copies of the gene are protected from liver disease, and Regeneron built the drug with Alnylam to copy that. A single injection cut liver fat by fifty-eight to eighty percent at week thirty-six, depending on dose, and the effect deepened over time. Sixty-two percent of treated patients got their liver fat back into the normal range, versus none on placebo, and liver enzymes came down. There was no nausea or vomiting, which is the obvious contrast with the G-L-P-one drugs. For scale, Regeneron notes that the approved MASH drugs cut liver fat by roughly half at about a year. This is thirty patients on drug, and fat on an M-R-I scan is not fibrosis on a biopsy. Phase 2b and Phase 3 trials are already recruiting, and Regeneron has an investor event on its MASH pipeline tomorrow morning. After HORIZON and ZEUS, a clean target-engagement story from human genetics is welcome, but the lesson from last week is that the outcome trial is what counts.

Third, A-I in discovery. Pierre Fabre, the French oncology and dermatology company, signed a multi-program deal with XtalPi, the Shenzhen and Hong Kong-listed company that pairs A-I design with robotic chemistry labs. XtalPi will design new small molecules from scratch against undisclosed, hard-to-drug targets in oncology, possibly dermatology, and rare diseases. Pierre Fabre picks the compounds and takes them into the clinic. There's an upfront payment, milestones and downstream economics, but no numbers. The deal is small, but it fits a pattern: mid-sized European pharmas without big in-house computational groups are renting A-I design capacity rather than building it, and Chinese platforms are winning a lot of that business.

Fourth, from SITC: Johnson and Johnson showed first-in-human data for a regimen of two bispecific antibodies in metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer. Pasritamig binds KLK2, a protease made almost only by prostate cells, and CD3 on T cells, which delivers the T cell's first signal. JNJ-9401 binds PSMA and CD28, which supplies the second, costimulatory signal. Two antigens, two signals, and the idea is that a T cell gets fully switched on only where both proteins are present. At the recommended regimen, both drugs every six weeks, eleven of fifteen heavily pretreated patients had their P-S-A fall by at least half, and seven by at least ninety percent. All seven of those stayed on treatment past six months without progression. There were no grade three or worse treatment-related side effects, and cytokine release was grade one in three patients. Fifteen patients is very few, but J&J has already moved the combination into a Phase 3 called KLK2-comPAS. Prostate cancer has resisted immunotherapy for a long time, so this is worth tracking. It's also worth noting for Vir, whose masked PSMA T-cell engager, partnered with Astellas, heads into pivotal trials next year.

Fifth, also from SITC: OncoC4's cesalatamig, a PD-1 VEGF bispecific, showed activity on its own in lung cancer that had already progressed on PD-1 drugs. At the highest dose reported, ten milligrams per kilogram every three weeks, a third of twenty-one patients responded and two-thirds were progression-free at six months. Lower doses did less, and response tracked drug exposure. OncoC4 says cesalatamig binds VEGF forty-six times more tightly than ivonescimab, and its Fc is silenced so it spares T cells. The cost is real: grade three treatment-related side effects in thirty-six percent at that dose, mostly hypertension and protein in the urine. A Phase 3 in second-line lung cancer starts in twenty twenty-seven. The class's first U-S verdict comes Friday, when the F-D-A decides on Summit and Akeso's ivonescimab.

Sixth, briefly, in lipids: Innovent's PCSK9 antibody tafolecimab, already sold in China, met its Phase 3 endpoint as monotherapy, with no statin, in a hundred ninety-eight lower-risk Chinese patients. L-D-L fell about sixty-five percent at twelve weeks and lipoprotein little a by nearly half. Innovent will file for a first-line label in China. A PCSK9 drug used without a statin, cheap enough to be on China's reimbursement list, is a different commercial model from anything in the U-S.

Seventh, a sobering result from AHA, published in the New England Journal: a randomized trial of community-wide training for 911 dispatchers, first responders and bystanders didn't improve survival after sudden cardiac arrest outside the hospital. About one in ten people survive these events in the U-S. As STAT's Elizabeth Cooney reports, investigators think more time, or a Danish-style requirement for CPR education, may be needed.
