Today's spotlight is PepGen, a small Boston company whose stock nearly doubled on Tuesday after six patients took four doses of an oligonucleotide. That sentence should make you suspicious, and it should also make you curious, because the number underneath it is the largest anyone has reported in myotonic dystrophy type one, and it landed five days after the biggest company in the field missed on a Phase three.

Start with the disease, because the biology here is unusual and it determines what counts as evidence. Myotonic dystrophy type one is caused by an expanded repeat of three D-N-A letters, cytosine-thymine-guanine, in a gene called D-M-P-K. The expanded repeat is transcribed into R-N-A, and that R-N-A is the toxin. It folds into hairpins that sequester a family of splicing regulators called muscleblind. Those proteins are supposed to be out in the nucleus deciding which exons go into which messenger R-N-As. Trapped in R-N-A foci, they can't do that, and hundreds of transcripts across the body get spliced the way they would be in a fetus rather than in an adult. Muscle doesn't relax properly, which is the myotonia patients describe as a hand that won't let go. The heart conducts badly. The gut slows. Cataracts come early. Roughly a hundred ten thousand people in the United States and Europe have it, average life expectancy in the adult-onset form is somewhere around fifty to sixty years, and nothing approved touches the cause.

What makes the disease unusual from a drug development standpoint is that the molecular defect is directly measurable in the tissue you care about. You can biopsy muscle, sequence the transcripts, and ask what fraction of the mis-spliced events have gone back toward normal. PepGen uses a twenty-two gene panel. That gives the field something most rare diseases don't have: a quantitative, mechanistically tight biomarker that sits one step downstream of the drug and upstream of every symptom.

Here's what PepGen reported. The ten-milligram-per-kilogram cohort of its Phase two FREEDOM2 study enrolled eight patients, randomized three to one, so six got drug and two got placebo, four monthly intravenous infusions. At week thirteen, mean splicing correction was forty-six and a half percent. Five of the six cleared twenty percent. Four of the six cleared fifty percent. The pooled placebo group, four patients drawn from this cohort and the one before it, sat at six point eight percent. The company says it's the highest splicing correction ever reported in a multi-dose D-M-one study, and as far as I can tell that claim holds.

The molecule is an oligonucleotide conjugated to a cell-penetrating peptide. PepGen calls the platform enhanced delivery oligonucleotide. The peptide is the whole point. Naked oligonucleotides reach the liver and the kidney and essentially stop there; muscle is a notoriously hard tissue to get into, and nuclear delivery inside muscle is harder still, which is where this drug has to go to free up those splicing proteins. So the forty-six percent number isn't really a statement about the oligonucleotide sequence. It's a statement about how much of the dose got where it needed to be.

Now the functional data, which is where I'd put most of the weight, and also most of the caution. Video hand opening time is an objective camera-based measure of how long it takes a patient to open a clenched fist. It improved three point nine seconds at week sixteen, about fifty-three percent off baseline, against two tenths of a second on placebo. Alongside that, PepGen saw dose-dependent correction of splicing in C-L-C-N-one, the muscle chloride channel whose mis-splicing is the leading mechanistic explanation for myotonia. That's the pairing that matters: the biomarker moved, the channel it should have fixed moved, and the symptom that channel controls moved, in the same patients. Grip strength improved about sixteen percent. Timed up-and-go improved about eight and a half percent, roughly six tenths of a second, while placebo got half a second worse. The ten-meter walk improved about seven percent. Patient-reported outcomes were inconclusive, and the company said so plainly; the health index scores improved in every arm including placebo, which is what small studies with motivated participants tend to produce. Safety was clean at this dose: no serious or severe adverse events, no dose-limiting toxicities, no discontinuations, nausea the most common event. Four mild kidney events, and transient protein in the urine after the first dose that decreased with later doses, with no sign of accumulating damage over sixteen weeks. Hold onto that detail.

Three caveats before the competitive picture. Six patients on drug. An unblinded sponsor press release, not a peer-reviewed paper or a regulatory review. And functional measures in a disease this slow, over sixteen weeks, are not the sort of thing that can distinguish real benefit from practice effects and placebo response. What makes this more than noise is the internal consistency — the dose-dependence, the chloride channel, the placebo arm that moved the wrong way on balance and mobility.

Which brings us to the competitive situation, because that's why this moved the stock ninety percent. Five days earlier, Novartis said del-desiran missed the primary endpoint of its Phase three HARBOR study. Del-desiran is an antibody-oligonucleotide conjugate that rides the transferrin receptor into muscle and knocks down D-M-P-K messenger R-N-A, and it came with Novartis's roughly twelve-billion-dollar acquisition of Avidity. The primary endpoint it missed was hand opening time — the same measure PepGen just reported moving by nearly four seconds. Novartis cited evidence of activity on secondary and exploratory endpoints and said it will talk to regulators about a path forward.

That contrast is the entire investment thesis, and it deserves scrutiny rather than acceptance. Note that the two drugs do different things. Del-desiran degrades the toxic transcript. PepGen's blocks it and frees the splicing proteins. Both should end at the same place if enough drug arrives. The honest reading of a Phase three miss followed by a six-patient win is not that the mechanism is wrong, and not that PepGen has solved it. It's that in this disease, delivery and dose are the variables that separate programs, and that a well-powered trial with real-world enrollment is a much harsher test than eight patients at a handful of expert sites. Dyne is the one to watch on this point: z-basivarsen's registrational expansion cohort closed at seventy-one patients and reads out in the first quarter of 2027, with an accelerated approval filing planned for the third quarter. That's the readout that will tell us whether the delivery-plus-splicing story survives contact with a real sample size.

And there's a complication specific to PepGen that most of Tuesday's coverage buried. FREEDOM2 has been under a partial clinical hold since March, placed over nonclinical findings in mice. The company is running additional mouse studies and remains in discussion with the agency. The end-of-Phase-two meeting it's pointing to in the second half of 2027 is explicitly contingent on that hold coming off. Remember the kidney signal I flagged: transient albuminuria after dosing is exactly the class of finding that makes a reviewer want more preclinical work on a systemically delivered, cell-penetrating peptide. The twelve-and-a-half-milligram cohort is fully enrolled with patients already dosed, reading out in the first half of 2027. PepGen ended September with a hundred four point eight million dollars and guides into 2028, which is enough to get through both of those catalysts and not much more.

So what should Calibr and Foresite take from this?

First, when a field has a tissue-accessible, mechanistically proximal biomarker, use it as the primary design variable from the beginning. PepGen can read a twenty-two gene splicing panel out of a muscle biopsy and know within three months whether more drug reached the nucleus. That is a dose-finding instrument most programs would pay enormously for, and it's the reason a six-patient cohort moved a market cap. If any Calibr program has an equivalent — a receptor occupancy, a pathway transcript signature, a direct engagement readout — the question worth asking is whether it's being run as a confirmatory nicety or as the thing that actually chooses the dose.

Second, the harder lesson, which runs the other way. The biomarker got PepGen a ninety percent day. It did not save del-desiran, and del-desiran also had target engagement. Splicing correction is an excellent instrument for picking a dose and a weak one for predicting whether patients will feel better. Treat it as the steering wheel, not the destination.

Third, delivery remains the product in muscle. Three different conjugation strategies — a cell-penetrating peptide, an antibody to the transferrin receptor, and Sarepta and Arrowhead's muscle-targeted approach — are competing on the same payload biology. The differentiated asset in this field is the thing that gets the cargo into the nucleus of a myocyte, not the oligonucleotide riding along. For any platform company, that's an argument for owning the delivery vehicle rather than the sequence.

And fourth, a regulatory note worth internalizing. PepGen's most important disclosure Tuesday was not the forty-six percent. It was the sentence about the partial clinical hold, which gates everything after the next cohort. The nonclinical package on a novel delivery peptide is not paperwork you clear on the way to a Phase three. It is the Phase three timeline.

What to watch: the twelve-and-a-half-milligram cohort and the open-label extension data in January and the first half of next year, whether the hold lifts, and above all Dyne's seventy-one-patient readout in the first quarter. If a properly sized trial reproduces what six patients showed this week, myotonic dystrophy becomes a real market. If it doesn't, Tuesday was a very good day for a very small number.
