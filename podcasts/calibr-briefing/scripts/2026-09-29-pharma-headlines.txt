Good morning Travis. Here are your biotech and pharma headlines for Tuesday, September twenty-ninth, twenty twenty-six. Monday was one of the heaviest deal days of the quarter. AstraZeneca put two billion dollars of equity into Summit Therapeutics and bought itself a position in the P-D-one by V-E-G-F race without licensing anything at all — that is today's spotlight. Around it, Merck went to China for a K-RAS G-twelve-D inhibitor, Novo went to China this morning for an oral weekly incretin, an off-the-shelf gamma delta C-A-R T-cell put half of a lupus nephritis cohort into complete renal response, Kodiak's stock more than doubled on a pair of eye readouts, Mirum won its first Phase three in hepatitis delta, and Roche killed its muscle-sparing obesity antibody. Eight items, then the week ahead.

First, and today's spotlight. After Monday's close, AstraZeneca agreed to buy two billion dollars of newly issued convertible preferred stock in Summit Therapeutics, taking rights equivalent to about twelve percent of Summit's common shares, or ten point six percent fully diluted. The conversion works out to eighteen dollars and thirty-six cents a common share, which is the five-day volume-weighted average price plus ten percent, and roughly nineteen percent above where Summit closed Monday. Shares jumped eighteen percent after hours. Alongside the equity there is a binding clinical collaboration to test ivonescimab, Summit's P-D-one by V-E-G-F bispecific, with sonesitatug vedotin, AstraZeneca's Claudin eighteen point two antibody-drug conjugate, in gastrointestinal cancers, plus a non-binding memorandum of understanding to pair ivonescimab with more of AstraZeneca's A-D-Cs later. What makes this worth eight minutes is what is not in the deal: no license, no milestones, no royalties, no revenue share, no right of first refusal. AstraZeneca bought exposure to the class leader, not rights to it, and it bought before the readout that actually settles the question.

Second, Merck became the latest large pharma to place a bet on K-RAS G-twelve-D, paying four hundred million dollars upfront to Shanghai's SciBrunch for worldwide rights to a preclinical inhibitor, with milestones that could take the total to two point one three billion. K-RAS was undruggable for decades because the protein has no well-defined binding pocket; Amgen and Mirati cracked the G-twelve-C variant with Lumakras and Krazati, and the field has since moved to the harder and more common variants. G-twelve-D shows up in roughly thirty-eight percent of pancreatic cancers, which is why Bayer paid Kumquat Biosciences up to one point three billion for a preclinical G-twelve-D asset earlier. Merck already has calderasib, a G-twelve-C inhibitor, in Phase three in colorectal and lung cancer, so this diversifies within the pathway rather than entering it. SciBrunch is two years old, was founded by Tao Hu and Yang Zhang, has raised sixty-five million dollars total, and has one brain-penetrant P-A-R-P-one selective inhibitor in the clinic. Four hundred million dollars upfront for a preclinical molecule from a two-year-old company is the clearest available price signal on where G-twelve-D is trading.

Third, this morning Novo Nordisk paid three hundred million dollars upfront to Hengrui Pharma for ex-China rights to H-R-S fifteen ninety-six, a preclinical dual G-L-P-one and G-I-P receptor agonist, with milestones that could bring it to two point six billion. The design point is the interesting one: Hengrui built it for once-weekly oral dosing. Lilly's Zepbound is a G-L-P-one and G-I-P agonist but it is injectable; Viking has an oral dual agonist in Phase two but it is daily. A weekly oral incretin would be a genuinely new dosing category. Hengrui has cleared Chinese regulators to start Phase one. This is Novo's second small deal in as many weeks after the one point three billion dollar Nanexa long-acting delivery agreement, and it fits the pattern Mike Doustdar has been describing to investors — a company buying optionality in small pieces while it tries to convince the market it has a plan.

Fourth, and this one runs straight through your interests. Adicet Bio reported one-year follow-up from its Phase one of prula-cel, an allogeneic gamma delta C-A-R T-cell, in lupus nephritis. Among evaluable patients, fifty percent had a complete renal response and fifty-four percent met a standard remission definition, and all of them came off immunosuppressants. These were patients who had failed multiple prior therapies. There were no serious cases of cytokine release syndrome and no neurotoxicity; about a quarter had mild or moderate C-R-S, more than half had infections, and around eight percent had grade three or higher infections. The comparison that matters is with autologous alpha beta C-A-R T in the same setting, where complete response rates have run around forty percent — and where, a few weeks ago, Novartis and Bristol Myers both halted individualized C-A-R T trials in autoimmune disease over serious toxicity, including deaths from a rare immune reaction. C-E-O Chen Schor's argument is that gamma delta cells secrete fewer and lower levels of the inflammatory cytokines that drive those events. Jefferies analyst Dennis Ding called the data competitive with cell therapy and antibody comparators and said it could push pharma toward what he called more elegant solutions. The stock fell twenty-two percent anyway. A pivotal study is planned for the fourth quarter, and Adicet wants to talk to regulators about widening it beyond lupus nephritis.

Fifth, Kodiak Sciences more than doubled. In the Daybreak Phase three, both tarcocimab tedromer — branded Zenkuda — and tabirafusp alfa matched aflibercept, Regeneron's Eylea, on the primary vision endpoint in wet age-related macular degeneration at one year. Shares opened Monday up one hundred twenty-eight percent, to just under seventy-three dollars from a Friday close of thirty-two. The durability claim is the product: more than half of tarcocimab patients were maintained on twenty-four-week dosing through year one against every eight weeks for aflibercept, and retreatment was guided by an A-I algorithm rather than a fixed schedule — worth noting as one of the few places an algorithm is embedded in a registrational dosing strategy. Kodiak plans a multi-indication filing in the fourth quarter spanning five Phase three trials. Two cautions. Tarcocimab was shelved in twenty twenty-three over an unexpected increase in cataracts and only revived on diabetic retinopathy data; Kodiak now reports numerically fewer cataracts than aflibercept but has not released the detailed comparison. And aflibercept is off patent, so the commercial fight is against biosimilars as well as Eylea H-D and Roche's Vabysmo. Tabirafusp, the interleukin-six and V-E-G-F trap bispecific, matched aflibercept but got almost no airtime in the release; Kodiak will go hunting post hoc for the subgroup where blocking interleukin-six actually adds something.

Sixth, Mirum Pharmaceuticals hit the primary endpoint in the Phase three portion of Azure-one with brelovitug in chronic hepatitis delta. Brelovitug is an antibody against hepatitis B surface antigen, dosed subcutaneously either three hundred milligrams weekly or nine hundred milligrams every four weeks. The primary endpoint combined virologic response with normalization of A-L-T, and the response rates were fifty-six percent on the weekly dose, forty-five percent on the monthly, and zero percent in the delayed-treatment control. Roughly eighty-five percent had a virologic response on its own. Nancy Shulman at Mirum made the case for the composite: virologic response alone does not mean liver inflammation has resolved, and inflammation is what drives fibrosis and cancer. Leerink called the profile commercially differentiated in a nascent market, flagging the enrollment of severe patients and the low rate of flu-like symptoms as the edges against Vir's elebsiran and tobevibart combination. Gilead's Hepcludex was approved on a forty-eight percent response on the same composite. Mirum picked this asset up in the six hundred twenty million dollar Bluejay Therapeutics acquisition late last year; a second Phase three, Azure-four, reads out in the fourth quarter, with a filing possible in the first half of next year.

Seventh, Roche gave up on emugrobart. The antibody blocks myostatin, the natural brake on muscle growth, and the thesis was to pair it with a G-L-P-one so patients keep lean mass while losing fat. An interim analysis of the Phase two Gyminda trial concluded success was unlikely, and Roche has handed the molecule back to Chugai. This was already a wounded program — emugrobart failed to improve muscle growth in spinal muscular atrophy and in facioscapulohumeral dystrophy back in March. Chugai now says it will prepare a Phase three in spinal muscular atrophy anyway, on the strength of the safety and dosing data, while looking for an out-licensing partner. The muscle-preservation field does not shrink from this — AstraZeneca, Biohaven and Wave are all still in it — but this is the second clean readout suggesting that myostatin blockade produces less functional muscle than the mechanism promises.

Eighth, Tolerance Bio licensed an anti-interleukin-thirty-three antibody from Tanabe Pharma for milestones up to five hundred sixty million dollars, ex-Japan. The asset, now T-L-B thirty-three, has been through five trials in more than a hundred fifty patients. Tolerance's whole thesis is the thymus: the organ that develops and selects T cells involutes with age, and the company is betting you can treat immune-mediated disease by preserving or restoring thymic function. Interleukin-thirty-three signaling has been implicated in thymic involution under inflammatory conditions, which is the link. This is the second in-licensing in two months after a two hundred sixty million dollar deal with NeoImmuneTech for a long-acting interleukin-seven fusion protein. A company that launched in October twenty twenty-four with seventeen million dollars in seed money now has two clinical-stage assets and is raising to fund two Phase two trials. Whether or not the thymic thesis holds, that is an efficient way to build a pipeline.

Three quick ones. The F-D-A approved Egetis Therapeutics' Emcitate, tiratricol, for peripheral thyrotoxicosis in M-C-T eight deficiency, also called Allan-Herndon-Dudley syndrome — an X-linked disorder where a broken thyroid hormone transporter starves the brain of thyroid signaling while flooding peripheral tissue with T-three. Median life expectancy is about thirty-five years and this is the first approved treatment in the United States. Second, Eccogene collected a fifty million dollar milestone from AstraZeneca for the start of the global Phase three program for elecoglipron, a once-daily oral small-molecule G-L-P-one agonist, which showed eleven point eight percent weight loss at thirty-six weeks and a one point nine point hemoglobin A-one-C reduction at twenty-six weeks in Phase two-b. The Phase three program runs into obesity, type two diabetes, heart failure with preserved and mildly reduced ejection fraction, and chronic kidney disease. Third, AstraZeneca submitted a US application for savolitinib plus osimertinib in M-E-T-overexpressed or amplified non-small cell lung cancer after progression on an E-G-F-R inhibitor, on the global SAFFRON Phase three, which beat platinum doublet chemotherapy on both progression-free and overall survival.
