Amlitelimab is an antibody against O-X-forty ligand, a T-cell costimulation switch, that Sanofi got when it bought Kymab back in twenty twenty-one for around one and a half billion dollars, and it was once one of Sanofi's top three pipeline assets with peak-sales hopes north of five billion euros. Second, a rare open window in gene editing. Scribe Therapeutics priced its I-P-O and raised about a hundred and twenty-nine million dollars, selling shares at fifteen dollars apiece and listing on the Nasdaq under the ticker S-C-T-X. What makes it notable is that this is the first I-P-O for a gene-editing developer in more than two years. Scribe was co-founded by Jennifer Doudna, the CRISPR Nobel laureate, and it engineers its own compact CRISPR enzymes rather than using the standard Cas9. Its lead programs are in vivo editors aimed at cardiovascular risk, going after P-C-S-K-nine and lipoprotein little-a. It's a small raise, but after a brutal stretch for the platform, it's a real signal that the I-P-O window is cracking open for the harder science.

Third, the F-D-A took on the peptide gray market. An agency advisory committee spent two days reviewing seven popular peptides, the kind sold widely through compounding pharmacies and telehealth for everything from healing to longevity, deciding whether they belong on the list of substances that can legally be compounded. The panel actually backed most of them, including B-P-C one-fifty-seven and T-B-five-hundred. But in its first real pushback, it voted down emideltide, a peptide pitched for insomnia and opioid withdrawal, on concerns the human data were thin and the mechanism carried addiction potential. Watch this one, because the compounding question is what underpins a big chunk of the direct-to-consumer wellness economy, and companies like Hims and Hers move on these votes.

Fourth, the obesity fight went to court. Novo Nordisk escalated its legal battle with Eli Lilly, asking a court for a preliminary injunction to block what it calls misleading comparative advertising around the two companies' G-L-P-one weight-loss drugs. This is the same rivalry we've been tracking on the science side, retatrutide and the rest, now spilling into litigation over how each side is allowed to describe the other in ads. It's a sign of how bare-knuckle the obesity market has gotten now that the clinical differences are getting litigated as marketing claims.

And fifth, a rare-disease setback. Ipsen said its drug Bylvay, generic name odevixibat, failed a Phase 3 trial in biliary atresia, a serious pediatric liver disease. Bylvay is a bile-acid transport blocker, an I-BAT inhibitor, that Ipsen acquired when it bought Albireo, and it's already approved in rarer cholestatic liver conditions. Biliary atresia was the big label-expansion bet, a much larger patient population, and this readout takes that off the table for now. It's a reminder that even a de-risked, already-approved mechanism doesn't automatically travel to the next indication.
