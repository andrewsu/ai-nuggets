Good morning, Travis. It's Wednesday, November fourth, 2026. Election night and a full slate of earnings, so there's a lot to get through. On the list: a one-and-a-half-billion-dollar Becker muscular dystrophy bet fails five months after the check cleared, Democrats take both chambers of Congress, Ultragenyx cuts a fifth of its staff right after two gene therapy approvals, Pfizer raises guidance and clears out its pipeline, Novo writes off two billion dollars of misses, a Chinese biotech gets a hundred and ten million dollars upfront for Japan rights alone, and two big companies walk away from things they paid for last year. Here's what you need to know.

First, and this is today's spotlight. Servier said the GRAND CANYON study of sevasemten in Becker muscular dystrophy missed its primary endpoint, the North Star Ambulatory Assessment at eighteen months, and every secondary endpoint too. Sevasemten is an oral inhibitor of fast skeletal muscle myosin, meant to protect dystrophin-deficient muscle from being damaged by its own contractions. Servier bought it from Edgewise in June for one point five five billion dollars upfront, plus up to one point one billion in milestones, and closed in July. The spotlight goes through why a Phase 2 that hit on muscle damage markers didn't predict function, and who ended up holding the risk.

Second, the midterms. According to the A-P count STAT cited early this morning, Democrats won control of both chambers, with at least fifty-four Senate seats and two hundred twenty-eight in the House. Races are still being called. That gives Democrats subpoena power and committee gavels over H-H-S, the F-D-A and the most-favored-nation pricing deals. Albert Bourla, asked before the results came in, told reporters he does not expect any changes to the M-F-N agreements drugmakers signed with the Trump administration. That's plausible for the deals themselves, which are contracts with the executive branch. Oversight of how they were negotiated is a different question, and it's now on the table.

Third, Ultragenyx. Two F-D-A gene therapy approvals in a month, Genglycos for glycogen storage disease type one-A and Fayuvi for Sanfilippo syndrome type A, and now a twenty percent cut in headcount, to about a thousand people. It's also terminating U-X seven-oh-one, its A-A-V nine gene therapy for Wilson disease, for "not achieving study goals," and finishing the wind-down of its Angelman program. The 2027 targets are R and D under three hundred fifty million dollars and S-G-and-A under three hundred seventy-five million, with a full-year GAAP profit in 2027. The detail that tells you where the money is coming from: the two priority review vouchers from those approvals sold for four hundred thirteen million combined, about three hundred fifty million net. That's nearly as much as the four hundred one million in cash it had at quarter-end. Third-quarter revenue was a hundred seventy-three million. So for now, the vouchers are funding the turn to profit more than the two launches are.

Fourth, Pfizer raised 2026 revenue guidance by one and a half billion dollars at the midpoint, to sixty-two to sixty-four billion. Third-quarter revenue was sixteen point six billion, flat operationally, but up seven percent excluding Comirnaty and Paxlovid. Nurtec grew thirty-three percent and Lorbrena forty-three. Pfizer also named Cecile Guegan chief financial officer. The pipeline update was a clear-out, and Fierce went through it. Pfizer terminated PF-oh-seven-eight-six-eight-four-eight-nine, a B-M-P nine antagonist for pulmonary arterial hypertension, for business reasons. That program came out of the counterintuitive finding that blocking B-M-P nine protects rodents from the disease. It also dropped an anti-C-X-C-R-five antibody, and in obesity it ended a Phase 1 that combined a G-I-P receptor agonist and a G-L-P-one agonist from the Metsera acquisition, along with an earlier-stage obesity asset. The Metsera G-L-P-one itself continues in other studies. So Pfizer paid up for an obesity portfolio and is now narrowing what it develops.

Fifth, Novo reported this morning, with about two point two billion dollars of impairments. Six hundred ninety million is from the failure of ziltivekimab, its anti-I-L-six antibody for cardiovascular disease, and six hundred million from scrapping monlunabant, a peripherally restricted C-B-one inverse agonist for obesity. Adjusted operating profit grew twenty-seven percent on oral Wegovy, but reported operating profit fell nine percent. C-F-O Karsten Munk Knudsen is leaving at year-end. C-E-O Mike Doustdar told Fierce Novo is still looking for deals to "close some of the gaps" in the pipeline.

Sixth, an I-G-F-one-R antibody worth watching. Hangzhou-based Minghui Pharmaceutical licensed Japan rights to M-H-B-oh-eighteen-A, a subcutaneous single-domain antibody against the I-G-F-one receptor for thyroid eye disease, to A-S-K-A Pharmaceutical. That's a hundred ten million dollars upfront, up to three hundred thirty million in milestones, and tiered royalties up to the mid-thirties percent, for one country. Minghui keeps everything outside Japan and is running four pivotal Phase 3s, two in China and two global. The incumbent here is Amgen's Tepezza, the intravenous I-G-F-one-R antibody teprotumumab. It fell six percent year over year to five hundred twenty-six million dollars in Amgen's third quarter, which came out last night with total revenue up eight percent to ten point four billion. A subcutaneous, every-four-weeks I-G-F-one-R would take on Tepezza's infusion burden directly. Paying a hundred ten million for Japan alone tells you what A-S-K-A thinks of the data.

Seventh, Rhythm and L-G Chem rewrote their license for bivamelagon, the oral M-C-four-R agonist for hypothalamic obesity. The deal's maximum goes from three hundred five million dollars to eight hundred fifty-five million. Rhythm pays three hundred fifty million more over four years, seventy million of it this month, in exchange for royalties dropping to one percent, plus a two hundred million dollar payment if the M-C-four-R franchise reaches three billion in sales. William Blair's Alethia Young: "Rhythm is paying more up front to lower its long-term costs on a platform it believes is highly valuable." Imcivree, the injectable, grew twenty-nine percent to ninety-two million in the quarter.

Eighth, two walk-aways. AbbVie discontinued livmoniplimab, an antibody that binds the GARP–TGF-beta-one complex on regulatory T cells to stop active TGF-beta from being released. It had been paired with AbbVie's own PD-1, budigalimab, in a Phase 2/3 against Keytruda in lung cancer. AbbVie said its benefit "appears limited relative to the current standards of care." That's another blow to the idea of fixing PD-1 resistance by blocking TGF-beta. And Novartis ended its BioArctic alliance about a year after paying thirty million dollars, a deal that could have been worth more than seven hundred seventy million. It paired BioArctic's BrainTransporter, a transferrin receptor shuttle for crossing the blood-brain barrier, with a Novartis antibody. Novartis called it a reprioritization of early research, unrelated to safety or efficacy. The contrast came the same day: Denali, which uses the same transferrin receptor shuttle idea, reported seventeen point eight million dollars in second-quarter-of-launch sales for Avlayah in Hunter syndrome, guided the fourth quarter to thirty to thirty-two million, and plans a 2027 filing for a second brain-penetrant enzyme in Sanfilippo type A. So shuttles are working. Novartis cut a program, not the technology.

Beam says a filing for risto-cel, its base-edited cell therapy for sickle cell disease, could come as early as year-end, and the pivotal cohort for BEAM-three-oh-two in alpha-one antitrypsin deficiency should finish dosing in the first half of 2027.
