Good morning, Travis. It's Friday, November twenty-seventh, 2026, the day after Thanksgiving. U-S markets were closed Thursday and close early today, and the American trade press took the day off. Europe and Asia didn't, so most of this briefing comes from there. HUTCHMED and AstraZeneca filed in China to move their MET-plus-EGFR pill combination into first-line lung cancer, and that's today's spotlight. The European Parliament finished the EU's biggest rewrite of drug law in twenty years. Neuralink has F-D-A clearance for the first pivotal trial of an implanted brain-computer interface. Bachem, the peptide maker that supplies the obesity drug business, fell twelve percent on its new long-range plan. Bayer is putting up to half a billion euros into a Beijing plant. The first generic shipments of the twice-yearly H-I-V prevention shot reached Africa. And InnoCare won a pediatric approval in China for its next-generation TRK inhibitor. Here's what you need to know.

First, the spotlight item. On Thursday HUTCHMED said China's drug regulator accepted its application for Orpathys, or savolitinib, plus AstraZeneca's Tagrisso, osimertinib, as first-line treatment for E-G-F-R-mutated non-small cell lung cancer whose tumors overexpress MET. Savolitinib is a selective inhibitor of MET, the receptor tyrosine kinase that E-G-F-R-mutant tumors switch on to get around E-G-F-R drugs. The filing rests on the Phase three SANOVO trial, shown at ESMO in October. Adding savolitinib to Tagrisso took median progression-free survival from about fourteen months to about twenty-five, and the overall survival hazard ratio was zero point four eight. The obvious question is whether a China-only trial in a biomarker-selected group changes first-line practice anywhere else. That's the spotlight.

Second, Europe. On Wednesday in Strasbourg, the European Parliament completed the EU pharmaceutical package, the first full rewrite of the bloc's medicines law since the early two-thousands. It also passed the Critical Medicines Act, five hundred ninety-two votes to thirty-five. The package cuts baseline protection for a new medicine to eight years of data protection plus one year of market protection, down from ten years in total today, with extra time up to eleven years for things like meeting an unmet need. It adds a transferable voucher, worth an extra year of data protection, for each new priority antibiotic. Every company holding a prescription-drug authorization will have to write a shortage prevention plan that maps its manufacturing sites. The Critical Medicines Act still needs final Council sign-off. It lets five or more countries buy medicines jointly and lets procurement reward European manufacturing. Industry groups warned that Europe is losing ground to the U-S and China. They lost the argument over the length of protection, and the shortage rules will land on manufacturers and their contract suppliers first.

Third, Neuralink. Late Wednesday the company said the F-D-A has approved its LINK pivotal study, the first pivotal trial of an implantable brain-computer interface in the United States. The trial will enroll people with severe paralysis from A-L-S and measure whether they can control digital devices by thought, with outcomes centered on independence in daily life. It builds on early feasibility studies in the U-S, Canada and the U-K, where more than thirty people have been implanted. Neuralink didn't disclose enrollment targets, endpoints or sites. A pivotal trial is the one that supports a marketing application, so the regulatory race with Synchron, Paradromics and Precision Neuroscience is now about who files first, and in which patients.

Fourth, Bachem. Bachem is a Swiss contract manufacturer of peptide and oligonucleotide ingredients, and its biggest customers include Lilly and Novo. At its capital markets day in Zurich on Thursday, it targeted fifteen to eighteen percent annual sales growth through 2031, from a 2025 base of six hundred ninety-five million Swiss francs. It also targeted an EBITDA margin of thirty-three to thirty-five percent by 2031, up from twenty-eight and a half. To get there, it will spend twenty-five to thirty percent of sales on capital projects from 2027 through 2031, mostly at two Swiss sites and new U-S capacity. That money will come from cash flow, customer prepayments and credit lines, and possibly bonds. The shares fell as much as twelve percent and closed down about eleven. They had hit a fifty-two-week high in October. The market had priced in a faster ramp from the obesity drug business. Bachem said it will take share in a market growing ten to fifteen percent a year, and that it will build ahead of demand to do it. Its big new Sisslerfeld peptide plant, which has a customer agreement with prepayments behind it, doesn't start commercial production until 2030.

Fifth, Bayer, which is having quite a week. On Wednesday it licensed Hansoh's EGFR-by-c-MET bispecific antibody-drug conjugate and said it would pull Kerendia from Germany over price. On Thursday it said it will invest up to five hundred million euros to expand its Beijing pharmaceutical supply center, its largest single pharma investment in China. The new capacity is for cardiovascular drugs and future oncology products. Washington is pushing companies to cut China ties, and a House committee this week urged curbs on licensing deals with Chinese biotechs. Bayer is doing the opposite on both fronts. It is building manufacturing in China while withdrawing a drug from its own home market over price.

Sixth, H-I-V prevention. India's Hetero has begun shipping generic lenacapavir to Africa, starting with three hundred thousand vials for Zambia, Uganda and Zimbabwe. Lenacapavir is Gilead's capsid inhibitor, sold in the U-S as Yeztugo, and given as a pre-exposure prophylaxis shot twice a year. Gilead signed non-exclusive voluntary licenses with six generic makers covering a hundred twenty countries. The Gates Foundation backed Hetero with upfront funding and volume guarantees last year. Hetero says it can make fifteen million vials a year. Gilead will keep supplying the oral starter tablets until generic versions arrive. For now, the question is less whether the drug works than how fast health systems can get it to people.

Seventh, InnoCare. China approved zurletrectinib for children aged two to twelve whose solid tumors carry N-TRK gene fusions, a year after its approval for adults and adolescents. Zurletrectinib is a next-generation pan-TRK inhibitor. It's designed to get into the brain and to overcome resistance to first-generation drugs like Bayer's Vitrakvi. In the pediatric registrational trial, the independent-review response rate was one hundred percent, and seventy-four percent of responses were still ongoing at two years. InnoCare didn't give the number of children in the trial, and that matters a lot when the response rate is a hundred percent. The pediatric version is a tablet that dissolves in the mouth, which in practice matters about as much as the response data.

One quick one: Bavarian Nordic named Allan Bødskov Andersen, now group finance chief at the Danish energy-tech company Topsoe, as its next C-F-O. He joins by June to replace Henrik Juuel.

Looking ahead: U-S markets close at one p-m Eastern today. HUTCHMED holds a corporate day on December eighth. Cogent's mastocytosis decision is December thirtieth, and Vertex's povetacicept decision is now due by the end of February. The San Antonio Breast Cancer Symposium and ASH are both in December. We found no new Foresite portfolio announcements on Thursday or Friday-morning wires.
