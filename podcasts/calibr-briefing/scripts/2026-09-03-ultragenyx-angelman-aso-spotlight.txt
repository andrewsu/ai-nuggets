This is your spotlight for Thursday, September third, twenty twenty-six. Ultragenyx, apazunersen, and the most expensive lesson in comparator design that this field has taken in years.

Start with the biology, because the biology is not the problem here and it is important to say that clearly. Angelman syndrome is caused by loss of function of the maternal copy of U-B-E-three-A, a gene encoding a ubiquitin ligase that neurons need. Here is the elegant part: neurons also carry a paternal copy, fully intact, and they silence it — a long non-coding antisense transcript called U-B-E-three-A-A-T-S runs across the locus and shuts the paternal allele down. So every person with Angelman syndrome from a maternal deletion is walking around with a healthy, working copy of the gene they need, switched off by a mechanism we understand. Degrade the antisense transcript and the paternal allele should come back on. You can do that with an antisense oligonucleotide delivered into the cerebrospinal fluid. As therapeutic hypotheses go, this is close to the cleanest one in neurology — no vector, no editing, no delivery of a new gene, just the removal of a repressive R-N-A.

Apazunersen, which most of us still call G-T-X one-oh-two, is that oligonucleotide. It came from GeneTx Biotherapeutics, a company founded for the single purpose of developing it, in partnership with the Foundation for Angelman Syndrome Therapeutics. Ultragenyx held an option and exercised it in July twenty twenty-two for seventy-five million dollars in cash, on the strength of Phase one-two data from eleven patients. That timing matters — the acquisition decision was made on eleven patients in an open-label study.

Aspire, which reported last night, enrolled roughly a hundred twenty-nine children aged four to seventeen with a genetically confirmed full maternal deletion. One-to-one randomization against a sham lumbar puncture. Forty-eight weeks. Three monthly eight-milligram loading doses, then quarterly maintenance escalating to fourteen milligrams. The primary endpoint was change from baseline in the Bayley four cognitive raw score. The key secondary, carrying ten percent of the alpha, was net response on a multi-domain responder index across five domains: cognition, receptive communication, behavior, gross motor function and sleep.

The result, in the company's own language: the randomized groups were comparable at baseline and consistent with the Phase two patients, and there were no differences between the treated and control groups that could support efficacy in the Bayley cognition raw scores, nor in the responder index — not on net response, and not on the mean change in any one of the five individual domains. Safety was consistent with Phase one-two. Six pre-specified measures, six nulls. This is not an underpowered near-miss that a bigger trial rescues. It is a flat line.

Now here is what makes this the story it is, rather than just another Phase three failure. Go back and look at what the Phase one-two data said, because it was not marginal — it was spectacular. At the Foundation for Angelman Syndrome Therapeutics summit in November twenty twenty-four, Ultragenyx reported that in the dose expansion cohorts, forty patients at week forty-eight showed a mean improvement in the Bayley four cognition growth scale value of six point seven points, against a minimally important difference of five. On the exact measure that became the Phase three primary endpoint — the Bayley four cognition raw score — the mean change from baseline was plus ten point nine. On the multi-domain responder index, twenty-eight patients in expansion cohorts A and B produced a total net response of plus two point zero with a p-value below zero point zero zero zero one. Seventy-nine percent of participants improved on at least one domain. There was a long-term extension out past day seven hundred fifty. Cognitive and receptive communication values exceeded what the Angelman Syndrome Natural History Study observed at a comparable timepoint.

And then the line that, read today, is the entire story. When Ultragenyx described the Phase three design, it said Aspire had greater than ninety-five percent power to detect a treatment effect even if the response in the sham arm ran up to three times higher than the changes observed in available natural history data. They knew the natural history comparator was the soft spot. They built in a three-fold margin against it. And the trial still saw nothing.

Think about what that arithmetic requires. If the treated arm in Aspire moved anything like the plus ten point nine seen in Phase one-two, and there was no difference between arms, then the sham arm moved by about the same amount. Children in a sham lumbar puncture arm, receiving no drug, gained on a cognitive raw score at roughly the rate that Ultragenyx had attributed to target engagement. Which means the plus ten point nine was never drug effect. It was some combination of three things that an open-label single-arm study cannot separate from pharmacology.

The first is simply development. These are children aged four to seventeen. Bayley four raw scores are not standardized against age — they are counts of items passed, and they go up as a child grows, even a child with a severe neurodevelopmental disorder. Any single-arm study that follows developing children for forty-eight weeks and measures a raw score will show improvement. The growth scale value transformation helps, but only partially, and the company chose the raw score for the Phase three primary.

The second is the practice effect. The Bayley is administered repeatedly, at fixed intervals, by assessors, to the same child. Children learn the test. In a population where the ceiling is low and the items are basic, repeat exposure produces gains that look exactly like clinical improvement.

The third is expectancy, and this is where the responder index was most exposed. Two of the five domains in that index — behavior and sleep — are caregiver-reported. In an open-label study where a family has driven across the country to enroll their child in the first-ever therapy for a disease that has had nothing, the direction of the reporting bias is not in doubt. Nor is it in doubt for an unblinded assessor scoring a receptive communication item. The multi-domain responder index was designed to be sensitive, and in an open-label setting sensitivity to expectation is indistinguishable from sensitivity to effect.

Add the fourth thing, which nobody labels a bias: children in these trials get intensive attention. Study visits, therapists, specialists, structured assessment. That is an intervention, and it accrues equally to a sham arm.

So the honest summary is not that the drug failed. It is that we still do not know whether the drug does anything, because it was never tested against a control until Aspire, and Aspire says it does no more than the sham. And the field spent a hundred twenty-nine children, four years, and roughly one point one billion dollars of market value to learn something that the design of the earlier study made unknowable from the start.

There is one substantive question left open that I want to flag because it will determine what the full dataset is worth. Ultragenyx's release does not disclose a target engagement biomarker. We do not know from what was published whether the oligonucleotide reduced the antisense transcript in these children's neurons and reactivated paternal U-B-E-three-A protein. Those are two very different failures. If U-B-E-three-A came back on and the children did not improve, the conclusion is developmental — that restoring this ligase in a four-to-seventeen-year-old brain does not undo circuits that formed without it, and the therapeutic window closed years before enrollment. If U-B-E-three-A did not come back on at these doses, the conclusion is pharmacological, and the mechanism is untested rather than refuted. Note that the earlier program has some evidence of biological activity — the twenty twenty clinical hold came from lower-extremity weakness in all five U-S patients, some of whom temporarily could not walk, which resolved after dose amendment. That looked like local activity at the injection site, not target engagement in cortex. Whichever it is, when the full data appear at a meeting, the biomarker table is the first thing to read.

Which brings us to Ionis, and this is where the read-across gets genuinely uncomfortable. Obudanersen, formerly I-O-N five-eighty-two, works by the same mechanism against the same transcript. Ionis has completed enrollment of the pivotal cohort of its Phase three REVEAL study — a hundred thirty-six participants — with topline data guided to the second half of twenty twenty-seven. And the evidence base underneath it has exactly the same architecture as the one that just failed. The Phase one-two HALOS study was open-label. It reported that improvements in communication, cognition and motor function exceeded the Angelman Syndrome Natural History Study on the Bayley four; that at six months roughly sixty-five percent of patients improved in cognition and about seventy percent in fine and gross motor skills; that ninety-seven percent of patients in the medium and high dose groups improved on a clinician global impression of change. Ninety-seven percent on an unblinded global impression scale is not a pharmacology result. It is a measurement of what an open-label study measures.

To be fair to Ionis, REVEAL is placebo-controlled — the failure mode I have described applies to the Phase one-two data that justified it, not to the pivotal study itself. And there are four real differences worth weighing. REVEAL enrolls from age two, not four, which is a meaningfully earlier window if the developmental hypothesis is the right one. It includes U-B-E-three-A mutation as well as deletion genotypes, which broadens the population but also dilutes it. Its primary endpoint is expressive communication on the Bayley rather than cognition, which is arguably the domain where caregivers see change first and where a floor effect is least likely. And its double-blind treatment period is about sixty weeks against Aspire's forty-eight, at a single eighty-milligram dose — Ionis dropped the forty-milligram arm in an amendment in December twenty twenty-five. Any of those could matter. But none of them changes the fact that Ionis's prior probability was built on the same kind of uncontrolled data that just proved not to predict a controlled result. If I were underwriting REVEAL today I would mark down the read-through from HALOS to close to zero and price the trial as a first look at the mechanism.

And then there is Roche, which now looks like the best-informed actor in the class. Roche had rugonersen, the same mechanism, and discontinued internal development in twenty twenty-three. Not for safety. Roche said it did not meet an internal, pre-set efficacy bar. Read that again against last night. Roche looked at its own controlled or internally benchmarked data three years ago, decided the effect was not there, and walked. Oak Hill Bio licensed the asset globally and is taking it into a Phase three called BEACON. That is a company buying a program its originator abandoned on efficacy, in a mechanism that has now failed a randomized trial. It is a hard position to defend this morning.

Now the company. Ultragenyx closed the after-hours session at fourteen dollars sixty-seven, down forty-four point seven percent, on a hundred forty times average volume, with a market capitalization of one point four five billion dollars. Evercore ISI cut from Outperform to In Line with a sixteen-dollar target; Baird and J-P-Morgan also downgraded. Emil Kakkis said the company will decide the program's disposition and implement significant expense reductions, and reiterated the commercial story: Crysvita, Dojolvi, Evkeeza, the August nineteenth accelerated approval of GENGLYCOS in glycogen storage disease type one-a, and the pending U-X one-eleven decision in Sanfilippo A.

The numbers around that are worth stating plainly, because they cut against the price action. Second-quarter revenue was a record two hundred fourteen million dollars, Crysvita alone a hundred fifty-six. Full-year guidance is seven hundred thirty to seven hundred sixty million. Cash and marketable securities were four hundred thirty-six million at June thirtieth against ninety-seven million of quarterly operating burn. The path to profitability in twenty twenty-seven rested on three legs: double-digit growth from marketed products, contributions from new launches, and non-dilutive capital from priority review vouchers if the gene therapies were approved. Apazunersen was not one of those legs. So a company guiding to seven hundred fifty million dollars of revenue this year now trades at roughly two times revenue with two gene therapies approved or pending.

The way to read that gap is that the market was not paying for the commercial business — it was paying for Angelman, and it has stopped. That is defensible in one sense: Angelman was the only asset with genuine blockbuster geometry, roughly a five-hundred-thousand-patient global population with nothing approved. But it leaves an odd situation where the September nineteenth Sanfilippo A action date is now carrying a much larger share of the equity story than it was on Tuesday, and where the priority review voucher attached to it is a materially bigger fraction of the market cap than it was. Watch that date closely. And watch what "significant expense reductions" turns out to mean, because there is a version of this where the cuts fall on the discovery pipeline and Ultragenyx converts into a stable, profitable, small-population commercial business with no shot on goal left — which is a perfectly good company and a completely different investment case from the one people owned on Tuesday.

The transferable lesson has nothing to do with Angelman syndrome specifically, and it is worth carrying into every pediatric rare-disease program you look at. When the disease is a developmental disorder, the endpoint is a functional score that rises with age, the assessment is repeated, part of the instrument is caregiver-reported, and the comparator is a natural history database — then the study cannot tell you whether the drug works, no matter how large the observed effect or how small the p-value. Plus two point zero net response at p less than zero point zero zero zero one told us nothing. It was never a measurement of the drug.

And there is a second lesson underneath it, for anyone whose thesis is that better target identification is what the industry has been missing. Angelman syndrome is arguably the most completely understood monogenic disorder in the central nervous system: one gene, a known imprinting mechanism, a healthy silent copy sitting in the very cells that need it, and a modality tailor-made to unsilence it. There was no target uncertainty here at all. It failed anyway. Knowing exactly what to do to the biology is necessary and it is nowhere close to sufficient, and the binding constraints — when in development you intervene, whether the tissue can still reorganize, and whether your measuring instrument can detect a real effect against a noisy human baseline — are not problems that better target discovery solves.
