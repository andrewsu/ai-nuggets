First, and today's spotlight. Telix Pharmaceuticals, the Australian radiopharmaceutical company listed in both Sydney and on the Nasdaq, announced a merger with I-T-M — Isotope Technologies Munich — for one point six five billion dollars up front on a cash-free, debt-free basis, plus up to seven hundred million more in contingent payments, for a headline value of two point three five billion. The upfront is almost entirely paper: one point two five billion dollars paid in one hundred five point eight million new Telix shares priced at eleven dollars eighty-four, which is the thirty-day trailing volume-weighted average at signing, released as Nasdaq depositary receipts after escrow. Telix also assumes three hundred two million dollars of net debt. Telix shareholders end up with about seventy-six percent of the combined company and I-T-M holders with about twenty-four percent. Telix's board and holders of more than ninety percent of I-T-M's shares have signed; Telix shareholders vote at an extraordinary meeting expected in November, with closing targeted by the end of the fiscal year. What Telix is buying is the isotope supply chain — I-T-M is the only producer of globally scaled, commercial-grade lutetium one-seventy-seven, with a distribution network across sixty-five countries, two good-manufacturing-practice sites, and two hundred seventy-three million dollars of revenue last year growing at a forty percent compound rate since twenty twenty-one, profitably. The whole seven hundred million dollars of contingent consideration is tied to a single asset, I-T-M eleven, the somatostatin-receptor therapy that the F-D-A rejected on manufacturing grounds six weeks ago. Telix shares fell in Sydney on the news, down as much as twelve percent at the worst of it and closing off about six to seven percent. Why this is the episode: the earnout structure tells you exactly what Telix thinks it bought, and it is not the drug.

Second, the artificial intelligence item, and it is a three-part announcement. Iambic, the San Diego company, unveiled Enchant version three, the next generation of its multimodal transformer — forty-one billion parameters, pretrained on four and a half trillion tokens, trained across more than six thousand molecular properties and sixteen biomedical data modalities including assays, text, images, multi-omics, protein and three-dimensional structures, pharmacokinetics and clinical trial data, with a mixture-of-experts architecture. The claim worth paying attention to is not the parameter count, it is the scaling behavior. Iambic's machine learning lead said that across three generations, every time they added parameters, data and modalities, predictions improved — including for endpoints where data are sparse. That is the central open question in this field: whether text-style scaling laws hold when your data are experimental rather than linguistic, and Iambic is asserting they do. On the same day, AbbVie signed a multi-year collaboration to apply the platform to small molecules in immunology, neuroscience and oncology, with an upfront payment, success-based milestones and tiered royalties, no numbers disclosed. That makes AbbVie the third large partner this year after Takeda in February and Bayer in June. And separately, Fierce reports Iambic is preparing to go public. Read those three together: a platform company converting model releases into pharma contracts and then into a public listing, which is the path Schrodinger took and the one Xaira and Isomorphic have not yet had to test.

Third, Novo put real numbers behind the low-dose CagriSema story at its capital markets day. In REIMAGINE five, in adults with type two diabetes inadequately controlled on metformin, an S-G-L-T-two inhibitor, or both, CagriSema at one milligram of each component beat tirzepatide five milligrams on weight — twelve point four percent versus nine point one percent at week sixty — and came in non-inferior on hemoglobin A-one-C, one point seven one versus one point six seven. In REDEFINE nine, in overweight and obesity, the one-milligram and one point seven-milligram doses produced twenty-one percent weight loss against two percent on placebo at week sixty-eight, with improvements in systolic blood pressure, waist-to-height ratio and lipids. The strategic point is dose flexibility. CagriSema's problem has never been peak efficacy, it has been tolerability and discontinuation at the top dose. Showing that a quarter-strength regimen still beats a starting dose of tirzepatide is an argument that the franchise has a wide usable dose range rather than one heroic setting. The weight management application is filed with an F-D-A decision expected in the fourth quarter.

Fourth, Johnson and Johnson reported the first Phase three win for Caplyta, lumateperone, in acute mania. In study four fifty-one, in adults with bipolar one disorder experiencing manic episodes with or without mixed features, Caplyta cut the Young Mania Rating Scale by four point eight points more than placebo at week three, effect size zero point six nine, p less than zero point zero zero zero one, with separation as early as day three. The only treatment-related adverse events over five percent and at least double placebo were dry mouth, about eight percent versus three, and nausea, about eight percent versus two. Lumateperone is a serotonin-dopamine modulator already approved in schizophrenia and in bipolar depression, and mania is the last major piece of the bipolar label. An effect size near zero point seven in acute mania is competitive with the atypicals, and the metabolic profile is the differentiator in a category where weight gain drives discontinuation.

Fifth, Alkermes produced the first clinical evidence anywhere that an orexin receptor agonist does something in attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder. A-L-K-S seven two nine zero, an orexin two receptor agonist, was tested in eighty-eight healthy volunteers and fifty adults with A-D-H-D, randomized four to one against placebo after a two-week washout of existing medication, dosed for fourteen days as inpatients. At day fourteen, median reductions from a baseline score of about thirty-nine on the adult A-D-H-D investigator symptom rating scale were fourteen points at twenty milligrams and nineteen points at fifty milligrams, with separation by day six, plus a one-to-two-point improvement on global severity. No serious adverse events, no discontinuations on drug, and the two dropouts were both on placebo. Now the caveats, and they matter. This was a Phase one safety and pharmacokinetics study. The efficacy endpoints were exploratory, the study was explicitly not powered to detect differences between groups, and the release does not report the placebo change — which in a fourteen-day inpatient A-D-H-D study after medication washout is typically substantial. So this is dose selection and mechanism confirmation, not efficacy. The real test is the ongoing Phase two, roughly three hundred twelve patients, primary endpoint at week four, data expected next year. Alkermes is taking orexin biology out of hypersomnolence and into psychiatry, which is the highest-value option in that pipeline if it works.

Sixth, an approval that deserved more attention than it got on Friday. The F-D-A approved IntraBio's Aqneursa, levacetylleucine, for ataxia in adults and children with ataxia-telangiectasia weighing at least fifteen kilograms. It is the first treatment approved anywhere in the world for this disease — an inherited neurodegenerative disorder caused by mutations in the A-T-M gene that causes progressive loss of coordination from early childhood along with immune deficiency and sharply elevated cancer risk. The evidence was a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled two-period crossover study in seventy-three patients aged four to fifty, median age thirteen, ninety-six percent of whom completed, using a functional version of the ataxia rating scale that scores gait, sitting, stance and speech. Patients scored better on drug than on their own placebo period. This is the second indication for Aqneursa after Niemann-Pick type C in twenty twenty-four, and it is worth noting the design: a within-patient crossover in a rare progressive disease, which is a template more rare disease programs should be looking at.

Seventh, Bayer is selling Stivarga, regorafenib, to Grunenthal for up to three hundred seventy-five million euros, about four hundred thirty-one million dollars. Regorafenib is a multikinase inhibitor approved in more than ninety markets for metastatic colorectal cancer, gastrointestinal stromal tumors and hepatocellular carcinoma. Exclusivity runs out in twenty twenty-nine in Europe and twenty thirty in the United States. Grunenthal expects up to about one hundred million euros of E-B-I-T-D-A contribution in twenty twenty-seven, and closing is expected by the end of this year or early next. This is the standard late-life-cycle trade — a large pharma clears a declining oncology asset off the books ahead of loss of exclusivity and a specialty buyer harvests the tail. Note the timing against last week's Exelixis news: regorafenib is the fourteen-year-old comparator that zanzalintinib was measured against in colorectal cancer, so the asset is being sold into a market that is about to be redefined.

Ninth, two things out of Washington. The New York Times reports the White House is drafting an executive order, written by the Office of Management and Budget, that would create an external committee with the power to veto National Institutes of Health grant awards that do not conform to administration priorities — political leverage over a forty-eight-billion-dollar portfolio. The reporting describes a months-long fight between the budget director and the N-I-H director, who has resisted. If this lands as described, it changes the risk profile of academic collaborations and of any translational program with federal funding in its critical path. Separately, Public Citizen obtained the actual most-favored-nation contracts through a Freedom of Information Act lawsuit and released them Saturday. Two things stand out. Pfizer agreed to share a portion of the net increased revenue it collects from raising prices abroad with Health and Human Services, effective from January first of this year through January twentieth, twenty twenty-nine — the rate and the covered medicines are redacted. And the Lilly agreement explicitly excludes Mounjaro and Zepbound from the products subject to certain most-favored-nation discounts. The two largest incretin brands in the world are carved out of the pricing framework that was sold as bringing American prices down to foreign levels.
