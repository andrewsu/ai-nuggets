Good morning Travis. Here are your biotech and pharma headlines for Thursday, September twenty-fourth, twenty twenty-six. Wednesday belonged to a diagnostic rather than a drug. An F-D-A advisory panel spent the day picking apart Grail's Galleri test and then voted three times in its favor, which puts the first multi-cancer early detection test ever submitted for approval one regulatory decision away from the Medicare market. That is today's spotlight, and Grail is a Foresite alumni company, so it is worth the full eight minutes. Around it, the artificial intelligence financing window stayed wide open with two rounds totaling four hundred fifty-one million dollars, Lilly signed another China deal overnight, Relay's F-G-F-R two inhibitor got approved under somebody else's name, and Immunovant lost an indication. Ten items, then the week ahead.

First, and today's spotlight. The Molecular and Clinical Genetics Devices Panel voted ten to nothing that Grail's Galleri test is safe, six to four that it is effective, and seven to two with one abstention that its benefits outweigh its risks in adults fifty and older. Galleri reads cancer-specific methylation patterns in cell-free D-N-A from a single blood draw and, when it detects a signal, predicts the tissue of origin to direct the diagnostic workup. The reason this vote is interesting rather than routine is that the test missed the primary endpoint of the largest randomized screening trial ever run on a blood test, and the panel voted for it anyway. The split between six to four on effectiveness and seven to two on benefit-risk is the whole story, and so is what the panel said about the label. We will take it apart in the spotlight.

Second, and this one sits directly in your wheelhouse. Basecamp Research raised a one hundred forty million dollar Series C, reportedly at an eight hundred million dollar valuation, led by S thirty-two, with participation from Anthropic's Anthology Fund, Nvidia, the NATO Innovation Fund, Catalio, Redalpine, Singular, True Ventures, the Rockefeller Foundation and King Philanthropies. Andy Conrad, the former Verily chief executive, joins the board. The company's asset is the Trillion Gene Atlas, a proprietary genomic training set assembled through access and benefit-sharing partnerships in more than thirty countries and built with Nvidia, Anthropic, PacBio and Ultima Genomics. On top of it they train a biological foundation model family called EDEN, and the therapeutic application they have chosen is the one worth paying attention to: in vivo cell therapy. The idea is to pair EDEN's ability to design long, complex D-N-A sequences with large serine recombinases that integrate those sequences precisely into the genome — so you reprogram a patient's cells inside the body instead of harvesting, engineering and re-infusing them at a cost of hundreds of thousands of dollars per patient. Note the thread: this is the third Anthropic-adjacent item in eight days, after Novo signing Claude Science into R and D and the confirmed Bay Area wet lab with Xaira inside the gate. Basecamp explicitly cites its Claude Science collaboration as the demonstration that its models can go from a disease description to a candidate. André Hoffmann, Roche's vice-chairman, put personal money in. Richard Pearce, formerly of Biogen, comes in as chief business officer to sell partnerships.

Third, overnight news. Eli Lilly committed one hundred million dollars in upfront and near-term payments to InnoCare Pharma of Beijing for a five-target discovery collaboration, with up to three point two five billion dollars in milestones and single-digit tiered royalties. Neither company disclosed the targets or the therapeutic areas. InnoCare is the company behind an oral T-Y-K two inhibitor now racing Bristol Myers and Takeda in psoriasis, a V-A-V one degrader that entered the clinic in China this year, and orelabrutinib, the B-T-K inhibitor Biogen licensed for one hundred twenty-five million dollars in twenty twenty-one and handed back nineteen months later. What is notable is the posture: InnoCare's chief executive Jasmine Cui told analysts last month that the company has plenty of cash and was in no rush to partner. Lilly, flush with incretin revenue, paid up anyway for research capacity rather than for a specific molecule — which is the same structure as the Boehringer and Roche target-discovery deals we covered last week, just aimed at a Chinese chemistry engine instead of an algorithm.

Fourth, the second big artificial intelligence round of the day. Enveda closed a three hundred eleven million dollar Series E crossover led by Catalio, with T. Rowe Price, Surveyor Capital, Durable Capital, ICONIQ and Lightspeed joining — bringing the company to more than eight hundred forty-five million dollars raised since inception and putting public-market investors on the register ahead of an eventual listing. Enveda's premise is that natural product chemistry is the best-validated library in existence and that machine learning on mass spectrometry finally makes it readable; the platform is called PRISM and has produced seventeen candidates, three of them now in humans. The lead asset, E-N-V three-oh-eight, is an oral weight-maintenance drug the company describes as exercise in a tablet, which cleared a Phase one tolerability readout in August with what they call an exceptional gastrointestinal profile — and the trial they plan next is in people coming off G-L-P one therapy, which is exactly the commercial gap Viking's every-other-week maintenance data opened up on Tuesday. A second asset reduced eczema severity in early atopic dermatitis work in July, and a third is in inflammatory bowel disease.

Fifth, an approval with a licensing twist. The F-D-A approved Lyrfigtu, generic name lirafugratinib, as a second-line treatment for cholangiocarcinoma driven by an F-G-F-R two fusion or rearrangement. In the Phase one-two ReFocus trial the confirmed response rate was forty-six percent, median duration of response eleven point eight months, and median progression-free survival eleven point three months. The mechanism is the selling point: it binds F-G-F-R two irreversibly and covalently, which covers many of the resistance mutations that emerge on earlier reversible inhibitors, and it spares the other F-G-F-R isoforms, which is where the hyperphosphatemia and nail and eye toxicity of the pan-F-G-F-R drugs comes from. Lipika Goyal at Stanford, the lead ReFocus author, called it a next-generation precision option; Robin Kate Kelley at U-C-S-F said the responses were remarkably deep and durable. The label does carry retinal pigment epithelial detachment in thirty-one percent of three hundred eighty-five patients, grade three in under two percent, median onset at fifty-seven days. The twist is that this is Relay Therapeutics' molecule — R-L-Y four thousand eight — licensed to Elevar, a subsidiary of Korea's H-L-B, in December twenty twenty-four for seventy-five million dollars in upfront and regulatory milestones against up to five hundred million total. Relay walked away from the asset to concentrate on its P-I three-K alpha franchise. Elevar says U-S availability in the fourth quarter.

Sixth, Immunovant lost an indication. Roivant reported that period one of Immunovant's proof-of-concept study of imeroprubart in cutaneous lupus erythematosus missed its primary endpoint, percent change in the cutaneous lupus disease area and severity index at week twelve, in fifty-seven patients. There were numerical trends in favor of the drug and a dose-response relationship worth noting — patients with deeper immunoglobulin G reductions were more likely to respond — but the company is stopping in cutaneous lupus, citing the competitive landscape as much as the data. That last part matters. Immunovant's whole thesis is that its second-generation F-c-R-n antibody can go broad across immunoglobulin G-mediated disease, and the remaining list is Graves' disease, difficult-to-treat rheumatoid arthritis, myasthenia gravis, chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy and Sjogren's. Cutaneous lupus was the test of whether F-c-R-n blockade reaches a disease where autoantibody pathogenicity is less clearly the whole mechanism, and the answer was no. Set it next to Amgen's Sjogren's win on Tuesday and the message is that the autoimmune field is still sorting which diseases are actually antibody-driven and which only look that way.

Seventh, Astellas dosed the first patient in a Phase three trial of setidegrasib against docetaxel in previously treated K-RAS G twelve D mutant non-small cell lung cancer. This is the second Phase three the company has opened for that molecule in six months, after a front-line pancreatic trial in April, and setidegrasib is a first-in-class targeted protein degrader rather than an inhibitor — it recruits the cell's disposal machinery to eliminate the mutant protein instead of occupying its active site, which is the way around the G twelve D problem that G twelve C chemistry does not solve. The Phase one data were published in the New England Journal this year. For competitive framing, G twelve D is the most common K-RAS mutation and the one Revolution Medicines is attacking with a covalent inhibitor alongside its pan-RAS program, so Astellas moving a degrader into two registrational settings first is a real claim on the space.

Eighth, Gossamer Bio submitted its first-ever N-D-A, for seralutinib in pulmonary arterial hypertension. Seralutinib is an inhaled P-D-G-F receptor, C-S-F one-R and c-KIT inhibitor, delivered to the lung to hit the proliferative vascular remodeling that vasodilators do not touch. The filing rests on the Phase three PROSERA study as the single adequate and well-controlled trial, with the Phase two TORREY study as confirmatory evidence. The sentence to read twice is the one about the June pre-N-D-A meeting: the F-D-A's written minutes characterized the degree of statistical significance and the magnitude of the treatment effect in PROSERA as review issues rather than filing issues. That is the agency telling a sponsor it will take the application but will argue about whether the effect is big enough — which is the same conversation Compass Therapeutics is having about tovecimig, and worth tracking as a pattern.

Ninth, Boehringer Ingelheim expanded its collaboration with Variant Bio into cardiovascular disease, adding an upfront payment plus more than two hundred fifty million dollars in potential license and milestone payments on top of the one hundred twenty million dollar cardiorenal deal the two signed in January. Variant's platform integrates genomic, deep phenotyping and multi-omic data from globally diverse populations, on the premise that genetically supported targets survive the clinic at a much higher rate. What makes this worth a line is the pace: this is Boehringer's second computational target-discovery deal in two days, after the billion-dollar Envisagenics R-N-A splicing agreement, and Novo Nordisk signed Variant for metabolic disease genetics in early twenty twenty-five. Boehringer is systematically buying target hypotheses rather than molecules.

Tenth, and this is a new kind of buyer. The U-S Department of War awarded Flagship Pioneering a forty-seven million dollar, five-year contract to develop medical countermeasure technologies against emerging biological threats, starting with two Flagship-founded companies. Apriori Bio, founded in twenty twenty, uses machine learning to forecast viral evolution and profile which variants will evade immunity; Prologue Medicines mines the viral proteome computationally to find and reprogram proteins as therapeutics. The government program explicitly lists integrating artificial intelligence and machine learning into countermeasure optimization as an objective. Noubar Afeyan framed it as building innovation infrastructure for strategic advantage. Small dollars, but it is the first contract of its kind for Flagship's model, and it establishes defense procurement as a non-dilutive funding channel for platform biotechs at a moment when N-I-H grant politics are unsettled.
