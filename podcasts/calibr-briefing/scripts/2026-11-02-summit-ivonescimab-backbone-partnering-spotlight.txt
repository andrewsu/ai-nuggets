Today's spotlight is ivonescimab, and specifically a question that isn't really about the drug: why is half of large-cap oncology lining up to put its assets next to a molecule whose only global Phase 3 missed its overall survival endpoint?

Start with what it is. Ivonescimab is a bispecific antibody discovered by Akeso in China and licensed to Summit Therapeutics for the U-S, Europe, Japan and the rest of the world outside China. One construct, two targets: P-D-one, the checkpoint that Keytruda blocks, and V-E-G-F, the angiogenesis signal that Avastin blocks. The combination of a checkpoint inhibitor and an anti-angiogenic is not new — that pairing has been standard in kidney and liver cancer for years. The claim for putting both on one molecule is that V-E-G-F is concentrated in the tumor, so the antibody preferentially accumulates where V-E-G-F is, and its binding to P-D-one gets stronger in that environment through avidity. Blocking V-E-G-F also normalizes the chaotic tumor vasculature and relieves the immunosuppression that V-E-G-F imposes on the local T-cell compartment. So the mechanistic story is tumor-selective checkpoint blockade with the immune microenvironment pre-conditioned by the same molecule.

Now the data ledger, because the strength of the evidence is uneven in a way that matters.

The headline result, and the one that set this off in September of 2024, is HARMONi-two. That's a China-only Phase 3 run by Akeso: three hundred ninety-eight patients with P-D-L-one-positive advanced non-small cell lung cancer, ivonescimab monotherapy against pembrolizumab monotherapy, head to head, no chemotherapy on either arm. The mature overall survival data, presented at the World Conference on Lung Cancer this September, show a hazard ratio of zero point seven three, median survival thirty point eight months against twenty-two point six, and a thirty-six-month survival rate of forty-five percent against thirty-three. That is the first time any molecule has beaten pembrolizumab head to head on survival in this setting. It is also entirely Chinese, where the patient mix, the subsequent lines of therapy and the smoking histories all differ from a Western population.

The global trial is HARMONi, and it's a different question: four hundred thirty-eight patients with E-G-F-R-mutant lung cancer who progressed after a third-generation E-G-F-R inhibitor, randomized to ivonescimab plus chemotherapy or placebo plus chemotherapy across a hundred fourteen centers in Asia, Europe and North America. Progression-free survival was unambiguous — six point eight months against four point four, hazard ratio zero point five two, published in Lancet Oncology in August. Overall survival was the problem. At the primary analysis in April 2025, the hazard ratio was zero point seven nine with a p-value of zero point zero five seven. A miss, by three thousandths.

Here's the part that deserves more attention than it got. The critique that formed around that miss was regional: that the Western patients weren't benefiting and the whole effect was Asian. Watch what happened to the Western subgroup as follow-up accumulated. At the April 2025 cut, median follow-up in Western patients was nine point two months — less than the median survival — and the hazard ratio was zero point nine eight, which is to say nothing. By September 2025, thirteen point seven months of follow-up, it was zero point eight four. By the June 2026 cut, twenty-three point two months, it was zero point seven six, identical to the global population. The global hazard ratio at that last cut is zero point seven six with a nominal p of zero point zero one five. The drug didn't change. The follow-up did. The original analysis read out a Western subgroup before the Western curve had anywhere to separate, and the field drew a biological conclusion from an immaturity artifact. That's worth filing as a general lesson about regional subgroups in trials where one region enrolled first.

The F-D-A decision on that filing is November fourteenth. Stifel's Dara Azar told BioSpace she doesn't expect major regulatory issues, and the setting is one where, as the MD Anderson lead author put it, anti-P-D-one and anti-P-D-L-one therapies have never established clear benefit after E-G-F-R progression. But it is an approval that would rest on progression-free survival with a formally negative survival endpoint, which is a live question in this agency, not a settled one.

So why the partnering stampede, and why now?

Look at the sequence. January, a collaboration with GSK on its B7-H3 antibody-drug conjugate. July, Arcus brings casdatifan in kidney cancer. Earlier, Pfizer brings several of its vedotin A-D-Cs from the Seagen acquisition. In June of last year, Revolution Medicines offered its entire RAS-ON franchise — the approved pancreatic drug plus the G-twelve-D and G-twelve-C selective inhibitors — and that study is now enrolling three hundred seventy patients in solid tumors. Then September twenty-eighth, AstraZeneca commits two billion dollars in convertible preferred shares at an effective price of eighteen dollars thirty-six, a premium to the close, and takes no license to the asset. October second, AstraZeneca and Daiichi Sankyo add Datroway, their TROP-two A-D-C, starting in triple-negative breast cancer. And on October twenty-first, ECOG-ACRIN, a U-S cooperative group, signs a Phase two-three collaboration.

That last one is the tell. Pharma collaborations are cheap optionality — a clinical trial collaboration typically costs each side its own drug supply and a share of trial costs, with no upfront and no license. A cooperative group committing its investigator network is a different kind of signal, because academic cooperative groups are slow, consensus-driven, and don't build protocols around molecules they expect to disappear.

The economic logic is straightforward. Keytruda did thirty-one billion dollars last year and loses exclusivity in 2028. Every A-D-C developer needs a checkpoint backbone to combine with, because single-agent A-D-C data in solid tumors are not enough to define a frontline regimen. If the backbone changes from pembrolizumab to a P-D-one-by-V-E-G-F bispecific, being first in line with your A-D-C is worth a great deal, and being late is worth nothing. The cost of a wrong bet is a trial; the cost of a missed bet is a franchise. That asymmetry, not the strength of the evidence, is what explains the queue. Vision Lifesciences puts the P-D-one-by-V-E-G-F market above thirty billion by 2032, and Merck already paid LaNova five hundred eighty-eight million upfront for its own version while Pfizer committed up to six billion to 3SBio's.

AstraZeneca is the interesting counterweight here, because it has the largest financial position and gave BioSpace the most cautious quote: "much of the positive data to date comes from China-only trials, and we recognize that single-region data have more limited relevance to populations outside China." A company does not usually write a two-billion-dollar equity check and then publicly discount the evidence. The way to read that is as a deliberate separation of the bet from the thesis: the equity buys participation in the whole ivonescimab opportunity regardless of which indication lands first, while the public position stays defensible if the global readout disappoints.

Which brings us to what actually resolves this. HARMONi-three is sixteen hundred patients across two hundred sixty sites, ivonescimab plus chemotherapy against pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy, first-line metastatic lung cancer, and it is designed as two independently analyzed histology cohorts — six hundred squamous, a thousand non-squamous — with dual primary endpoints of progression-free survival and overall survival. Summit guides to final progression-free survival data before the end of the year. Note that an earlier interim on that same endpoint disappointed, which is why the final readout carries this much weight.

Three things to hold as you wait for it. First, progression-free survival in a multiregional first-line trial is necessary but not sufficient; given what HARMONi taught us about immature survival curves, a clean P-F-S win with survival still immature will be ambiguous for another year, and the market will probably trade it as settled anyway. Second, the squamous cohort is the one to watch on safety — V-E-G-F blockade in squamous lung cancer carries a hemorrhage history that long predates this molecule. Third, if HARMONi-three delivers, the partnering queue stops being optionality and becomes a landgrab, and the scarce resource turns out to be Summit's own capacity to run combination trials. That is an unusual place for a company with one asset to find itself, and it's the real reason the AstraZeneca equity, which funds Phase 3 work without giving up rights, was structured the way it was.
