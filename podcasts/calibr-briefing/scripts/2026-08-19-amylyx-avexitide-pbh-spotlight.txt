Today's spotlight: Amylyx just won a Phase 3 trial with a drug it bought out of a bankruptcy auction for thirty-five million dollars, in a disease created by surgery, using a mechanism that is the exact inverse of the biggest drug class in the industry. The stock had its best day ever. And the most interesting thing about the result isn't the size of the effect — it's what the effect tells you about how much of endocrine disease is now iatrogenic.

Start with the disease, because the pathophysiology is the whole argument. Roux-en-Y gastric bypass reroutes the stomach directly to the mid-jejunum. Food arrives in the distal gut fast and undigested, and the L-cells there — the ones that secrete GLP-1 — see a nutrient bolus they were never meant to see. The result is a surge of GLP-1 far above what an intact gut produces, which drives a surge of insulin, which arrives after the glucose has already been absorbed. One to three hours after eating, blood sugar crashes. Patients describe confusion, cognitive dysfunction, loss of consciousness, seizures — neuroglycopenia, the brain running out of fuel. It's not a mild condition and it's not rare: about eight percent of Americans who've had a sleeve gastrectomy or a gastric bypass develop it, roughly a hundred and sixty thousand people. Some estimates of milder forms run to a third of surgical patients.

There are no approved therapies. Care is dietary modification plus off-label drugs — acarbose first, which works by slowing carbohydrate absorption and is limited mostly by how badly it's tolerated in the gut; then diazoxide, octreotide and calcium channel blockers, all with variable efficacy and real tolerability problems. Worth noting, since we covered the Vykat safety signal in Prader-Willi last week, that diazoxide is one of the agents already being borrowed here, with the same edema and cardiorespiratory liabilities.

So the mechanistic logic is unusually clean. If the disease is driven by excessive GLP-1 receptor signaling on beta cells, block the receptor. Avexitide is exendin nine to thirty-nine — a truncated fragment of exendin-4, the Gila monster peptide that gave the world exenatide. Cut off the first eight residues and you convert a full agonist into a competitive antagonist that still binds the receptor at sub-nanomolar affinity. It occupies the receptor and does nothing, which is precisely the point: you're not suppressing insulin globally, you're removing the inappropriate incretin drive on top of normal glucose-stimulated secretion. That specificity is why weight didn't move in either arm of the trial — the participants weren't losing the metabolic benefit of their surgery, they were losing the complication.

Now the data. LUCIDITY randomized seventy-eight adults with post-bariatric hypoglycemia after gastric bypass, three to two, to ninety milligrams of avexitide subcutaneously once daily or placebo, across twenty-one U.S. sites. Six-week screening including a three-week run-in — important in a disease where event rates are noisy and diet-dependent — then sixteen weeks double-blind, then a thirty-two-week open-label extension. The primary endpoint, agreed with the FDA in advance, was the composite rate of Level two and Level three hypoglycemic events. Level two is glucose below fifty-four milligrams per deciliter; Level three is severe, meaning the patient needed someone else's help. Fifty-five percent reduction versus placebo, p equals three times ten to the minus sixth. Every secondary endpoint hit: Level two events by fingerstick, Level two events by continuous glucose monitor, and independently adjudicated Level three events. No treatment-related serious adverse events; what showed up was diarrhea and injection-site redness and bruising.

Three things make that number more convincing than a single p-value usually is. First, the bar was pre-specified and the margin over it is wide — Citi's Geoff Meacham pointed out that regulators had set thirty-five percent as the threshold for success while clinicians regard fifty percent as the level patients actually notice. Fifty-five clears both. Second, this is a replication. The Phase 2b study at the same daily dose produced a sixty-four percent reduction in the same composite, along with fifty-three percent on Level two alone and sixty-six percent on Level three. That trial was open-label crossover. Going from sixty-four percent unblinded to fifty-five percent double-blind and placebo-controlled is exactly the attenuation you'd predict if the effect is real — the number came down modestly and the confidence went up a lot. Six trials now, consistent direction. Third, the adjudicated severe-event reduction matters more for the label than the composite does, because Level three events are the ones that put people in ambulances, and they're the hardest to move.

The financial arc here is worth sitting with. Eiger BioPharmaceuticals filed Chapter eleven in April twenty twenty-four. Amylyx won the court-supervised auction that June and closed in July for thirty-five point one million dollars — a Phase 3-ready asset carrying FDA breakthrough therapy designation, orphan drug designation in hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia, and rare pediatric disease designation in congenital hyperinsulinism. Amylyx at that moment was a company whose only marketed product, Relyvrio in ALS, had just failed its confirmatory trial and been pulled from the U.S. and Canada. It bought a de-risked late-stage program for less than the cost of running one Phase 3, because the seller was in liquidation. That's the same arbitrage PTC just ran on Sangamo's Fabry gene therapy. In a market where late-stage assets trade at hundreds of millions, bankruptcy dockets are becoming a real sourcing channel.

The sell side moved fast. Mizuho's Graig Suvannavejh took the target to thirty dollars from twenty-four, keeping Outperform, and models risk-adjusted revenue near one point three billion by twenty forty. Guggenheim went to forty. TD Cowen has U.S. peak sales around one and a half billion; the broader figure being quoted is one point seven billion worldwide. For a hundred-and-sixty-thousand-patient orphan population with no approved competition those are defensible — a chronic, self-injected specialty product with an obvious prescriber base in bariatric surgery and endocrinology.

And then, the same evening, the offering. Three hundred fifty million dollars of common stock, with a fifty-two-and-a-half-million-dollar underwriters' option, run by Leerink, Morgan Stanley, Guggenheim and LifeSci. Proceeds go to pre-commercial work, including securing additional manufacturing capacity. Coming into this Amylyx had two hundred fifty-one million in cash against a forty-three-million quarterly loss, with runway into twenty twenty-eight. So they didn't need the money to survive — they raised it because a peptide launch needs supply locked down eighteen months ahead, and because you sell equity on the best day you'll get. It's dilutive, and it's the right call. Read it as a company financing a launch rather than a trial.

The competitive risk is the part the price targets are least likely to be capturing. Avexitide's half-life is roughly three and a half hours, which is why it's dosed daily by injection. MBX Biosciences is developing imapextide, a long-acting GLP-1 receptor antagonist engineered on their precision-peptide platform explicitly for once-weekly dosing, with Phase 2a proof of concept in hand in the same population. Amylyx cannot re-engineer a peptide it doesn't own the backbone of. So the likely shape of this market is that Amylyx gets there first, defines the endpoint, builds the diagnostic pathway and the prescriber relationships — and then defends a daily injectable against a weekly one. Behind that, Vogenx's mizagliflozin takes a different route entirely, inhibiting SGLT-one in the gut to slow glucose absorption, and Recordati is working pasireotide, a somatostatin receptor five agonist that suppresses insulin more bluntly.

What I'd watch. The offering's final pricing, for the real dilution number. Whether the NDA lands by year end and whether breakthrough designation converts into priority review — that determines whether the twenty twenty-seven launch guidance holds. The thirty-two-week open-label extension, for durability, because a competitive receptor antagonist dosed daily is exactly the kind of molecule where you'd want to rule out escape. The congenital hyperinsulinism program, where the rare pediatric disease designation puts a priority review voucher in play — a nine-figure asset on its own, and material against a market cap this size. And the calpain-two antisense oligonucleotide in ALS, which is the free option nobody is paying for.

The bottom line. Commercially, this is a well-executed orphan asset purchased at a distressed price and about to become a real product. Scientifically, it's a reminder that the incretin axis now runs in both directions. We have spent five years learning to amplify GLP-1 signaling for obesity and diabetes; post-bariatric hypoglycemia is what the same axis looks like when it overshoots, and the first drug approved to treat it will be a GLP-1 blocker. As bariatric volumes and pharmacological GLP-1 exposure both keep climbing, that side of the ledger is going to get bigger, not smaller — and the companies that understand incretin pharmacology best are the ones with the least incentive to look at it.
