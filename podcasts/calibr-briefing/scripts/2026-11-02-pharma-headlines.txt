Good morning, Travis. It's Monday, November second, 2026. The week opens with a crowded regulatory calendar and one asset that half of large-cap oncology is trying to attach itself to. On the list today: the partnering stampede around Summit's P-D-one-by-V-E-G-F bispecific, BridgeBio's first-in-disease muscular dystrophy decision later this month, a gene editing tape that moved on a piece of F-D-A paperwork, an A-I-native buyer quietly assembling a portfolio out of other people's shelved assets, two global filings off an ESMO Presidential Symposium readout, and a new Phase 3 in C-O-P-D aimed at the patients inhaled biologics don't help. Here's what you need to know.

First, and this is today's spotlight, so I'll keep it short here. BioSpace reported this morning that the queue of companies trying to put their drug next to ivonescimab has gotten long enough to be its own story. Ivonescimab is Akeso's bispecific antibody, licensed to Summit Therapeutics, that hits P-D-one and V-E-G-F in a single construct. AstraZeneca, Pfizer, GSK, Arcus, Daiichi Sankyo and Revolution Medicines all have clinical trial collaborations to test their assets in combination with it, AstraZeneca put two billion dollars of equity into Summit in September, and the F-D-A decision on the second-line lung cancer filing lands November fourteenth. Mizuho's Graig Suvannavejh told BioSpace the drug "theoretically could stand to become the next major backbone of treatment in cancer." I'll go through what the data actually support, and what the partnering behavior tells you that the data don't.

Second, BridgeBio. The F-D-A has an action date of November twenty-seventh for B-B-P four-eighteen in limb-girdle muscular dystrophy type two-I, also called R-nine. If it clears, it's the first disease-modifying therapy for the condition. The mechanism is unusual and worth holding onto: the disease is caused by mutations in the fukutin-related protein gene, which cripples a glycosylation step that decorates alpha-dystroglycan, and without that sugar coating the protein can't anchor muscle fiber to the surrounding matrix. B-B-P four-eighteen is an oral glycosylation substrate, essentially flooding the impaired pathway with raw material rather than replacing the gene. The Phase 3 FORTIFY trial in a hundred twelve patients hit its interim biomarker endpoint on glycosylated alpha-dystroglycan, reduced serum creatine kinase, and at twelve months showed a thirty-one second difference versus placebo on a hundred-meter timed test. Jefferies' Andrew Tsai puts approval odds above ninety percent, partly because the agency scheduled no advisory committee, and called the data, quote, pretty darn compelling. He models roughly a four hundred thousand dollar annual price and says nobody would be surprised at a million. BridgeBio has identified more than five hundred genetically confirmed U-S patients against an estimated three thousand, and the competition, Sarepta's gene therapy and Bayer's AskBio program, is years behind.

Third, the gene editing tape moved last week on a document rather than a dataset. The F-D-A's October twenty-seventh draft guidance on individualized genome editing products let four editing companies rise between four and twenty-nine percent. Prime Medicine led at twenty-nine percent, Beam rose thirteen, Editas and Intellia each eleven. Beam's move also carried real data: first clinical proof of concept for B-E-A-M three-oh-one in glycogen storage disease type one-A, where two of three patients in the forty milligram cohort reached the protocol's maximum fifteen-hour controlled fast and eliminated their daily cornstarch entirely. William Blair's Myles Minter called it impressive and models four hundred seventy-five million in peak sales, which would be above his own colleague's forecast for the approved gene therapy in the same disease. Jefferies' Maury Raycroft wrote that editing companies have underperformed for a year but that the agency's posture should keep momentum going. Not everyone participated: Caribou fell twenty-one percent on its strategic review and layoffs, and is down two-thirds in a month.

Fourth, an item that hasn't been framed as a trend yet but should be. On Friday Alligator Bioscience sold worldwide rights to mitazalimab, a C-D-forty agonist antibody with positive Phase 2 data in pancreatic cancer, for seven hundred fifty thousand dollars in cash, with up to a hundred eight million in milestones and a capped royalty behind it. The buyer is Biossil, which describes itself as an A-I-native drug development company with teams in Toronto and Boston. Biossil is the same buyer that took the Phase 3 asset ridinilazole off Summit's hands in July. So the pattern is a small A-I-first shop assembling late-stage, de-risked, orphaned clinical assets at near-zero upfront from sellers who have refocused elsewhere. Whether the A-I adds anything is unproven, but the acquisition cost structure is the point: if your thesis is that model-driven development can rescue assets that failed on execution rather than biology, this is the cheapest way to test it, and the supply of sellers right now is large.

Fifth, HUTCHMED and AstraZeneca got savolitinib plus osimertinib accepted for review in both Europe and Japan, following the U-S filing in September. This is the SAFFRON Phase 3, presented at the ESMO Presidential Symposium and published simultaneously in the New England Journal: three hundred thirty-eight patients with E-G-F-R-mutant non-small cell lung cancer who progressed on osimertinib and whose tumors are M-E-T-overexpressed or amplified, randomized to the combination versus platinum doublet chemotherapy. Progression-free survival hazard ratio zero point five five, median seven point six months by blinded central review, and a significant overall survival benefit as well. The strategic read is that roughly one in three patients develop high M-E-T expression after a third-generation E-G-F-R inhibitor, so this is a targeted salvage for the single largest resistance mechanism in the biggest driver-mutation population in lung cancer.

Sixth, Boehringer Ingelheim registered a Phase 3 in C-O-P-D for verducatib, and the patient selection is the interesting part. Verducatib is an oral D-P-P-one inhibitor, preferentially distributed to bone marrow, that blocks activation of neutrophil serine proteases at the source. The AIRWARD trial will enroll about eighteen hundred patients across more than four hundred fifty sites, and it deliberately restricts to blood eosinophil counts below three hundred, which is to say exactly the patients the eosinophil-targeted biologics like Dupixent and the I-L-five agents were never going to help. This is the first D-P-P-one inhibitor taken into Phase 3 for C-O-P-D. It's a bet that neutrophilic inflammation is a separable, druggable axis in this disease, and the eosinophil cutoff is what makes it a clean test.

Seventh, briefly, Pelthos Therapeutics is merging with Castle Creek Biosciences. Castle Creek brings D-Fi, an injectable, redosable gene-modified fibroblast therapy for dystrophic epidermolysis bullosa, which delivers cells that produce type seven collagen; Pelthos brings three approved dermatology products and a commercial organization. It's a late-stage cell therapy acquiring a sales force rather than building one.
