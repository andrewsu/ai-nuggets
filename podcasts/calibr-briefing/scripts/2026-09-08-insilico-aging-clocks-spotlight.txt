Travis, today's spotlight is the Insilico Medicine paper that went up in Nature Biotechnology yesterday, and I want to be careful about what it is, because the press release and the paper are telling slightly different stories.

The claim in circulation is that an A-I-designed drug reversed biological age, unanimously, across six independent aging clocks. That is not wrong, exactly. But the paper is a forty-two-patient exploratory proteomic analysis embedded in a twelve-week Phase two-a trial, and it is written with unusual candor about its own limits — the authors state plainly that they cannot separate the drug's anti-fibrotic effect from a genuine anti-aging effect inside a fibrosis cohort. What makes it worth twenty minutes of your attention is not the age reversal headline. It is that this is the first serious attempt to build geroscience readouts into a registrational-track program from the beginning, and that the paper contains a finding which, if you take it seriously, complicates the Phase three that started two months ago.

Start with the drug, because the provenance is the whole reason this program matters to anyone thinking about A-I in discovery. Rentosertib, formerly I-N-S zero-one-eight zero-five-five, is a small-molecule inhibitor of T-NIK — TRAF-two and N-C-K interacting kinase. Insilico's target discovery platform nominated T-NIK by scoring genes against the hallmarks of aging; T-NIK came up implicated in six of them, and was then prioritized as what Insilico calls a dual-purpose target, meaning relevant both to aging biology and to a specific disease. The disease they chose was idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, on the argument that I-P-F is unusually collinear with aging hallmarks — cellular senescence, aberrant Wnt signaling, a mean onset around sixty-five. Then their generative chemistry engine designed the molecule. Target identification to preclinical candidate took roughly eighteen months. The preclinical and Phase one package went into Nature Biotechnology in twenty twenty-four; the Phase two-a went into Nature Medicine in June twenty twenty-five. In that Phase two-a, seventy-one patients across twenty-two Chinese sites got placebo, thirty milligrams once daily, thirty milligrams twice daily, or sixty milligrams once daily for twelve weeks. The primary endpoint was safety, which it met. The efficacy signal was exploratory and dose-dependent: in the sixty-milligram once-daily arm, forced vital capacity improved by a mean of ninety-eight point four milliliters at twelve weeks against a decline of twenty point three in placebo. In a disease where the approved antifibrotics only slow the rate of decline, an absolute F-V-C gain is the kind of number that gets attention. Phase three opened on the seventh of July this year — three hundred twenty patients, forty-seven centers in China, once-daily dosing, fifty-two weeks, with the primary endpoint being the annual rate of F-V-C decline. Hold onto the words "once daily."

Now the new paper. Forty-three of the seventy-one Phase two-a participants consented to serum proteomic profiling; one was dropped for a missing terminal sample, leaving forty-two — mean age sixty-seven, all Asian, blood drawn at baseline, week two, week four and week twelve, run on the Olink Explore three-thousand-seventy-two panel. Onto that they applied six published proteomic aging clocks: ProtAge from the Argentieri group, the proteomic aging clock from Kuo, two variants of OrganAge from Goeminne — one trained on chronological age, one on mortality risk — a proteome-aware organ proxy clock from the Han lab, and one of Insilico's own, ipfP3GPT. Note that last one. The press release describes six independently developed clocks from Harvard, Oxford, Peking University and Insilico, which is accurate, but one of the six is the sponsor's own model and one of the others is still a bioRxiv preprint. Five of six externally built is still a real test. It is not six.

The clock validation is the least contested part and it is genuinely useful field data. The four chronological clocks tracked actual age well in this cohort — Spearman correlations from zero point seven zero to zero point eight four, and after correcting a constant offset, root mean square errors under four years. That is notable because all six were trained on U-K Biobank participants who were mostly healthy, and here they are being applied to Chinese I-P-F patients. The two mortality-trained clocks correlated poorly with chronological age, zero point one six to zero point two three, which is what you would expect: they are reading disease burden, and everyone in this cohort has a fatal fibrotic lung disease. So far, so sane.

Then the treatment effect. They defined the change in predicted biological age from baseline, compared each treated arm to placebo at weeks two, four and twelve, and got fifty-four comparisons per arm — six clocks by three timepoints by three regimens. Twenty-one of those reached significance at a false discovery rate under zero point one zero, and they cluster hard at week four, where eleven of eighteen comparisons were significant. A permutation test shuffling patient labels put the chance expectation at zero point one five significant hits, so twenty-one is not noise. The effect sizes are large in the units people will quote: at week four in the sixty-milligram once-daily arm, all four chronological clocks registered reductions of between two point seven one and three point four six years of predicted biological age. And placebo drifted the other way — proteomic changes in the placebo group tracked the direction of normal aging, consistent with disease progression accelerating age-related proteomic decline. As a picture, it is coherent.

Here is the finding that matters most, and it is the one the authors lean on hardest. If the clocks were simply reading "this patient's lungs got better," then the arm with the best lung function should show the strongest age reversal. It does not. The sixty-milligram once-daily arm had the best F-V-C in the original trial, but the thirty-milligram twice-daily arm had the most consistent aging signal — nine significant comparisons versus seven, and agreement across five of six clocks at week four including both mortality clocks, which the sixty-milligram arm did not move at all. They then regressed change in F-V-C against change in predicted biological age across all forty-two patients: median R-squared of zero point zero six, range zero point zero one to zero point one eight. Lung function explains essentially none of the clock movement. And when they compared the direction of drug-induced protein changes against age-associated protein trajectories in fifty-five thousand three hundred nineteen older U-K Biobank adults, the thirty-milligram twice-daily regimen showed a significant negative correlation — it was preferentially reversing the direction proteins normally move with age — while the sixty-milligram arm showed nothing, despite its superior F-V-C. Pathway analysis lines up with the dissociation: proteins uniquely moved by the twice-daily regimen were enriched for metabolic and redox pathways — pentose phosphate, glutathione, cholesterol metabolism — while the once-daily arm's unique set was enriched for Wnt signaling and immune activity, which is target engagement and fibrosis, not aging.

There is a mechanistic layer underneath that I found more persuasive than the clocks themselves. Across three hundred twenty-six proteins with significantly altered trajectories, the classic fibrosis effectors came down — collagen one alpha one, matrix metalloproteinase ten, fibroblast activation protein, platelet-derived growth factor B — while stress-resistance and metabolic proteins went up, including N-A-M-P-T, the rate-limiting enzyme in N-A-D-plus biosynthesis, superoxide dismutase two, and A-L-D-H one-A-one. Gene set enrichment against three established senescence signatures showed placebo patients accumulating the senescence-associated secretory phenotype — matrix metalloproteinases one, three, nine, ten and thirteen, interleukins one-beta, six, ten and thirteen, chemokines — and every treated arm reversing it. Seven proteins appeared in the senescence leading edge of every treated arm. And against the K-E-double-G Medicus collection, the only significantly enriched pathways were growth factor signaling — receptor tyrosine kinase into P-I-three-K, into R-A-S and E-R-K — coordinately suppressed in all treated arms and elevated in placebo. Growth factor and nutrient sensing suppression is the most conserved anti-aging mechanism there is; it is what caloric restriction and rapamycin do. So there is a plausible biological story here that is not just "fibrosis got better."

Now the problem, which the authors put in print themselves. When they asked which individual proteins were actually driving the biological age shifts, the single most influential feature — and the only feature present in all six clocks — was L-T-B-P two, latent transforming growth factor beta binding protein two. A master regulator of fibrosis in the T-G-F-beta axis. The rest of the top thirty-five is heavy with S-P-P one, keratin nineteen, elastin, collagen six-A-three, matrix metalloproteinase twelve. So the aggregate clock signal is disproportionately driven by fibrotic proteins in a fibrosis trial. The dissociation arguments — the regimen mismatch, the low F-V-C R-squared, the U-K Biobank direction test — are all indirect, and the authors say so: full disentanglement is not achievable in an I-P-F cohort and requires validating the drug in healthy volunteers or in a non-fibrotic older population. There are three further constraints I would put in your notes. T-NIK is not on the Olink panel, so there is no direct measure of target engagement anywhere in this paper — everything is inferred from network and pathway analysis. Only one hundred forty-five of one thousand seven hundred eighty-seven Reactome pathways had enough measured proteins to be testable, so the pathway analysis is a keyhole. And the arms are nine to eleven patients each at any given timepoint. Thirty milligrams once daily produced exactly one significant time-dependent protein change in the entire panel, which is either clean dose-response or an underpowered arm, and with n equals eleven you cannot tell which.

So what should you actually do with this. Three things.

First, the geroscience framework is the real product, and it is more sophisticated than the press release suggests. The authors lay out a stepwise path that is explicitly built around what the F-D-A will and will not accept: collect aging and senescence biomarkers as prespecified exploratory endpoints inside trials for recognized age-related diseases; replicate the effect in a non-fibrotic, risk-enriched older population where disease modification is not driving the signal; and only then pursue formal biomarker qualification through the F-D-A's Biomarker Qualification Program and the joint F-D-A and N-I-H BEST framework. They cite the right precedents — the TAME concept, which proposed testing metformin against a composite of incident age-related diseases rather than against aging itself, and the evolution of the incretins from weight loss into cardiovascular risk reduction. Both are cases where a biology-linked risk state became a regulatory-tractable indication. Nobody is getting an aging indication. Somebody might get a qualified aging biomarker, and the company that has longitudinal proteomics from a controlled trial when that happens has an asset. That is the actual strategic move being made here.

Second, the dosing finding is a live commercial problem, not a footnote. Rentosertib has a seven-to-eleven-hour half-life, so once-daily sixty milligrams gives you two to three times the peak concentration of either thirty-milligram regimen, with deep troughs. The best fibrosis number came from the high peak. The best aging and metabolic signal came from steady coverage. And the Phase three that opened in July is once daily over fifty-two weeks with annual F-V-C decline as its primary endpoint. That is the right call for a registrational trial — you optimize for the endpoint the regulator recognizes — but it means the geroprotection story and the approvable asset are now on diverging regimens. If Phase three succeeds on F-V-C, Insilico owns an antifibrotic and the aging thesis becomes a research program requiring separate trials at a different schedule. If Phase three fails, the aging clock data does not save it. Watch for whether they run a parallel twice-daily cohort in a non-I-P-F older population — that is the single action that would tell you they believe their own paper.

Third, on how to price the platform. Insilico is now publicly traded in Hong Kong, has reported roughly seven point three billion dollars in total announced contract value in twenty twenty-six, and nominated nine development candidates in nine months. Set the aging story aside entirely and the durable fact is this: an A-I-nominated novel target, against a protein nobody was developing, with an A-I-designed molecule, is in a three-hundred-twenty-patient Phase three with a fifty-two-week hard endpoint. That is further than any other pure A-I discovery claim has gotten, and it is the only version of the claim that a corporate development group can price. Compare it to the counterargument I flagged in the headlines — the Avicenna piece arguing that foundation models are corroded by irreproducible literature and that A-I's real near-term value is in target-network identification and translational patient selection. Insilico's result is exactly that shape: a target-discovery win, plus a biomarker and patient-selection story. It is not the hundred-years-in-ten claim. It is the narrower claim, and the narrower claim is the one with a Phase three attached.

One last observation about how this was handled. Two of the peer reviewers, Tamir Chandra and Samuel Crofts, waived anonymity and their reports are published alongside the paper. On a contested claim from a company whose founder and chief executive is the first author, and where six co-authors are company employees, that transparency is worth something — and if you are going to underwrite this, read the reviewer reports before the press release. The company's own discussion section is more useful to you than its own headline. That is the tell of a real paper, and it is also the reason to keep the age reversal claim in the "interesting hypothesis with a controlled dataset behind it" column rather than the "validated mechanism" column.
