This is your spotlight for Saturday, November seventh, 2026. Today: Sarepta has decided it will not restart enrollment in ENVISION, the Phase 3 trial of its Duchenne gene therapy Elevidys in older and non-ambulatory boys. It sounds like a line item from an earnings call, and that's roughly how it was delivered. But it's the end of the largest randomized test of micro-dystrophin gene therapy in the patients where the benefit-risk question was hardest, and it lands in a week when the whole systemic A-A-V field is being re-underwritten.

Start with what Elevidys is. It's delandistrogene moxeparvovec, a one-time intravenous A-A-V-rh-seventy-four infusion that delivers a shortened, functional version of the dystrophin gene, a micro-dystrophin, to skeletal and heart muscle. Boys with Duchenne lack dystrophin; their muscle fibers tear and are replaced by fat and scar. The bet behind micro-dystrophin is that a truncated protein, the kind that milder Becker patients naturally make, can turn a lethal disease into a slower one. The list price is about three point two million dollars a dose.

The regulatory history matters here, so briefly. Elevidys got accelerated approval in June 2023 for young ambulatory boys, on micro-dystrophin expression. In 2024 the F-D-A granted full approval for ambulatory patients four and older, and accelerated approval for non-ambulatory patients, despite the pivotal EMBARK trial missing its primary functional endpoint. ENVISION was the trial that was supposed to confirm benefit in that harder population: boys who can no longer walk, and ambulatory boys aged eight to seventeen. Then came 2025. Two non-ambulatory teenagers who received Elevidys died of acute liver failure, in March and June, and a third patient died in a limb-girdle trial using the same vector backbone. Sarepta paused ENVISION dosing that summer, after enrolling about half of its hundred-fifty patient target. After a very public fight with the agency, shipments paused, a boxed warning for liver toxicity went on, and the non-ambulatory indication came off the label. Today Elevidys is indicated only for ambulatory patients four and older.

So what happened on Thursday's call? New C-E-O Michael Severino, ninety days into the job after senior roles at AbbVie and Amgen, said ENVISION enrollment will not resume. Instead, Sarepta plans to bring the F-D-A twelve-week safety data in the first quarter of 2027 from the non-ambulatory cohort of a different study, ENDEAVOR, cohort eight, which is testing whether prophylactic sirolimus, an m-TOR inhibitor and immunosuppressant, given around the infusion can blunt the acute liver injury. Chief scientist Louise Rodino-Klapac framed it as continuity: "Notwithstanding our decision to not resume enrollment in ENVISION, our commitment to serve non-ambulatory individuals with Duchenne remains steadfast." And: "ENDEAVOR Cohort 8 remains central to this commitment."

Here's the analytical point. Those are two different kinds of evidence. ENVISION was a randomized, placebo-controlled efficacy trial. Its job was to answer whether micro-dystrophin changes the course of disease in boys whose muscle is already substantially gone. Cohort eight is an open-label safety cohort. Its job is to answer whether you can infuse those boys without killing their livers. If the path back onto the label runs through cohort eight, Sarepta is effectively arguing that the efficacy question for older patients has been answered well enough by everything else it has, the integrated placebo-controlled data, the long-term external-control comparisons, and that the remaining barrier is safety mitigation. BioSpace reports the company says it has collected a larger placebo-controlled dataset and established a benefit-risk profile, and is working with the agency to restore the non-ambulatory population. That may be right. But it means the cleanest randomized answer for non-ambulatory boys, half-enrolled and paused for more than a year, will never exist.

Severino's own framing on effect size is telling. Asked about Sarepta's mixed history on clean trial wins, he said, "if you want to have a positive study, you need to have the most substantial effect size you can." Read charitably, that's a statement about patient selection: run trials where the drug has room to work, which in a degenerative muscle disease means younger and earlier. Read less charitably, it's an admission that ENVISION's population was always where a clean efficacy win was least likely, and that finishing it carried real risk of a public negative in the very population Sarepta wants back.

Now the commercial picture. Elevidys did eighty-three and a half million dollars in net revenue in the third quarter. That's against a hundred thirty-one and a half million a year ago, and a hundred eighty-one million the year before that. It came in just above William Blair's eighty-one and a half million estimate and below the ninety-five million consensus. Sarepta guides the fourth quarter roughly flat. William Blair still found something to like: "key leading indicators and enrollment forms for Elevidys were in line with the second quarter, demonstrating stabilization of the franchise," noting more physician willingness to treat and families resuming treatment journeys after pausing. Jefferies expects a rebound in 2027 behind a marketing push. About fifteen hundred patients have been treated since launch.

Two other line items say more than the headline. First, cost of sales included a sixty-two million dollar take-or-pay charge on non-cancelable manufacturing obligations with Catalent. That's what it looks like when a gene therapy was built for a demand curve that didn't arrive: you pay for capacity you can't use. Second, contract manufacturing revenue rose thirty-one million on commercial Elevidys supply delivered to Roche, Sarepta's ex-U-S partner, and earlier this year Sarepta booked three hundred sixty-five million in collaboration revenue when Roche declined an option on certain program rights. The partner relationship is still producing cash, but in the direction of Roche stepping back.

Meanwhile, the business is now mostly not gene therapy. Of three hundred twenty-four million in net product revenue, two hundred forty million came from the P-M-O exon skippers, Exondys, Vyondys and Amondys, the antisense morpholinos that make cells skip a mutated exon so they produce a shortened dystrophin. Sarepta raised the bottom of full-year guidance by fifty million to a range of one point two five to one point three billion, posted positive cash flow, and ended with just under a billion dollars in cash. The growth story it's telling investors is the Arrowhead s-i-RNA portfolio it licensed in late 2024: SRP-one-thousand-three in myotonic dystrophy type one, knocking down toxic D-M-P-K R-N-A, and SRP-one-thousand-one in facioscapulohumeral dystrophy, knocking down D-U-X-four. Multiple ascending dose data for both are due by year end, and the company is already manufacturing Phase 3 supply.

And every pillar of the Duchenne franchise is under pressure at once. The exon skippers: Vyondys 53 and Amondys 45 both failed their confirmatory trial last November, and Sarepta resubmitted anyway; a decision on converting them to full approval is due in February 2027. Exondys 51 faces Dyne's zeleciment rostudirsen, an exon-fifty-one skipper conjugated to a transferrin-receptor-binding antibody fragment to get far more oligonucleotide into muscle, with an F-D-A decision by January twenty-first. Oppenheimer said in July it's likely to be approved and poised to "capture the majority of market share." Severino's response was that "wholesale switching of stable patients is not likely early on," while conceding erosion could start in the second half of 2027. And on the gene therapy side, REGENXBIO reiterated Thursday that it'll file RGX-two-oh-two, its own micro-dystrophin with the C-terminal domain included, in the first quarter of 2027, aiming at accelerated approval in the second half. Severino's argument there is urgency: muscle loss is irreversible, and families won't wait for a second product when a fully approved one exists.

Add one more piece of context. Capricor's deramiocel, a cardiosphere-derived cell therapy for Duchenne, has an F-D-A decision on November twenty-second, after a nine-to-three advisory committee vote against it in July. Duchenne has become the stress test for how this F-D-A handles accelerated approvals with weak or failed confirmatory evidence: the exon skippers, Elevidys, deramiocel, and soon Dyne. ENVISION's quiet end adds a new variant to that test: what happens when a sponsor walks away from the confirmatory trial after the indication it was meant to confirm has already been taken off the label?

So, three takeaways for Foresite and Calibr.

First, on gene therapy as a category. Yesterday we covered Novartis confirming tumors with vector integration in Zolgensma-treated children. Today the most commercially successful systemic A-A-V product in muscle is ending its randomized trial in the highest-dose, highest-risk population and staking its comeback on immunosuppressive prophylaxis. Both stories point the same way. High-dose systemic A-A-V is not dead, but the burden has shifted from proving expression to proving you can manage the vector: the liver, the immune response, and now integration. Programs that can't make that argument cleanly will struggle for capital.

Second, on trial design. Severino's effect-size comment is the lesson. Sarepta's confirmatory trials have repeatedly been run where the drug's effect was hardest to see, older patients, functional endpoints with high variance, and the result has been a string of misses followed by regulatory arguments. For any program heading toward an accelerated approval, the confirmatory trial is the asset. Design it, enroll it and finish it while the leverage is yours.

Third, on modality rotation. Sarepta is becoming an oligonucleotide company, and the competitive threat to its oligonucleotide franchise is a better-delivered oligonucleotide. Dyne's antibody-conjugated approach, Avidity's, now Novartis's, and Arrowhead's own muscle-targeted s-i-RNA platform all say the same thing: in muscle disease, delivery is the product. That's directly relevant to anyone building conjugate or targeting platforms.

What to watch: the Arrowhead-partnered D-M-one and F-S-H-D dose-escalation data by year end; Capricor on November twenty-second; Dyne on January twenty-first; the ENDEAVOR cohort-eight safety package in the first quarter; REGENXBIO's filing, with its own clinical holds and yesterday's integration questions hanging over the A-A-V-nine class; and the Vyondys and Amondys decision in February. By spring, we'll know whether Sarepta's Duchenne franchise has a floor.
