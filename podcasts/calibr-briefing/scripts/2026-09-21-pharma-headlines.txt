Good morning Travis. Here are your biotech and pharma headlines for Monday, September twenty-first, twenty twenty-six. The tape woke up. Two genuinely net-new items landed this morning, and they are both consequential — Novo laid out its twenty-thirty ambitions in London and the stock fell hard on them, and a Syncona-backed gene therapy company posted the first pivotal win anyone has ever recorded in X-linked retinitis pigmentosa, in a disease where Johnson and Johnson and Biogen both failed. That second one is the spotlight. Around those, a head-to-head-flavored anti-immunoglobulin-E readout from Sunday, a Reuters scoop on how Treasury intends to treat China licensing, and a few items from late last week that did not make the weekend briefings. Eight items, then the week ahead.

First, Novo. The company held its capital markets day in London this morning, the first under Maziar Doustdar, and it published its strategic ambitions for twenty thirty. The headline numbers: more than five products with multi-blockbuster potential launched by twenty thirty; a risk-adjusted pipeline generating more than one hundred fifty billion Danish kroner in sales by twenty thirty-five; at least five Phase three programs in obesity and diabetes and at least five Phase three programs in other therapy areas; capacity scaled to serve ten times more people with obesity on an oral G-L-P-one; and more than sixty million patients served globally. Then the sentence that moved the stock. Novo said it expects compound annual revenue growth from twenty twenty-six through twenty thirty to be in line with industry peers, with operating margins broadly stable. The peer basket it named has fourteen companies in it — Lilly, AstraZeneca, Gilead, Johnson and Johnson, AbbVie, Novartis, Sanofi, Roche, GSK, Amgen, Merck, Biogen, Pfizer and Bristol Myers. Shares fell about six percent on the day. Read it plainly: a company that has spent a decade growing at multiples of the sector just told investors to model it as a normal large pharma. The ambitions themselves are the tell — two of the four pipeline targets are explicitly outside diabetes and obesity, which is what the Anthropic, Orbis and Kallyope deals of the last ten days were buying. Novo is announcing a transition from a franchise company to a portfolio company, and the market is charging it for the transition.

Second, and today's spotlight. Beacon Therapeutics reported positive topline data from the pivotal VISTA trial of laru-zova, a subretinal gene therapy for X-linked retinitis pigmentosa caused by mutations in the R-P-G-R gene. The trial met its primary endpoint, which was the proportion of patients gaining at least fifteen letters of low-luminance visual acuity at twelve months against an untreated control. Thirty-one percent of high-dose patients responded, twenty-four percent of low-dose patients responded, and zero percent of controls did. Eighty-five men and boys, ages twelve to forty-eight. Beacon says this is the first pivotal study in this disease ever to hit its primary endpoint with statistical significance, and that is correct — Biogen's cotoretigene missed in twenty twenty-one, and Johnson and Johnson's bota-vec missed in twenty twenty-five. Beacon plans a rolling biologics license application starting later this year, with the full dataset at the American Academy of Ophthalmology meeting on October tenth. Syncona owns thirty-eight point four percent of Beacon, carried at one hundred eighty-three point four million pounds as of the end of June. Why this is the episode: the interesting question is not whether the drug works, it is why three companies with broadly similar biology got three different answers, and the answer appears to be the endpoint.

Third, an anti-immunoglobulin-E readout that picked a fight. Cue Biopharma reported topline Phase two results Sunday for C-U-E two twenty-one in chronic spontaneous urticaria — one hundred forty-five patients with moderate-to-severe disease inadequately controlled on antihistamines, run with its partner Genesis Life Sciences in China. The primary endpoint was complete resolution of hives at week twelve. At the four milligram per kilogram dose, fifty-four percent of patients achieved it, against eleven percent on placebo — and forty-one percent on omalizumab, which was in the trial as an active arm. On the key secondary, complete response on the urticaria activity score, it was forty-six percent versus eleven on placebo and twenty-nine on omalizumab. Cue is moving to a Phase two-b-three and still plans a Phase two in food allergy. Two caveats before you get excited. This was run in China, so the population and the standard of care are not identical to a U-S registrational setting, and the omalizumab arm was not powered as a formal superiority comparison. But a numerical beat on Xolair in a trial that contained Xolair is the kind of result that reprices an anti-immunoglobulin-E asset, and the chronic urticaria market just got its first oral B-T-K entrant, so the competitive floor is rising.

Fourth, the most commercially important thing that happened last week may have been a Reuters exclusive. Treasury is drafting outbound investment rules for pharma, and the reporting says the rules will likely preserve the ability of U-S companies to strike most licensing deals for Chinese-developed drugs, with carve-outs limited to pathogens and biotechnologies with weapons potential. GlobalData puts out-licensing from Chinese biotech at one hundred fifteen billion dollars last year. That is a materially looser posture than the semiconductor and artificial intelligence precedent, and looser than what some members of Congress have been pushing for. Reuters also reports Treasury is unlikely to publish anything before the Xi-Trump meeting. Set this next to Friday's Chinese five-year plan, which targets twenty-five percent of the world's first-in-class drugs originating in China: the supply side is accelerating and the U-S demand side is about to be told it can keep buying. For anyone modeling in-licensing as a pipeline strategy, that is the single most important regulatory signal of the month.

Fifth, a government program worth knowing about. ARPA-H launched SPECTRA last Thursday — the acronym stands for Systems for Phenotypic Evaluation, Clinical Trajectories, Response, and Agency — a major autism research effort announced by Health and Human Services. The design is the interesting part: rather than funding another etiology hunt, SPECTRA is meant to build an end-to-end system that fuses biological, clinical, behavioral and real-world data with computational models to enable earlier diagnosis, predict individual developmental trajectories, and route patients to interventions. Four technical areas — analysis, modeling, precision intervention, and enabling technologies. Funding was not disclosed, proposals open next month, and the first solution-summary deadline is October eighteenth for the enabling-technologies track. It uses Other Transaction agreements, which means it can move fast and fund non-traditional performers. If you have a portfolio company doing multimodal phenotyping or trajectory modeling, this is a real non-dilutive channel.

Sixth, more from Psych Congress, which produced most of last week's clinical news. Otsuka presented Phase three-b results for Simtriyo, centanafadine, in adults with attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder and comorbid anxiety disorders. Centanafadine is a norepinephrine, dopamine and serotonin reuptake inhibitor, approved earlier this year for A-D-H-D in adults and in children six and up. At week eight the drug cut the adult A-D-H-D investigator symptom rating scale by eighteen point five points against twelve point six for placebo, a treatment difference of about five point nine, with consistent improvements in anxiety symptoms, executive function and emotional dysregulation. The commercial point is straightforward: comorbid anxiety is present in a large fraction of adult A-D-H-D and is precisely the population where prescribers hesitate to use stimulants. A non-stimulant with clean data in that subgroup is a label expansion argument and a positioning argument at the same time.

Seventh, a small trial start with an unusual patient selection. SpliSense has begun a Phase two-b of S-P-L eighty-four, an inhaled antisense oligonucleotide, in cystic fibrosis patients carrying the thirty-eight forty-nine plus ten kilobase C-to-T splicing mutation. Roughly forty participants, randomized four to one against placebo, fifty milligrams once weekly by inhalation for twelve weeks — and critically, all of them stay on Trikafta, Kaftrio or Alyftrek. This is a drug for the patients the modulators do not fully fix, dosed on top of the modulators. The company reports up to seventy percent of Phase two-a patients gained at least five points of lung function. Topline in the second half of twenty twenty-seven. Worth tracking as a template: inhaled oligonucleotides for splicing mutations is a modality-plus-genotype niche that the big C-F franchise cannot address.

Eighth, briefly, a chief executive change. Bavarian Nordic named Tarja Stenvall, a Sanofi and AstraZeneca veteran, as chief executive effective October fifteenth, succeeding Paul Chaplin after a long tenure.
