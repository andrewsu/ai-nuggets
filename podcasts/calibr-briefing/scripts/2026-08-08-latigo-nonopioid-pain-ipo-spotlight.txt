Today's spotlight is Latigo Biotherapeutics, which went public Thursday — and it's worth your attention for three separate reasons at once: it's a Foresite Capital portfolio company reaching the public market, it's the clearest test yet of whether the new non-opioid pain class can be more than a single Vertex drug, and it's another data point that the biotech financing window is genuinely, durably open.

Start with what happened. Latigo priced an upsized initial public offering at eighteen dollars a share — nineteen point two million shares — for gross proceeds of three hundred forty-five point six million dollars, with an underwriters' option that could push it higher. Shares began trading on the Nasdaq on Thursday. The banks running it were Goldman Sachs, Jefferies, Leerink, and Guggenheim — a top-tier syndicate. And the investor base going in tells you the pedigree: Foresite Capital, Westlake Village BioPartners, Five A-M Ventures, and Blue Owl. So for Travis this is doubly relevant — a Foresite name, and a marquee entry in the space the portfolio cares about.

Now the science, because this is the part that makes Latigo more than just another hot IPO. The target is a sodium channel called Nav one-point-eight. Here's the intuition: pain signals travel from your body to your spinal cord as electrical impulses in peripheral sensory neurons, and those impulses depend on sodium channels opening to let the nerve fire. Nav one-point-eight is a sodium channel that sits almost exclusively on those peripheral pain-sensing neurons — it's not in your brain, it's not in your heart. So if you block it, you can turn down pain signaling at the source without the central-nervous-system effects that make opioids addictive and sedating, and without the cardiac and neurological liabilities that sank older, non-selective sodium-channel blockers. That's the whole thesis of the class: peripheral, selective, non-addictive analgesia.

This isn't hypothetical anymore. Vertex proved the target is real when it won FDA approval last year for suzetrigine — brand name Journavx — the first genuinely new class of acute pain medicine in decades and the first Nav one-point-eight blocker to market. So the category is de-risked at the level of biology. But Journavx's launch has been more measured than the hype suggested — the efficacy in its pivotal trials was solid against placebo but did not clearly beat the opioid comparator, and payers and prescribers have moved cautiously. That gap — a validated target, but a first drug whose real-world punch left room — is precisely the opening Latigo is selling into.

And this is where the data matter. Latigo's lead drug is an oral Nav one-point-eight inhibitor for acute pain, and its phase 2-b trial was published in the New England Journal of Medicine — which itself is a signal, because that journal doesn't publish middling mid-stage pain data. The study enrolled three hundred forty-three patients undergoing abdominoplasty — a tummy-tuck — which is a standard, well-controlled acute post-surgical pain model. The headline number is a pain-relief score the field calls SPID-48, essentially the summed reduction in pain intensity over the first forty-eight hours. Latigo's high dose scored about sixty-two — which the company says is the highest analgesic effect ever reported for any drug tested in that abdominoplasty model. For contrast, the opioid comparator in the same trial, a hydrocodone-acetaminophen combination, scored about forty-one. In other words, the non-opioid numerically outperformed the opioid — which is exactly the bar Journavx did not clear, and exactly the differentiation a second entrant needs.

The opioid-sparing data reinforce it: about fifty-two percent of patients on the drug stayed completely opioid-free through treatment, versus twenty-two percent on placebo. That's the metric that actually matters to hospitals, to payers, and to a health system still trying to dig out of the opioid crisis — not just "does it relieve pain," but "does it let you avoid opioids altogether."

Now, the honest caveats, because a spotlight owes you those. First, cross-trial comparisons are treacherous. Latigo's claim that it beats Journavx rests on comparing its abdominoplasty numbers to Vertex's separately run trials — different studies, different patients, different sites. The only way to truly settle it is head-to-head or a much larger phase 3, and we don't have that. Second, the pivotal phase 3 will move to a different pain model — bunionectomy, foot surgery, which is the FDA's preferred acute-pain model and the one Vertex used — so the phase 2-b abdominoplasty result has to translate to a new setting. Acute-pain drugs have failed exactly at this translation step before. Third, this is an IPO-stage company: the money raised funds the phase 3 program, but approval, launch, and reimbursement are all still ahead, and the acute-pain commercial model — short courses, mostly hospital and post-surgical — is a harder business to build than a chronic therapy.

On that last point, Latigo has a second act. Beyond the acute-pain lead, it has a next-generation Nav one-point-eight inhibitor aimed at chronic pain — starting with osteoarthritis of the knee — with a proof-of-concept phase 2 reading out in the second half of next year. Chronic pain is the far larger prize and the far harder problem; if a selective, non-addictive channel blocker works there, that's a multi-billion-dollar market that opioids and anti-inflammatories serve badly. So the company is a two-shot story: near-term, out-execute Journavx in acute pain; longer-term, extend the class into chronic pain where nothing good exists.

Let me put the competitive landscape in one frame. Vertex is the incumbent and the validator — Journavx on the market, deep pockets, and its own next-generation and chronic-pain programs behind it. Latigo is the fast follower betting on better efficacy and the opioid-beating data. And there's a broader pack of Nav one-point-eight and related channel programs across the industry, because once Vertex proved the target, capital rushed in. The risk for Latigo is the classic fast-follower squeeze — being second to market against a company that can iterate. The opportunity is that the first drug underdelivered relative to the biology, and there's real clinical and commercial room for a better one.

Zoom out and here's why this lands on today's briefing. One: it's a Foresite portfolio company converting private conviction into a public position at a healthy valuation — a genuine mark for the portfolio. Two: it's the strongest evidence yet that non-opioid pain is becoming a category, not a one-drug curiosity, which is one of the more consequential shifts in medicine given the stakes around opioids. And three: it's the latest in a remarkable run of upsized biotech IPOs this summer — Latigo, Attovia, Braveheart, Apnimed, and more — the twelfth company to clear a quarter-billion-dollar raise this year. That window matters to everyone in this space, portfolio companies included, because it's the difference between building on your own terms and selling early. Watch three things from here: the bunionectomy phase 3 design and timeline, whether the opioid-beating efficacy holds up outside the abdominoplasty model, and how Vertex responds now that it has a follower claiming better numbers.
