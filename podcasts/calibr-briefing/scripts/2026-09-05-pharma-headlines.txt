Good morning Travis. Here are your biotech and pharma headlines for Saturday, September fifth, twenty twenty-six, covering Friday's news.

After a week of Phase three wreckage and a first-ever approval, Friday broke the pattern completely: almost no clinical data at all, and instead a full day of capital. A San Diego R-N-A company filed to go public on Friday night with four hundred twenty-seven million dollars already in the bank. Intellia borrowed four hundred million without issuing a share. Menarini bought its way into European obesity from China. And Truist published a survey of sector leaders whose thesis is, more or less, that the drought is over. Nine items, then the week ahead.

First, and today's spotlight. ADARx Pharmaceuticals filed its S-one with the Securities and Exchange Commission on Friday evening, seeking to list on the Nasdaq Global Market under the ticker A-D-R-X, with J-P-Morgan, Morgan Stanley and T-D Cowen leading. ADARx sits on Oberlin Drive in San Diego, about fifteen minutes from Calibr, and it develops small interfering R-N-A therapeutics — drugs that recruit the cell's own silencing machinery to chew up a target messenger R-N-A before it is ever translated into protein. Three programs are in the clinic and two more are heading into investigational new drug enabling studies. The clinical three all go to the liver: agazisiran against complement factor B, onvuzosiran against prekallikrein for hereditary angioedema, and A-D-X six-twenty-six against factor eleven for secondary stroke prevention. The two preclinical ones are the interesting part of the story, because they go somewhere else entirely — one into fat, one into neurons. And the headline financial fact is that this company does not need the money. As of June thirtieth it held four hundred twenty-seven point three million dollars in cash and short-term investments, against a lifetime accumulated deficit of only two hundred sixty-nine million. It is going public because the window is open. The spotlight takes the whole filing apart.

Second, and it rhymes in a way worth pausing on. Intellia Therapeutics closed a four hundred million dollar non-dilutive senior secured term loan with OrbiMed — seventy-five million funded at closing, five further tranches totaling up to two hundred twenty-five million drawable against milestones tied mainly to lonvoguran ziclumeran, and another hundred million subject to mutual agreement over a five-year term. T-D Cowen advised Intellia. Lonvo-z is an in vivo C-R-I-S-P-R Cas-nine therapy that permanently inactivates the kallikrein B-one gene in the liver — a one-time, one-and-done treatment for hereditary angioedema, heading toward a U-S approval and launch. Chief financial officer Edward Dulac framed it as freedom to execute the launch without going back to the equity market. Now hold that thought against item one, because ADARx's lead Phase three program, onvuzosiran, silences prekallikrein — the product of that same gene — reversibly, with a subcutaneous injection every three to six months. On the same Friday, OrbiMed is the largest shareholder in the company silencing the gene and the lender to the company deleting it. That is not conviction about a modality. That is a hedge across two ways of hitting one target, and the spotlight comes back to it.

Third, Italy's Menarini Group bought its way into European obesity, licensing bofanglutide — the molecule also known as G-Z-R eighteen — from China's Gan and Lee. Bofanglutide is a G-L-P-one receptor agonist engineered for once-every-two-weeks subcutaneous dosing, against weekly for semaglutide and tirzepatide. Menarini takes exclusive rights to register and commercialize across thirty-nine countries — the E-U twenty-seven plus the U-K, Switzerland, Norway, Iceland, Liechtenstein and the Balkans — for sixty-two million euros up front, up to six hundred sixty-four million in milestones, and double-digit royalties. Roughly seven hundred twenty-six million euros all in, call it eight hundred forty million dollars. Gan and Lee keeps the global Phase three program; Menarini owns the regulatory filings and the commercial build in Europe. Two things are worth noting. This is Gan and Lee's largest ex-China licensing deal to date, and its stock limited up on the news. And the differentiation claim is dosing interval rather than weight loss — which is the correct read of where the incretin market is going now that efficacy is roughly table stakes and adherence is the actual failure mode. Recall that roughly eighty-five percent of patients discontinue incretin therapy over time.

Fourth, the mood music, and it is loud. Truist Securities published a note Thursday after surveying sector leaders, and the headline is that biotech is back. The S-and-P Biotech E-T-F, the X-B-I, ended the first half up thirty-four percent year to date and was still there as of Friday. Mergers and acquisitions have run a hundred thirty-six billion dollars across fifty-seven deals, already clearing the entire deal count for twenty twenty-five. Initial public offerings did five point nine billion dollars across twenty-one debuts in the first half, against one point five four billion for all of twenty twenty-five. Companies trading below their net cash have fallen from twenty-two percent to fifteen. The Food and Drug Administration has cleared thirty-six novel drugs out of the drug evaluation center through August twenty-eighth, ten of them through the Commissioner's National Priority Review program — including Revolution Medicines' Rasonque, approved six and a half months early. Truist's read on policy is that regulatory risk is largely played out and that most favored nation pricing now reads as an accepted cost of doing business. Their caution, and it is the sentence to keep: the drivers are largely positive, but with less room for error than at the start of the year. On mega-deals the surveyed leaders were split, with a slim majority leaning yes. Abivax gets named as the most likely near-term takeout, at a roughly ten-billion-dollar market capitalization.

Fifth, and underneath that headline number, a concentration worth naming. BioPharma Dive's weekly chart package puts Eli Lilly at twelve acquisitions announced since the start of twenty twenty-six — four times the pace of Gilead, GSK or Novartis — and thirty-one and a half billion dollars in upfront payments, double the next-busiest acquirer, funded by incretin cash. Separately, cross-border licensing deals with Chinese biotechs have now passed a hundred since the start of twenty twenty-five, with upfronts from large pharma exceeding five hundred fifty million dollars per deal in oncology and obesity. So when Truist says M-and-A is back, part of what that means is that one company is doing an outsized share of it, and a growing share of the assets are being sourced from Asia. That is a different market than a broad recovery.

Sixth, Tarsus Pharmaceuticals closed its acquisition of Alkeus Pharmaceuticals on Friday — two hundred seventy million dollars in cash and a hundred eighty million in Tarsus stock, with up to three hundred fifty million more contingent on gildeuretinol reaching the market. Gildeuretinol, formerly A-L-K double-oh-one, is an orally dosed deuterated form of vitamin A designed to slow the formation of toxic vitamin A dimers in the retina while leaving the normal visual cycle intact. The indication is Stargardt disease, an inherited macular dystrophy with no approved therapy, and the drug carries Breakthrough Therapy, Orphan Drug, Rare Pediatric Disease and Fast Track designations. The global Phase three, NORTHSTAR, reads out in twenty twenty-nine. Tarsus is a commercial eye-care company on the back of Xdemvy in Demodex blepharitis, so this is a revenue-generating specialty commercial platform buying a long-dated rare-disease pipeline asset — the same lifecycle logic driving the large-cap deals, at one-hundredth the scale.

Seventh, on the venture side, NeuShen Therapeutics closed an oversubscribed Series B of more than eighty million dollars for three clinical-stage central nervous system programs. NeuShen runs out of Shanghai and Boston and is led by Joan Shen, previously head of research and development and then chief executive of I-Mab — the company that listed on Nasdaq and struck a global collaboration with AbbVie under that leadership. The round drew B Capital, Legend Capital, Matrix Partners China, ZhenFund and Shanghai State-owned Capital Investment, with existing investor Lilly Asia Ventures returning. Note that Lilly Asia Ventures also holds fifteen point seven percent of ADARx. The U-S-China founder-operator model keeps clearing rounds even as Washington tightens on foreign trial data.

Eighth, a small item with an interesting shape. Lindus Health, the Peter Thiel-backed transatlantic startup that raised fifty-five million dollars last year explicitly branding itself the anti-C-R-O, has sold that contract research business and rebranded as Lindus Therapeutics. The new model is in-licensing clinical-stage assets from other people's pipelines and running them through its own trial execution platform. Deals on initial assets are targeted to close through the end of this year, first trials in the first half of twenty twenty-seven. Read it as a verdict on the economics: the operational efficiency was real, but selling efficiency to sponsors captures a service margin, and owning the asset you make more efficient captures the asset. That is the same arbitrage several artificial intelligence discovery platforms have quietly made over the past two years.

And ninth, a global health note that will not move a stock but should be on the radar. The Coalition for Epidemic Preparedness Innovations has now put roughly a hundred million dollars across five-plus Ebola vaccine candidates, including work at Moderna and Hilleman Labs, targeting the Bundibugyo strain, with Gavi committing up to fifty million more to secure manufacturing and outbreak response. C-E-P-I says it still needs another hundred twenty-eight million to keep the program on pace, and the broader global health funding contraction is putting that at risk. This is the clearest current example of the push-pull financing model for a vaccine with no commercial market, and if it stalls it stalls for reasons that have nothing to do with the science.
