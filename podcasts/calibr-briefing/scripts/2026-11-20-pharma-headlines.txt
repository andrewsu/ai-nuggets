Good morning, Travis. It's Friday, November twentieth, 2026. Sanofi is reportedly sizing up the biggest biotech takeover in years, Isomorphic signed Merck two days after raising five billion dollars, Novartis lowered its growth outlook, and Washington put more pressure on pharma's ties to China. Here's what you need to know.

First, the potential deal of the year. Bloomberg reported Thursday that Sanofi is working with advisers on a possible takeover of Insmed. Insmed sells Brinsupri, or brensocatib, an oral inhibitor of D-P-P-one, the enzyme that activates neutrophil-damaging proteases, approved last year for non-cystic fibrosis bronchiectasis. According to the report, a deal could cost thirty-five to forty-three billion dollars. That would make it the largest biopharma acquisition since Pfizer bought Seagen in 2023. Talks are early, there's no guarantee of a deal, and other bidders could still appear. Both companies declined to comment. Insmed closed up about twenty-one percent at one hundred forty-four dollars, roughly a thirty-two billion dollar market cap, and Sanofi fell almost three percent in Paris. Brinsupri made four hundred million dollars last quarter, and Dupixent, which is about forty percent of Sanofi's sales, loses U-S patent protection in 2031. One detail stands out: Paul Hudson, the Sanofi C-E-O whom the board replaced in February, joined Insmed's board last month. It's today's spotlight.

Second, A-I drug discovery. Merck signed a research collaboration with Isomorphic Labs, the Alphabet spinout run by Demis Hassabis, two days after Isomorphic's record five billion dollar Series C. The scope is narrow: one oncology target that Merck calls strategically important, and small molecules only. Isomorphic runs its drug design engine, called Iso-D-D-E, on a selection of Merck's proprietary datasets and hands over molecular designs. Merck does everything from preclinical work onward: development, manufacturing and sales. Isomorphic gets an upfront payment, milestones and tiered royalties, and an option to co-fund development for higher royalties. Neither company disclosed the numbers. Merck's discovery head, George Addona, said the key to A-I molecular design is close coordination between the wet lab and the dry lab. Merck joins Lilly, Novartis and Johnson and Johnson as Isomorphic's announced partners. As we said on Wednesday, the Lilly and Novartis deals together paid a little over eighty million dollars upfront, and the company still hasn't named a clinical candidate. Pharmaphorum now puts its first trials most likely in 2027. For context, McKinsey estimates that only about two percent of the twenty-nine and a half billion dollars in headline value from big pharma's 2025 A-I discovery deals was paid upfront. So the useful signal here is the co-fund option. If Isomorphic uses it, the company is putting some of that new money into Merck's program rather than just selling designs.

Third, Novartis lowered its sights at its annual investor day in London. It now expects sales to grow about five percent a year at constant currency from 2026 through 2031, the bottom of its previous five to six percent range, and it says that assumes no large acquisitions. Shares fell about three percent. Two late-stage failures this year forced a two point two billion dollar writedown in the third quarter. One of them was del-desiran, the R-N-A drug for myotonic dystrophy that came with the twelve billion dollar Avidity buyout, and that has put C-E-O Vas Narasimhan's deal record under scrutiny. Novartis did raise peak sales forecasts for three drugs. Kesimpta, its C-D-twenty antibody for multiple sclerosis, goes to eight billion dollars. Scemblix, the allosteric B-C-R-ABL inhibitor, and Leqvio, the PCSK9 siRNA, each go to five billion. Losing patent protection on Entresto costs it about four billion a year. Narasimhan said most growth after 2031 will come from medicines Novartis developed itself.

Fourth, China, from two directions. The National Security Commission on Emerging Biotechnology, the congressional panel set up in 2022, has released its closing report. It warns that China could corner the biopharma sector, and it focuses on the licensing deals that have moved so much early-stage Chinese science into Western pipelines. The commission argues that repeat deals with increasingly favorable terms teach Chinese partners how to develop, register and sell drugs in the U-S, the same kind of knowledge transfer that happened in cars and semiconductors. Separately, STAT reports that the House Select Committee on China disclosed letters from Merck, Lilly, AbbVie, Pfizer and Bristol. All five said they won't start new clinical trial sites at hospitals tied to the People's Liberation Army, and they also won't open new sites in Xinjiang. None of this stops a deal yet. But this week Pfizer paid three hundred thirty million dollars upfront for Starna's in vivo CAR-T, and the political cost of that kind of deal keeps going up.

Fifth, a Chinese-origin drug moving toward the U-S market. The F-D-A accepted AstraZeneca's application for sonesitatug vedotin and granted it priority review, with a decision expected in the second quarter of 2027. It's an antibody-drug conjugate that targets claudin eighteen point two, a tight-junction protein exposed on gastric tumor cells, and it carries a microtubule-disrupting payload. AstraZeneca licensed it from a Chinese biotech in 2023. The proposed use is second-line or later gastric, gastroesophageal junction or esophageal adenocarcinoma in patients with claudin eighteen point two on at least a quarter of tumor cells. In the Phase three CLARITY-Gastric zero one trial, median overall survival was ten point six months versus six point four on the doctor's choice of chemotherapy, a hazard ratio of zero point five eight. Progression-free survival just missed significance. Astellas's antibody Vyloy, which targets the same protein, is approved first-line. Sonesitatug vedotin would be a later-line option for patients whose disease has progressed, using the same target but delivering a cytotoxic payload.

Sixth, another Alzheimer's miss from CTAD. Australia's Actinogen said its only drug, Xanamem, also called emestedastat, failed a two-hundred-forty-seven-patient Phase two b three trial. Xanamem is a pill that blocks eleven beta H-S-D one, the enzyme that regenerates active cortisol inside tissues including the brain. The difference from placebo on the clinical dementia rating at week thirty-six was one tenth of a point, which was not significant. The company is pointing to patients over seventy-five, where it reports thirty percent slowing, and says it will take that to partners and the F-D-A. The stock fell more than eighty percent.

Seventh, a cleaner CTAD story. Bristol presented open-label Phase three data for Cobenfy, the xanomeline and trospium combination. Xanomeline stimulates M-one and M-four muscarinic receptors in the brain, and trospium blocks the peripheral side effects. In ADEPT-one, hallucinations and delusions scores in Alzheimer's psychosis fell twelve points from baseline over twelve weeks. About four and a half percent of patients stopped because of side effects. Without a placebo arm these numbers don't establish efficacy. The double-blind portions of the trials will decide whether Bristol files in Alzheimer's psychosis.

Quick hits. Chinese A-I lab-automation company MegaRobo raised nearly two hundred million dollars, with Oman's sovereign wealth fund among the backers, to have A-I run real lab experiments. Sellas, a day after its Phase three win for the W-T-one peptide vaccine galinpepimut-S in A-M-L, sold one hundred fifty million dollars of stock at thirteen dollars a share, and the stock fell about seventeen percent on Thursday.

Looking ahead: Capricor's deramiocel decision for Duchenne cardiomyopathy is due Sunday, and by the thirtieth the F-D-A decides on Vertex's povetacicept and Roche's giredestrant in early breast cancer. We didn't find any Foresite portfolio news on Thursday's wires.
