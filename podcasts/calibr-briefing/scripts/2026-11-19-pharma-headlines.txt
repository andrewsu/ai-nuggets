AbbVie and Pfizer each wrote a multibillion-dollar check for a new modality, an open-source A-I drug design company raised one hundred ten million dollars, and the F-D-A approved two drugs that reach places older therapies can't. Here's what you need to know.

First, a deal this morning. AbbVie signed an option-and-license agreement with DISCO Pharmaceuticals, a German company, worth up to two point five four billion dollars. AbbVie pays forty million dollars upfront, and everything else is milestones. The target is what DISCO calls AND-gated bispecifics for solid tumors: antibodies that only switch on fully when they find two different antigens on the same cell. The idea is to hit tumor cells that carry both markers and spare healthy tissue that carries only one, which is the usual toxicity problem for bispecifics and T cell engagers in solid tumors. The antigen pairs come from DISCO's platform, which uses mass spectrometry to map the proteins on cancer cell surfaces. AbbVie already knew the company well: AbbVie Ventures was in DISCO's seed round alongside lead investor Sofinnova, and DISCO signed a deal with Amgen worth up to six hundred eighteen million dollars in January. That's a low upfront for the headline value, but it's the second big pharma to buy into the same target-discovery engine this year.

Second, Pfizer is getting into in vivo CAR-T. It licensed three preclinical autoimmune programs from China's Starna Therapeutics for three hundred thirty million dollars upfront and up to two point three billion in milestones, plus royalties. In vivo CAR-T uses an R-N-A payload in a targeted lipid nanoparticle to turn a patient's own T cells into CAR-T cells inside the body. That means no cell harvesting, no manufacturing, and ideally no chemotherapy conditioning. Pfizer gets rights outside Greater China, plus use of Starna's technology in immunology and oncology. Pfizer says it's the company's first in vivo CAR-T program, and it's leaning on the R-N-A delivery know-how it built with its COVID vaccine. Pfizer is late to this. AstraZeneca bought EsoBiotec, AbbVie bought Capstan, Gilead bought Interius, Bristol bought Orbital, and Lilly has done two buyouts this year. It's also buying in at an awkward moment: Novartis and Bristol have both paused autoimmune CAR-T trials over safety events, including deaths.

Third, A-I drug discovery. Boltz, the M-I-T spinout behind the open-source Boltz structure and binding models, raised a one hundred ten million dollar Series A led by Bessemer Venture Partners. Andreessen Horowitz and the other seed investors came back in, and F-Prime, Greycroft and Benchling joined. Boltz says six of the top twenty pharma companies are paying customers, including Pfizer, GSK, Takeda and Bristol, and that paid usage has grown fifty-fold since June. It's today's spotlight, and it's a very different bet from Isomorphic's five billion.

Fourth, an early approval in rare disease. The F-D-A approved Sanofi's venglustat, brand name Infilbray, as the first treatment for the neurological symptoms of type three Gaucher disease, a week ahead of its November twenty-fifth deadline. Venglustat is an oral pill that blocks glucosylceramide synthase, the enzyme that makes the fatty substrate that builds up in Gaucher disease. Unlike enzyme replacement infusions, it crosses the blood-brain barrier. In the Phase three LEAP2MONO trial, forty-three patients already stable on enzyme replacement were randomized to the daily pill or to infusions every two weeks. At one year, the pill did significantly better on a combined measure of ataxia and cognition and stayed close to enzyme replacement on spleen, liver and hemoglobin. The label warns about serious psychiatric effects and possible lens opacities in children.

Fifth, myasthenia gravis. The F-D-A approved Regeneron's Livsayis, cemdisiran, for adults with acetylcholine receptor antibody-positive generalized myasthenia gravis. It's an siRNA, licensed from Alnylam, that shuts down the liver's production of complement C5, so it's dosed as a shot under the skin four times a year. The approved C5 drugs from AstraZeneca and its Alexion unit bind the protein after it's made. In the Phase three NIMBLE trial, cemdisiran beat placebo by two point three points on the M-G daily-living score, and about seventy-seven percent of patients had a clinically meaningful response, against forty-four percent on placebo. Adding Regeneron's C5 antibody pozelimab didn't help, so cemdisiran launches alone. Like the rest of the class, it carries a boxed warning for meningococcal infection. It's the fourth complement drug approved in this disease, and Regeneron's first approved drug in neurology.

Sixth, Merck pulled the plug on nemtabrutinib, the non-covalent B-T-K inhibitor at the center of its two point seven billion dollar ArQule buyout in 2019. A data monitoring committee for the Phase three BELLWAVE-eleven trial in untreated C-L-L said the potential benefits may not outweigh the risks. Merck confirmed that and shut down the whole BELLWAVE program, including two other Phase three studies. That leaves Lilly's Jaypirca as the only approved non-covalent B-T-K inhibitor, and it's another write-off for Merck as the Keytruda patent cliff approaches in 2028.

Seventh, the last day of the Alzheimer's meeting CTAD. Lilly's mevidalen failed its Phase two. Mevidalen is a positive allosteric modulator of the dopamine D-one receptor, meant to boost the brain's own dopamine signal. In three hundred four patients with mild to moderate disease, neither dose came close to the trial's Bayesian success bar, and both raised systolic blood pressure by eight to eleven points in the hours after dosing. Lilly said the results don't support D-one potentiation as a meaningful benefit in Alzheimer's. It's the second miss for the drug, after Lewy body dementia.

A few quick ones. Amgen presented full Phase three data for dazodalibep in Sjögren's disease at the rheumatology meeting ACR. The drug blocks C-D-forty ligand, the signal T cells use to activate B cells. In six hundred forty-eight patients, the every-four-week dose cut systemic disease activity by five point six points against three point eight on placebo, and it improved dryness. Cytokinetics showed heart imaging data for aficamten in non-obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and still plans to file this quarter. The efficacy edge was modest, and more patients on the drug saw their ejection fraction drop below fifty percent. And Endpoints reports that nChroma Bio, the epigenetic editing company formed from the merger of Chroma Medicine and Nvelop, stopped its only clinical study early and laid off half its staff.
