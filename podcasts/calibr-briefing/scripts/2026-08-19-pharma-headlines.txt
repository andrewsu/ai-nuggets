Good morning, Travis — this is your biotech and pharma briefing for Wednesday, August nineteenth, twenty twenty-six. Yesterday had a genuine surprise in it: the biggest one-day move belonged to a company most people had written off two years ago, and the drug doing the work is the mechanistic opposite of the class everyone is chasing. Plus a new nominee to run the FDA, landing after the close. Seven items.

Number one, and today's spotlight: Amylyx said avexitide hit the primary endpoint in LUCIDITY, its Phase 3 trial in post-bariatric hypoglycemia. A fifty-five percent reduction in the composite rate of moderate and severe hypoglycemic events versus placebo, with a p-value of three millionths, and every secondary endpoint hit as well. The stock had its best day ever, up between forty-five and fifty-six percent depending on when you looked. Avexitide is a first-in-class GLP-1 receptor antagonist — it blocks the receptor Wegovy and Zepbound activate — which makes sense because post-bariatric hypoglycemia is a disease of too much GLP-1 signaling, not too little. Amylyx bought the asset out of Eiger's bankruptcy for thirty-five million dollars. There are no approved therapies, an NDA is planned by year end, and within hours of the data the company launched a three-hundred-fifty-million-dollar equity offering. All of that in the spotlight.

Number two, the news that dropped last night: President Trump is expected to nominate Heidi Overton as the next FDA commissioner. Overton is a physician who currently serves as deputy director of the White House Domestic Policy Council, with a stint in the first Trump administration and time at the America First Policy Institute in between. This fills a seat that has been unsettled since Marty Makary stepped down earlier this year, with Kyle Diamantas holding it in an acting capacity. The confirmation cuts both ways. She was visibly present at last week's signing of the executive order cutting back the federally recommended childhood vaccine schedule — the order outgoing Senate health committee chair Bill Cassidy flatly called wrong — which gives Senate Republicans something to press on. And she draws suspicion from the other direction too, from parts of the Make America Healthy Again coalition, for her role in earlier White House decisions to steer away from the most aggressive parts of Secretary Kennedy's agenda. For the industry the practical question is narrower: whether the review-timeline stability of the last few months survives another handoff.

Number three, BioMarin is buying Alesta Therapeutics, a private Dutch company, for two hundred seventy-five million dollars up front and up to two hundred fifteen million in development milestones — call it four hundred ninety million all in, cash on hand. What they're getting is a single asset: ALE-one, a Phase one-two-a oral candidate for hypophosphatasia. Everything else at Alesta, including the employees, gets spun out into a new entity before closing. Hypophosphatasia is a mineralization disorder driven by deficient alkaline phosphatase, and the mechanism here is to go after the substrate that accumulates — inorganic pyrophosphate — rather than replacing the enzyme. That matters commercially, because the standard of care is Alexion's Strensiq, asfotase alfa, an injected enzyme replacement given three to six times a week for life. An oral would be a very different product. One detail I like: a week ago BioMarin dropped BMN four-oh-one in ENPP-one deficiency after a split Phase three. ENPP-one is the enzyme that makes inorganic pyrophosphate. So BioMarin just walked away from a program for too little pyrophosphate and bought one for too much — same axis, opposite ends. It's their second small skeletal-disease deal in short order, and it fits the pattern: buy early, buy cheap, keep the bone franchise around Voxzogo growing.

Number four, and this one has real read-through: Endpoints reported a third death in China's investigator-initiated trial pathway, this time in cell therapy. RiboX Therapeutics confirmed that a patient with an autoimmune disease died after receiving its lead candidate — a circular-RNA-encoded in vivo CAR-T therapy, meaning the CAR is delivered as RNA and expressed in the patient's own T cells rather than manufactured outside the body. That follows two previously undisclosed gene-editing deaths, including a child, that surfaced over the last few weeks. The investigator-initiated pathway lets Chinese hospitals run experimental medicines without direct oversight from the national regulator; built for physicians with no other options, it has become in practice the front door for industry-sponsored early development that would face longer review elsewhere. The sharp detail: RiboX cleared an FDA investigational new drug application for that program on August eighth, and the package was built on the investigator-initiated dataset — the agency granted an accelerated dose-titration scheme on the strength of it. In vivo CAR-T is one of the hottest areas in the industry. This is the first hard safety data point on it, and it came through a channel with a transparency problem.

Number five, Regeneron discontinued a trial of its anti-CD-three antibody in non-infectious uveitis after what the company described only as a safety event, cause not yet identified. That lands on top of the itepekimab failure in COPD — the interleukin thirty-three blocker meant to be the Dupixent successor — and the melanoma combination that lost to Keytruda. Analysts have been pushing Leonard Schleifer toward acquisitions for a while; he keeps using the word disciplined. Each of these makes that conversation louder.

Number six, a federal appeals court revived part of Teva's challenge to how CMS runs the Medicare negotiation program. The live pieces are Teva's Administrative Procedure Act arguments about how the agency defines a qualifying single-source drug and applies its bona fide marketing standard. Timing is what makes it matter: a thirty-eight percent cut to Austedo, Teva's VMAT-two inhibitor for tardive dyskinesia and Huntington's chorea, takes effect in January twenty twenty-seven. The government had been essentially undefeated in this litigation, which is why Lilly, J and J, Pfizer and Sanofi all filed in support. This is the first crack, and it's procedural rather than constitutional — which, for anyone modeling negotiated prices, is arguably the more dangerous kind.

Number seven, four quick ones. Sandoz paid three hundred twenty-two million dollars to Henlius for rights to as many as ten biosimilars. Chugai granted GSK an exclusive worldwide license to AID three-fifty-one, an anti-dengue-virus antibody discovered with A-Star in Singapore, with GSK running development from Phase one and Chugai taking royalties. Evaxion killed its next-generation solid tumor vaccine to put everything behind EVX-oh-five, an off-the-shelf glioblastoma vaccine its AI platform designed against endogenous retrovirus antigens — reactivated fragments of ancient viral DNA in the tumor genome — with Duke running the Phase one. And the oral obesity scorecard for the second quarter: Novo's oral Wegovy did about four hundred ninety-six million dollars against ninety-eight million for Lilly's Foundayo, orforglipron, which only launched in April. Sixteen-point-six percent weight loss versus eleven-point-two explains most of the gap, though consensus still has Lilly taking the lead back by twenty twenty-eight on retatrutide.
