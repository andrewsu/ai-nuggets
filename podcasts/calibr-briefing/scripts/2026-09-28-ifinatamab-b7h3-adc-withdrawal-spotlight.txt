Today's spotlight is the withdrawal of ifinatamab deruxtecan in small cell lung cancer, and the reason it deserves the full slot is that the drug is probably fine. What failed here was a regulatory strategy, and the specific way it failed tells you something useful about what accelerated approval is now worth in a crowded second-line oncology setting.

Here is what happened. On Friday afternoon, Daiichi Sankyo and Merck announced that the biologics license application for ifinatamab deruxtecan in adults with extensive-stage small cell lung cancer whose disease progressed on or after platinum chemotherapy had been voluntarily withdrawn. The stated reason is that discussions with the agency established that the supporting data, including the IDeate-Lung one Phase two trial, do not satisfy the requirements to support an accelerated approval for the proposed indication. That application had priority review, granted in April, with an action date of October tenth. Marjorie Green, who runs oncology global clinical development at Merck Research Laboratories, said the company is disappointed the current dataset is not supportive of approval at this time. Abderrahmane Laadem at Daiichi pointed to the Phase three, IDeate-Lung two, where enrollment is near complete.

The molecule itself is a good one on paper. Ifinatamab deruxtecan is a humanized anti-B-seven-H-three immunoglobulin G-one antibody carrying multiple copies of a topoisomerase-one inhibitor payload — an exatecan derivative — on tetrapeptide-based cleavable linkers. It is the same DXd chemistry that produced trastuzumab deruxtecan, which is to say the payload and linker are the most clinically validated combination in the field. B-seven-H-three is an interesting target. It is a transmembrane protein in the B-seven family, the same family that produces the ligands binding C-D-twenty-eight-family receptors including P-D-one, and it is heavily overexpressed in small cell lung cancer, where overexpression tracks with poor prognosis. There is still no approved B-seven-H-three-directed medicine in any tumor type anywhere in the world. So this was a first-in-class shot at a target with real biology behind it, in a disease with roughly two hundred fifty thousand new diagnoses a year globally and a brutal five-year survival rate.

And the efficacy was not bad. IDeate-Lung one enrolled one hundred eighty-seven patients across Asia, Europe and North America, in two parts: a dose optimization stage randomizing eight against twelve milligrams per kilogram every three weeks, then a dose expansion at twelve. Primary endpoint was objective response rate by blinded independent central review. In the total twelve-milligram group, one hundred thirty-seven patients, the confirmed response rate was forty-eight point two percent — two point two percent complete responses, forty-six percent partial. Among patients with at least two prior lines, three quarters of whom had prior immunotherapy, the rate was fifty-four point eight percent. Any-grade treatment-related adverse events were about ninety percent, grade three or higher thirty-six point five percent, which for this class and this disease is not an outlier.

So why did that not clear the bar? Look at the second number. Median duration of response was five point three months. Median progression-free survival, four point nine months. Nine-month overall survival estimate, fifty-nine point one percent. That is the whole story. Accelerated approval on a single-arm response-rate endpoint does not run on the response rate alone; it runs on the response rate being durable enough that the regulator is willing to call it reasonably likely to predict clinical benefit. Half the patients respond, and the median response lasts a bit over five months. You can construct an argument for that in a disease with nothing else. The problem is that small cell lung cancer in the second line no longer has nothing else.

This is the part I would want in front of anyone modeling an accelerated approval filing right now. Consider the precedent everybody was reasoning from. Amgen's tarlatamab — a D-L-L-three by C-D-three T-cell engager — got accelerated approval in May twenty twenty-four on the DeLLphi three-oh-one Phase two, with an objective response rate of forty percent and a median duration of response of nine point seven months. Lower response rate than ifinatamab deruxtecan. Nearly twice the durability. And critically, in twenty twenty-four the relevant available therapy was topotecan or lurbinectedin, both of which are mediocre. Then in November twenty twenty-five that accelerated approval converted to full approval on DeLLphi three-oh-four, a randomized Phase three showing median overall survival of thirteen point six months against eight point three with chemotherapy, a hazard ratio of zero point six-zero.

So by the time ifinatamab deruxtecan's application was actually under review, the landscape underneath it had changed. Accelerated approval requires a meaningful advantage over available therapy, and available therapy in second-line extensive-stage small cell lung cancer is now an approved drug with a demonstrated randomized survival benefit. Arguing that a forty-eight percent response rate with a five-month median duration of response, from a single-arm trial, represents a meaningful advantage over that is a fundamentally different conversation than the one Amgen had two years earlier with a better-looking durability curve and a worse-looking comparator. The exact same data package, filed in twenty twenty-three, very plausibly gets through. Filed in twenty twenty-six, it does not. Nothing about the drug changed. The denominator changed.

Now the part that should genuinely sting for Daiichi, because it is the cleanest natural experiment we have had in a while. The B-seven-H-three thesis was validated this year. It just was not validated by them. GSK and Hansoh's risvutatug rezetecan — also a B-seven-H-three antibody-drug conjugate, also carrying a topoisomerase-one payload — reported the ARTEMIS oh-oh-eight Phase three in relapsed small cell lung cancer in China: median overall survival of eighteen point five months against ten point three with topotecan, stratified hazard ratio zero point four-six, a fifty-four percent reduction in the risk of death, at a median follow-up of twelve point two months. GSK's framing is that this is the first positive Phase three overall survival result for any B-seven-H-three-targeted conjugate in any tumor type, and as far as I can tell that is accurate.

Two caveats before anyone over-reads that. ARTEMIS oh-oh-eight ran entirely in China, and the topotecan control arm did notably better there — ten point three months — than the chemotherapy arm did in the global DeLLphi three-oh-four, at eight point three. That is a real population difference and it means the effect size will not transfer cleanly. And these are separate trials with separate designs, so the eighteen point five against anything is a cross-trial comparison, not a head-to-head. The global test is EMBOLD S-C-L-C three-oh-one, with pivotal data expected in twenty twenty-seven.

But the strategic point survives both caveats, and it is this. Daiichi was first into the clinic with a B-seven-H-three conjugate and tried to get to market on a single-arm Phase two. GSK and Hansoh ran the randomized trial against the actual standard of care. The randomized trial is the one that produced a number the agency cannot argue with, and it is going to read out globally in twenty twenty-seven while IDeate-Lung two — which is the right trial, randomizing against physician's choice of amrubicin, lurbinectedin or topotecan in patients after one prior platinum line — has a primary completion date in twenty twenty-eight. The company that was behind on the target is now ahead on the evidence, by roughly a year, because it did not try to take the shortcut.

Then there is the deal math, which is uglier than the single-drug story. Merck paid Daiichi four billion dollars upfront in October twenty twenty-three to co-develop and co-commercialize three DXd conjugates — ifinatamab deruxtecan, patritumab deruxtecan against H-E-R-three, and raludotatug deruxtecan against cadherin-six — with total consideration running up toward twenty-two billion. In August twenty twenty-four they expanded it to include gocatamig, the D-L-L-three T-cell engager. Track what has happened since. Patritumab deruxtecan got a complete response letter in twenty twenty-four on manufacturing grounds, and then in twenty twenty-five the partners voluntarily withdrew that submission after failing to show an overall survival benefit. Ifinatamab deruxtecan is now withdrawn. Raludotatug deruxtecan's Phase two-three in ovarian cancer has a primary completion date of February twenty twenty-eight. Three years and four billion dollars in, the collaboration has produced zero approvals, and its nearest meaningful readouts are in twenty twenty-eight. For a company staring at the Keytruda patent cliff at the end of that decade, that is precisely the wrong timing, and it is why Fierce described this as another blow to a deal that has disappointed to date.

What I would take away from this, in three pieces. First, the narrow read: the accelerated approval pathway in oncology has not changed on paper, but the practical bar in any indication where a randomized survival benefit already exists has moved substantially, and response rate without durability no longer carries a filing. Note that industry spent last week asking the agency for more detail on its draft guidance about when a single pivotal trial is enough — the uncertainty is being felt across the sector, not just here. Second, the broader read: target validation and asset validation are different things, and this week separated them cleanly. B-seven-H-three works as a target in small cell lung cancer. This particular molecule, at this dose, in this line, with this durability, did not clear. Do not let the withdrawal talk you out of the target — and if you are looking at B-seven-H-three in any other setting, the GSK and Hansoh result is the number to benchmark against, not this one. Third, the thing that is easy to miss: none of this touches ifinatamab deruxtecan in castration-resistant prostate cancer or esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, both in Phase three, and it does not touch Merck's D-L-L-three asset. Merck's small cell franchise is not dead. It is just now entirely dependent on trials that read out at the end of the decade rather than a label this October.
