Good morning Travis. Here are your biotech and pharma headlines for Friday, October sixteenth, twenty twenty-six, covering Thursday. It was a quieter news day, and the biggest item was another pharma deal for A-I drug discovery: AstraZeneca signed up insitro to find targets in liver and heart disease, and that's the spotlight. Around it, the I-P-O window got two data points, with Iambic's first day of trading and City Therapeutics pricing a bigger deal than planned. Merck and Moderna laid out where their cancer vaccine goes after melanoma, a French universal flu vaccine missed its Phase two-b, and Washington moved to open the door to compounded peptides. Eight items, then the week ahead.

First, today's spotlight. AstraZeneca will pay insitro forty million dollars up front, with up to one point four six billion in milestones plus royalties, to find new drug targets in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis, or MASH, and in cardiomyopathies. insitro is the South San Francisco company Daphne Koller founded in twenty eighteen, and Foresite Capital is one of its investors. Its "Virtual Human" platform is built to find causal disease biology from human genetic and cellular data. insitro proposes the targets and AstraZeneca takes the molecules forward. BioSpace pointed out the timing: AstraZeneca's cardiomyopathy bench just lost a pillar when Wainua, its Ionis-partnered drug, failed a Phase three in transthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy. The spotlight looks at what AstraZeneca is buying for forty million dollars, and whether insitro's earlier pharma deals ever turned into clinical programs.

Second, the I-P-O window. Iambic, the San Diego A-I drug discovery company we covered yesterday, opened at sixteen fifteen, just above its sixteen-dollar offer price. After a volatility halt it closed at eighteen ninety-one, up about eighteen percent, for a market value near nine hundred million dollars, and it was indicated higher again in early Friday trading. So a deal priced below Iambic's last private round traded up on day one. That suggests the discount was a cushion, not a sign of weak demand. Then last night City Therapeutics, an R-N-A interference company, priced an upsized I-P-O: about eleven point one million shares at eighteen dollars, the midpoint of its range, for two hundred million dollars in gross proceeds. It had been targeting about a hundred seventy-five million. City's lead programs silence factor eleven for thrombosis, R-B-P-four, the retinol-binding protein, for Stargardt disease, and T-F-R-two, transferrin receptor two, for anemia of chronic disease. It starts trading on Nasdaq today under C-T-Y. Taken together, the market is paying for RNA interference against well-validated targets, and it's taking A-I platform stories when a clinical asset carries the valuation.

Third, Merck and Moderna on what comes after melanoma. At the STAT Summit in Boston, Merck's research chief Dean Li and Moderna president Stephen Hoge talked through the plan for intismeran autogene. That's their individualized m-R-N-A cancer vaccine. An algorithm picks up to thirty-four mutations from each patient's tumor, and the vaccine is given with Keytruda. In August a Phase three showed it significantly delayed recurrence and metastasis after surgery for melanoma, and the companies are preparing to file. Li said they'll keep going where checkpoint inhibitors already work, meaning adjuvant settings with hot tumors, rather than cold or metastatic disease. That looked like a jab at BioNTech, whose head and neck vaccine just missed on progression-free survival. Small studies in adjuvant pancreatic and gastric cancer are already running. Li added, "Anyone has positive data and it's out there, you will never see Moderna and Merck pivot so fast." Hoge's point was that data from several thousand Phase three patients could be used to retrain the neoantigen-selection algorithm. That's where the long-term advantage could come from.

Fourth, a setback for universal flu vaccines. Osivax, of Lyon, reported that OVX836 missed its primary endpoint in a two-thousand-eight-hundred-fifty-person Phase two-b in Europe. OVX836 is a nanoparticle vaccine built on the influenza nucleoprotein, an internal protein that mutates far less than the surface antigens, and it's designed to drive T-cell immunity. The target was fifty percent efficacy. The point estimate was twenty-four percent and not statistically significant. Secondary estimates ranged from twenty-five to twenty-eight percent across the H-one and H-three subtypes. The company released no confidence intervals or case counts. Osivax now plans to combine OVX836 with standard strain-matched flu shots, working with G-C Biopharma and an undisclosed global pharma partner, and the first combination study is slated for the fourth quarter of twenty twenty-seven. The broader lesson for T-cell flu vaccines is that a T-cell response alone gives a modest benefit, and the best use may be adding it on top of antibody protection rather than replacing it.

Fifth, peptides policy. The Department of Health and Human Services sent an "interim access policy" for peptides to the White House budget office for review, according to a government posting Thursday. Two people familiar with it told the Associated Press, as republished by STAT, that it would use enforcement discretion to let compounding pharmacies make some of the most popular unapproved peptides, including B-P-C-one-fifty-seven. The F-D-A had put B-P-C-one-fifty-seven and more than a dozen other injectable peptides on its list of substances too risky to compound. A Kennedy-picked advisory panel voted in July to drop restrictions on six of seven peptides, overruling the agency's own scientists. Telehealth companies have said they'd sell them, and analysts size the market at two to three billion dollars. For drug developers, this is the clearest sign yet that unapproved products can reach patients through compounding pharmacies without going through F-D-A review.

Sixth, money and mood. OrbiMed closed three billion dollars across two funds. Its tenth private investments fund raised more than one point nine billion for U-S and European venture, and its sixth Asia fund raised over one billion. Managing partner Carl Gordon named A-I, new modalities and "China's emergence as a biotechnology powerhouse" as the drivers. That comes a week after Forbion closed two point three billion euros. The public side is less cheerful. STAT's Adam Feuerstein wrote that the X-B-I is still up twenty-four percent this year but down eleven percent from its August high, and many investors are losing money underneath the index. The pattern is plenty of private capital, with public investors getting choosier.

Seventh, Washington voices at the STAT Summit. Representative Jake Auchincloss, Democrat of Massachusetts, said he plans a bill modeled on Australia's system, where early-phase trials can start without the national regulator's prior review. His argument is that U-S trials will never be as cheap as China's, but they can be as fast, and that Washington should fix its own house rather than try to wall off Beijing. He also criticized the F-D-A's Commissioner's National Priority Voucher program for lacking transparent standards. Separately, three former F-D-A commissioners, Robert Califf, Scott Gottlieb and Ned Sharpless, criticized current H-H-S leadership at the same summit. STAT's headline on the session quoted former F-D-A officials calling DOGE "one of the biggest disasters in American history."

Eighth, quick ones. Spinogenix added an autism arm to its basket trial of codabakalner, an oral positive modulator of B-K potassium channels that lowered gamma power in a Fragile X Phase two-a. Enrollment is limited to people whose resting E-E-G shows high gamma-band power. That Fragile X-like signature showed up in twenty-two percent of an autism cohort, and no standard clinical test could pick those patients out. On the same theme, Anavex got F-D-A clearance to start a Phase two-a of blarcamesine, its sigma-one receptor agonist, in Fragile X, citing improvement in the same gamma E-E-G signature in mice. NorthStar Medical Technologies, the radioisotope maker, signed Beijing's Quarkmed to be its registration agent in China for actinium-two-twenty-five, the isotope behind many alpha-emitting radiopharmaceuticals. On Wednesday, Lonza raised its mid-term targets to ten to thirteen percent annual sales growth at constant currency, with core EBITDA margins in the high thirties. Endpoints reported that Virginia is the early leader among states in pharma onshoring, with twelve point six billion dollars in projects. And a C-D-C web page backing Covid-nineteen vaccination for people trying to conceive went up briefly, then started redirecting elsewhere.
