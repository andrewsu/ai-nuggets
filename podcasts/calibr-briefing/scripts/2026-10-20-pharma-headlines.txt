First, Disc Medicine. Eight months after the FDA rejected bitopertin's first application, the confirmatory Phase 3 APOLLO trial hit every primary and secondary endpoint in erythropoietic protoporphyria, the rare blood disease that makes sunlight exposure excruciating. The stock jumped about thirty-seven percent Monday, BMO and Truist both raised price targets, and Disc plans to resubmit its new drug application by year end. Full details in the spotlight.

Second, the biggest deal of the day. Germany's Merck K G a A agreed to buy Silence Therapeutics, the UK siRNA company, for twenty dollars a share in cash, an all-cash deal with an equity value of about one point three billion dollars and an eighty-two percent premium to Silence's thirty-day average price. The prize is divesiran, a Phase 3 asset for polycythemia vera, a blood cancer marked by dangerously thick blood. Divesiran silences a gene called T M P R S S six, which raises the hormone hepcidin, which in turn restricts the iron supply red blood cells need to be made. In Phase 2 it cut therapeutic blood draws from about two per patient to roughly zero through week thirty-six. Silence has been running without a permanent CEO since December, which a sell-side note cited as part of why the board took the offer. The deal closes in the first quarter of twenty twenty-seven.

Third, EyePoint. Its intravitreal eye injection duravyu failed a second pivotal trial in wet age-related macular degeneration, missing non-inferiority against Eylea on visual acuity in the Lucia study, the same primary endpoint it missed in August's Lugano trial. Both pivotals are being stopped early. EyePoint is cutting about fifty-three percent of its staff, roughly a hundred and thirteen jobs, and the stock opened down more than eighty percent, at fifty-five cents. The one notable secondary win: duravyu did reduce how often patients need an injection. The market's verdict was that treatment convenience is worth nothing until you match the market leader on vision outcomes, and EyePoint is now pivoting its remaining cash toward diabetic macular edema, where two pivotal readouts are due this quarter.

Fourth, Roche went back to the well in China. A year after their first antibody-drug conjugate tie-up, Roche expanded its partnership with Hansoh Pharmaceutical to cover a broad portfolio of next-generation A D C programs, some already in Hansoh's preclinical pipeline, others built around targets Roche is suggesting. Hansoh gets four hundred million dollars upfront, another hundred ten million on the first anniversary, and up to fifteen billion dollars in potential milestones plus royalties. Hansoh runs the programs through Phase one in China, then Roche takes them global outside Greater China. This is now one of several Big Pharma bets on Hansoh's A D C engine; G S K's B seven H three antibody-drug conjugate from the same company just posted an eighteen-and-a-half-month median overall survival in lung cancer, which Jefferies called a shift from an impressive China dataset to a globally validated asset.

Fifth, a number finally attached to a tease. Remember Sunday's note that AbbVie had shown an abstract title for A B B V four six six, its combination of Skyrizi and the integrin blocker trosunilimab in Crohn's disease, with no data released. The number is now out: forty and a half percent endoscopic remission at week twenty-four for the combination, against twenty-two percent for Skyrizi alone and twenty percent for trosunilimab alone. That is a real combination effect, not just two drugs working independently, and it is AbbVie building toward the successor franchise ahead of Humira-era patents starting to fall in twenty thirty-two. This comes from a three-hundred-patient Phase two called TARGET C D, so it is early, but it is the first piece of evidence that pairing an interleukin twenty-three blocker with gut-selective integrin blockade beats either one alone.

Sixth, on the AI-in-drug-discovery front. Oracle and AstraZeneca announced a non-binding collaboration to use AI and real-world data to identify eligible patients for clinical trials faster, with initial disease areas including oncology, cardiovascular and metabolic disease, respiratory and immunology, and rare disease. No financial terms disclosed; this is a data-and-workflow partnership aimed at the industry's most persistent bottleneck, which is finding and enrolling the right patients, rather than a discovery-stage deal.
