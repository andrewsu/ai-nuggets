Good morning, Travis. It's Tuesday, October 27th, 2026. ESMO wraps up in Madrid today, so this briefing covers a long weekend of Presidential Symposium data, plus a filing that matters for anyone working on AI in drug discovery. Here's what you need to know.

First, the Presidential Symposium produced three Phase 3 wins over an active standard of care. Roche's divarasib, a next-generation KRAS G12C inhibitor, went head to head against the two approved drugs for that mutation, Amgen's Lumakras and Bristol Myers' Krazati, in previously treated lung cancer. It won on both progression-free and overall survival, with a hazard ratio for death of zero point five six. Median survival was seventeen point seven months against twelve. It's rare for a follow-on drug to beat the approved drugs for the same mutation on survival, head to head.

Second, Amgen and AstraZeneca. Imdelltra, Amgen's bispecific T cell engager that binds DLL3 on small cell lung cancer cells and CD3 on T cells, was added to AstraZeneca's PD-L1 antibody Imfinzi as first-line maintenance in the DeLLphi-305 trial. Five hundred and sixty-three patients, all of whose cancer hadn't progressed after chemo plus Imfinzi. Median overall survival was twenty-one point one months against twelve point seven, a hazard ratio of zero point four two, a fifty-eight percent cut in the risk of death, and that was at an interim analysis. Progression-free survival went from one point nine months to seven point four. Severe treatment-related side effects roughly doubled, from about eleven percent to twenty-one, and about half of patients had cytokine release syndrome, almost all of it low grade. Truist called it potentially practice-changing and estimates the move to first-line maintenance expands Imdelltra's addressable U.S. population about one point seven times. Guggenheim called it the largest incremental opportunity of the franchise. The practical catch is that patients still need monitoring after the first two doses, so it adds clinic time.

Third, Akeso and its Western partner Summit Therapeutics. Ivonescimab, the PD-1 by VEGF bispecific antibody, beat AstraZeneca's Imfinzi, each on top of gemcitabine and cisplatin, in first-line biliary tract cancer. In six hundred and eighty-two patients, median survival was fourteen point nine months against twelve point one, a hazard ratio of zero point seven five. The caveat is the same one that has followed this drug for two years: every patient was in China, and Akeso alone ran the analysis. Summit's U.S. path in this indication runs through a new cooperative-group Phase 3 that hasn't started enrolling. Its lung cancer application has an FDA goal date of November fourteenth.

Fourth, Merck and Kelun-Biotech's TROP2 antibody-drug conjugate, sacituzumab tirumotecan, or sac-TMT. In the TroFuse-005 trial in endometrial cancer that had already progressed after platinum chemo and immunotherapy, sac-TMT beat chemotherapy on survival, nineteen months against twelve point one, cutting the risk of death by thirty-two percent. The data are published in The Lancet, and the FDA has accepted the application with priority review. Severe side effects ran higher, fifty-four percent against forty-one, driven mainly by anemia and neutropenia. Kelun also showed fuller first-line lung cancer data with Keytruda, where BMO said the result likely exceeds investors' already-high expectations. Leerink has called that trial the first direct proof that an ADC can replace platinum chemo in a first-line regimen.

Fifth, Genmab's rinatabart sesutecan, a folate receptor alpha-directed ADC, in platinum-resistant ovarian cancer. In five hundred and eighty-four patients, it cut the risk of progression or death by seventy-one percent and the risk of death by fifty-seven percent against chemotherapy. For comparison, AbbVie's Elahere, which hits the same target, showed thirty-five and thirty-three percent in its own Phase 3. The bigger point is that Genmab enrolled patients regardless of folate receptor level, while Elahere needs a companion diagnostic. H.C. Wainwright estimates that would double the addressable population. Leerink raised its probability of success to a hundred percent. Genmab stock is up about ten percent since Friday morning. This is the first of three readouts that Genmab needs to replace Darzalex royalties.

Sixth, the early-data standout. Johnson and Johnson's JNJ-6143, from last year's three-billion-dollar Halda Therapeutics acquisition, posted a seventy-five percent confirmed response rate, twelve of sixteen measurable patients, in heavily pretreated metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer. The mechanism is induced proximity, but not degradation. The molecule glues a cancer-specific protein to a protein the cell needs to survive, and that complex kills the cell. Leerink called it immaculate.

Seventh, the AI story of the week. Anthropic filed its S-1 with the SEC yesterday to list on the Nasdaq under the ticker A-N-T, with Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley leading. For this audience, the life sciences disclosures are the point. The filing says nine of the ten largest pharma companies are customers. It confirms the acquisition of Coefficient Bio, the startup founded by alumni of Genentech's Prescient Design group, and it says the company has built its own molecular biology lab. It also pitches Claude operating lab equipment like liquid handlers and microscopes through what it calls a Model Hardware Standard. What it doesn't say is just as telling: no therapeutic areas, no headcount, and no breakdown of life sciences spend. Research and development spending quintupled to nine point seven billion dollars in the first half of the year. Eighth, in the same lane, Pathos AI closed a two-hundred-and-thirty-seven-million-dollar Series E at about a three-billion-dollar valuation, and the lead investor is Bristol Myers Squibb. Alongside the round, Bristol gets three years of exclusive use of the Pathos platform for its neuroscience programs, and Tempus will build a neurology dataset covering hundreds of thousands of patients that Pathos is licensing for fifty-five million dollars over five years. That's a pharma buying equity in an AI company and putting the data bill on the AI company's side of the table.

Ninth, deals. Gilead paid one point three billion dollars upfront, in cash, for PepStar Therapeutics' oral macrocyclic peptide that blocks the interleukin-23 receptor. That's the Johnson and Johnson icotrokinra mechanism. Gilead says it wants to pair the drug with emvistegrast, the oral integrin blocker we did a spotlight on last Monday, for a fully oral regimen in inflammatory bowel disease. Separately, Roche licensed Zymeworks' ZW209 for fifty-five million upfront and up to eight hundred and thirteen million total. ZW209 is a DLL3 by CD3 by CD28 trispecific T cell engager, so it's the same target as Imdelltra, with a costimulatory arm added. It's Roche's fourth deal this month after Hansoh, Rona and Alector.

Tenth, safety. Bristol Myers disclosed a death in its Phase 1 trial of zola-cel, its CD19 CAR T for autoimmune disease. A patient with highly active relapsing multiple sclerosis developed late-onset motor neurotoxicity that steroids and immunoglobulin didn't resolve, and then died of septic pneumonia forty-four days after infusion. Bristol says the summer pause came from a rise in neurotoxicity events in its pivotal lupus trial, and that this death happened afterward. Bristol reports no other deaths among a hundred and ninety autoimmune patients, but along with Novartis' three deaths in August, the benefit-risk debate for CAR T in nonfatal disease is now a live one.

Eleventh, policy. Treasury published its proposed outbound-investment rules for biotech under last year's COINS Act. The rules would restrict U.S. investment in Chinese small molecules, antibodies, cell and gene therapies and biomanufacturing, but licensing deals are explicitly exempt. China made the same carve-out in its own rules the week before. So both governments are closing the equity door and leaving the licensing door open, which is exactly the structure most cross-border deals already use. BIO's John Crowley said, "We cannot restrict our way to success."

Finally, capital markets. Lycia Therapeutics upsized and priced at eighteen dollars, the top of its range, raising about three hundred and eighteen million dollars for its extracellular protein-degradation platform. Aspen Neuroscience and Bambusa set terms for IPOs of about a hundred and thirty-five million each, and Corxel plans to take its oral GLP-1 public through a SPAC. J.P. Morgan counts seven and a half billion dollars raised across twenty-two biopharma IPOs through the third quarter. The XBI biotech index is up almost thirty percent for the year, but it was flat in the third quarter. Venture funding is heading for about thirty-three billion this year, and San Diego was one of the next three hubs after the Bay Area and Boston.

Looking ahead, the FDA holds its rare disease evidence meeting tomorrow, Gilead reports third-quarter earnings Thursday, and Summit's ivonescimab lung cancer decision is due November fourteenth.
