Let's go deeper on Capricor, because yesterday's advisory committee meeting was a case study in how a drug can fail its regulatory test not on the biology but on the statistics — and it's the second time this week the F-D-A's evidence bar has taken down a company the market thought had a real shot.

Start with what deramiocel actually is, because it's an unusual kind of therapy. It's a cell therapy — specifically what Capricor calls cardiosphere-derived cells, an off-the-shelf, donor-derived cell product infused into the bloodstream. And here's the key to understanding it: these cells aren't meant to engraft and become new heart muscle. The thinking is that they act more like a signaling depot — releasing exosomes and other factors that tamp down inflammation and slow the scarring, the fibrosis, that progressively wrecks the heart. That mechanism matters for why the trial was hard to read, so hold onto it.

The disease is Duchenne muscular dystrophy. Most people picture Duchenne as a muscle-wasting disease that puts boys in wheelchairs, and it is — but the thing that actually kills patients, more and more as care for the skeletal muscle improves, is cardiomyopathy. The heart is a muscle too, and it fails. So a therapy aimed squarely at the Duchenne heart is going after the leading cause of death in this population, and there is essentially nothing approved that does that directly. The unmet need is real, and everyone in that room yesterday acknowledged it.

Now the regulatory saga, because it's important context. The F-D-A rejected deramiocel once already, with a complete response letter in July of last year. Capricor went back, ran its Phase 3 trial — called HOPE-3 — and in December announced it had met its endpoints. The agency accepted the resubmission, set a decision date of August twenty-second, and scheduled yesterday's panel. So this was a second attempt, and the company walked in believing it had the data in hand.

Here is where it comes apart, and it's almost entirely a fight about statistics. HOPE-3 was designed with a primary endpoint measuring upper limb function — how well boys can use their arms and hands as the disease progresses. But Capricor came into this review wanting to reposition the application around the heart, around cardiac function. The F-D-A pushed back and insisted the primary endpoint stay what it was originally designed to be. And then came the deeper dispute — over the statistical analysis plan itself. Capricor used a version of that plan, finalized before the data were unblinded, that switched to a rank-based analysis — a method that reduces the influence of outliers. The agency refused to accept that version. Its reviewers used an earlier version as the benchmark and argued that the late changes were made after unblinding and, in their words, lacked adequate scientific justification. Under the agency's preferred analysis, deramiocel showed no statistically significant difference from placebo at twelve months.

Think about how stark that is. The company says it hit its endpoints. The agency, analyzing the same patients with a different plan, says it missed. Capricor's chief executive, Linda Marbán, pushed back hard — she compared the F-D-A grading the company on an earlier draft plan to a professor grading a term paper the student never actually submitted. It's a vivid line, and it captures the core problem: when you change your analysis method late in a trial, even for defensible reasons, you hand a skeptical regulator a reason to say you went looking for the answer you wanted after you had already seen the data. That suspicion is corrosive, and it is very hard to undo once you're in the room.

The cardiac data didn't rescue it. The panel kept reaching for one word — fragile — about the heart-function measures, the ejection-fraction numbers. The agency said the cardiac imaging, taken together, was difficult to interpret and didn't provide conclusive evidence of benefit. And remember the mechanism — a signaling, anti-fibrotic effect produces slow, modest changes, exactly the kind of small and variable signal that's easy to dismiss as noise. On the upper limb side, Capricor pointed to a fifty-four percent slowing of decline versus placebo, which sounds impressive, but even the advisers who found it the most favorable said they simply weren't convinced it works.

So the vote was nine to three against substantial evidence of effectiveness. And the quotes tell you the tenor of the room. One statistician on the panel said flatly that she didn't see enough evidence of benefit to vote yes. Another member acknowledged the unmet need but said the evidence just wasn't that compelling. The two supporting voices were patient advocates, and one of them was candid about the reason — these families don't have an alternative. That's the hardest version of this dilemma: a devastating disease, no other option, and data that a panel of scientists can't quite bring itself to call convincing.

Capricor tried one more move worth noting. On the morning of the meeting it announced that the HOPE-3 results had been published in The Lancet, framing peer review as external validation. It didn't move the room. Publication and regulatory approval are simply different bars, and a journal accepting your analysis doesn't resolve the F-D-A's objection to how that analysis was chosen in the first place.

The market had already rendered its verdict. When the briefing documents posted a couple of days earlier, the stock fell about sixty-seven percent in a single day, taking the company's market value down to somewhere around three hundred and seventy million dollars. Notably, not everyone on the sell side gave up — Oppenheimer's analyst flagged the difference between the company's and the agency's methods but kept an outperform rating, which tells you some investors think the underlying signal is real and the fight is procedural. That's the bull case in a nutshell: the biology might be fine, and the company lost on a technicality it can still fix.

Now the part that should be on your radar for the portfolio, because this is bigger than one company. Two days in a row this week, the same F-D-A advisory committee — the cell and gene therapy panel — has been handed a program and asked, in effect, is this evidence even interpretable. Yesterday, Capricor. Today, Replimune. Different modalities, different diseases, the same underlying question. And the answer coming from the agency's career review staff is consistent and structural: they are not going to be talked into approving on the strength of a striking-looking number when the way that number was produced is in doubt — whether that's a single-arm trial with no control, as with Replimune, or a statistical plan changed late in the game, as with Capricor. A lot of people expected the evidence bar to loosen after the departures of Vinay Prasad and Marty Makary from F-D-A leadership. What we're seeing instead is that the skepticism lives in the review staff, not in one or two political appointees, and it is not going away.

There's also a specific, teachable lesson buried in the Capricor case that applies to anyone designing a rare-disease program. Don't change the question after you see the answer. Repositioning the endpoint from upper limb to cardiac, switching the analysis method after unblinding — each of those may have been individually defensible, but stacked together they let the agency tell a story about a company chasing significance. In a small trial in a rare disease, where you often get only one shot and the effect sizes are modest by nature, protecting the credibility of your pre-specified analysis is not a statistical nicety. It is the whole ballgame.

What happens next. The vote is advisory — the F-D-A makes the actual call, and its decision is due August twenty-second. The agency can approve over a negative panel, and it has occasionally done so, but a nine-to-three no from its own advisers, sitting on top of its own reviewers' objections, is a deep hole. If there is a path forward, the panel itself hinted at it — the upper limb function data drew more sympathy than the cardiac data, so a cleaner application built around that endpoint, or a properly pre-specified confirmatory trial, may be the way back. But that's a longer road than the one Capricor was hoping to be on this week.

So the narrow story is one Duchenne therapy stumbling at the last gate. The wide story — the one worth tracking — is that we now have two data points in two days telling us the same thing about where the F-D-A's evidence bar sits for small, single-shot programs in desperate diseases. It's high, it's held by the career staff, and no amount of unmet need is going to lower it. Plan your trials accordingly. Talk soon.
