Good morning, Travis. It's Saturday, August first, twenty twenty-six, and here's your biotech and pharma briefing. It's a quieter weekend tape, but the lead is a big one — a landmark cardiovascular trial just failed, and it matters far beyond the company that ran it. Four things today.

First, and it's the spotlight: Novo Nordisk said Thursday that its ZEUS phase three trial of ziltivekimab missed. Ziltivekimab is a monoclonal antibody that blocks interleukin six, the central inflammatory signaling molecule that sits downstream of the whole inflammation-drives-atherosclerosis story. Novo tested it in more than six thousand three hundred patients who had atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, and elevated inflammation — exactly the enriched population where an anti-inflammatory should have the best shot. The drug did everything it was supposed to biologically: it crushed high-sensitivity C-reactive protein and drove down free interleukin six. And it produced no cardiovascular benefit whatsoever — a hazard ratio of zero point nine nine, dead flat, with more serious infections in the treated arm. Novo's shares fell around seven percent, and there's a non-cash impairment charge coming in the third quarter. I'll spend the spotlight on why this is one of the most important negative results in cardiovascular medicine in years, and what it does to the residual-inflammatory-risk thesis that a whole category was built on.

Second, an artificial-intelligence deal in the discovery world — the kind of item on your radar. GSK expanded its collaboration with Relation Therapeutics, the London-based machine-learning biotech, in a deal worth up to a hundred and ten million dollars in upfront and milestone payments. What they're actually building is interesting: not a single drug program but a data factory. Relation will generate large-scale perturbation datasets — measurements of how human cells respond to genetic and pharmacological pokes — and use them to train foundation models, including its MORGAN platform, to predict cellular response. It builds on an earlier GSK–Relation pact in fibrosis and osteoarthritis. The bet is that the bottleneck in AI drug discovery isn't the model, it's high-quality biological training data, and GSK is paying to own a pipeline of it.

Third, the biotech IPO window is genuinely open. Apnimed priced an upsized offering Thursday night, raising a hundred and ninety-two million dollars, and the stock jumped roughly thirty-eight percent in its Nasdaq debut Friday. Apnimed is developing what could be the first FDA-approved pill for obstructive sleep apnea — a drug called AD109, a combination of a novel anti-muscarinic and a selective norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor that keeps the upper airway open during sleep. Its application already has a target action date at the end of February next year. The company is backed by Shionogi, Morningside, and Alpha Wave, and by BioPharma Dive's count it's roughly the fifteenth biotech IPO this year, most of them upsized on strong demand. In a market where a sleep-apnea pill can go out at the top of its range and pop, the funding environment for late-stage stories is as good as it's been in a while.
