Good morning Travis. Here are your biotech and pharma headlines for Saturday, August twenty-second, twenty twenty-six. Friday was a deals-and-policy day rather than a data day, and the most interesting item on the tape is a shell company doubling on a bisphosphonate that a large European pharma already gave up on. Six items.

First, and today's spotlight. Werewolf Therapeutics, a Waltham immuno-oncology company trading under the ticker HOWL, announced a definitive all-stock merger with Ambros Therapeutics of San Diego, alongside an oversubscribed one hundred fifty million dollar private placement co-led by RA Capital Management and Janus Henderson. The combined company keeps the Ambros name, moves to San Diego, and expects to trade as A-M-B-X. The whole point of the transaction is a single asset: neridronate, an amino-bisphosphonate for complex regional pain syndrome type one, a severe orphan pain disorder with roughly sixty-five thousand newly diagnosed U.S. patients a year and no FDA-approved therapy at all. The exchange ratio implies five hundred million dollars for Ambros against forty-seven and a half million for Werewolf — legacy Werewolf holders end up with about six point eight percent of the combined company plus a contingent value right on whatever the old oncology assets fetch. Werewolf closed Friday at eighty-seven cents, up about one hundred and three percent. The reason this is worth eight minutes rather than thirty seconds is that Grünenthal ran this exact drug at this exact dose through a global phase three program and killed it for futility in twenty nineteen. Ambros is betting the failure was the patient population, not the molecule. That's the spotlight.

Second, and this one is live today. Capricor Therapeutics has an FDA action date of August twenty-second — that is today — for deramiocel, its cardiosphere-derived cell therapy for the cardiomyopathy of Duchenne muscular dystrophy. Going into that date, an activist showed up. Kaos Capital, describing itself as a significant and growing shareholder, publicly demanded an immediate meeting, board change, and a capital-preservation plan, and said it intends to nominate two independent directors and push for a board-led M-and-A and strategic alternatives committee chaired by a shareholder-backed director. Their argument is arithmetic: two hundred thirty-seven point nine million in cash and marketable securities as of June thirtieth, down roughly eighty million from year-end, at a company that has already paused everything outside the lead program including its exosome-based COVID vaccine. Remember the backdrop — an advisory committee voted nine to three against approval on July twenty-ninth, and management has said it plans to file an amended application with twenty-four-month open-label extension data on upper-limb function. So today's decision lands on a company that is simultaneously fighting its regulator and its own shareholder base. We covered deramiocel in depth in the August fifteenth spotlight; the setup has only gotten tighter since.

Third, on policy. Health and Human Services on Friday issued a request for information titled, roughly, categories used in federal vaccine recommendations and the role of shared clinical decision-making. It publishes in the Federal Register on Monday. On its face it is a procedural notice asking whether the current three-tier structure — routine, risk-based, and shared clinical decision-making — is adequate. Read the actual solicitation and it is asking the public to weigh in on what considerations should govern vaccine recommendations, and it explicitly names individual autonomy alongside strength of scientific evidence. That is the tell. This administration has already cut the childhood universal-recommendation list from roughly eighteen diseases to eleven and moved influenza, hepatitis, COVID and meningococcal products into shared decision-making, and courts have pushed back on the process used to do it. An open comment docket is a much harder thing to enjoin than a unilateral schedule rewrite. For anyone with a vaccine franchise, the category a product sits in is the difference between an automatic order and a conversation, and that is now formally on the table.

Fourth, on obesity. Novo Nordisk has quietly started OASIS five, a phase three placebo-controlled trial testing two lower maintenance doses of oral semaglutide in adults with overweight or obesity. Four hundred fifty patients, sixty-two sites, first patient dosed August twelfth, primary endpoint percent change in body weight at week sixty, data in early twenty twenty-eight. The approved oral Wegovy regimen titrates to a twenty-five milligram daily maintenance dose by day ninety-one. Novo's public framing is patient flexibility. The commercial framing is more interesting: oral semaglutide is a peptide, and at twenty-five milligrams a day the amount of drug substance you have to manufacture is enormous, which is why the pill's gross margin trails the injection and why Lilly's small-molecule Foundayo has a structural cost advantage. If a materially lower maintenance dose holds efficacy, Novo closes part of that gap without changing the molecule. Worth noting that despite the manufacturing disadvantage, the Wegovy pill has outsold Foundayo since launch.

Fifth, Bavarian Nordic reported first-half results and the market liked them — second-quarter revenue up twenty-three percent to about two point zero billion Danish kroner, full-year guidance moved to the top of the range at roughly five point seven billion kroner with the EBITDA margin now guided to about thirty percent instead of twenty-eight, and a new share buyback of up to seven hundred fifty million kroner starting this quarter, bringing announced buybacks to one and a quarter billion. Buried in the pipeline slides is the item worth flagging: their Lyme disease vaccine, a six-strain Borrelia candidate built on a self-assembling antigen particle platform, is now targeted for phase one initiation in twenty twenty-eight. Lyme has been a graveyard — Pfizer and Valneva have already pushed their filing — and a phase one that starts in twenty twenty-eight is a program that will not matter commercially this decade.

Sixth, quick hits. Neumora Therapeutics executed a leadership transition effective August fourteenth: Paul Berns moves from chief executive to executive chairperson, and president Joshua Pinto takes the CEO seat. Replimune named Michelle DiNapoli chief commercial officer, its first, following the hard-won accelerated approval of its melanoma oncolytic. And note that Ambros-Werewolf is the second reverse merger in five days — Slate and Fulcrum announced theirs on August seventeenth. When private companies with fundable phase three assets keep choosing a broken shell over an S-one, that is telling you something about the relative cost of the two doors, and it is the thread we pull on in today's spotlight.
