Good morning, Travis. It's Wednesday, November eighteenth, 2026. An A-I drug designer just raised the largest private round in biotech history, the White House pulled its pick for F-D-A commissioner hours before the vote, and GSK got a second approval out of its Nuvalent buyout. Here's what you need to know.

First, Isomorphic Labs, the Alphabet drug-design company run by Demis Hassabis, raised five billion dollars in a Series C. Greenoaks and Sequoia co-led, Andreessen Horowitz came in as a new investor, and Alphabet and every existing backer took part. That's more than double the two point one billion it raised in May, and it beats Altos Labs' three billion dollar launch in 2022 as the biggest private biotech round ever. Isomorphic didn't disclose a valuation. Bloomberg reported earlier this month that talks put it at about fifty billion. The company has now raised about seven point seven billion dollars since March of last year, and it still hasn't named a single drug candidate or started a clinical trial. It's today's spotlight.

Second, Washington. On Tuesday evening, the White House withdrew Heidi Overton's nomination to lead the F-D-A, hours before the Senate health committee was set to vote. Overton, a thirty-seven-year-old physician and deputy director of the Domestic Policy Council, faced opposition from Democrats, from some Republicans, and from parts of Health Secretary Kennedy's MAHA movement. Committee chair Bill Cassidy had said on Sunday he still had questions. The agency has had no permanent commissioner since Marty Makary left in May. Kyle Diamantas stays on as acting commissioner, and Semafor reports Senator Roger Marshall, who lost his reelection bid this month, has been floated as a replacement. For sponsors, that means the leadership vacuum continues into 2027.

Third, an approval in lung cancer. The F-D-A granted accelerated approval to GSK's Nelkarmi, neladalkib, for advanced A-L-K-positive non-small cell lung cancer after prior A-L-K inhibitors. Neladalkib is a brain-penetrant kinase inhibitor built to stay active against the resistance mutations that defeat older A-L-K drugs. In the ALKOVE-one trial, patients previously treated only with Roche's alectinib responded at forty-nine percent. For patients past two or more inhibitors plus chemotherapy, the rate was thirty-two percent. About half of responders in both groups were still responding at a year. Patients who'd already had lorlatinib did worse, and liver enzyme elevations were the main toxicity. This is GSK's second approval from its ten point six billion dollar Nuvalent acquisition, after the ROS-one drug zidesamtinib earlier this year. Jefferies has put the three Nuvalent drugs at five to seven billion dollars a year at peak. The big test is ALKAZAR, a Phase three against alectinib in untreated patients.

Fourth, a Phase three win for a cancer vaccine. Sellas Life Sciences says galinpepimut-S, a vaccine made of four peptides from the W-T-one protein, which leukemia cells overexpress, extended survival in acute myeloid leukemia. The REGAL trial enrolled one hundred twenty-six patients in second remission, or who couldn't get a stem cell transplant. Median overall survival was twenty-four months, against fourteen point seven on the best available treatment, a forty-one percent cut in the risk of death. The trial was open-label and small, and the secondary endpoints are still being analyzed. Sellas plans to meet the F-D-A about accelerated approval and file in the first half of next year.

Fifth, a rare-disease approval with a long history. The F-D-A approved Beren Therapeutics' Lembaz, adrabetadex, for infantile-onset Niemann-Pick disease type C, a disorder in which cholesterol gets trapped inside neurons. Adrabetadex is a cyclodextrin given into the spinal fluid every two weeks to pull that cholesterol out. It's the first therapy for the disease shown to extend survival. In thirty-six treated children, median survival was eight and a half years, against four point nine in matched external controls. The F-D-A accepted that external comparison as the adequate and well-controlled study. The label carries a boxed warning for permanent hearing loss, and Beren also received a priority review voucher.

Sixth, the Alzheimer's meeting CTAD. Biogen's diranersen is an Ionis antisense drug that cuts production of the tau protein. At two years, the lowest dose slowed decline on the C-D-R sum of boxes by thirty-eight percent, up from twenty-six percent at eighteen months. There's a catch. The two-year comparison comes from the open-label extension, against former placebo patients who'd had one dose or none, and the trial missed its dose-response primary endpoint in May. Still, the gap is widening, which is what Biogen needs for Phase three. Elsewhere at CTAD, Cognito's Spectris, a headset that delivers forty-hertz light and sound, missed its primary endpoint in six hundred seventy-three patients but showed slowing in the mild subgroup, and the company still plans an F-D-A submission. Priavoid's amyloid-dissolving pill failed in Phase two. Acadia's remlifanserin narrowly missed in Alzheimer's psychosis, with a p-value of zero point zero six.

Seventh, more on last week's ivonescimab rejection. Endpoints reports that the F-D-A's letter to Summit and Akeso also raised a possible safety concern linked to V-E-G-F, the drug's second target. That reaches beyond the survival miss the companies disclosed. Summit is downplaying the risk. If the concern holds up, it matters for every P-D-one-by-V-E-G-F bispecific in development, not just this one.

Eighth, sickle cell. Marrow Therapeutics launched with one hundred seventy-three million dollars: a fifty-three million dollar Series A co-led by Scion Life Sciences and venBio, and a one hundred twenty million dollar Series B led by OrbiMed. Marrow uses antibodies against the transferrin receptor to deliver siRNA to bone marrow cells. Its lead drug, MAR-one-oh-one, silences B-C-L-eleven-A to switch fetal hemoglobin back on. That's the same target Casgevy edits, but without the chemotherapy conditioning and the cell manufacturing. It's preclinical. On the same day, Genetix, formerly bluebird bio, said its gene therapies are now profitable, with three hundred twenty-seven million dollars in U-S sales so far this year, up ninety-five percent. It expects about five hundred million dollars for 2026. Its shareholders' contingent value right pays out at six hundred million in any twelve-month period before the end of 2027.

A few quick ones. The F-D-A approved Novartis' Cosentyx, an I-L-seventeen-A antibody, for polymyalgia rheumatica in patients who can't taper off steroids. It's the first I-L-seventeen drug in that disease. Karyopharm's filing for selinexor, an X-P-O-one inhibitor, plus ruxolitinib in myelofibrosis was accepted, with a decision date of June twenty-eighth. In its Phase three, the combination won on spleen shrinkage but missed its co-primary symptom endpoint. And the U-S formally lost its measles elimination status.

Looking ahead: CTAD wraps up Thursday. Capricor's deramiocel decision is due Sunday. By the thirtieth, the F-D-A decides on Vertex's povetacicept and on Roche's giredestrant in early breast cancer. We didn't find any Foresite portfolio news in Tuesday's wires.
