Good morning Travis. Here are your biotech and pharma headlines for Saturday, September nineteenth, twenty twenty-six. Friday was a policy-and-platform day rather than a data day. The single biggest item for your world is that Anthropic has stopped being only a vendor to drug discovery and started doing the biology itself — there is now a physical wet lab, and a separate program that hands outside biologists the model Anthropic had been refusing to let anyone point at biology. That is the spotlight, and one of the named launch participants is a Foresite Labs company. Beyond that, Lilly won a full approval that is also an argument about when to switch therapy, the most-favored-nation program went to all fifty states, Beijing published its next five-year biotech plan, and Europe cleared twelve new medicines. Nine items, then the week ahead.

First, and today's spotlight. Reuters reported Friday that Anthropic has quietly built a wet lab in the San Francisco Bay Area, confirmed on the record by Eric Kauderer-Abrams, the company's head of life sciences. His framing was that the final test in biology is still real lab work, and that Anthropic's split between in-house experiments and external partners looks like what you would see at most biotech companies. A spokesperson then clarified that the lab is not specifically for drug discovery, and declined to say more. Separately, Anthropic opened its Life Sciences Verification Program, which for the first time gives vetted outside researchers access to Mythos, its most capable model, with safeguards deliberately loosened for biology. Xaira Therapeutics is one of three named launch participants — and Xaira was incubated by ARCH Venture Partners and Foresite Labs, with Vik Bajaj as a co-founder. The other two named are Edison Scientific and Manifold Bio. Put the two announcements side by side and you get the actual story, which is Anthropic crossing from selling intelligence to running the experiment. That is the episode.

Second, the approval that is really an argument. The F-D-A granted full approval to Lilly's Inluriyo — imlunestrant, an oral selective estrogen receptor degrader — in combination with Verzenio, abemaciclib, a C-D-K four-six inhibitor, for adults with estrogen-receptor-positive, H-E-R-two-negative, E-S-R-one-mutated advanced or metastatic breast cancer after progression on at least one line of endocrine therapy. The data come from a subgroup of the Phase three EMBER-three trial: one hundred fifty-nine patients with E-S-R-one-mutated disease, ninety-two on imlunestrant alone and sixty-seven on the combination, with median progression-free survival of eleven point one months versus five point five, a hazard ratio of zero point five three. Guardant three-sixty was approved alongside it as the companion diagnostic. Now the argument. Jacob Van Naarden, who runs Lilly Oncology, used the announcement to make an explicit case that switching endocrine therapy and the C-D-K four-six inhibitor at clinical progression beats switching earlier — his words were that all available evidence suggests it improves outcomes more, and that early switching means early exposure to side effects, patient anxiety from unnecessary testing, and avoidable cost. That is aimed directly at AstraZeneca's SERENA-six strategy, which uses circulating tumor D-N-A to catch the E-S-R-one mutation and switch to camizestrant before the scan turns. We covered that trial two weeks ago. You now have two large companies with opposite commercial incentives arguing over whether molecular progression is a treatment decision or just an early warning, and no head-to-head to settle it. Komal Jhaveri at Memorial Sloan Kettering, the EMBER-three principal investigator, framed the combination more neutrally as addressing resistance by hitting two distinct drivers at once.

Third, most-favored-nation pricing is now universal in Medicaid. President Trump announced Friday that all fifty states, the District of Columbia and Puerto Rico have joined the program the administration calls the GENEROUS model, under which participating manufacturers offer Medicaid prices benchmarked to the lowest paid by comparable developed countries. The White House Council of Economic Advisers puts the savings at twenty-seven point six billion dollars for states and thirty-six point six billion for the federal government over ten years, roughly sixty-five billion combined. Twenty-six manufacturers, covering about ninety percent of the branded U-S market by the administration's count, have signed on. The practical read for anyone modeling U-S net price: the reference-pricing floor is no longer a pilot in a handful of states, it is the Medicaid baseline nationwide, and the next question is whether commercial contracts drift toward it.

Fourth, Beijing put a number on its ambition. China's Ministry of Industry and Information Technology published its biotech five-year plan for twenty twenty-six through twenty thirty on Friday. The targets: grow the sector to three point five trillion yuan, about five hundred twenty-two billion dollars, at twenty percent annually; have at least twenty-five percent of the world's first-in-class drugs originate in China; produce fifty domestic companies each above ten billion yuan in revenue; and land at least five Chinese-developed drugs above a billion dollars in global sales. The plan itself notes that licensing deals for China-developed drugs grew thirty-six percent year on year with total value past one hundred twenty billion dollars. On the U-S side, Caitlin Frazer of the National Security Commission on Emerging Biotechnology has been characterizing China's approach as steal, scale and strangle, and warning about dependence on a potential adversary for life-sustaining medicines; B-I-O's John Crowley frames the same problem more narrowly as avoiding inescapable dependencies. Neither framing changes the arithmetic, which is that a very large fraction of the in-licensing opportunities crossing your desk now originate there.

Fifth, Europe cleared twelve new medicines. The C-H-M-P recommended twelve new approvals plus eleven label extensions at its September meeting. Two matter commercially. Novo's Frehemgo, denecimig, is a next-generation factor eight-a mimetic bispecific antibody for hemophilia A with or without inhibitors, and the pitch is dosing flexibility — monthly, every two weeks, or weekly, all from a single-use pre-filled pen. That is a direct run at Roche's Hemlibra, which built the category. The pivotal was FRONTIER two, which cut annualized bleeding rates against both prior factor prophylaxis and on-demand treatment. Novo expects first European launches in the fourth quarter and a broad rollout starting early next year. The other is AstraZeneca's Klygefa, gefurulimab, from Alexion — a dual-binding nanobody against complement C-five for generalized myasthenia gravis, which puts a third A-Z complement asset into a market where the company is already competing with itself, and against argenx and U-C-B.

Sixth, Novo bought three more shots on goal in obesity. Fierce Biotech reported Friday that Novo has taken three new early-stage obesity programs from New York's Kallyope. The point of interest is that none of them are incretins — the lead is described as a potential first-in-class peptide that is I-N-D-ready, plus a follow-on small molecule against a novel target and a small-molecule receptor agonist. Kallyope's platform maps gut-brain neural circuits, which is the most credible non-incretin route to appetite regulation anyone has commercialized so far, and the two companies have been working together since twenty eighteen under an option structure covering up to six products. Kallyope is also running its own non-incretin peptide, K-five-fifty-four, in Phase one. Note the pattern: this is Novo's fourth platform deal in about ten days, after Anthropic, Orbis and the rebrand. A company with a dominant incretin franchise is spending heavily to not be a one-mechanism company.

Seventh, the F-D-A pushed back on the narrative that it is rejecting more drugs. The agency published data showing first-cycle rejection rates have fallen from about forty-seven percent in two thousand eight to roughly twenty-seven percent in twenty twenty-four, which it offered as a rebuttal to claims that initial rejections are rising. Worth holding lightly in both directions — first-cycle rejection rate is a function of what sponsors choose to file as much as what the agency chooses to reject, and the same agency has spent this year loosening resubmission requirements. But if you have been pricing in a tougher F-D-A, the agency's own denominator does not support it.

Eighth, a small A-I financing with an unusually specific thesis. Mithrl raised a twenty million dollar Series A led by Obvious Ventures, with Headline, A-G-I House and several pharma executives participating. The product is a biomedical world model plus an agentic layer, and the design choice is the interesting part: the agents reason only over curated, validated, published biology plus the client's own data, rather than searching an open corpus. Founder and chief executive Vivek Adarsh argues the industry's bottleneck is no longer generating hypotheses but triaging them, and that the constraint is which ones survive downstream experiments. Two claims worth testing if this crosses your desk — that restricting the reasoning substrate cuts token use forty-five percent against the same frontier base models uncustomized, and that every hypothesis ships with its sources and a confidence score. The platform deploys inside the client's environment, several top-ten pharmas are already using it, and second-generation early access opens on the twenty-first.

Ninth, a genetics result worth thirty seconds. A paper in Science on Thursday reported that the germline E-G-F-R T-seven-ninety-M variant raises lung cancer risk about sixty-two-fold in never-smokers and about twenty-five-fold in smokers. The analysis ran across three point three seven million genotyped individuals from twenty-three-and-Me, putting prevalence at roughly one in fifteen thousand eight hundred fifty, and of seventeen cancers and non-pulmonary conditions screened, the association was specific to lung. The authors also traced the variant to a founder event in southern Appalachia roughly two hundred years ago. T-seven-ninety-M is familiar as the acquired resistance mutation that osimertinib was built to overcome; finding it inherited, at that effect size, in a geographically concentrated population, is an argument for a targeted screening program rather than a therapeutic one.
