Welcome back, Travis. Today's spotlight is on a failure — but one of those failures that teaches the field more than most successes. Thursday, Novo Nordisk reported that its ZEUS trial of ziltivekimab, an anti-inflammatory antibody for cardiovascular disease, missed its primary endpoint. To understand why cardiologists spent the day absorbing this rather than shrugging it off, you have to understand what ZEUS was actually testing. It wasn't just testing a drug. It was testing an idea that has organized a large part of cardiovascular biology for the last decade.

Start with the idea. For most of the twentieth century, atherosclerosis was a plumbing problem — cholesterol builds up, pipes narrow, you have a heart attack. Statins turned that into one of medicine's great success stories. But even with cholesterol driven to the floor, a large fraction of patients keep having events. And over the last twenty-five years, the explanation that took hold is that the plaque isn't just a passive grease deposit — it's an active, chronic, immune-driven lesion. Inflammation initiates it, propagates it, and ultimately destabilizes it into the rupture that kills you. That's the inflammation hypothesis of atherosclerosis, and the residual-inflammatory-risk thesis is its clinical corollary: after you've handled the cholesterol, the risk that remains is inflammatory risk, and if you could safely turn down the inflammation, you'd save lives that statins can't.

The molecular spine of that hypothesis is a signaling cascade. An inflammasome fires and releases interleukin one-beta; interleukin one-beta drives production of interleukin six; interleukin six tells the liver to pump out C-reactive protein. That last molecule, measured as high-sensitivity C-reactive protein, is the blood test cardiologists use to read a patient's inflammatory temperature, and high levels predict events independent of cholesterol. Interleukin six sits right in the middle of that chain — which is exactly why it looked like the perfect drug target. Hit the middle of the cascade and you should shut down everything downstream.

There was real evidence this could work. The landmark trial was CANTOS, back in twenty-seventeen, which used canakinumab to block interleukin one-beta — one step upstream. CANTOS was positive: it cut major cardiovascular events by around fifteen percent with no change in cholesterol at all, and it's the result that let the field say inflammation isn't just a marker, it's causal. And human genetics agreed — people who inherit a naturally dampened interleukin-six receptor have lower cardiovascular risk, the kind of Mendelian evidence that usually means a target is real. So the logic for ziltivekimab was tight: go one rung down from canakinumab, block interleukin six directly, and you should get the CANTOS benefit — maybe more.

Now here's why ZEUS was the clean test, the one everyone was waiting for. Novo didn't run it in a general population. They enriched it — more than six thousand three hundred patients who had established atherosclerotic disease, and chronic kidney disease, and an elevated C-reactive protein above two. That is the most inflamed, highest-residual-risk population you can assemble; kidney disease in particular is a state of grinding systemic inflammation. If anti-inflammatory therapy helps anyone, it should help these patients. And ziltivekimab is a potent, direct blocker — in earlier studies it drove high-sensitivity C-reactive protein down by more than ninety percent. So the setup was as favorable as drug development gets: the right hypothesis, the right target, the right population, and a drug that unquestionably hits it.

And it did hit it. That's the part that makes this so striking, and so uncomfortable. The trial confirmed target engagement — free interleukin six fell, C-reactive protein fell, exactly as designed. The biology worked. The hazard ratio for major adverse cardiovascular events was zero point nine nine, with a confidence interval running from about zero point eight-eight to one point one-one. That is not a near-miss you can spin. That is a flat line. The drug did everything the model said it should do to the biomarkers, and none of it reached the patients. As Novo's development chief put it, the expected biological effect simply did not translate into benefit. And there was a cost on the other side of the ledger — more serious infections in the treated arm, which is what you expect when you suppress interleukin six, because that pathway also runs your defense against bacteria.

So what do you do with a result where you crushed the biomarker and moved the outcome not at all? This is the deep lesson, and it's why this matters well beyond Novo. It is a textbook decoupling of target engagement from clinical benefit. There are a few ways to read it, and they're not mutually exclusive. One: maybe the inflammation hypothesis is narrower than the field hoped — perhaps it's specifically interleukin one-beta biology that's causal, and interleukin six, despite sitting in the pathway, isn't the right node to block, because it also carries protective and repair functions you don't want to switch off. Two: maybe this particular population was wrong — advanced kidney disease brings competing causes of death and calcified, burnt-out plaque that anti-inflammatory therapy can no longer reverse. Three: maybe the infection penalty quietly ate whatever cardiovascular benefit was there. But the cleanest, most sobering reading is the simplest: C-reactive protein is a beautiful marker of risk and a poor drug target readout. Lowering it tells you the drug is working on the liver. It does not tell you the drug is working on the artery.

For a scientific audience that's the real takeaway, and it generalizes far past cardiology. We increasingly build and de-risk drugs — including AI-designed drugs — around target engagement and biomarker movement, because those readouts are fast and clean. ZEUS is a large, expensive, rigorous reminder that a validated biomarker is a hypothesis about the disease, not a guarantee about the therapy. Novo paid for this conviction directly: it acquired ziltivekimab through its twenty-twenty purchase of Corvidia Therapeutics — seven hundred and twenty-five million dollars upfront, up to two point one billion in total — as the anchor of its push beyond diabetes and obesity into cardio-renal disease. Thursday's non-cash impairment is that bet being written down.

Two things keep this from being a clean burial of the whole idea, and they're worth holding onto. First, the target isn't dead everywhere. Novo is continuing two other phase three trials of ziltivekimab — HERMES, in heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, and ARTEMIS, in acute myocardial infarction — both reading out in the first half of next year. Those are genuinely different settings: acute heart attack involves a sharp inflammatory surge where blocking interleukin six might matter in a way it doesn't in chronic, smoldering disease, and the preserved-ejection-fraction heart failure population has a distinctly inflammatory phenotype. So the interleukin-six story could still post a win — just not the one it was designed around. Second, the broader anti-inflammatory approach to heart disease still has its one approved, cheap champion: low-dose colchicine, which succeeded in chronic coronary disease and reached the market. Inflammation as a cardiovascular target isn't over. But the specific, expensive, antibody-based, interleukin-six version of it — the one a nine-figure acquisition and a decade of residual-risk narrative were built on — just took the hardest possible hit, in the population most likely to have proven it right.

The competitive read for your world is straightforward. Anyone with a cardiovascular inflammation asset, or a program leaning on C-reactive protein as its efficacy story, now has a much higher bar and a much more skeptical audience. And anyone building the next generation of biomarker-led, computationally-designed programs should tape the ZEUS hazard ratio to the wall: zero point nine nine, with the biomarker down ninety percent. Engaging the target is the price of entry. It was never the proof. That's the spotlight. Talk soon.
