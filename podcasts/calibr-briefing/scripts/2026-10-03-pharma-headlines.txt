Good morning Travis. Here are your biotech and pharma headlines for Saturday, October third, twenty twenty-six, covering Thursday afternoon through Friday's close. The lead is a Foresite portfolio story and it is also today's spotlight: Xaira came out of its long quiet period on Friday with the first technical account of its antibody design model and the first look at two pipeline programs, including a G-protein-coupled receptor that defeated both a naive library campaign and a llama immunization before the model got a binder. Around it, Novartis put more than half a billion dollars up front on an mRNA-encoded T-cell engager from China, Lilly's non-covalent B-T-K inhibitor moved into first-line leukemia, Pfizer's triple-target eczema antibody put up numbers that belong in the Dupixent-successor conversation, and the Lilly-Foghorn synthetic lethality collaboration collapsed and took forty percent of Foghorn's staff with it. Ten items, then the quick ones and the week ahead.

First, Xaira. On Friday the company published a detailed post from its design team on X-Design, its molecule design model, and disclosed two pipeline programs for the first time. The headline result is in two parts. On a lead oncology program, the model designed a hundred eighty-two single-domain antibodies against a precision-constrained epitope and produced a molecule that binds the human target at seventeen nanomolar, cross-reacts with the surrogate species, and passes every developability and humanness filter — three weeks of wet-lab work to a progressable binder, seven weeks from D-N-A synthesis to a lead. On the second program, a G-protein-coupled receptor with heavy post-translational modification and no clear epitope, two rounds of conventional naive-library screening and a llama immunization campaign both produced nothing usable, and the model produced a complete functional antagonist that cross-reacts with mouse and cynomolgus monkey. Xaira launched in April twenty twenty-four with over a billion dollars from ARCH and Foresite Labs, with David Baker as a co-founder. Full analysis, including where I think the claim is strong and where it is thinner than it sounds, in the spotlight.

Second, Novartis is paying Abogen Biosciences of Suzhou five hundred seventy-five million dollars up front, with about seven point two billion in milestones plus royalties, for an mRNA-encoded T-cell engager. The asset is a messenger-R-N-A drug that gives the patient's own cells the genetic code to produce a C-D-three by C-D-nineteen bispecific in vivo, rather than infusing the protein. The pitch is safety: encoding the engager and letting expression ramp smoothly is supposed to reduce cytokine release syndrome, which carries a boxed warning on Roche's Columvi, J-and-J's Tecvayli and Amgen's Blincyto. First-in-human data presented in April covered nine patients with relapsed or refractory B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma, with no cytokine release syndrome, no dose-limiting toxicities and no neurologic events reported, and response rates the company said reached a hundred percent at the top dose. The companies talked up autoimmune disease without committing to it, and Novartis declined to name target indications. Novartis also takes an option on a set of additional programs off the platform. Two things to hold onto. Nine patients is nine patients, and the absence of cytokine release syndrome in nine lymphoma patients is a hypothesis, not a property. And the strategic logic runs against the usual argument for engagers — you are giving up infusion-level dose control and reversibility in exchange for smoother exposure, and somebody just paid half a billion dollars up front for that trade. This is also Novartis's second China deal in about a month, after a radioligand license from BoomRay worth up to nine hundred million.

Third, the F-D-A approved Lilly's Jaypirca — pirtobrutinib — on Thursday for adults with previously untreated chronic lymphocytic leukemia or small lymphocytic lymphoma without a seventeen-p deletion. This is the first and only non-covalent B-T-K inhibitor, meaning it binds the kinase reversibly and does not depend on the cysteine residue that covalent drugs like ibrutinib and acalabrutinib attach to — which is why it works in patients whose disease has mutated that residue away. Until now that was its whole identity: the drug you use after the covalent ones fail. The approval is based on BRUIN C-L-L-three-thirteen, where at a median twenty-eight months of follow-up progression-free survival improved with a hazard ratio of zero point two zero against bendamustine plus rituximab, with a ninety-four percent overall response rate against eighty-one percent. Note the comparator. Bendamustine-rituximab is chemoimmunotherapy, not a modern covalent B-T-K inhibitor, so this establishes first-line utility without answering the question everybody actually has, which is how pirtobrutinib stacks up against acalabrutinib or zanubrutinib up front. Serious adverse reactions in twenty-eight percent, pneumonia in five percent, discontinuation for adverse events in a bit over four percent.

Fourth, and read this one against yesterday's spotlight. Pfizer put out detailed Phase two data at the European dermatology meeting for tilrekimig, a trispecific antibody that blocks interleukin-four, interleukin-thirteen and T-S-L-P at once, in moderate-to-severe atopic dermatitis. In the first stage, sixty-three percent of patients on four hundred fifty milligrams every two weeks hit the seventy-five percent skin-clearance threshold against twenty percent on placebo. In the second stage, with monthly dosing, forty-eight, sixty-one and fifty-seven percent across the fifty, two hundred and four hundred milligram arms against nine percent on placebo. R-B-C's Trung Huynh called the numbers impressive and competitive with Dupixent and Lilly's Ebglyss. One analyst's caution was that the drug is strong but not clearly differentiated in an increasingly crowded field. Pfizer has already pushed it into late-stage testing in asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and atopic dermatitis, including a head-to-head against Dupixent — which is the only study that will settle it. Keep the running tally: in four days we have had Sanofi paying a billion dollars up front for long-acting interleukin-four and thirteen antibodies, AbbVie's seventy-seven-day-half-life interleukin-thirteen antibody clearing Phase two, and now a trispecific that adds T-S-L-P. Dupixent did almost eleven billion dollars in the first half of this year and loses U-S patent protection around twenty thirty-one. Everyone is running at the same gap.

Fifth, the Lilly and Foghorn oncology collaboration is over, and Foghorn is cutting about forty percent of its workforce, down to roughly sixty-five employees. The two companies reviewed Phase one dose-escalation data on F-H-D-nine-oh-nine, a selective S-M-A-R-C-A-two inhibitor, and decided not to take it into expansion; a selective S-M-A-R-C-A-two degrader program is also shelved. Chief executive Adrian Gottschalk's statement is the honest kind and worth quoting: they built a drug that selectively hits S-M-A-R-C-A-two with a favorable safety profile at exposures above their preclinical targets, and the biology of the S-M-A-R-C-A-two and four synthetic lethality relationship did not translate into the efficacy required. That is a target failure, not a molecule failure, and it is a meaningful data point for anyone carrying synthetic lethality programs — the paralog-dependency thesis has now been tested with a clean tool at adequate exposure and did not deliver. Lilly's Loxo subsidiary paid three hundred million up front plus an eighty-million-dollar equity stake at twenty dollars a share back in twenty twenty-one. The stock opened Thursday around two dollars and change, down more than forty percent. Foghorn says the cuts extend its runway into the second half of twenty twenty-nine and it will concentrate on an E-P-three-hundred degrader, a C-B-P degrader, an oral immunology program and its induced-proximity platform.

Sixth, Summit Therapeutics signed a clinical trial collaboration with AstraZeneca and Daiichi Sankyo to combine ivonescimab, its P-D-one by V-E-G-F bispecific, with Datroway — datopotamab deruxtecan, the T-R-O-P-two-directed antibody-drug conjugate — across multiple solid tumors, starting with a Phase three in first-line triple-negative breast cancer. Each company contributes its own drug and shares trial costs, AstraZeneca or Daiichi sponsors the studies, and everybody keeps their own rights. This comes four days after AstraZeneca took a two-billion-dollar, roughly twelve percent equity position in Summit, and it tells you what that money was for: Summit's stated ambition is for ivonescimab to be the global P-D-one by V-E-G-F partner of choice for combinations, and this is the first proof that a major is willing to spend trial dollars on that premise. It also expands Summit beyond lung cancer for the first time. The ivonescimab action date is November fourteenth.

Seventh, Aviceda Therapeutics is shutting down. Its sialic-acid-coated nanoparticle for geographic atrophy, A-V-D-one-oh-four, failed to beat Astellas's Izervay on lesion growth in a Phase two-b, and after initially saying it would push into Phase three anyway, the company is winding up. It had raised a two hundred seven and a half million dollar Series C. Geographic atrophy remains one of the most capital-destructive indications in the industry — the approved complement drugs have modest anatomic effects and contested functional benefit, and a nanoparticle immunomodulator that could not separate from that bar had nowhere to go.

Eighth, Kailera and Hengrui reported Phase two data at the diabetes meeting in Milan for ribupatide, their weekly injectable G-L-P-one and G-I-P dual agonist, in a hundred fifty-eight Chinese women with polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome — the condition formerly called polycystic ovary syndrome — who also had obesity or overweight. Weight loss at thirty-two weeks ran ten percent at one milligram, fifteen point one at two, and twenty point two at four, against two point six percent on placebo, and all three doses increased menstrual frequency while placebo patients got less regular. No serious adverse events and no discontinuations for adverse events. Leerink called it compelling with supportive menstrual effects and early support for activity beyond weight loss. The strategic point is that no one has run a serious registrational program here even though a Truveta analysis found seventeen point six percent of women with the condition were already on semaglutide or tirzepatide last year, up from two point four percent in twenty twenty-one. That is a large, documented off-label population with no label — which is exactly the kind of gap a company with a differentiated incretin and no U-S franchise to protect should want.

Ninth, on the artificial intelligence file, BioPharma Dive ran a piece this week that is worth your time precisely because the failure it describes is boring. An AI chemistry agent was asked whether a molecule had a known hepatobiliary side effect. It fed a malformed molecular string to its lookup tool, got an error, tried to repair the molecule using a second tool, supplied essentially the same bad input, got another error — and then, instead of stopping, switched to a generic model and returned a confident answer. No misalignment, no autonomy drama. Bad input, failed error handling, an improvised workaround, and a plausible wrong scientific conclusion downstream. Set that next to the Stanford work published in September that stood up a virtual biotech in which more than thirty-seven thousand agents annotated outcomes from almost fifty-six thousand clinical trials, and you get the shape of the real risk: the question is not whether an agent is right, it is whether a wrong answer becomes the input to the next decision without anyone noticing. In the same week Stanford's Euan Ashley wrote in STAT that he had Claude analyze his whole genome in thirty minutes — a task that took thirty people and the better part of a year in two thousand nine — and that it recovered the most consequential finding, his two copies of the A-P-O-E epsilon-four variant, from a decade-old file built on an older reference genome. His conclusion was not that this is unsafe, it was that there are no standards for what such a report should contain. Both stories point the same direction: the capability is real and arrived faster than the evaluation layer.
