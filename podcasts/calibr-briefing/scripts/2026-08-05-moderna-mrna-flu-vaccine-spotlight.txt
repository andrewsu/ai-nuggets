Good morning, Travis. Today's spotlight is a landmark that's also a paradox. Sometime today, the F-D-A is due to decide on Moderna's messenger-R-N-A seasonal flu vaccine — M-R-N-A ten ten, which Moderna is branding m-Flusiva. If it's approved, it becomes the first messenger-R-N-A influenza vaccine ever licensed in the United States. That alone is a milestone. What makes it genuinely strange is that the approval is landing inside an administration that has spent the entire year dismantling messenger-R-N-A vaccine work. Let me walk through the science, the whiplash that got us here, and why the approval may matter less than what happens after it.

Start with what the vaccine is and the case for it. M-R-N-A ten ten uses the same platform as the COVID vaccines: instead of growing influenza virus in eggs over roughly six months, you encode the flu surface proteins as messenger R-N-A and let the body make them. The pitch has always been speed and precision. Every year, manufacturers have to guess which strains will circulate and commit to egg-based production months in advance, and in bad-match years, efficacy craters. Messenger R-N-A compresses that timeline and lets you match circulating strains more closely. The pivotal trial, published in the New England Journal, delivered on that thesis: in adults fifty and older, M-R-N-A ten ten cut influenza illness by about twenty-seven percent relative to a standard-dose flu shot — not versus placebo, but versus the existing standard of care. Nearly a thousand confirmed cases accrued, so the effect is real and reasonably precise.

Now the whiplash, because it's the whole story. Back in February, the F-D-A refused even to file Moderna's application. The agency's then-vaccine chief, Vinay Prasad, argued the trial wasn't adequately controlled. Moderna did something unusual — it published the rejection letter and pushed back publicly, saying the agency had backtracked on its own prior guidance for flu shots. There was a public backlash, a formal Type A meeting, and the F-D-A reversed course and agreed to review after all. Then both Prasad and the commissioner, Marty Makary — the two figures who had pushed the stricter vaccine bar — left the agency. When career reviewers finally worked the file, they flagged no major deficiencies, and in June the advisory committee voted nine-to-nothing, twice, that the benefits outweigh the risks for both the fifty-to-sixty-four and the sixty-five-plus groups. There were real scientific caveats aired — the trial ran a single season and was cut short, so the data on the very elderly and on influenza B specifically are thin — and Moderna answered those with a commitment to a confirmatory trial of up to eight hundred thousand people over two seasons. That's the likely shape of today's approval: full approval for the fifties-to-early-sixties, accelerated approval for sixty-five-plus with the confirmatory study to follow.

Here's the paradox I keep coming back to. While one arm of the government is poised to approve this, another arm has spent the year gutting messenger R-N-A. The health department under Secretary Kennedy canceled twenty-two messenger-R-N-A vaccine projects at Barda — nearly half a billion dollars — on the stated rationale that messenger R-N-A, quote, fails to protect effectively against upper-respiratory viruses like COVID and flu. And it specifically terminated Moderna's own seven-hundred-and-sixty-six-million-dollar contract to develop a pandemic bird-flu vaccine. So the same government that just declared messenger R-N-A ineffective against flu is, days later, about to license a messenger-R-N-A flu vaccine on the strength of a trial showing it beats the standard shot. The resolution is that these are two different chambers — political leadership at the department, versus career reviewers and an independent advisory committee at the F-D-A — and, at least on this specific product, the science-driven chamber is winning.

So what should you take from it. Three things. First, this is a platform-durability signal. If M-R-N-A ten ten clears, it's proof that the messenger-R-N-A platform can be updated season to season and still clear the F-D-A's evidentiary bar for a routine, non-pandemic indication — not just an emergency. For anyone building in genetic medicine broadly, including the R-N-A and gene-therapy names in your world, the message is that the science can still win at the agency even in a hostile political climate.

Second, and this is the more important nuance: approval is not access. An F-D-A license lets Moderna sell the vaccine; it does not put it into arms. That depends on the recommendation from the immunization advisory committee — the body that guides who should get it and whether insurers and pharmacies cover it — and that committee now sits under a health department openly skeptical of messenger R-N-A. So you can very plausibly get the strange outcome of an approved product that struggles to win a strong recommendation or clean reimbursement. The binding constraint on this franchise has moved downstream, from the F-D-A to the policy and payer layer. That's the part to watch over the fall.

Third, the commercial read. Moderna badly needs a second act as COVID revenue keeps eroding, and flu is a huge, recurring market. But it's crowded — Sanofi, G-S-K, and C-S-L Seqirus own the incumbent egg-based and protein franchises, and rivals including Pfizer have their own messenger-R-N-A flu programs in development. Being first to market with a messenger-R-N-A flu shot is a real edge, but only if Moderna can convert it into recommendations and uptake against entrenched competition and a skeptical public. First-approved and first-adopted are not the same thing.

Let me be clear on the caveat: as of this morning the decision isn't public, and even at this late stage the F-D-A could issue a complete response letter or attach a narrower label than the panel endorsed. But the likeliest outcome, given a clean staff review and a unanimous panel, is an approval — and if it comes, the headline writes itself as a scientific milestone, while the real story is the one underneath: whether a landmark vaccine can actually reach patients in an environment where the government approving it is the same one telling people not to trust the technology.
