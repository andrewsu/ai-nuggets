Good morning Travis. Here are your biotech and pharma headlines for Thursday, October eighth, twenty twenty-six, covering Tuesday and Wednesday. The lead and today's spotlight is A-I: Biohub, the Chan Zuckerberg research nonprofit, turned its virtual cell project into a one point eight billion dollar coalition with the Department of Energy, the N-I-H, Google DeepMind, Isomorphic Labs and Meta, and the money is going into data rather than models. Around it: AbbVie and Genmab's Epkinly posts the first Phase three bispecific win in newly diagnosed lymphoma, Zealand's amylin drug shows it works in diabetes too, a Takeda spinout gets a Parkinson's Phase three win, ArriVent loses half its value, Caribou is shutting down, and Halozyme gets subcutaneous Keytruda blocked in eight European countries. Ten items, then the week ahead.

First, Biohub. On Wednesday Biohub, the Department of Energy and the National Institutes of Health announced one point eight billion dollars in funding, data, compute and measurement technology for what they call the Virtual Biology Initiative. Google DeepMind, Isomorphic Labs and Meta are putting in three hundred million dollars together. The Energy Department commits more than five hundred million over five years through its Genesis Mission A-I program, and the N-I-H contributes existing datasets built with more than five hundred million dollars of earlier federal money, so not all of the headline is new cash. All of it sits on Biohub's own five hundred million dollar commitment from April. The goal is an open, A-I-ready dataset of how human cells respond to drugs and genetic changes, big enough to train a model that predicts the experiment before you run it. The spotlight covers why the money went to data, why companies that compete on models would pay for an open commons, and what it means for startups whose moat is proprietary cell data. That same day, Paris-based Rivercell launched with a twenty-five million dollar seed round led by H-V to do the proprietary version of exactly that.

Second, the clinical story of the week. AbbVie and Genmab said Epkinly, epcoritamab, a bispecific antibody that binds C-D-twenty on B cells and C-D-three on T cells, added to standard R-CHOP chemotherapy cut the risk of progression or death by fifty-one percent in the nine-hundred-patient EPCORE D-L-B-C-L-two trial in newly diagnosed diffuse large B-cell lymphoma. Investors expected about forty percent, and Genmab rose as much as fourteen percent. It's the first Phase three win for a bispecific in front-line lymphoma. TD Cowen's Yaron Werber wrote that Epkinly should become the undisputed standard of care and reach three billion dollars a year in sales. Jefferies' Benjamin Jackson called his one point four billion dollar first-line forecast possibly conservative, but said market share is the open question. One caveat matters: the comparator was plain R-CHOP, not Roche's Polivy regimen, which is how many U-S patients are now treated. Roche's Lunsumio and AstraZeneca's Calquence, which reads out in front-line disease early next year, are the next challengers.

Third, obesity. Zealand Pharma reported Phase two ZUPREME-two results for petrelintide, its once-weekly amylin analog partnered with Roche, in two hundred twenty people with obesity and type two diabetes. Weight loss reached nine point two percent at twenty-eight weeks against two percent on placebo, and A-one-C fell by up to about nine tenths of a point relative to placebo. Discontinuation for stomach side effects was one point nine percent, basically placebo's one point seven. Diabetics lose less weight than non-diabetics on every incretin drug, so nine percent alone at twenty-eight weeks is respectable. But petrelintide's value to Roche is as a gentler partner for its G-L-P-one and G-I-P drug enicepatide, and that combination study starts later this year. Roche's three Phase three trials, about seven thousand people, are already enrolling.

Fourth, Parkinson's. Cerevance, a private Boston company formed in twenty sixteen as a joint venture between Takeda and Lightstone Ventures, said its once-daily pill solengepras met its Phase three goal as an add-on for patients still fluctuating on levodopa. Solengepras is an inverse agonist of G-P-R-six, a receptor found on the indirect-pathway neurons of the striatum, so it works on movement without touching dopamine and should avoid the jerky movements dopamine drugs cause. In three hundred forty-one patients, the high dose cut daily off time by about thirty-seven minutes versus placebo at twelve weeks, and on time without troublesome dyskinesia rose by a similar amount, only just reaching significance. That's a modest effect, roughly in line with other add-ons, and it arrives just after AbbVie's tavapadon approval. Cerevance will talk to the F-D-A about next steps.

Fifth, ArriVent. Its brain-penetrant E-G-F-R inhibitor firmonertinib, licensed from Shanghai's Allist, missed its Phase three FURVENT trial in lung cancer with E-G-F-R exon twenty insertion mutations. Median progression-free survival was eleven months versus nine and a half on chemotherapy by blinded central review, not significant. Investigators scored it eleven point one versus seven point one, and Truist called that gap "a meaningful discordance in control-arm assessment." The stock fell from about twenty-eight dollars to about twelve, and Truist cut its target from forty-five to eighteen. Exon twenty already has J and J's Rybrevant with chemo in first line and Taiho's zipalertinib coming, so the case now rests on the ALPACCA trial in a different mutation class.

Sixth, Caribou Biosciences, the CRISPR company Jennifer Doudna co-founded, is ending R and D, laying off most of its staff and exploring strategic alternatives. Its lead program, vispa-cel, an off-the-shelf C-D-nineteen CAR-T from donor cells partially matched to the patient's immune type, had an F-D-A-agreed Phase three design and no one to pay for it. Leerink's Daina Graybosch put it bluntly: evidently investors were unwilling to fund the trial, given doubts about clinical risk, the matching strategy and the data. The stock topped thirty dollars in twenty twenty-one and now trades under a dollar. Allogeneic CAR-T is running out of runway while in-vivo CAR-T takes the capital.

Seventh, Halozyme won a Dutch court ruling that blocks Merck's subcutaneous Keytruda Qlex in eight European countries, finding it infringes Halozyme's patents on the enzyme technology that lets large antibodies go under the skin. Merck's version uses a hyaluronidase variant licensed from Korea's Alteogen rather than taking a Halozyme license, as Bristol and Roche did for Opdivo and Tecentriq. Halozyme already won in Germany and is suing in the U-S. TD Cowen's Brendan Smith wrote that it improves the path to a license or other economic resolution. With Keytruda's U-S patent cliff in twenty twenty-eight, the under-the-skin version is Merck's main defense, so the question is the price of a settlement.

Eighth, deals and money, including one Foresite name. KymaThera raised an eighty-million-dollar Series B led by Alta Partners, with Foresite Capital and Venrock participating. Its drug, K-seventeen-twenty-eight, targets cancers and vascular malformations driven by mutant P-I-three-kinase alpha, and Phase one starts this quarter in vascular malformations and with combination therapy in hormone-positive breast cancer. Mutant-selective P-I-three-K-alpha is crowded, with Relay and Scorpion's program now at Lilly, so the vascular-malformation entry point is the differentiator. Roche signed its fourth China deal in seven weeks, with Hangzhou's Defand Therapeutics for molecular glue degraders, worth just under a billion dollars but with under ten million up front. Matchpoint Therapeutics, already Novartis-partnered with sixty million up front, raised a hundred fifty million dollar Series B to take M-P-T-zero-sixty-two into the clinic. Forbion closed two point six billion dollars across two funds, Europe's biggest life-science raise, with Eli Lilly among its investors. Chen Yu's T-C-G-X launched a six hundred million dollar fund for China and Asia. And Ultragenyx sold a priority review voucher for two hundred ten million dollars, one of the richest this year, with a second still to sell.

Ninth, the F-D-A. Pfizer's Tukysa, tucatinib, a HER-two-selective kinase inhibitor, was approved with trastuzumab and pertuzumab as chemotherapy-free maintenance after first-line induction in HER-two-positive metastatic breast cancer. In HER-CLIMB-zero-five, median progression-free survival was twenty-four point nine months versus sixteen point three, a hazard ratio of zero point six four. Novartis' Rhapsido, the oral B-T-K inhibitor remibrutinib, won a second indication, symptomatic dermographism, hives triggered by rubbing the skin, where twice as many patients as on placebo were completely clear at week twelve.

Tenth, the quick ones. PhRMA sued the administration over GLOBE, the Medicare demonstration that ties U-S prices to those paid abroad, the industry's latest lawsuit over price controls. MimiVax's SurVaxM, a peptide vaccine against survivin, narrowly missed overall survival in its Phase two-b trial in glioblastoma, though the company points to a four-month gain in patients sixty-five and younger, a subgroup argument that rarely survives a regulator. Bayer committed two point two billion dollars to a pharma plant in Ohio, the latest tariff-driven U-S manufacturing pledge. And Bexorg got a twenty-eight million dollar A-R-P-A-H award to keep donated human brains metabolically active longer as a drug-testing platform.
