This is your spotlight for Wednesday, September second, twenty twenty-six. Alumis lost fifty-eight percent of its market value yesterday on a lupus trial that missed, and the entire remaining argument for the program rests on a prespecified subgroup defined by an interferon gene signature. That subgroup question is the whole story, and it is worth going slowly on, because the answer determines not just what happens to a Foresite portfolio company but whether biomarker enrichment is a real path in systemic lupus or a place where programs go to die politely.

Start with what was actually reported, because what was left out matters as much as what was included. LUMUS was a global Phase two-b trial of envudeucitinib — the compound you have followed as E-S-K-zero-zero-one — in adults with moderately-to-severely active, autoantibody-positive systemic lupus erythematosus. Four hundred eight patients randomized to one of three doses or placebo for forty-eight weeks. The primary endpoint was BICLA, the British Isles Lupus Assessment Group-based Composite Lupus Assessment, at week forty-eight. The trial did not meet its primary endpoint, and it did not meet its key secondary endpoints, in the overall trial population. Alumis then reported that in a prespecified subgroup of patients with a high interferon gene signature, classified using a commercially available assay, there were what it called robust clinical responses on BICLA and on the key secondaries — the cutaneous disease index, the SRI-four responder index, and low disease activity state. Pharmacodynamic data showed dose-dependent interferon pathway target engagement with maximal suppression at forty milligrams twice daily. And the company's explanation for the overall miss is that interferon-high patients were unexpectedly under-represented in the enrolled population, diluting the result.

Here is the thing that should shape how you read all of that. Alumis released no numbers. Not a BICLA response rate in the overall population, not a rate in the subgroup, not a placebo rate, not a delta. The word doing all the work in that press release is "robust," and it is doing that work unaccompanied. The full dataset goes to a medical meeting later. Until then, every statement about effect size in the interferon-high group, including the company's, is a characterization rather than a result.

The mechanism is worth stating precisely, because it explains why this particular miss is confusing. Envudeucitinib is an oral allosteric inhibitor of tyrosine kinase two. Allosteric here means it binds the regulatory pseudokinase domain rather than the catalytic site, which is the structural trick that buys this class its selectivity against the other Janus kinases and lets it avoid the boxed-warning liabilities that come with pan-J-A-K inhibition. Tyrosine kinase two sits immediately downstream of the interleukin twenty-three and interleukin twelve receptors and downstream of the type one interferon receptor, so a selective inhibitor should in principle turn down both the interleukin twenty-three-slash-seventeen axis and interferon signaling. Alumis's differentiation claim against Bristol Myers's approved Sotyktu has always been duration: that envudeucitinib is the only tyrosine kinase two inhibitor shown to deliver maximal target inhibition across a full twenty-four hours in humans. And the pharmacodynamic data from LUMUS support that claim. Interferon pathway suppression was dose-dependent and maximal at the top dose. So the drug did the thing the drug is supposed to do, and the trial still missed. That is not a potency problem or a coverage problem. It is a problem in the space between suppressing a pathway and moving a composite clinical index in a heterogeneous disease.

Now the subgroup. The reason investors did not extend credit is not that subgroup analyses are always illegitimate. It is that the specific defense being mounted here is close to unfalsifiable without another trial. BioWorld put it plainly yesterday: management focused on the interferon-high analysis, where the response curves separated over forty-eight weeks, and investors focused on the overall population. Shares traded at nine dollars twenty-seven midday, down twelve dollars fifty-four. BioPharma Dive noted that erases nearly all the appreciation the stock earned this year on January's positive psoriasis data.

The structural problem is this. A prespecified subgroup is better than a post-hoc one — it means the hypothesis existed before the unblinding, and it means the assay and the cut point were fixed in advance, which are not small things. But prespecified is not the same as powered. A subgroup drawn from a four-hundred-eight-patient trial split across three doses and placebo, then filtered to interferon-high, then reported at a single dose, is a small number of patients per arm. And the company's mechanism for the overall failure — that the enriched population happened to be under-represented — is an explanation that cannot be tested with the data in hand. It might be exactly right. It is also precisely what you would say if the drug had a modest effect that surfaced only where you went looking hardest.

Which brings us to the precedent that actually governs this, and it is not a tyrosine kinase two precedent. It is anifrolumab, AstraZeneca's anti-interferon-alpha-receptor-one antibody, marketed as Saphnelo — the only approved therapy in lupus that is explicitly a type one interferon drug, and the only program that has run this exact experiment at Phase three scale with this exact biomarker. Two lessons come out of it, and they point in opposite directions.

The encouraging lesson is about endpoints. TULIP-one used SRI-four at week fifty-two as its primary endpoint and failed — including, notably, in the interferon gene signature-high subgroup, where the SRI-four response was a key secondary and did not differ from placebo. TULIP-two then promoted BICLA, which had been a secondary in TULIP-one, to primary, and hit: roughly forty-eight percent response versus about thirty-one and a half percent on placebo at one year. Same drug, same disease, different composite index, opposite outcome. Anifrolumab was approved on that basis. So in lupus, the choice of composite instrument is very nearly as consequential as the pharmacology, and a program that misses on one index is not automatically a dead program. Alumis has that history on its side, and it already ran BICLA as primary.

The discouraging lesson is the one that cuts directly at Alumis's stated rationale. Alumis's press release argues that interferon-high patients "typically respond more favorably to interferon pathway-targeted therapies and show lower placebo response rates." In TULIP-two, the interferon-high subgroup response was about forty-eight percent against a placebo rate of roughly thirty and a half percent — essentially indistinguishable from the overall population's forty-eight versus thirty-one and a half. In other words, in the closest available precedent, in the same disease, using the same class of biomarker, enriching for interferon-high neither lowered the placebo rate meaningfully nor widened the treatment delta. If that holds, then the interferon signature is a good marker of who has interferon-driven biology and a poor marker of who will show a bigger separation from placebo in a composite score. And a Phase three built on the premise that enrichment will manufacture separation is then built on the wrong premise.

I would hold that as the central uncertainty rather than a verdict, because there is a real counterargument: anifrolumab blocks the receptor for one cytokine family, while tyrosine kinase two inhibition sits at a node where interferon and interleukin twenty-three signaling converge, so the biology being selected for is not identical. But the burden of proof runs against the company here, and the regulators will know the anifrolumab history at least as well as the analysts do.

Zoom out to the class, because that is the read-through for anyone holding tyrosine kinase two exposure. Psoriasis and psoriatic arthritis remain the only ground where this mechanism has clearly won. Sotyktu is approved in plaque psoriasis, has positive Phase three data in psoriatic arthritis, and has commercially underperformed expectations. Bristol Myers's Phase two PAISLEY study in lupus was positive — significant on SRI-four at week thirty-two and on SRI-four, BICLA, low disease activity state, cutaneous index and joint counts at week forty-eight — and the two confirmatory Phase threes, POETYK-S-L-E-one and -two, about nine hundred eighty patients between them, are still running and have not reported. Takeda paid four billion dollars for Nimbus's tyrosine kinase two program and has positive Phase three psoriasis data, and the class has otherwise struggled in inflammatory bowel disease and in lupus. So as of yesterday the scoreboard reads: pathway validated, indication expansion unproven, and the single most informative remaining datapoint is Bristol's POETYK readout rather than anything Alumis does next. If POETYK hits, Alumis's enrollment-composition story becomes much more credible. If it misses, tyrosine kinase two in lupus is finished regardless of interferon status.

The competitive field around all of this is unusually crowded for a disease with almost nothing approved. Belimumab against B-cell activating factor and anifrolumab against the interferon receptor are the two approved biologics in systemic lupus. Obinutuzumab, an anti-C-D-twenty antibody, is approved in lupus nephritis. Telitacicept, a dual B-cell activating factor and A-P-R-I-L inhibitor, is approved in China and pursuing global registration. In late-stage development you have ianalumab against the B-cell activating factor receptor, dapirolizumab pegol against C-D-forty ligand, and cenerimod, an S-one-P-one modulator. And then there is the cell-therapy and T-cell-engager wave, which took its own hit on Monday when Novartis paused eight autoimmune trials of its C-D-nineteen C-A-R-T over three deaths from immune effector cell-associated hemophagocytic syndrome. So inside thirty-six hours lupus rejected an oral small molecule on efficacy and a cell therapy on safety — while Roche paid seventy-five million dollars upfront and up to one point five three billion for a C-D-seventy-nine-a-by-C-D-nineteen-by-C-D-three trispecific engager designed specifically to deplete B cells while limiting cytokine release. Read together, those three events say the same thing from three directions. What is missing in lupus is not another mechanism. It is an instrument that can be dosed to a defined immunological effect, verified, and stopped.

What this means for Alumis as a company is narrower and more concrete. It had five hundred two point three million dollars in cash, equivalents and marketable securities at June thirtieth, which is a real balance sheet, and management says it does not foresee significant changes to its overall development timeline. The near-term value is now almost entirely the psoriasis filing: positive Phase three ONWARD data, N-D-A on track for the fourth quarter. Beyond that, the pipeline is thinner than it looks. Last quarter Alumis took a forty-one point eight million dollar impairment on the lonigutamab intangible — the I-G-F-one-receptor antibody for thyroid eye disease it acquired in the ACELYRIN merger — after deciding to pursue strategic alternatives for the program. Which is a quiet irony worth noting: Alumis wrote down a thyroid eye disease asset roughly a quarter before Lilly agreed to pay up to two point eight seven five billion dollars for Merida, whose lead is a Phase one program in Graves' disease and thyroid eye disease. Different mechanisms — an antibody against the receptor versus selective degradation of the causal autoantibody — but the same lesson about which end of a disease the market is now willing to pay for. And chief executive Martin Babler's line that the company is "actively evaluating opportunities to maximize the value of our oral tyrosine kinase two portfolio" is not filler. In this context that sentence means partnering or divestiture is on the table.

Three things to watch. First, the full LUMUS dataset at a medical meeting — specifically the interferon-high response and placebo rates, the number of patients per arm in that subgroup, and how much of the reported separation lives at the top dose alone. Second, the outcome of the regulatory meeting, which is a genuinely novel question: will the F-D-A accept a biomarker-defined Phase three population justified by a prespecified but underpowered subgroup from a failed trial, and is the commercially available interferon gene signature assay robust enough to become a companion diagnostic? Those are two separate approvals hiding inside one plan. Third, POETYK, because Bristol will settle the class question with a properly powered trial regardless of what Alumis does.

The takeaway for the broader thesis is not really about Alumis. It is that precision immunology keeps promising to rescue efficacy through patient selection, and lupus keeps demonstrating that the biomarker which identifies the right biology is not necessarily the biomarker that produces a bigger number against placebo. Those are different claims. Programs that conflate them get repriced in a single session. Talk soon.
