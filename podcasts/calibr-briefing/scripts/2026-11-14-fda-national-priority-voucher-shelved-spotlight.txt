Today's spotlight is a program that's ending without anyone announcing it. On Thursday, Reuters reported that the F-D-A has shelved the Commissioner's National Priority Voucher pilot, the fast track Marty Makary launched in June of last year. Reuters cited six people familiar with the matter and internal documents. The agency hasn't confirmed it, and H-H-S says the pilot is ongoing. But the details line up, and the companies that hold vouchers, or were hoping for one, now have to plan around what is left. So let's go through what the program was, what it actually delivered, who is exposed, and what it means for planning a filing.

Start with the design. A national priority voucher promised a decision on a marketing application in one to two months, instead of the usual ten to twelve, or six under standard priority review. To qualify, a drug had to serve one of five national priorities: a public health crisis, an innovative breakthrough, a large unmet need, bringing manufacturing back onshore, or affordability. Review teams presented to a multidisciplinary council that the agency compared to a tumor board, and the final decision stayed with the relevant F-D-A center. That council is what made the program both fast and controversial. Its members were political appointees, including Tracy Beth Hoeg, the former acting drug center director, and Vinay Prasad, the former biologics chief. Both have since left the agency.

Now the record. Twenty-two drugs received a voucher. Twelve have been approved, five of them after Makary resigned in May, and there has been at least one rejection. Disc Medicine's bitopertin, which we covered last month, got a fast answer, and the answer was no. Nine drugs are still in the program. Reuters reports that no new vouchers have been awarded since May. The council has gone dormant, the program's top administrator has left, and career scientists have taken back control of review timelines for the remaining drugs. One source said companies were told the agency isn't moving the program forward but is keeping applications open, so a company can submit, and nothing happens.

One detail matters most for this audience. The two most recent voucher approvals, Revolution Medicines' Rasonque and Merck's oral P-C-S-K-nine inhibitor Lipfendra, were never vetted by the executive council, according to the documents Reuters saw. Rasonque, daraxonrasib, the RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibitor, is the program's showcase. It was approved in August for previously treated metastatic pancreatic cancer, more than six months early, on overall survival of thirteen point two months against six point seven on chemotherapy. If that approval moved without the political council, then the speed came from the review commitment and from career staff working the file, not from appointees. That's the most useful lesson in the whole episode. The pace was achievable inside the normal structure, given a strong dataset and an agency that had committed to move.

The criticism started on day one, and it hasn't changed. Holly Fernandez Lynch, a health policy professor at Penn, called the program "the worst of both worlds": a serious risk of political influence, with limited practical benefit, because many of the drugs would have qualified for an existing expedited pathway anyway. A former F-D-A reviewer told Pharmaceutical Technology that the tight timelines pushed selection toward "easy, low-hanging fruit" rather than high-profile first-in-class drugs. A large share of the first fifteen vouchers went to big pharma, many of them companies that had signed drug pricing agreements with the White House. Representative Jake Auchincloss has questioned whether the F-D-A ever had statutory authority to run the program. There was also the teplizumab episode in May. Sanofi reportedly asked for Tzield to be taken off the voucher track after Hoeg tried to overturn the review staff's recommendation to approve it. Rich Danker, a former H-H-S official, put it bluntly: the program is being quietly phased out.

Who is exposed? Not the companies that already have approvals. The ones to watch are the voucher holders with applications under review or about to be filed. Two psychedelic programs are the clearest cases: Compass Pathways' psilocybin, COMP-three-sixty, in treatment-resistant depression, and the Usona Institute's psilocybin in major depression. Both were filed in the past two weeks. R-B-C Capital Markets wrote on Thursday that there's been no official guidance, "nor is it clear the extent to which this may impact companies with an existing" voucher. For Compass, R-B-C called it "incremental risk to timelines" but said the impact "should be manageable." COMP-three-sixty has breakthrough designation, so it should get standard priority review anyway, and R-B-C kept its eighty-five percent probability of approval, because the data "should clear a traditional F-D-A review." Merck's sacituzumab tirumotecan, its Trop-two antibody-drug conjugate, also holds a voucher but hasn't been filed. For holders like these, the realistic downside is losing a few months, not losing the approval.

The second shoe is transparency. Fierce Pharma noticed the same day that the F-D-A hasn't posted a complete response letter to its public database in roughly three months. The last entry is a manufacturing-related letter to I-T-M for its radiopharmaceutical candidate in early August. Publishing rejection letters was another Makary initiative. A law firm's citizen petition in April argued it could breach confidentiality protections, and the agency's 2027 budget request asked Congress for explicit authority to keep doing it. The F-D-A says it remains committed to releasing redacted letters. In practice, though, investors and competitors have lost a window they had come to rely on, the agency's own account of why a rival's application failed, as opposed to the company's.

Now the politics, because this is about who runs the agency. The F-D-A has had no permanent commissioner since May. Heidi Overton's nomination has a committee vote scheduled for Wednesday. William Blair wrote on Thursday that there is "clearly uncertainty over whether a majority can be reached," given changes coming to the committee after the midterms. If she fails, they said, Sara Brenner could return as acting commissioner in 2027. The regulatory consultant Steven Grossman was more direct: "controversial nominations rarely advance during a lame-duck session," and her odds would be worse next year under a Democratic Senate. Democrats won both chambers in the midterms, and pharmaphorum noted the voucher program could become a target in a broader inquiry into the administration's most-favored-nation pricing deals, because some vouchers sat alongside those agreements. So the program isn't only dormant. It's a political liability for the next commissioner, whoever that is.

Here's the contrast that matters for planning. On the same day the Reuters story broke, BioNTech paid Scholar Rock two hundred five million dollars for a rare pediatric disease priority review voucher. Egetis has sold one for a hundred ninety million, and Ultragenyx sold one for two hundred ten million. Those vouchers come from a statutory program, they can be sold, and the law authorizes them through September thirtieth, 2029. The national priority voucher was none of those things. It couldn't be sold, it rested on no statute, and it depended on one commissioner's personal support. One went away when its sponsor left, and the other kept trading at two hundred million dollars. The F-D-A is also still launching new pilots. Arrowhead says its plozasiran filing in severe hypertriglyceridemia got into a new split real-time application review pilot. Pilots like that carry the same fragility.

So what should Foresite and Calibr take from this? Three things.

First, plan regulatory strategy around the tools written into law: breakthrough designation, priority review, accelerated approval, real-time oncology review, and the rare pediatric voucher if a program qualifies. Treat anything that rests on one leader's discretion as a possible bonus, never as the base case in a financing model. A company that put a two-month review into its cash runway should redo the numbers this week.

Second, the RevMed precedent holds up. The fastest approval in the program came on a large overall survival benefit in a disease with almost nothing else, and it apparently moved through career staff. The most dependable accelerant is still a dataset the reviewers don't need to argue about.

Third, watch Wednesday's vote. A confirmed commissioner could formally close the program, rebuild it with statutory backing, or replace it with something new. An acting commissioner through 2027 probably means more drift. Either way, the companies holding the nine pending vouchers deserve a call to ask what their F-D-A review teams are actually telling them about timelines, because the official line and the reported reality don't match yet.
