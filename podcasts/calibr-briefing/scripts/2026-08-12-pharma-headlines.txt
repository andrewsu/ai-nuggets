Good morning, Travis. Here are your biotech and pharma headlines for Wednesday, August twelfth. A surprise rejection in one of the hottest corners of oncology leads today, and the spotlight goes deep on it. Five items.

First, and leading today, a surprise from the FDA in radiopharmaceuticals. ITM — the German isotope specialist, Isotope Technologies Munich — disclosed that the agency handed it a complete response letter, a rejection, for its lead drug, a lutetium one-seventy-seven radioligand called ITM-eleven, in gastro-entero-pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors. Here is why it stung: the rejection had nothing to do with the data. The FDA raised no concerns about efficacy or safety — the drug's pivotal trial, COMPETE, had cleanly beaten Novartis's Afinitor, everolimus, on progression-free survival, roughly twenty-four months versus fourteen. The rejection was entirely about manufacturing — chemistry, manufacturing and controls, plus inspection items at a third-party commercial facility. In a field where the medicine literally decays over days and has to be built and shipped just in time, that is the whole ballgame, and it is today's spotlight. The immediate read: Novartis's Lutathera keeps its monopoly in these tumors a while longer.

Second, and the big one for your AI file, Novo Nordisk and Amazon Web Services signed a strategic partnership to accelerate drug discovery with AI. AWS becomes Novo's preferred cloud and AI partner, and the two are standing up a co-innovation hub in London, at Novo's King's Cross site, to compress the path from drug target to first human dose. The technical guts are what matter here — they are deploying agentic AI built on Amazon Bedrock and its AgentCore agent layer, plus a purpose-built tool called Amazon Bio Discovery that wires computational design to lab testing. Novo says more than twenty-five thousand of its employees already use Bedrock-powered tools. This is the largest drugmaker in the world betting that agentic AI, not just chatbots, becomes core infrastructure for discovery — and a marquee validation for the cloud-plus-agents thesis that names like Xaira are built on.

Third, a clean win for RNA medicine. Silence Therapeutics reported positive top-line Phase 2 data, the SANRECO trial, for divesiran in polycythemia vera — a blood cancer where the marrow overproduces red cells and patients need repeated bloodletting to stay safe. Divesiran is a first-in-class small interfering RNA that silences a gene suppressing hepcidin, the body's master iron regulator; dial hepcidin up, starve the marrow of iron, and red-cell overproduction falls. The numbers were striking — eighty-eight percent of patients responded versus nineteen percent on placebo, with phlebotomies cut from about two per patient to nearly zero, and a clean safety read. It sets up a Phase 3 and puts Silence in direct competition with Takeda's hepcidin-mimetic, rusfertide.

Fourth, a licensing deal in rare blood cancers. Sweden's Sobi is paying Innate Pharma seventy-five million dollars up front to license lacutamab, a first-in-class antibody targeting KIR3DL2, a marker on the malignant T cells in cutaneous T-cell lymphoma. The money unlocks the confirmatory Phase 3, called TELLOMAK-3, aimed at an accelerated-approval filing in Sézary syndrome — the aggressive leukemic form of the disease — with the more common mycosis fungoides to follow. It is a classic structure: a rare-disease specialist buying commercial reach while the biotech runs the trial.

And fifth, a rare-disease setback worth logging. BioMarin is discontinuing B-M-N four-oh-one across all indications after its Phase 3 in children with ENPP1 deficiency split its two co-primary endpoints. The drug significantly raised the plasma pyrophosphate biomarker, but produced no matching improvement on the radiographic skeletal measure that actually defines benefit in these kids. That ends the lead asset from BioMarin's roughly two-hundred-seventy-million-dollar Inozyme acquisition, and it is a clean reminder that a moved biomarker and a helped patient are not the same thing.
