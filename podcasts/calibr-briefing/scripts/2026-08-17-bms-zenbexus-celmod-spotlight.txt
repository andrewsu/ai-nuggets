Today's spotlight is on an approval that's easy to file under "another myeloma drug" and is actually two firsts stacked on top of each other — one about how the drug works, and one about how the FDA agreed to judge it. Late last week, Bristol Myers Squibb won accelerated approval for Zenbexus, generic name iberdomide, in combination with Johnson and Johnson's Darzalex Faspro and dexamethasone, for adults with multiple myeloma who've had at least one prior line of therapy. Let me take the two firsts in turn, because together they tell you where oncology drug development is heading.

Start with the molecule, because the class is new. Iberdomide is the first approved CELMoD — a cereblon E3 ligase modulator. To understand what that means, rewind to the drugs it descends from. Revlimid and Pomalyst — lenalidomide and pomalidomide — are the immunomodulatory drugs, the IMiDs, that have anchored myeloma treatment for two decades. They work by grabbing a protein called cereblon, which is part of the cell's ubiquitin ligase machinery — essentially the tagging system that marks proteins for destruction. When an IMiD binds cereblon, it redirects that machinery to destroy two transcription factors, Ikaros and Aiolos, that myeloma cells depend on to survive.

A CELMoD does the same fundamental thing — glue a disease-driving protein to the cell's degradation machinery — but it's engineered to do it far more efficiently: tighter binding to cereblon, deeper and faster destruction of those target proteins. This is targeted protein degradation as a deliberate design goal, rather than a lucky mechanism discovered after the fact. The field spent years reverse-engineering why thalidomide's descendants worked at all, and iberdomide is the first product built forward from that understanding. The practical bet Bristol is making is potency in patients whose disease has already learned to shrug off the older IMiDs.

Now the second first, which may matter more for the industry than the drug itself. The FDA granted this approval on the basis of MRD-negative complete response — minimal residual disease. In a complete response, standard tests can't find the cancer; MRD-negative means that even ultrasensitive testing, down to one myeloma cell in a hundred thousand or a million, comes up empty. In the pivotal Phase 3 trial of roughly nine hundred forty patients, forty-one percent of those on the iberdomide combination reached MRD-negative complete response, versus twenty-one percent on a standard Darzalex, Velcade and dexamethasone regimen — essentially double. And crucially, the agency accepted that depth-of-response readout as the basis for accelerated approval, rather than requiring the trial to first show longer progression-free or overall survival.

Why does that matter beyond this one drug? Because survival endpoints in myeloma now take years to mature — patients live a long time, through many lines of therapy — and that timeline has become a genuine bottleneck for bringing new drugs forward. MRD gives you a deep, biologically meaningful readout years earlier. Regulators have been circling this for a while; the FDA's oncology advisors endorsed MRD as a surrogate endpoint back in twenty twenty-four. But endorsement in principle and a first approval in hand are different things, and iberdomide is the drug that converted the principle into precedent. Every myeloma developer now has a faster, cheaper path to a first approval — with the catch that accelerated approval is conditional, and Bristol still has to confirm the clinical benefit with progression-free survival data, expected later this year.

Step back, and the strategic logic is almost tidy. Iberdomide was discovered by Celgene, which Bristol bought in twenty nineteen for seventy-four billion dollars — a deal built substantially on the myeloma franchise. That franchise is now under the patent cliff: Revlimid is already eroding to generics, and Pomalyst is next. Bristol's answer is to move the entire treatment backbone from IMiDs to CELMoDs — iberdomide first, and a second candidate, mezigdomide, under FDA review with a decision expected in May of next year. The plan is to have the successors approved and entrenched before the legacy drugs finish rolling off, and to run head-to-head trials that show the new class is genuinely better, not just newer.

The competitive reality is more crowded than that plan implies. Myeloma is arguably the most competitive tumor type in oncology — CAR-T cell therapies and T-cell-engaging bispecific antibodies are pushing into earlier lines and delivering their own deep responses. Iberdomide's pitch isn't to displace those; it's to be the convenient, oral, combinable backbone that the fancier modalities get layered on top of. It slots into a familiar triplet, taken as a once-daily capsule — exactly the kind of drug a community oncologist reaches for. Analyst Matt Phipps at William Blair projects that translates into more than a billion dollars in annual sales by twenty thirty-one, at a list price around twenty-nine thousand five hundred dollars per cycle. The trial's lead investigator, Sagar Lonial, framed it as the potential foundation of a new treatment regimen in myeloma — which is the marketing, but it's also the intent.

The caveats are real. This is accelerated, not full, approval — contingent on that confirmatory survival data. And the safety profile carries the class's baggage: because these drugs are thalidomide's molecular descendants, there's a black-box warning for birth defects, plus flagged cardiovascular risks that require monitoring. Commercial positioning won't fully resolve until the progression-free survival numbers land.

But the durable takeaway for anyone building in oncology is the endpoint story. A first-in-class targeted protein degrader is a nice headline. A regulator accepting MRD-negativity as the currency of approval is a structural change — it compresses the timeline from candidate to approval across an entire disease, and it's the kind of precedent that quietly reshapes which programs get funded and how trials get designed. That's the development to watch. The drug is Bristol defending its own franchise; the endpoint is everyone's new option.
