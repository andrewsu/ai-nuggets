Good morning Travis. Here are your biotech and pharma headlines for Saturday, October seventeenth, twenty twenty-six, covering Friday. The biggest story came out of Beijing. China proposed export controls that would stop foreign companies from taking ownership of Chinese cell and gene therapy and siRNA platforms, while leaving drug licensing deals open. That's the spotlight. Europe's drug committee also backed Lilly's oral GLP-1 and Revolution Medicines' pancreatic cancer drug, Roche won a second indication for an IL-6 receptor antibody in thyroid eye disease, Fractyl's duodenal procedure hit its first pivotal endpoint for keeping weight off after a GLP-1, and a Yale kidney gene therapy startup launched with a hundred and three million dollars. Seven items, then the week ahead.

First, the spotlight. China's Commerce and Science ministries published a draft revision of the country's list of technologies that are prohibited or restricted from export. It adds forty items across artificial intelligence, solar and biomedicine. Five are biomedical. Foreign ownership transfers of Chinese cell and gene therapy process platforms and siRNA process platforms would be banned outright, along with pathogen gain-of-function methods. Transfers of novel target discovery and validation technology in those two fields would need approval. The ministry says out-licensing, contract research and contract manufacturing are not covered, and licensing gets "automatic" approval. Bloomberg counted eighty-one Chinese licensing deals worth up to a hundred ten billion dollars in the first half of this year, so Beijing is protecting the deal flow while keeping the platforms at home. Comments are due November fifteenth, but a registration requirement took effect on Friday. The spotlight covers which deal structures this closes off and which ones it leaves open.

Second, Europe's Committee for Medicinal Products for Human Use, the C-H-M-P, wrapped its October meeting with six new medicines recommended. Two matter most. Foundayo is Lilly's orforglipron, a once-daily oral small-molecule agonist of the G-L-P-one receptor, recommended for weight management and type two diabetes. It's a pill with no food or water restrictions, competing with injectables and with Novo's oral semaglutide. Rasonque is Revolution Medicines' daraxonrasib, the RAS-on multi-selective inhibitor that blocks the active, G-T-P-bound form of mutant and wild-type RAS. It was recommended for metastatic pancreatic cancer after at least one prior therapy, following a phased review under the agency's high-priority cancer pathway. That's notable because the F-D-A's own review documents, which we covered last week, stirred debate over a weaker response rate in the G-twelve-D subgroup. Europe didn't let that hold it back. The committee also backed Ascendis' once-weekly navepegritide, a prodrug of C-type natriuretic peptide, for achondroplasia in children, and recommended extending Ionis and Sobi's Tryngolza to severe hypertriglyceridemia. Tryngolza is olezarsen, an antisense drug against apolipoprotein C-three. On the negative side, the committee upheld its refusal of Omeros' narsoplimab, a MASP-two antibody that blocks the lectin complement pathway, in transplant-associated thrombotic microangiopathy, even though the F-D-A approved it last December. Omeros responded by ending its European compassionate-use program, citing U-S demand. AstraZeneca also withdrew its bid to extend Truqap, the AKT inhibitor capivasertib, into PTEN-deficient hormone-sensitive prostate cancer.

Third, an F-D-A approval. Genentech's Enspryng, satralizumab, is now approved for thyroid eye disease, the first subcutaneous option a patient can inject at home. It's a Chugai-engineered IL-6 receptor antibody with pH-dependent "recycling" binding: it releases its target inside the cell, returns to circulation and binds again, which lets it be dosed every four weeks. The data are split. SatraGO-one missed its primary endpoint at week twenty-four. SatraGO-two hit, with proptosis response, meaning at least a two-millimeter reduction in eye bulging, of fifty-three percent versus twenty-three percent on placebo, and diplopia improvement of sixty-one versus twenty-six percent. Amgen's Tepezza, an I-G-F-one receptor antibody given by I-V infusion, is the incumbent. The pitch here is convenience and an upstream inflammatory mechanism, not better efficacy, and with one of two trials missing, payers will push on that. Enspryng has a priority review pending in MOGAD as well.

Fourth, obesity maintenance. Fractyl Health said the pivotal cohort of REMAIN-one met its six-month co-primary endpoint. The trial randomized three hundred eight people who had stopped G-L-P-one drugs to Revita or a sham procedure. Revita is an endoscopic procedure that ablates the lining of the duodenum to reset nutrient sensing. Revita patients kept about eighty percent of their G-L-P-one weight loss versus sixty-seven percent with sham, regaining five point one percent of body weight versus eight point two, with a p-value of point zero zero two three. There were no device-related serious adverse events. In a post hoc cut, people with longer ablations regained less, which supports a dose response. Fractyl plans a De Novo filing late this year and has a one-year co-primary readout due in the first quarter. The absolute gap is about three points of body weight. The question is whether payers see that as worth a procedure, versus simply staying on a cheaper oral G-L-P-one.

Fifth, a startup. Estuary Biotherapeutics launched with a hundred and three million dollars: a ninety-three-million-dollar Series A led by RA Capital, with GV, Deep Track, 4BIO and Biogen's venture arm, on top of a ten-million-dollar seed. It's built on work from Yale's Stefan Somlo and Michael Caplan showing that restoring polycystin can shrink cysts and reverse fibrosis in mouse models of autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease. EST-two-oh-one delivers full-length PKD-two. EST-one-oh-one delivers only a functional fragment of PKD-one, which is too large to fit in an A-A-V vector whole. Both are meant as one-time, locally administered kidney gene therapies, entering the clinic in twenty twenty-seven and twenty twenty-eight. The CEO named delivery as the "most crucial remaining barrier," and that is the bet. The precedent is Novartis buying Regulus last year for eight hundred million dollars for an oligonucleotide in the same disease.

Sixth, pipeline pruning and quality control. Novartis terminated its Phase two of Fabhalta, iptacopan, the oral factor B inhibitor of the alternative complement pathway, in lupus nephritis. It enrolled eighty-one patients, and the registry now says the study will end after Part one for "strategic considerations." No efficacy data were given. Separately, the F-D-A sent Eurofins a warning letter over its Horsham, Pennsylvania microbiology lab. Analysts there had been "selectively recording" pour-plate results since February twenty twenty-five, and the agency said none of the affected results can be confirmed after the fact. Any sponsor who used that lab for release testing should be checking its lot records.
