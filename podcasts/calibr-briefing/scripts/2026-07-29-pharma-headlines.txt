Four things today, and the spotlight goes deep on the one that has the whole small-cap oncology world holding its breath — Replimune's melanoma drug faces its F-D-A panel tomorrow, and the briefing documents that just posted are brutal.

First, and it's the spotlight, the F-D-A released its reviewers' briefing documents ahead of tomorrow's advisory committee meeting on Replimune's R-P-one, an oncolytic virus therapy for advanced melanoma, and the agency's staff came out swinging. They called the pivotal single-arm data "not interpretable," and in their own reanalysis they cut the response rate roughly in half — from the thirty-four percent Replimune reported down to about sixteen. The stock fell about twenty-eight percent the day the documents posted. The panel votes tomorrow, the agency's own decision is due August second, and I'll walk through exactly what the F-D-A objected to and why this vote matters far beyond one company in the spotlight.

Second, Incyte reported second-quarter earnings, and the more interesting part is what it's quietly building underneath the numbers. Earlier this month the company closed its roughly one-and-a-quarter-billion-dollar purchase of Vega Therapeutics, which brings in an antibody called latarcibart, now in Phase 3 for von Willebrand disease — the most common inherited bleeding disorder. The drug works by dialing down a natural brake on clotting, and early data showed something like an eighty percent reduction in bleeding across every subtype of the disease. It's a clear signal Incyte wants a hematology franchise that outlives its aging blood-cancer mainstay.

Third, a nice piece of science with real commercial teeth for the cell-therapy world. Researchers published a genome-wide analysis of two hundred thirty-six lymphoma patients treated with Kite's C-A-R-T therapy Yescarta and found genetic signatures that predict who suffers the dangerous side effects — the cytokine storms and the neurotoxicity that make these therapies so hard to give. One gene variant drove excess inflammatory signaling; another one appeared protective. The promise is that you could screen patients ahead of time, or even engineer the next generation of C-A-R-T cells to design that toxicity out. For anyone developing cell therapies, that's a roadmap toward a safer, more outpatient-friendly product.

Fourth, mark your calendar for Friday. The administration's one-hundred-percent Section two-thirty-two tariffs on patented, imported pharmaceuticals take effect July thirty-first for the first seventeen named large-cap drugmakers. The catch is that most of them have already negotiated their way out — companies that signed the most-favored-nation pricing agreements get a zero-percent rate that holds until twenty twenty-nine. So the headline number is dramatic, but the real story is that the tariff has become a lever to force pricing concessions and domestic-manufacturing commitments, not a blanket tax. Everyone else's clock starts September twenty-ninth.
