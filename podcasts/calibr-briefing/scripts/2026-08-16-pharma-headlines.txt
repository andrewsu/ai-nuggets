Good morning, Travis — this is your biotech and pharma briefing for Sunday, August sixteenth, twenty twenty-six. It's a quiet summer weekend, so today is about the stories reshaping the structure of the industry, plus the week ahead. Five items.

Number one, and today's spotlight: a federal judge has blocked the Pentagon from branding WuXi AppTec a Chinese military company. Chief Judge James Boasberg of the D.C. district court granted WuXi a preliminary injunction, freezing the Defense Department's so-called 1260H designation while the case plays out. The court found the listing was likely arbitrary and capricious — and one detail is devastating: the Pentagon's headline rationale was that WuXi is indirectly owned by the Chinese state, but the actual state-linked ownership stake turned out to be about one one-thousandth of one percent. WuXi gets roughly seventy percent of its revenue from the United States and serves more than a thousand U.S. clients, and the judge noted that customers and suppliers had already canceled contracts and moved business to rivals. I'll get into why this matters for every biotech that outsources its chemistry and manufacturing in the spotlight.

Number two: the data-integrity saga around Amgen's Tavneos has reached the market-removal stage. Tavneos is avacopan, an oral inhibitor of the C5a complement receptor, used for ANCA-associated vasculitis. The European Commission has revoked its marketing authorization across the European Union, after an investigation found that staff at the original developer, ChemoCentryx, altered outcomes for nine patients in the pivotal ADVOCATE trial — enough to flip the result from not significant to significant. The New England Journal of Medicine retracted the trial paper, and the FDA has proposed withdrawing the U.S. approval; Amgen has requested a public hearing to fight it. It's a hard reminder that a data problem can unwind an approval years later — Amgen paid three point seven billion dollars for this asset.

Number three, staying with China's rise: Akeso won approval from China's regulator for ivonescimab plus chemotherapy in first-line squamous lung cancer. Ivonescimab is a PD-1 and VEGF bispecific antibody, and here's the line that has oncologists talking — in the HARMONi-6 trial, it became the first regimen ever to beat a PD-1 inhibitor plus chemo on overall survival in a randomized Phase 3, in any tumor type. Summit Therapeutics holds the U.S. and European rights and is running confirmatory global trials. If that survival signal holds up outside China, it's a direct threat to the Keytruda franchise.

Number four: the first mRNA flu shot is now cleared in the U.S. The FDA approved Moderna's mFlusiva for seasonal influenza in adults fifty and older — full approval in the fifty-to-sixty-four group, and accelerated approval, contingent on a confirmatory study, in those sixty-five and up. In trials it was about twenty-seven percent more effective than a standard flu shot, and Moderna expects it available for the coming season. It's a notable vote of confidence in mRNA at a moment when vaccine policy in Washington is turbulent.

Number five, the week ahead — four FDA decisions worth watching. Tomorrow, Merck faces a decision on expanding subcutaneous Keytruda, its Qlex formulation of the anti-PD-1 antibody pembrolizumab, across most solid-tumor indications — a lifecycle move to defend the franchise ahead of biosimilars. On Friday the twenty-second, the same day as Capricor's Duchenne decision, Savara has a decision on Molbreevi, an inhaled GM-CSF for the rare lung disease autoimmune pulmonary alveolar proteinosis. On the twenty-fifth, Jazz has a decision on Ziihera — that's zanidatamab, a HER2-targeted bispecific — in HER2-positive biliary tract cancer. And on the twenty-seventh, Gilead faces a decision on its combination of bictegravir and lenacapavir, pairing an integrase inhibitor with its first-in-class capsid inhibitor, for HIV.
