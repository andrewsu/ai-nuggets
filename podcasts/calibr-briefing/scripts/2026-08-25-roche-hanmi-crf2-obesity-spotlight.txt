This is your spotlight for Tuesday, August twenty-fifth, twenty twenty-six. Roche, Hanmi, and a hundred ninety million dollars spent on a receptor almost nobody in obesity is working on.

Here are the mechanics before the argument. Genentech, on behalf of Roche, has taken an exclusive worldwide license — everything except South Korea — to Hanmi Pharmaceutical's H-M seventeen three twenty-one, for obesity and the conditions that travel with it, type two diabetes and cardiovascular disease. A hundred ninety million dollars up front. Up to two point three billion dollars in development, regulatory and commercial milestones. Tiered royalties on net sales. Hanmi finishes the phase one it is running now, roughly ninety subjects, healthy volunteers and people with obesity, safety, tolerability, pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics. Genentech takes the wheel at phase two.

Now the molecule, because the molecule is the whole story. H-M seventeen three twenty-one is a long-acting analog of urocortin-two, an endogenous peptide in the corticotropin-releasing-factor family. It is selective for the second C-R-F receptor, C-R-F-two, and that selectivity is the design. C-R-F-one is the classic stress-axis receptor in the pituitary — the one you do not want to touch. C-R-F-two is a different distribution entirely, and the tissue that matters here is skeletal muscle, where C-R-F-two signaling is anabolic. There is a second arm in adipose and in the periphery that pushes energy expenditure and lipolysis. So the pharmacological proposition is: activate a receptor that tells muscle to grow and fat to burn, and do it without going anywhere near the incretin system.

That last clause is the point. Every drug that matters commercially in this field right now — semaglutide, tirzepatide, retatrutide, petrelintide, the orals — works, at bottom, by making you eat less. Appetite suppression produces weight loss, and weight loss in a human being who is eating less is not selective. You lose fat and you lose muscle in roughly the proportions the body loses them in any caloric deficit. The number that gets quoted is something like twenty-five to forty percent of lost mass being lean tissue, depending on the trial and the imaging method. For a forty-year-old that is a cosmetic and functional footnote. For a seventy-two-year-old with sarcopenia already underway, or for anyone who is going to stop the drug and regain, it is a real clinical problem, because the fat comes back and the muscle does not.

The field's answer to that, so far, has been to block the brakes on muscle. Myostatin and activin are negative regulators of muscle mass; block them and muscle stops being catabolized. Lilly bought Versanis for about one point nine billion dollars to get bimagrumab, an antibody against the activin type two receptor, and in the BELIEVE trial bimagrumab plus semaglutide produced twenty-two point one percent weight loss at seventy-two weeks against fifteen point five for semaglutide alone, with ninety-two point seven percent of the lost weight coming from fat. That is a striking result — and Lilly has since halted the phase two-b combination with tirzepatide in obesity with type two diabetes for what it called strategic business reasons, while continuing the non-diabetic study. Regeneron's trevogrumab, an anti-myostatin antibody, is in COURAGE alongside semaglutide, reading out late this year. And Scholar Rock — which you will recall from yesterday's briefing for entirely different reasons, its Catalent manufacturing problem — showed in EMBRAZE that apitegromab on top of tirzepatide produced twelve point three percent weight loss at twenty-four weeks with only fourteen point six percent of the lost mass being lean, versus thirty point two percent for tirzepatide plus placebo. About one point nine kilograms of muscle preserved.

Hold those three results next to each other and you see what they have in common. They are all adjuncts. Each is an antibody you add to an incretin, whose job is to change the composition of somebody else's weight loss. None of them is a weight-loss drug on its own, and none of them adds muscle — they prevent its removal.

H-M seventeen three twenty-one claims to be a different kind of object. In diet-induced obese mice, Hanmi's data show weight loss comparable to semaglutide, with more of it coming from fat, and with lean mass going up rather than merely holding — plus reduced intramuscular lipid, which is a quality-of-muscle claim, not just a quantity one. In obese rhesus monkeys, selective fat reduction with lean mass preserved. And in combination with semaglutide, greater total weight loss and greater fat loss than semaglutide alone while lean mass rose. Hanmi also ran it with its own triple agonist. So the pitch to Roche was: this works as monotherapy, it works as a combination partner, and as a peptide it can go into a fixed-dose combination pen rather than requiring a second injection.

Why Roche, and why now. Roche has been assembling a metabolic franchise by acquisition for three years — two point seven billion dollars for Carmot Therapeutics to get an incretin portfolio, then one point six five billion dollars up front to Zealand for petrelintide, the amylin analog, in a deal worth up to five point three billion. The company has said publicly it intends to be a top-three player in obesity. The problem with that ambition is arithmetic. Lilly and Novo have the two best incretins, enormous manufacturing head starts, and prescriber preference data that is now completely lopsided — the Spherix survey we covered Sunday had eighty percent of prescribers highly satisfied with Zepbound. You do not become the third player in a two-horse race by bringing a fourth incretin. You become relevant by owning an axis the leaders do not own, and then selling it back to them as a combination, or defending your own combination with it. Boris Zaitra's framing — selectively reduce fat mass while improving muscle mass — is exactly that positioning. In-Young Choi at Hanmi put it as the paradigm moving beyond simple weight reduction toward body composition and metabolic health. Both of those sentences are strategy statements about differentiation, not efficacy statements.

Now the case against, and there are three arguments.

The first is simply the stage. A hundred ninety million dollars is a large up-front for an asset with zero human efficacy data. Hanmi got its I-N-D cleared in November of last year; the phase one is still recruiting healthy volunteers. Roche is paying a preclinical price on a preclinical package, and the mouse-to-human translation record in muscle biology is not encouraging — myostatin blockade has been producing beautiful mouse data since the late nineties, and it took until this decade to find a human context where it mattered. Note also the shape of the deal: eight percent of the headline number is real money, ninety-two percent is contingent. That is a call option written on a mechanism, and the industry has been unusually willing this year to structure early bets as options rather than acquisitions.

The second argument is the receptor's other job, and this is the one I would put at the top of the diligence list. C-R-F-two is a cardiovascular receptor. Urocortin-two is an arterial vasodilator with positive inotropic and lusitropic effects on the left ventricle. It reduces systemic vascular resistance, it lowers arterial pressure, it increases cardiac output, and in the animal work it increases heart rate. This is not incidental pharmacology — urocortin-two was developed as a heart failure drug on exactly those properties. The UNICORN study infused it in acute decompensated heart failure and reported clear hemodynamic effects, tolerable, with cardiac output markedly augmented. There are long-acting C-R-F-two agonists in preclinical development right now for pulmonary hypertension and right heart failure. So the question for a chronic, long-acting, systemic C-R-F-two agonist given to a large population of people with obesity — a population that is already enriched for hypertension and heart failure with preserved ejection fraction — is whether you can get the muscle effect without the hemodynamic one. Tachycardia is the specific thing to watch. The field has been here before: the reason a whole generation of thermogenic and sympathomimetic weight-loss agents died was resting heart rate. If the phase one shows a persistent rise in heart rate at doses that move body composition, this program has a very hard road, regardless of how good the D-E-X-A scans look. Conversely, if heart rate is flat, Roche looks smart.

The third argument is regulatory, and it is the one the entire muscle-preservation field shares. There is no approval pathway for body composition. The F-D-A does not approve drugs for a favorable ratio of fat to lean tissue on imaging. It approves them for weight reduction, or for a clinical outcome. Which means every company in this lane eventually has to show that the preserved or added muscle produces something a regulator recognizes — physical function, strength, fewer falls, less weight regain after discontinuation, better glycemic control. Nobody has done that yet. Bimagrumab's twenty-two percent weight loss is the one result in the class that would stand on its own as a weight number, and that is the program Lilly just partially pulled back from. If H-M seventeen three twenty-one produces meaningful weight loss by itself in humans, the pathway is conventional and easy. If it produces modest weight loss with great composition, Roche has bought a drug that needs a new endpoint, and that is a much more expensive proposition than two point three billion dollars in milestones.

One more data point on validation. Hanmi is not alone at this receptor. Gubra, the Danish peptide house, has taken its own urocortin-two program into phase one-two-a for obesity. Two independent groups arriving at C-R-F-two at the same time is weak evidence that the biology is real and strong evidence that the window for exclusivity in the mechanism is already narrowing.

So how should you hold this. I think it is the most interesting obesity deal of the quarter, and I think it is genuinely high-risk in a way the press release does not convey. The strategic logic is unimpeachable: the incretin war is over on efficacy and is now being fought on tolerability, on cost, on oral formulation, and on what exactly comes off the body. Roche cannot win the first three and has correctly identified the fourth as the open axis. The mechanism is more ambitious than the antibodies — it is trying to be both the weight-loss drug and the muscle drug, which is the only version of this that ever becomes a standalone product rather than a permanent adjunct. And the entire thesis will be adjudicated by a small phase one in healthy volunteers, on endpoints that are not efficacy endpoints at all. Watch that readout. Watch heart rate and blood pressure at the top dose, watch whether the exposure that produces a body-composition signal is anywhere near the exposure that produces a hemodynamic one, and watch whether Genentech starts a monotherapy phase two or goes straight to a semaglutide combination — because that choice will tell you what Roche actually believes it bought.
