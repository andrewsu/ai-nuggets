Good morning Travis. Here are your biotech and pharma headlines for Sunday, August thirtieth, twenty twenty-six.

It is the middle weekend of the European Society of Cardiology congress in Munich, and the business wire is essentially empty — no deals, no approvals, no financings since Friday. So today is a cardiology day, and it is a substantial one. Over the last thirty hours the congress has delivered the definitive autopsy on the biggest anticoagulant program in the industry, the first large randomized answer on statins in people over seventy, a first-in-class oral drug with a real signal from AstraZeneca, and four separate trials whose main contribution is telling the field that something everyone assumed was true is not. Ten items, then what is still to come today.

First, and today's spotlight. Milvexian, the oral factor eleven-a inhibitor that Bristol Myers Squibb and Johnson and Johnson are jointly developing, failed to reduce cardiovascular events after acute coronary syndrome in the final results of LIBREXIA A-C-S, presented in a hotline session yesterday and published simultaneously in the New England Journal of Medicine. Fourteen thousand one hundred ninety-four patients, eight hundred ninety-three sites, forty-four countries, randomized to twenty-five milligrams twice daily or placebo within seven days of an acute coronary syndrome, on top of whatever antiplatelet therapy the investigator chose. After a median twelve point two months, cardiovascular death, heart attack or ischemic stroke occurred in five point four percent on milvexian and five point one percent on placebo — a hazard ratio of one point zero five, p equals zero point five zero. No difference in any component, no difference in all-cause death. The trial had already been stopped for futility last November, so the number itself is not new; what is new is the full dataset and the discussion around it, and that is where it gets interesting. The bleeding results were completely clean — intracranial or fatal bleeding at zero point three percent in both arms — and the drug was demonstrably active, with trough clotting times prolonged about one point six fold at four weeks. Principal investigator Gabriel Steg of Hôpital Bichat in Paris offered three explanations and the discussant, Marc Sabatine of the TIMI Study Group at Harvard, offered a sharper question. The spotlight goes through all of it, plus what it does and does not say about the two ongoing trials that actually carry the commercial value.

Second, and this is this morning's news. AstraZeneca's oral relaxin agonist, A-Z-D five four six two, showed a real pharmacologic signal in chronic heart failure in the LUMINARA phase two-b trial, presented a few hours ago and published simultaneously in Circulation. Relaxin is the pregnancy hormone that relaxes the vasculature; the receptor has been chased for fifteen years and every previous attempt — most famously serelaxin — either failed or ran into problems that the investigators now attribute to supra-physiological dosing. This is the first oral molecule at that receptor. Three hundred seventy-five patients across sixty-nine sites in ten countries, randomized to placebo or twenty, eighty, or three hundred sixty milligrams once daily, with two separate cohorts and two separate primary endpoints. In the reduced-ejection-fraction cohort, end systolic volume index — a measure of adverse remodeling — fell five point four milliliters per square meter at twenty-four weeks, with a p value of zero point zero five four. In the mildly-reduced cohort, systemic vascular resistance fell fifteen to twenty-one percent across all three doses, all statistically significant. Here is the detail that matters: the effect was largest at the lowest dose. Presenter James Januzzi of the Baim Institute and Mass General framed that as vindication of the dosing hypothesis — the earlier relaxin failures were an overdose problem, not a target problem. No excess adverse events, mild blood pressure lowering, and specifically no volume overload of the kind seen with higher-dose relaxin drugs. AstraZeneca funded it and Januzzi called for outcome trials. Read it as a target rehabilitated, not a drug de-risked — a p value of zero point zero five four on a surrogate is a permission slip, not a result.

Third, the biggest population-health number of the weekend. STAREE, the first large randomized trial of statins for primary prevention in people over seventy, cut major cardiovascular events by thirty percent. Nine thousand nine hundred seventy-one Australians aged seventy and up with no cardiovascular disease, no diabetes and no dementia, randomized to forty milligrams of atorvastatin or placebo, followed a median five point nine years. Major cardiovascular events: six point zero percent versus eight point three percent, hazard ratio zero point seven zero, p less than zero point zero zero one. Published in the New England Journal of Medicine. But there were two co-primary endpoints, and the second one missed — disability-free survival, meaning survival free of dementia and physical disability, was twelve point eight versus thirteen point six percent, not significant. Muscle, liver and diabetes-related adverse events were more common on statin. Sophia Zoungas of Monash is calling for guideline changes. The honest reading is that you prevent heart attacks and strokes in this population without measurably changing how long people stay independent, which is the outcome geriatricians actually care about.

Fourth, a pragmatic trial with a methodology story attached. SWITCH SWEDEHEART randomized seven Swedish healthcare regions — not patients, regions — to switch their default P2Y-twelve inhibitor from ticagrelor to prasugrel at staggered times, then captured all seventeen thousand ninety-five consecutive acute coronary syndrome patients undergoing stenting through the national registry. Death, heart attack or stroke at one year: eleven point one percent under prasugrel versus eleven point eight under ticagrelor, not significantly different. Major bleeding was lower on prasugrel — four point two versus four point four percent, odds ratio zero point eight zero. Also in the New England Journal. Elmir Omerovic's second point is the one worth stealing: they ran a seventeen-thousand-patient randomized trial inside a registry at a fraction of the cost of a conventional trial, in a completely unselected population including the elderly and patients on oral anticoagulants who are normally excluded. That is a template, and it is going to get copied.

Fifth, a clean negative that pushes back on a fashionable idea. PREMIUM tested whether you can skip aspirin entirely and start prasugrel monotherapy at the time of primary stenting for a S-T-elevation heart attack. Two thousand two hundred eighty patients, sixty-nine Japanese centers, funded by Boston Scientific. Noninferiority was not met — the composite of death, heart attack or stroke at twelve months was eleven point zero percent on monotherapy versus eight point five percent on standard dual therapy, hazard ratio one point three four. Bleeding was lower on monotherapy, five point six versus eight point four percent, but because noninferiority failed that was not formally tested. Interestingly the excess was not stent thrombosis, which was similar — it was non-stent events elsewhere in the coronary tree. Gaku Nakazawa's conclusion: you need dual therapy for at least the first month. The de-escalation movement just found its floor.

Sixth, Amgen gets a target-attainment win and nothing more. AMUNDSEN randomized two thousand one hundred sixty-one high-risk heart attack patients in the catheterization lab, before the procedure, to standard care plus evolocumab — Repatha, the P-C-S-K-nine antibody — or standard care alone, for a year. Eighty-two percent of the evolocumab arm hit the guideline L-D-L target of under fifty-five with at least a fifty percent reduction, versus forty percent on standard care; median L-D-L at six weeks was sixteen versus fifty-six. But death or unplanned cardiovascular hospitalization at one year was fourteen point six versus fifteen point four percent, not different. The trial was powered for the surrogate, not the outcome, so this is not a failure — but it is the eighth or ninth time the field has shown that giving a P-C-S-K-nine antibody on day one gets the number down and does not visibly change what happens in the following twelve months.

Seventh and eighth, two Danish trials that closed old questions. DANHEART had a factorial design and both halves reported yesterday and today. Met-HeFT randomized nine hundred forty heart failure patients with diabetes or prediabetes to metformin or placebo over a mean three point seven years: no difference on death, worsening heart failure, heart attack or stroke — twenty-five point one versus twenty-three point four percent, hazard ratio one point one zero. A companion meta-analysis across heart failure and ischemic heart disease agreed. Metformin lowers glucose; it does not have an independent cardioprotective effect. And H-HeFT tested hydralazine plus isosorbide dinitrate — the combination that cut mortality forty-three percent in Black patients in A-HeFT two decades ago and has never been tested more broadly — in five hundred ninety-two patients. No significant difference on the primary composite, hazard ratio zero point nine zero, with high treatment discontinuation. All-cause death was nineteen point four versus twenty-four point two percent, hazard ratio zero point seven two, confidence interval crossing one at one point zero two. Lars Køber's line is that a mortality benefit would need a new trial. A meta-analysis including A-HeFT does show a mortality reduction, but with enough heterogeneity that nobody is willing to conclude anything.

Ninth, the trial that arguably helps the most patients. TIME-HF cluster-randomized twenty-two centers in India, fifteen hundred seven patients with reduced ejection fraction, to a nurse-coordinated collaborative care model with a mobile app or to usual care. Adherence to all four guideline-directed drug classes at two years was thirty-seven point three percent in the intervention arm versus twenty-two point one percent in usual care. Probability of surviving two years without hospitalization: eighty-four point zero versus seventy-nine point four percent. And a twenty-two percent reduction in deaths, p equals zero point zero two eight. Published in Circulation, funded by the Wellcome Trust India Alliance. A mortality benefit from a delivery model, in a setting where fifty-seven percent of patients live rurally, at a cost structure a low-income health system can carry.

Tenth, two housekeeping items with real reach. PRESC1SE-MI — a stepped-wedge trial across twenty hospitals in eleven countries and sixty-seven thousand six hundred twenty-four emergency department presentations — found that the rapid zero-slash-one-hour troponin pathway is as safe as the older zero-slash-three-hour pathway, one point one versus one point two percent for death or new heart attack at thirty days, but did not shorten emergency department stay at all. Median length of stay: three hundred nine minutes in both arms. A meta-analysis across five trials and a hundred ten thousand presentations said the same. In Jasper Boeddinghaus's words, the bottleneck is specialist review, admissions workflow and bed availability, not the assay. And separately, the Fifth Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction landed — a joint European, American College, American Heart Association and World Heart Federation document that replaces five numbered infarct types with three clinical categories, primary, secondary, and procedure-related, adds sex-specific troponin thresholds, and brings I-C-D-eleven coding including a dedicated code for spontaneous coronary artery dissection. If you run trials with a heart attack endpoint, your adjudication charter needs rewriting.
