This is your spotlight for Thursday, October eighth. Today: Biohub's one point eight billion dollar Virtual Biology Initiative, and why the biggest coordinated bet yet on A-I for cell biology is paying for measurements rather than models.

Start with what was announced. On Wednesday Biohub, the nonprofit research institute funded by Mark Zuckerberg and Priscilla Chan, announced with the Department of Energy and the National Institutes of Health what it calls the largest coordinated commitment to generating A-I-ready biological data to date. The stated total is one point eight billion dollars in funding, data, computation and measurement technology. The goal is an open resource that lets anyone train models predicting how a human cell responds to a drug, a mutation or a disease, the virtual cell.

The structure matters, because the total mixes very different kinds of money. Biohub's own five hundred million dollars, announced in April, is the anchor: four hundred million for measurement technology, meaning cryo-electron tomography that resolves near-atomic detail inside cells, microscopy that can image millions to billions of cells in living tissue, and tools to perturb biology from molecules up to whole organisms, plus a hundred million for outside labs. Google DeepMind, its drug-discovery sister company Isomorphic Labs, and Meta are putting in three hundred million together. The Energy Department commits more than five hundred million over five years through the Genesis Mission, the administration's A-I-for-science program, and that includes its exascale supercomputers, beamlines, cryo-E facilities and autonomous labs. The N-I-H contribution is different. It will coordinate datasets, repositories and knowledge bases built with more than five hundred million dollars of earlier federal spending, and Biohub will standardize them for model training. So roughly a quarter of the headline is existing public data being reorganized, and part of the D-O-E piece is facility time. That doesn't make it smaller in practice, since standardizing existing data is real work, but new cash is well under one point eight billion.

Around the money there's a scientific coalition: the Allen Institute, the Broad, Gladstone, the Human Cell Atlas, the Human Protein Atlas and the Wellcome Sanger Institute, with Nvidia on compute and Renaissance Philanthropy raising more funds for data generation. Biohub's head of science is Alex Rives, who started the E-S-M protein language model project at Meta and whose company, EvolutionaryScale, joined Biohub last year. Rives called the virtual cell one of the most important challenges for the next era of science. Pushmeet Kohli, who runs A-I for science at Google DeepMind, said "we will not solve this challenge without open, experimental biological data at an unprecedented scale." Coverage of the launch puts the first large dataset about a year out and working predictive models about five years out.

Now the main point. Why did the money go to data? The usual comparison is AlphaFold, and it explains why. AlphaFold worked because the Protein Data Bank had already collected decades of carefully determined structures, one shared format, publicly available. Nobody had to generate the training set. Nothing like that exists for how cells respond to interventions. There are big single-cell atlases, but an atlas describes cells at rest, and a predictive model needs cause and effect: this cell type, this perturbation, this dose, this time point, this outcome. At the April launch, Biohub said a high-accuracy model would need orders of magnitude more of that data than exists.

The field already has evidence for that. In August twenty twenty-five, Constantin Ahlmann-Eltze, Wolfgang Huber and Simon Anders published a Nature Methods benchmark testing five single-cell foundation models, including scGPT and scFoundation, plus the graph model GEARS, on predicting gene-expression changes after single and double genetic perturbations. None beat deliberately simple linear baselines. That result has become a reference point for skeptics. Either the architectures are wrong or the training data can't teach what we want, and this initiative is betting on the second. It's a reasonable bet, but it's still a bet. If five years of data generation produce models that still can't beat a linear baseline on held-out cell types and perturbations, that's an answer too, and an expensive one. Watch whether the initiative commits early to a public benchmark with properly held-out cell types and perturbations, the way CASP did for protein structure. Without one, a data commons can grow for years while every group reports wins on its own test split.

Next, the strategic question: why would three companies that compete on models fund an open commons? The simplest answer is commoditizing the complement. If the training data is a shared public good, the edge moves to what DeepMind, Isomorphic and Meta already have, which is model architecture, compute and talent. They gain from making the input cheap and standardized for everyone, because their models can take more advantage of it than anyone else's. Isomorphic's president Max Jaderberg said the data needed is beyond "what any single organization can produce today," which is the same point from the company side. Two things to read when details come out. First, access terms. The release says open, but one secondary report says companies that help generate data get about a year of exclusive access before public release. That isn't in the Biohub release, and if it's true, it's worth a great deal. Second, who is missing. No large pharma company is a named funder. The only drug developer in the group is Isomorphic. Pharma companies have the richest perturbation data in the world, their screening archives, and none is putting it in yet.

Then the competitive landscape, which is where this hits the portfolio. Several well-funded companies have pitched proprietary cell-response data at scale as their moat. Recursion built its company on phenomic screening. Tahoe Therapeutics built a hundred-million-cell drug-perturbation atlas with Arc Institute. On the same day as the Biohub news, Rivercell in Paris launched with a twenty-five million dollar seed round led by H-V to build an automated wet lab and its own perturbation dataset. Its chief science officer, Eric Durand, comes from Novartis and Owkin, and Fabian Theis and Charlotte Bunne are on its advisory board. Rivercell's release says it outright: AlphaFold had decades of public data, cells don't, so we'll generate it ourselves. That's a fine thesis today. It's less clearly a moat once a government-and-big-tech consortium is producing the same kind of data in the open at many times the scale.

Xaira, which Foresite Labs launched with ARCH in twenty twenty-four with over a billion dollars, is the most interesting case. Xaira has already gone partly open. Last year it released X-Atlas Orion, a genome-wide Perturb-seq dataset of about eight million cells and then the largest public one. That suggests Xaira decided bulk perturbation data was worth more as a field-wide standard it shapes than as a private asset. The Biohub expansion supports that call. Bulk perturbation data is turning into a public utility. What stays defensible is closed-loop data tied to your own molecules, your own targets and your own patient samples, measured in assays the commons won't run. That's the same conclusion as last week's X-Design spotlight: generating binders is becoming a commodity, but choosing the epitope and understanding the target biology is not. For any Foresite company whose pitch rests on having more single-cell data than the next company, it's worth asking how that pitch holds up in two years.

For Calibr-Skaggs the read is different. An institute that runs compound screens, including a drug-repurposing library of about twelve thousand compounds, is exactly the kind of data source this initiative needs, and a coalition asking the "worldwide scientific community to join" is effectively asking for perturbation data with known chemistry attached. Being a contributor brings influence over standards and early familiarity with the data. Staying out keeps control of assay design. That's worth deciding deliberately rather than by default.

Last, where this could fall short. There are three risks. First, the open problem in this field has never been data volume alone. It's whether a model generalizes to cell types and contexts it hasn't seen, and that depends on how the data is designed, not how much there is. Second, a coalition this broad has to agree on shared standards, identifiers and a single access point, and the release describes that layer as still being built. Third, the five-year model timeline outlasts most of today's leadership, and a Genesis Mission line item depends on future appropriations. The useful signal over the next year isn't the dollar figure. It's whether the first dataset ships on schedule, how open it really is, and whether a held-out benchmark comes with it.

So the takeaway. The Virtual Biology Initiative treats the virtual cell as a data problem, not a modeling problem, and pays to generate the data in the open at a scale no startup can match. That raises the floor for everyone, makes bulk perturbation data less valuable as a moat, and pushes the value toward closed-loop data linked to real molecules and patients. For Foresite's A-I discovery companies, the question is whether their data advantage is in what this commons won't measure. That's your spotlight.
