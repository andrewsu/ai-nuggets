Good morning, Travis. It's Sunday, November fifteenth, 2026. The trade press went quiet after Friday afternoon, so this briefing picks up what landed late Friday and over the weekend. Yesterday's episode didn't have room for it. The F-D-A approved the first drug for a kidney disease that has never had one. Taiho finished its first-line lung cancer filing for zipalertinib. And the neuro-oncology meeting produced four-year data on a pediatric brain tumor drug. Here's what you need to know.

First, a first-ever approval in nephrology. On Friday the F-D-A approved Roche's Gazyva, the anti-C-D-twenty antibody obinutuzumab, for adults with primary membranous nephropathy who are at risk of progression. In that disease, autoantibodies attack the kidney's filters and patients leak large amounts of protein into their urine. No drug had ever been approved for it in the U-S or Europe. Doctors have relied on off-label rituximab, calcineurin inhibitors and cyclophosphamide. In the Phase three MAJESTY trial, thirty-seven percent of patients on Gazyva were in complete remission at two years, against six percent on tacrolimus. This is today's spotlight, including why the trial didn't test Gazyva against rituximab, the drug most nephrologists actually use.

Second, lung cancer. Taiho Oncology has completed the new drug application it began last month for zipalertinib with platinum chemotherapy as first-line treatment for non-small cell lung cancer with E-G-F-R exon twenty insertion mutations. Zipalertinib is an irreversible oral E-G-F-R inhibitor built for the exon twenty insertion variants that older E-G-F-R drugs largely miss, and Taiho co-develops it with Cullinan Therapeutics. The filing rests on the REZILIENT-three Phase three we covered from the world lung conference in September: a progression-free survival hazard ratio of zero point five, but serious treatment-related side effects in about four of five patients and a two percent rate of fatal treatment-related sepsis. It went in under real-time oncology review but gets a standard review clock. The F-D-A is already reviewing zipalertinib for previously treated patients, and the first-line bar is J-and-J's infused Rybrevant plus chemotherapy. Zipalertinib's pitch is that it's a pill.

Third, from the Society for Neuro-Oncology meeting, which wraps up today. On Saturday, Servier presented four-year results from FIREFLY-one, the Phase two trial of Ojemda, tovorafenib. It's a pan-RAF kinase inhibitor for relapsed B-RAF-altered low-grade glioma in children. The response rate held at fifty-three percent. The new finding concerns what happens when children stop the drug as planned. Of forty-one who entered observation, seventy-six percent stayed off treatment for at least a year, and fifty-four percent for at least two. The median time to next treatment was about fifty-one months. The main serious side effect was slower growth, in about a third of children, and eighty-eight percent of those recovered once treatment stopped. Daniel Landi of Duke said the ultimate hope is to stop therapy for many of these patients, and the dataset backs it.

Fourth, Novartis has a label-expansion case for Rhapsido, remibrutinib, its oral B-T-K inhibitor for hives. In the Phase three RemIND study in chronic inducible urticaria, meaning hives triggered by scratching, by cold or by body heat, Rhapsido beat placebo within two weeks in all three subtypes. In cold urticaria, sixty-seven percent responded against twenty-nine percent on placebo. The effect held through fifty-two weeks. Rhapsido is already approved in the U-S for chronic spontaneous urticaria and for the scratch-triggered form, and Novartis now plans to take the cold and heat-triggered data to regulators.

Fifth, a peanut allergy readout. Aravax reported that its Phase two trial of P-V-X-one-oh-eight met its primary endpoint in children aged four to eleven. P-V-X-one-oh-eight is a monthly injection into the skin of short peanut-protein peptides. The idea is to retrain the T cells that drive the allergy without giving enough whole protein to trigger the antibodies that cause reactions. After a year, the amount of peanut children could tolerate rose two point four-fold on drug against one point two-fold on placebo. About thirty-nine percent tolerated a cumulative four hundred forty-three milligrams of peanut protein, against seventeen percent on placebo. It's a small, early study, but the dosing is far lighter than daily oral immunotherapy.

Sixth, the I-P-O market got a good result. Windward Bio closed its first day of trading on Friday sixty-eight percent above its seventeen-dollar I-P-O price, according to Bloomberg. Reuters put the company's value at about a billion dollars at pricing. That's a strong debut for a Kelun-licensed long-acting T-S-L-P antibody in asthma. It came on the same day Rezera pulled its own offering, so the window is wide open for some companies and shut for others.

Seventh, Synchron has paused clinical trials of its second-generation brain-computer interface. That's the wireless version of its stent-based device, which goes in through a vein and sits next to the motor cortex. The company says the pause isn't safety-related. It's cutting jobs in New York and working on a new device. C-E-O Tom Oxley had hinted at it on a panel last week, saying one delayed component had held up their F-D-A submission. STAT calls it the first major hiccup for one of the leading companies in a field that also includes Neuralink and Precision Neuroscience.

A few quick ones. Vivani Medical reported Phase one data on N-P-M-one-thirty-nine, a semaglutide implant under the skin meant to last six months to a year. In twenty patients it raised drug levels slowly and caused no gastrointestinal side effects over four weeks. Vivani plans a Phase two in weight maintenance in 2027. Milestone Pharmaceuticals is cutting about twenty percent of its staff, including its chief medical officer, after Cardamyst, its etripamil nasal spray for episodes of rapid heart rhythm, sold only about a million dollars in the third quarter. And Endpoints reports that Moderna is getting commercial production of its individualized cancer vaccine ready, at its Marlborough, Massachusetts site, ahead of the year-end filing with Merck.
