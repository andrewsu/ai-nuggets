Good morning Travis. Here are your biotech and pharma headlines for Wednesday, September second, twenty twenty-six.

Lupus took two hits in thirty-six hours from two entirely different directions. Monday it was cell therapy on safety. Yesterday it was an oral small molecule on efficacy, and that one is a Foresite Capital portfolio print — Alumis lost more than half its market value in a single session. Meanwhile Roche quietly bought its way into the same disease with a third instrument. Eleven items, then the week ahead.

First, and today's spotlight. Alumis reported topline results from LUMUS, its Phase two-b trial of envudeucitinib in moderate-to-severe systemic lupus erythematosus, and the trial did not meet its primary endpoint or its key secondary endpoints in the overall population. Envudeucitinib, which you will know as E-S-K-zero-zero-one, is a next-generation oral allosteric tyrosine kinase two inhibitor — it binds the regulatory pseudokinase domain rather than the catalytic site, which is what buys the class its selectivity against the other Janus kinases, and it sits downstream of the interleukin twenty-three and type one interferon receptors. Four hundred eight patients, three doses against placebo, forty-eight weeks, primary endpoint the BICLA composite. The rescue narrative is a prespecified subgroup: patients with a high interferon gene signature responded on BICLA and on the key secondaries, including the cutaneous index, the SRI-four responder index and low disease activity state. Pharmacodynamics confirmed dose-dependent interferon pathway suppression, maximal at forty milligrams twice daily. Alumis says the interferon-high group was unexpectedly under-represented in enrollment, which diluted the overall result, and it intends to take an interferon-high Phase three plan to regulators. The market did not buy it. BioWorld reported the shares at nine dollars twenty-seven in midday trading, down twelve dollars fifty-four, or fifty-eight percent, and BioPharma Dive noted that erases nearly all of the gains the stock made this year on January's psoriasis data. BioWorld's framing was blunt: management focused on the subgroup, investors focused on the overall population. Two things to hold before the spotlight. Alumis released no response rates at all — the effect size is a claim, not yet a number. And safety was not the problem: the chief medical officer said adverse events ran below placebo, so this is a question about the disease and the endpoint, not about the drug's tolerability. Foresite Capital led the upsized two hundred fifty-nine million dollar Series C in March twenty twenty-four alongside Samsara BioCapital and venBio.

Second, the instrument nobody was watching. Roche has signed a licensing deal with Simcere for S-I-M zero-six-six-zero, a trispecific antibody, paying seventy-five million dollars upfront and up to one point five three billion dollars in milestones plus royalties. The construct engages C-D-seventy-nine-a and C-D-nineteen on the B cell and C-D-three on the T cell — two B-cell anchors instead of one — and Simcere's stated design goal is to trigger T-cell-mediated killing while limiting the cytokine release that drives adverse events. Indications are B-cell-mediated disease broadly: newly diagnosed and relapsed-refractory B-cell lymphoma in the clinic now, with lupus and arthritis named as the autoimmune targets. Read that against Monday. The autoimmune C-A-R-T programs at Novartis and Bristol Myers are paused over a hyperinflammatory syndrome driven by uncontrolled in vivo expansion, and the very next day a large pharma pays real money for a dose-titratable engager whose selling point is explicitly that it depletes B cells without the cytokine surge. That is the read-across taking a price. Note also the pace: this is Roche's third Asia licensing deal in eight days, after Genentech's up-to-two-point-three-billion-dollar obesity deal with Hanmi and an up-to-one-billion-dollar A-D-C deal with DualityBio.

Third, the other side of the cell therapy trade. ArsenalBio is laying off ninety-nine employees — the majority of its workforce, across all teams and locations — as it pivots entirely to in vivo C-A-R-T, engineering T cells inside the patient rather than in a facility. This is the staffing detail behind the strategic announcement it made on August thirty-first, and it is the second cut in a year; the company reduced headcount by half in twenty twenty-five and was at a hundred twenty-seven people as of last September. Gone from the pipeline are A-B twenty-one-hundred, the autologous integrated-circuit cell therapy in renal cell carcinoma whose Phase one data were due in March and never disclosed, and A-B ten-fifteen. What remains is A-B thirty twenty-eight in prostate cancer and an undisclosed early program. ArsenalBio raised three hundred twenty-five million dollars in a Series C in September twenty twenty-four specifically to fund A-B twenty-one-hundred, and its Bristol Myers collaboration from late twenty twenty — seventy million dollars upfront — remains in effect. Put this next to the Novartis and Bristol holds and next to Cellares laying off sixty-eight people in New Jersey, and the pattern is not that C-A-R-T stopped working. It is that the ex vivo autologous model is losing the argument on both cost and control at the same time.

Fourth, a Bristol Myers item that bears on something we went deep on last weekend. BioSpace reports that with the AstraZeneca merger talks collapsed, Bristol has no safety net under its patent cliff — roughly eighty percent of revenue faces generic or biosimilar competition by twenty thirty-one, with Eliquis at eight point six billion dollars in first-half sales and Opdivo at four point six billion both losing exclusivity. That puts an unusual amount of weight on milvexian, the oral factor eleven-a inhibitor, in the LIBREXIA atrial fibrillation Phase three reading out in twenty twenty-seven. H-C Wainwright's Raghuram Selvaraju said a failure could take the share price down forty to fifty percent and leave the company "hanging by a thread," adding that "if Bristol blows up with milvexian, there may be a feeding frenzy once the carnage is over." Guggenheim's Seamus Fernandez puts the probability of success at sixty percent. Nothing new happened to the drug — what changed is that the hedge went away.

Fifth, a clean royalty win. BioMarin has settled its achondroplasia patent fight with Ascendis. Ascendis will pay eighteen to twenty percent royalties, depending on region, on net sales of Yuviwel — that is navepegritide, the long-acting C-type natriuretic peptide approved in February — through May twentieth, twenty thirty. In exchange BioMarin grants a non-exclusive worldwide license under the European patent covering long-acting C-N-P variants, the same family that protects Voxzogo. Analysts liked it: B-M-O called the rates relatively high versus recent biotech patent settlements while noting the duration is relatively short, and Stifel called the rate at the high end for this kind of agreement. Strategically it converts a share-loss problem into an annuity while BioMarin develops B-M-N three-thirty-three, its next-generation C-N-P.

Sixth, analysts splitting on Arrowhead. At the European Society of Cardiology meeting Arrowhead presented Phase three data on plozasiran, branded Redemplo — an A-P-O-C-three-directed siRNA — in severe hypertriglyceridemia. Median triglyceride reductions of seventy-nine to eighty-one percent at one year, fifty-two to fifty-five percent of patients getting below a hundred fifty milligrams per deciliter, serious adverse events at eight point three percent versus ten on placebo, and a seventy-eight percent reduction in pancreatitis. Stifel called the profile arguably best-in-class and pointed at quarterly dosing versus monthly for Ionis's approved Tryngolza, plus no anaphylaxis against roughly seven percent hypersensitivity events and no meaningful liver enzyme elevations. B-M-O went the other way, flagging worsening glycemic control in fourteen point three percent of plozasiran patients as a real problem in a population thick with diabetes. Supplemental filing later this year.

Seventh, m-R-N-A flu gets a second late-stage entrant. G-S-K is starting a Phase three trial this month of its m-R-N-A influenza vaccine, and the design is the interesting part: it encodes both hemagglutinin and neuraminidase, where licensed vaccines target hemagglutinin essentially alone. That makes it the first late-stage flu vaccine going after both surface antigens. The Phase two enrolled nine hundred seventy-one adults, standard dose in younger and high dose in older participants, and beat licensed inactivated comparators on immune response in both. Fast Track came in July. Context: Moderna's m-R-N-A flu vaccine was approved last month after the F-D-A initially refused to review it, and last season's vaccine effectiveness was thirty-six percent by C-D-C's own estimate. The modality is being rehabilitated on the strength of a target the old platform could not easily reach.

Eighth, federal money on distributed manufacturing. A-R-P-A-H awarded up to a hundred twenty-five million dollars across five teams, including Waterfall Scientific, to build automated systems that produce personalized R-N-A medicines in under a week. The bottleneck this attacks is not chemistry, it is logistics — centralized plants, cold chain, and the fact that an n-of-one genetic medicine costs the same to manufacture as a blockbuster. If it works, the unit economics of bespoke therapy change, and so does who can make them.

Ninth and tenth, your A-I-in-drug-discovery pair, and both are milestones rather than announcements. BigHat Biosciences dosed the first patient in a Phase one of B-H-B eight-ten, a C-D-H-seventeen-directed antibody-drug conjugate built as a compact single-domain antibody fused to an F-c region. C-D-H-seventeen is a gastrointestinal-restricted adhesion protein that, in the investigator's words, has been hard to drug well. Chief executive Peyton Greenside called it the first program the company has taken from A-I-generated design into human studies. Preclinically it produced complete or near-complete tumor clearance across about thirty patient- and cell-derived models. The principal investigator is Alexander Spira at NEXT Oncology Virginia; a C-E-A-C-A-M-six T-cell engager follows in twenty twenty-seven, and BigHat has collaborations with Merck, Johnson and Johnson, and Lilly. Separately, Ono has signed a discovery partnership with Aitia in neurological disease. Aitia's pitch is causal rather than correlative A-I — its platform builds what it calls Gemini digital twins from patient clinical and omics data to infer mechanism, and chief executive Colin Hill's claim is that this uncovers disease mechanisms conventional A-I cannot. Aitia identifies targets and biomarkers; Ono holds an exclusive worldwide option to develop and commercialize anything that comes out. Neurology is a reasonable place to test a causal-inference platform, since it is precisely where correlative target discovery has failed most expensively.

Eleventh, the capital markets picture, which is worth your attention because it changes exit planning. Seventeen biopharma reverse mergers closed or are set to close in the third quarter, against exactly one in the second — nineteen for the year, nearly matching the twenty-five I-P-Os, which is itself the strongest I-P-O year since twenty twenty-one. Leerink's Jack Bannister explains the shift as transparency: "the I-P-O process is a little bit of a black box for investors," whereas a reverse merger comes with substantial public disclosure and a P-I-P-E, so investors can see the syndicate before trading starts. The cost is time — four to six months versus weeks. The Obsidian Therapeutics merger with Galera carried three hundred fifty million dollars of P-I-P-E financing and the shares stayed illiquid until this month. And Endpoints has a useful companion piece on how Lilly's thirteen acquisitions this year have spread proceeds across an unusually broad set of venture syndicates.

The week ahead: the European Respiratory Society meets September fifth through ninth with Generate Biomedicines' golukibart posters getting their formal presentation; Morgan Stanley's healthcare conference and the Cantor and H-C Wainwright meetings run through the middle of the month, which is where most of yesterday's press-release traffic was pointed; Ultragenyx has a September nineteenth action date in Sanfilippo A; Scholar Rock's apitegromab is September thirtieth; MSToronto is October twenty-first through twenty-third with the remibrutinib late-breaker; and Capricor's deramiocel action date is November twenty-second.
