Good morning Travis. This is your spotlight for Saturday, October seventeenth. Today's topic is a policy document, not a drug: China's draft export controls on biotech platforms. It deserves the deep dive because it changes how Western drug companies can buy innovation from China, and China has become the single biggest outside source of new molecules for big pharma. Licensing a drug stays open; taking ownership of the engine that made it gets shut.

Start with what was actually published. On Friday, China's Ministry of Commerce and Ministry of Science and Technology issued a joint announcement, number forty-eight of twenty twenty-six. It proposes revising the country's catalogue of technologies prohibited or restricted from export, and it adds forty technologies: twenty-three in artificial intelligence, twelve in solar manufacturing and five in biomedicine. The public comment period runs to November fifteenth. Two parts of it are already in force, though. From the day of publication until the revised catalogue takes effect, anyone exporting a listed technology has to register first on the Commerce Ministry's online platform, and that includes deals that are already underway but not yet completed. The registration covers outright ownership transfers, licensing of the technology for overseas use, and changes of ownership or control that put the holder in foreign hands. The filing asks for the counterparty, the destination, the intended use and the expected deal value.

The five biomedical items fall into two tiers. In the prohibited tier there are three. The first is original cell and gene therapy technology, defined as process platforms with independent intellectual property. The second is the same thing for small interfering RNA, again meaning process platforms with independent intellectual property. The third is pathogen gain-of-function work, meaning artificial modification or directed passage that enhances a pathogen. In the restricted tier, which means an export license is required, there are two: novel target discovery and validation technology in cell and gene therapy, and the same for siRNA. The annex defines "completely novel" narrowly. It means a target not publicly disclosed anywhere in the world, through any channel.

The important part is the carve-out. For the two platform bans, the annex notes say only the outbound transfer of ownership is prohibited. Out-licensing, outsourced research, and contract manufacturing are explicitly outside the scope. So are the technical processes a partner needs for development, testing, production, quality control and regulatory compliance in a cross-border deal. The announcement adds that licensing arrangements that don't transfer ownership get "facilitation measures, including automatic licensing." The ministries' spokesperson said the measure "is not directed at any particular country, region, or entity," and called the prohibitions "an institutional measure intended to safeguard national security."

Now, why do this, and why now? The number Bloomberg cited is the answer. Government statistics count eighty-one Chinese out-licensing deals worth up to a hundred ten billion dollars in the first half of twenty twenty-six, on pace to beat last year's record. You've heard the drumbeat on this show. Earlier this week Roche signed its fifth China deal since the end of August, paying Rona Therapeutics a hundred million dollars up front for full ownership of an siRNA against ALK-seven, a fat-tissue receptor, for obesity. BioPharma Dive counts that as Roche's tenth China deal since the start of twenty twenty-five. Beijing's message is that it's happy to keep selling the fruit. It just doesn't want to sell the orchard. A finished molecule, licensed or even sold, brings in cash and international validation. A reusable siRNA chemistry and delivery platform, or a cell therapy manufacturing process, is what produces the next twenty molecules, and the state now wants that to stay domestic.

There's an obvious American mirror image too. Bloomberg notes that the U-S Treasury is expected to issue rules this year that could put biotech licensing deals under the same outbound investment review as semiconductors and A-I, and some U-S lawmakers and investors have been pushing to restrict these deals. China is drawing its own line first, and drawing it in a place that protects the flow of deals both sides currently want.

So what does it actually close off? Three structures stand out. The first is the outright acquisition of a Chinese platform company. The change-of-control clause is the key one: if a foreign buyer takes control of a Chinese company that owns a qualifying cell therapy or siRNA platform, that counts as an export. The template that disappears is AstraZeneca's purchase of Gracell, which closed in early twenty twenty-four for up to about one point two billion dollars, and which bought a Chinese autologous CAR-T manufacturing platform outright. Under this draft, a deal like that would be prohibited, not just reviewed.

The second is the NewCo structure, the arrangement this market has relied on for two years. Investors set up an offshore company that holds global rights to a Chinese asset. If the NewCo licenses a molecule, the carve-out appears to cover it. If it's designed to take ownership of the underlying siRNA platform, it falls on the prohibited side. Plenty of these deals have been somewhere in the middle, so expect lawyers to spend the comment period asking where the line falls.

The third is target discovery. The restricted tier covers novel, undisclosed targets in cell and gene therapy and siRNA. A foreign company that wants to own a Chinese partner's target package outright now needs a license, while licensing the same package gets the automatic route. That connects to the A-I discovery theme we've been following. AstraZeneca paid insitro this week for genetics-anchored target discovery. In China, the same kind of output, a novel validated target, is now classed as a controlled export when ownership changes hands. The A-I section of the same document restricts large-model core technology, agent planning, multi-agent and tool-calling technology, and very large compute clusters. Anything already open-sourced or published is exempt.

And what does it leave alone? Most of what Western pharma has actually been buying. The headline-grabbing China deals of the past two years have overwhelmingly been asset licenses or asset purchases: a bispecific, a GLP-1, an antibody-drug conjugate, a single siRNA molecule. The annex is explicit that the platform ban targets reusable platforms, not the process for a single drug. So the Roche-Rona deal looks like it fits the open lane. Contract research and manufacturing are untouched, which matters for anyone with work at WuXi or similar groups.

Expert reaction so far is thin, and I'd rather say that than make it up. Endpoints summed it up as Beijing "clamping down on the kinds of deals domestic companies can make," while free-flowing drug licensing "appears to" survive. Fierce headlined it as a ban on foreign acquisitions of critical platforms. Bloomberg called the licensing carve-out "some relief for the pharmaceutical industry globally." I couldn't find a sell-side note on it yet. Expect those on Monday.

A few open questions are worth tracking through the comment period. First, how broadly will "independent intellectual property" and "process platform" be read? A company's lipid or GalNAc conjugation chemistry is clearly a platform. A disclosed manufacturing process for one CAR-T product probably isn't. Second, the registration requirement already applies to licensing, so Beijing now sees the counterparty and the dollar value of every covered deal before it closes. Automatic approval is still a checkpoint, and it adds a step to closing timelines. Third, multinationals with research labs in China. If a Western company's Shanghai subsidiary develops an siRNA delivery platform, moving that intellectual property to the parent looks like an ownership transfer abroad. That deserves a careful read from anyone running that kind of footprint.

For Calibr and the Foresite portfolios, the practical read is mostly positive. Calibr's switchable CAR-T work and the U-S-built platforms in the Foresite portfolios aren't touched by any of this. If anything, clean-title Western platforms in cell therapy and RNA interference just became somewhat scarcer and more strategic. The timing fits. City Therapeutics, an RNAi company pitching itself as a next-generation Alnylam, priced an upsized two-hundred-million-dollar I-P-O on Thursday. For anyone in-licensing from China, the playbook still works: license the molecule, contract the manufacturing, and don't plan on owning the platform. Diligence should now include whether the Chinese partner has registered the deal, and whether anything in the package counts as an undisclosed target.

The final catalogue comes after November fifteenth. Two things will tell us how much bite it has: whether the comment period narrows the definition of "platform," and whether Washington's outbound investment rules land first. That's your spotlight for Saturday.
