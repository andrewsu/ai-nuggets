Travis' biotech and pharma headlines for Saturday, August 8, 2026. Five things, and today's lead is a Foresite portfolio company hitting the public market.

First, and today's spotlight: Latigo Biotherapeutics went public Thursday, pricing an upsized offering at eighteen dollars a share and raising three hundred forty-five point six million dollars, with shares beginning to trade on the Nasdaq. Latigo is a non-opioid pain company, and its investor base includes Foresite Capital alongside Westlake Village BioPartners, Five A-M Ventures, and Blue Owl. Its lead drug is an oral inhibitor of the Nav one-point-eight sodium channel — the same pain-signaling target Vertex validated last year with Journavx — and Latigo is coming to market on New England Journal of Medicine-published phase 2 data that it argues beats not just placebo but the opioid comparator. We go deep on this one in today's spotlight.

Second, one of the more striking financings of the week: LifeMine Therapeutics raised two hundred sixty-three million dollars — an oversubscribed Series E plus a Series D — to push its lead transplant drug into the clinic. LifeMine is Greg Verdine's company, and it mines fungal genomes with machine learning to find natural-product drugs. The lead program is what the company calls a calcineurin activation inhibitor for organ transplant — mechanistically distinct from the decades-old calcineurin inhibitors like tacrolimus, and pitched as a way to suppress the immune system without the kidney toxicity that makes tacrolimus so hard to live with. New money came from Bezos Expeditions, Gates Frontier, and R-A Capital, with GV, ARCH, GSK, and Invus staying in. It has shown meaningful T-cell suppression and no substantial toxicity across more than a hundred healthy volunteers, with a phase 2 kidney-transplant study and a phase 1-b islet-cell study planned.

Third, a genuine de-risking moment for in-vivo gene editing: Intellia Therapeutics says it has pinpointed the source of the liver-enzyme elevations seen with its CRISPR therapy for hereditary transthyretin amyloidosis. The company traced the worst liver signals to patients carrying one specific immune-system gene variant — which, as analysts put it, converts a scary safety question into a screenable, actionable one. Patient stratification instead of a program-threatening liability. That matters for the whole in-vivo editing field, where a single delivered dose to the liver has been the modality's promise and its risk at the same time.

Fourth, a leadership change at the top of an mRNA icon: BioNTech named Guido Oelkers as its next chief executive, succeeding co-founder Ugur Sahin no later than early next year. Oelkers comes from the Swedish rare-disease company Sobi, where he more than quadrupled revenue over nine years — a commercial operator taking the wheel as BioNTech pivots from its COVID windfall toward oncology. Sahin isn't retiring; he's leaving to run a new, still-unnamed mRNA startup, which is its own story worth watching.

And fifth, a competitive move in cardiometabolic RNA drugs: Arrowhead Pharmaceuticals bought an FDA priority review voucher to speed up the review of plozasiran in severe hypertriglyceridemia. That's a direct shot at Ionis, whose rival therapy in the same indication just reached the market — buying a voucher to compress the timeline tells you how tight Arrowhead thinks the race is.
