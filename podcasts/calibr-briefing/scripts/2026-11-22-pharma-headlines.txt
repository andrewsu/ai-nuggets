Good morning, Travis. It's Sunday, November twenty-second, 2026. The trade press was quiet from Friday night through Saturday, so this morning we pick up the stories from late in the week that we didn't have room for. Big pharma's top scientists told Reuters that A-I is cutting early discovery time by up to half, with Anthropic as a possible rival. Danaher and Microsoft are building an agent layer for lab instruments. Recursion settled its Phase three design with the F-D-A. And Capricor's rejection gets the full spotlight. Here's what you need to know.

First, A-I in drug discovery. On Friday, Reuters published interviews with research heads at GSK, Bristol Myers, Novartis, Lilly, Novo Nordisk, AstraZeneca and Sanofi. The common line was that A-I has moved past regulatory paperwork and into designing molecules, triaging candidates and spotting safety risks early. Some of the numbers were specific. Novartis research president Fiona Marshall said the average time from project start to drug candidate has dropped from three years to about eighteen months to two years. Novo said A-I cut its design, make and test cycles from about eight to three, and halved each cycle from eight weeks to four. Sanofi said an A-I approach to translating early biology let it skip a dose-selection study for lunsekimig, its asthma nanobody, saving at least a year. Bristol said one of its sickle cell candidates wouldn't exist without its A-I systems. Sanofi's Matt Truppo expects early-discovery timelines to shrink twenty to twenty-five percent within two years, and possibly by half within five.

The tension in the piece is Anthropic, which listed this week in an I-P-O that Reuters puts at one point five trillion dollars. It has opened its own lab, sells Claude Science to companies including Novo, works with Bristol on drugs, and has Novartis C-E-O Vas Narasimhan on its board. GSK's chief scientific officer, Tony Wood, called Anthropic "another competitor." Lilly's Aliza Apple, who runs its TuneLab program, said the labs started out as vendors, are increasingly collaborators, and "a year from now, it's anyone's guess." She also had a line for anyone promising to cure all disease: "It would be great for them to cure one, and then let's talk about the rest." Novo and Sanofi both said they don't give proprietary data to frontier labs for training. Truppo said that without data to train on, the models break down very quickly. The reality check is that A-I-derived drugs in the clinic only went from about one hundred seventeen to one hundred twenty-nine over eight months. A University of Chicago researcher who tracks them doubts they'll be approved any faster, because trials and regulators are still the bottleneck. The takeaway: big pharma thinks its moat is proprietary data, and it's keeping that data away from the labs.

Second, the lab-automation side of the same trend. Danaher announced a partnership with Microsoft on Friday to build a common agentic platform for its scientific instruments, on Microsoft's Azure and Foundry. The goal is one software layer that controls instruments and generates insights across Danaher's businesses, which include Beckman Coulter, Leica, Sciex and Cytiva, while staying open to outside agents. C-E-O Julie Sawyer Montgomery said the aim is labs that are "not just connected, but intelligent." First commercial applications are due by the end of this year, with agent-enabled instruments in 2027. Danaher signed a similar deal with Nvidia in October and announced an autonomous antibody-reagent lab at Abcam. Instrument makers want to be the layer A-I agents use to run experiments, not just the hardware underneath.

Third, from Wednesday, an A-I-discovered drug moving toward a pivotal trial. Recursion said it reached agreement with the F-D-A on a registrational Phase three for REC-forty-eight-eighty-one in familial adenomatous polyposis, or F-A-P, an inherited condition caused by A-P-C mutations that carpets the gut with polyps. The drug is an oral allosteric inhibitor of MEK one and two. Takeda made it, and Recursion licensed it in 2020 after its phenotypic screens pointed to F-A-P. The trial will enroll about two hundred adults who have already had their colon removed. The primary endpoint is event-free survival, where an event is removal of a polyp ten millimeters or larger, F-A-P-related surgery, high-grade dysplasia, cancer, or death. Recursion plans to start in the second half of 2027. One caution: in the Phase one-b/two TUPELO study, polyp burden fell a median of forty-seven percent in fifteen patients, but that study lumped together every polyp over five millimeters. So there's no data yet on the ten-millimeter polyps the Phase three will count. Biodexa is already about two-thirds enrolled in its own Phase three, with an oral rapamycin. There's no approved drug for F-A-P.

Fourth, an approval on Friday. The F-D-A approved Novo Nordisk's Sogroya, or somapacitan, a once-weekly long-acting growth hormone, for children two and a half and older with growth failure from Turner syndrome. That's its fifth pediatric growth indication. In the Phase three REAL eight trial in one hundred five girls, height velocity at one year was eight point nine centimeters per year on weekly Sogroya, versus nine point seven on daily somatropin. Novo calls that comparable, but the weekly drug was slightly behind, against a daily dose below the U-S maximum. The pitch is three hundred thirteen injection-free days a year. Families will be weighing convenience against a small growth deficit.

Fifth, Capricor, which is today's spotlight. Since yesterday's episode, we've read the F-D-A's own statement on its website. It confirms what had only been secondhand: its inspectors found unauthorized post hoc changes to the statistical analysis plan in the HOPE-three trial, plus breaches of blinding and other protocol violations. It also says the data "cannot be remediated post-hoc." Capricor's press release didn't mention any of that.

BridgeBio's BBP-four-eighteen, a ribitol pill for limb-girdle muscular dystrophy type two-I, has an F-D-A date on Friday the twenty-seventh, the day after Thanksgiving, so a decision could come early. By the thirtieth, the F-D-A owes decisions on Vertex's povetacicept in I-g-A nephropathy and Roche's giredestrant in early breast cancer. Savara is still waiting for a new date on molgramostim.
