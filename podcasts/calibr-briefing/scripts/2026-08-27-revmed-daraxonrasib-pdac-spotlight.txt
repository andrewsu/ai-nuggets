This is your spotlight for Thursday, August twenty-seventh, twenty twenty-six. RAS was described as undruggable for something close to forty years. Yesterday morning the F-D-A approved a RAS drug, in the cancer RAS drives hardest, thirty-five days after the application was filed.

Here are the mechanics first. The F-D-A approved Rasonque, daraxonrasib, from Revolution Medicines of Redwood City, for adults with metastatic pancreatic adenocarcinoma who have received at least one prior systemic therapy or who are not candidates for multi-agent systemic therapy. It is a three-hundred-milligram tablet, once daily, until progression or unacceptable toxicity. It is available by prescription now. There is no boxed warning. And there is no companion diagnostic — the label covers patients with or without an identified RAS mutation in their tumor, which is a sentence worth reading twice and which I will come back to, because it is the most commercially and scientifically consequential line in the document.

Now the molecule, because the molecule is why this took forty years. RAS is a small G-T-Pase that cycles between an inactive G-D-P-bound state and an active G-T-P-bound state. In the active state it recruits downstream effectors — R-A-F, P-I-three-K — and drives proliferation. Oncogenic mutations lock it toward the active state. The problem for medicinal chemistry was never that RAS is unimportant. It was that active RAS presents a smooth, high-affinity, picomolar-nucleotide-binding surface with no druggable pocket. There is nothing to bind.

The first breakthrough, sotorasib from Amgen in twenty twenty-one and adagrasib from Mirati, now Bristol Myers, went around the problem rather than through it. Both are covalent inhibitors that react with a cysteine created by the K-RAS G-twelve-C mutation, and both bind the switch-two pocket that only exists in the inactive, G-D-P-bound conformation. So they are doubly constrained: one allele, one conformational state. They work by waiting for the protein to cycle off and then locking the door.

Revolution Medicines did something structurally different. Daraxonrasib is a tri-complex inhibitor. It does not try to find a pocket on RAS. It binds cyclophilin A — an abundant intracellular chaperone — and the resulting drug-chaperone pair creates a composite binding surface that then engages RAS in its active, G-T-P-bound, O-N state. The tri-complex sterically occludes the effector interface. RAS is still on; it simply cannot talk to anything downstream.

If that sounds familiar it should. Cyclosporine binds the same cyclophilin A and the resulting complex inhibits calcineurin, a protein cyclosporine alone does not touch. This is induced-proximity pharmacology, and it is the oldest trick in immunosuppression repurposed for the hardest target in oncology. You can actually see the mechanism confirmed in the label's drug interaction section, which says to avoid concomitant cyclosporine A. The two molecules compete for the same chaperone. That is a satisfying piece of internal evidence that the mechanism in the paper is the mechanism in the patient.

Two consequences follow, and they are the whole story. First, because the drug recognizes the O-N-state conformation rather than a mutation-specific chemical handle, it is multi-selective — it inhibits wild-type and a broad range of mutant RAS proteins. Second, and this is where pancreatic cancer becomes the right first indication rather than the hardest one: more than ninety percent of pancreatic adenocarcinomas carry a RAS mutation, but the dominant alleles are G-twelve-D, G-twelve-V and G-twelve-R. G-twelve-C is a low single-digit percentage. Which means the entire first generation of approved RAS drugs was, by construction, irrelevant to essentially all of pancreatic cancer. Ten years of RAS drug development produced two approved medicines that could not touch the most RAS-addicted major tumor type in humans. Yesterday closed that gap.

RASolute three-oh-two was a global, randomized, open-label phase three enrolling five hundred previously treated patients with metastatic pancreatic adenocarcinoma — two hundred forty-eight to once-daily daraxonrasib, two hundred fifty-two to investigator's choice of four different cytotoxic regimens representing standard of care across geographies. The dual primary endpoints were progression-free survival by blinded independent central review and overall survival, both specified in the RAS G-twelve mutant population. Progression-free survival and overall survival in the full intent-to-treat population were key secondary endpoints, and the trial deliberately enrolled patients with no identified RAS mutation at all. Data cutoff was February tenth of this year.

In the RAS G-twelve population, two hundred twenty-eight against two hundred thirty-one, median overall survival was thirteen point two months against six point six, hazard ratio zero point four zero. Progression-free survival was seven point three months against three point five, hazard ratio zero point four five. Objective response rate thirty-three point two percent against eleven point eight.

In the intent-to-treat population, median overall survival was thirteen point two months against six point seven, hazard ratio zero point four zero. Progression-free survival seven point two against three point six, hazard ratio zero point four nine. Response rate thirty-one point six percent against eleven point two.

Read those two paragraphs back to back. The hazard ratio for death is zero point four zero in the mutation-selected population and zero point four zero in the all-comer population. The median survival is thirteen point two months in both. Brian Wolpin of Dana-Farber, who directs the Hale Family Center for Pancreatic Cancer Research and was the trial's principal investigator, described the result as supporting a new paradigm where RAS-O-N inhibition enters standard of care. Mark Goldsmith called it a monumental step. Those are the expected quotes. The unexpected thing is the arithmetic: selecting for the mutation bought you nothing. That is why there is no companion diagnostic on the label, and it is the single most valuable asset Revolution Medicines walked away with yesterday, because it means every metastatic pancreatic adenocarcinoma patient in second line is on-label without a test, a turnaround time, or a tissue requirement. In a disease where biopsies are hard to get and patients deteriorate in weeks, removing the diagnostic step is not a marketing convenience. It is access.

Here is the thing I would want to see and cannot. The identical hazard ratios are consistent with two very different underlying stories. One is that wild-type pancreatic tumors are genuinely RAS-dependent through amplification, upstream receptor signaling or wild-type RAS-O-N flux, and the drug is treating the pathway rather than the allele — which is the company's thesis and, if true, is worth vastly more than this indication. The other is that the so-called wild-type patients are largely mutation-positive patients whose mutation was missed, since RAS testing in pancreatic cancer is frequently done on scant cytology material with imperfect sensitivity. Those two explanations predict very different results in tumors that are actually RAS-independent. The publicly presented data do not break out the no-identified-mutation subgroup, and the trial was not powered to. Until somebody publishes that subgroup with the sequencing depth disclosed, treat "works regardless of RAS status" as a commercially settled claim and a scientifically open one.

The safety profile is where this drug is genuinely unusual, and it cuts in an unexpected direction. Grade three or higher treatment-related adverse events were forty-three point six percent on daraxonrasib against fifty-seven point five percent on chemotherapy. Treatment-related serious adverse events, ten point eight percent against eighteen point seven. Discontinuation for treatment-related toxicity, one point two percent against eleven point two percent. Median dose intensity ninety-three point one percent on drug, against sixty-five to ninety-five percent across the chemotherapy regimens. There was one grade five treatment-related event, a pneumonitis, in the daraxonrasib arm. So the targeted agent was better tolerated than the comparator on essentially every aggregate measure, and the patient-reported outcomes agree — median time to deterioration in global health status was five point seven months against two point six, and in clinically relevant pain, nine point two months against three point eight. Both hazard ratios around zero point five to zero point six, both significant.

But do not read that as a clean drug, because the label tells a different story about the texture of daily life on it. Dermatologic and soft tissue toxicity occurred in eighty-six percent of patients, ten percent of it grade three. Stomatitis in fifty-seven percent, nine percent grade three. Diarrhea in sixty-three percent, six percent grade three. G-I perforation in zero point nine percent, one fatal. Interstitial lung disease or pneumonitis in two point four percent, one fatal. The label instructs prophylaxis before the first dose — topical corticosteroid to the face and chest, emollients, broad-spectrum sunscreen, limited sun exposure, and consideration of prophylactic doxycycline or minocycline. There are also real CYP-three-A and P-glycoprotein interaction constraints.

Translate that into clinic reality. This is an oral drug whose toxicity is high-incidence, low-grade, chronic and dermatologic, replacing an intravenous regimen whose toxicity is myelosuppressive and episodic. The trial says patients strongly prefer the trade. But the trade only holds if the rash and the mouth ulcers get managed, and rash management in community oncology has historically been the failure mode for E-G-F-R-class drugs. Eighty-six percent dermatologic toxicity with a prophylaxis protocol written into the label is a nursing-workflow problem more than a pharmacology problem, and how well it is solved will show up in real-world dose intensity, not in the trial's ninety-three percent.

There is one honest limitation, and it is about time rather than about effect size. Median follow-up at the February cutoff was eight point five months, with a range topping out at fifteen point nine. The median overall survival is thirteen point two months, and its ninety-five percent confidence interval runs from ten point zero to "not estimable." That means the upper bound of survival benefit is not yet defined, and the point estimate rests on the tail of a curve with relatively few mature events. The hazard ratio is robust and the separation is unambiguous — nothing about zero point four zero with a confidence interval of zero point three zero to zero point five three is going to reverse. But "thirteen point two months" is a number that will move as the data mature, and it could move in either direction. If you are building a model, model the hazard ratio, not the median.

Now the regulatory story, which is arguably the bigger news for everyone who is not Revolution Medicines. The new drug application was accepted on July twenty-second. Approval came August twenty-sixth. That is roughly thirty-five days, and six and a half months ahead of the user fee goal date. The stack that made it possible: Breakthrough Therapy and Orphan Drug designations, Priority Review, the Real-Time Oncology Review pilot with an Assessment Aid, Project Orbis with Health Canada as a partner and E-M-A and Japan's P-M-D-A as observers, and a Commissioner's National Priority Voucher awarded last fall. The F-D-A's Angelo de Claro, who directs the Oncology Center of Excellence, framed it as unprecedented results in an area of high unmet need. Acting Commissioner Kyle Diamantas made the political point explicitly.

Two readings, and both are true. The favorable one is that this is exactly the case the voucher program was designed for — a plenary-session hazard ratio of zero point four zero in a disease with a three percent five-year survival rate, on a package the agency had already been reading in real time for months, where more than two thousand patients had already received the drug under an expanded access protocol the F-D-A itself authorized in May. There was almost nothing left to review. The skeptical reading is that the same machinery will next be pointed at an application with a hazard ratio of zero point eight and a contested endpoint, and that a thirty-five-day review leaves very little room for the kind of second-guessing that catches problems. Your practical takeaway is narrower and more useful: the F-D-A has now demonstrated, with a real approval, that a company which secures a voucher and runs real-time review can compress the filing-to-launch interval from roughly a year to roughly a month. That changes launch planning, commercial hiring, manufacturing scale-up timing and financing windows for every oncology program with a shot at a voucher. Plan for it before your competitors do.

The economics are worth stating plainly. List price is thirty-nine thousand eight hundred dollars for a thirty-day supply, which annualizes to roughly four hundred eighty thousand dollars. R-B-C models peak sales around eleven and a half billion dollars. Revolution Medicines is now worth about forty-five billion, larger than Biogen or Alnylam, with three point nine billion in cash as of June thirtieth and a stock that has roughly quintupled in a year. Notably, the stock did almost nothing yesterday — it traded around two hundred twelve dollars, essentially flat on the day. The approval was fully priced. That tells you the market is no longer paying for second-line pancreatic cancer; it is paying for first line, adjuvant, and lung.

There is a cautionary precedent buried in that valuation, and it is the same class. Sotorasib and adagrasib were also hailed as the end of undruggability, and both have been commercial disappointments — modest response rates, short durations, and a narrow addressable slice. The bull case for Rasonque is that the difference is real and mechanistic: broader genotype coverage, an O-N-state mechanism that does not depend on nucleotide cycling, an all-comer label, and a randomized overall survival benefit rather than a single-arm response rate. That is a defensible case. But the discipline is to notice that the last two times this story was told, the biology was right and the commercial thesis was wrong.

The competitive landscape reorganized itself within hours, which tells you where the field thinks the value is. Immuneering put out a press release yesterday whose entire purpose was to congratulate the second line and claim the first. Ben Zeskind's argument is that the priority now moves to first-line patients, where atebimetinib, an oral once-daily deep cyclic inhibitor of MEK, is in the global randomized phase three MAPKeeper three-oh-one. Their single-arm phase two-a in fifty-five first-line patients, atebimetinib plus modified gemcitabine and nab-paclitaxel, reported seventeen point three months median overall survival with eighty-four percent maintaining or gaining weight at three months, and grade three or higher treatment-related events above ten percent limited to anemia and neutropenia from the chemotherapy backbone. Discount a single-arm number against a randomized one — but notice the framing. They led on weight maintenance and on the explicit absence of rash, diarrhea, stomatitis and fatigue. They are running directly at Rasonque's label, and MEK is a downstream node in the same pathway, which makes it a legitimate mechanistic alternative rather than a me-too.

Revolution Medicines is of course also running at first line itself, with phase three trials in first-line pancreatic cancer and in the adjuvant setting, plus a pivotal study in second- and third-line RAS-mutant non-small cell lung cancer where daraxonrasib already holds Breakthrough designation for K-RAS mutations other than G-twelve-C. And the pipeline behind it is the part I would pay attention to: elironrasib, a G-twelve-C-selective O-N inhibitor; zoldonrasib, G-twelve-D-selective; R-M-C five-one-two-seven for G-twelve-V; plus Q-sixty-one-H and G-thirteen-C programs. The strategic logic is that you use the multi-selective agent as the pathway backbone and layer a mutant-selective agent on top for depth, which lets you push RAS suppression harder in the tumor than in normal tissue. If that works, the ceiling on this franchise is not pancreatic cancer.

One last thread for your world specifically. Revolution Medicines is a Foresite Capital portfolio alumnus, so the RAS thesis just paid off in the most legible way possible. But the durable lesson is methodological, and it generalizes past RAS. The reason this target fell is not that someone finally found a pocket. It is that someone stopped looking for one and instead recruited an abundant endogenous protein to manufacture a binding surface that does not exist in either partner alone. Every "undruggable" list in every portfolio review is populated by proteins with smooth surfaces and no allosteric site — transcription factors, scaffolds, the flat protein-protein interfaces that dominate signaling. The tri-complex result says the right question for those targets is not what pocket can I bind, but what chaperone is abundant in the relevant compartment and what composite surface can I build with it. That is the same intellectual move as molecular glues and bifunctional degraders, arrived at independently, and yesterday it produced a hazard ratio of zero point four zero in pancreatic cancer.

What to watch: the no-identified-mutation subgroup, whenever it is published with sequencing methodology disclosed, because that decides whether the all-comer label is biology or assay noise; mature overall survival as follow-up doubles; real-world dose intensity and dermatologic discontinuation in community practice against the trial's ninety-three percent; the first-line readout, which is the eleven-billion-dollar number rather than the current one; N-C-C-N guideline language, which governs uptake more than any sales force; the E-M-A's phased review under the Cancer Medicines Pathfinder, which is the test of whether this compression travels internationally; and the next application to use a national priority voucher, because that one will tell you whether yesterday was a well-chosen showcase or a new default. That is your spotlight.
