This is your Calibr spotlight for Thursday, November nineteenth. Yesterday we looked at Isomorphic Labs raising five billion dollars to build its own drugs on a closed model nobody outside the company can use. Today is the opposite bet. Boltz, the company behind the most widely used open-source structure and binding models, raised a one hundred ten million dollar Series A to sell molecular design to everyone else, priced by usage. The two rounds landed a day apart, and together they frame the main strategic question in A-I drug discovery right now: does the value end up with the companies that own the molecules, or with the ones that supply the models?

Start with the round. Bessemer Venture Partners led, and Bessemer's Andrew Hedin, a healthcare investor and F-Prime alum, and Byron Deeter, one of its best-known A-I and cloud investors, are joining the company. Every seed investor came back in, including Andreessen Horowitz, Amplify, Zetta and Obvious Ventures. New investors include Greycroft, F-Prime Capital, Hummingbird and Benchling, the lab software company. Boltz raised a twenty-eight million dollar seed in January, so it has about one hundred thirty-eight million in total. It didn't disclose a valuation. The team is about forty people.

The founders are Gabriele Corso, Jeremy Wohlwend and Saro Passaro, out of Regina Barzilay's lab at M-I-T. Corso co-wrote DiffDock, one of the first models to treat docking as a generative problem. Their open models came out in quick succession. Boltz-one, in late 2024, claimed AlphaFold three-level accuracy on complex structures. Boltz-two, in mid-2025, added binding affinity prediction alongside structure. BoltzGen, last fall, designs protein and peptide binders. All three shipped with code and weights under a permissive license that allows commercial use. That matters, because AlphaFold three's weights go only to academics who apply, so commercial use of that lineage runs through Isomorphic. Bessemer says the Boltz models have more than five million downloads and over a hundred thousand users. Andreessen Horowitz says every top-twenty pharma was already using them by the time of the seed round.

Then the company changed direction. In January it launched Boltz Lab, a hosted platform, along with a multi-year deal with Pfizer. Pfizer fine-tunes Boltz models on its own historical discovery data to build exclusive versions, and it keeps ownership of any molecules that come out. In June, Boltz released its first models that are not open, BoltzMol-one for small molecules and BoltzProt-one for protein binders. The company says it used them to find small-molecule hits and design binders for, quote, a majority of the challenging targets we pursued, at a fraction of the usual time and cost. Bessemer goes further. It says BoltzMol-one can get to a validated hit in weeks instead of months, for as little as ten thousand dollars, and that BoltzProt-one has nearly three times BoltzGen's hit rate. Hold those claims loosely. Boltz hasn't named the targets or published the hit rates, and these numbers come from the company and its lead investor on the day of the raise.

The most interesting part is the business model. Boltz charges in what it calls molecular tokens, units of molecular information its models process. A quick structure prediction costs a few tokens, and a full design campaign against a demanding target profile costs many. Andreessen Horowitz's investment memo calls it intelligence by the dose. Its pitch is that scientists will budget for computational exploration the way they already budget for sequencing reads or assay reagents. The headline metric is that tokens processed for paying customers grew fifty-fold since June. As reported by the Korean outlet Wowtale, on a four-week rolling average usage was about eight times the June baseline by August, passed twenty-five times in late October, and hit fifty times on November eighth. That's a steep curve. But it's relative to a baseline Boltz hasn't disclosed, the company hasn't said how much of it comes from the proprietary models versus hosted open ones, and there's no revenue figure.

The commercial footprint is real, though. Six of the top twenty pharma companies have signed on, and Pfizer, GSK, Takeda and Bristol Myers are named. In October, Bristol agreed to run the Boltz platform on its own NVIDIA supercomputing cluster and co-develop custom models on its own data. Customers own what they design, and customer data isn't used to train Boltz's general models. Boltz also exposes its models through an A-P-I that coding agents like Claude Code and Codex can call directly. Andreessen Horowitz makes a point here that's worth noticing: just as long-running agents have gotten better results in math by spending more compute, Boltz says extra compute on virtual screens and design campaigns improves the candidates its models rank highest. If that holds, demand for molecular tokens grows with agent adoption, not just with headcount.

Now put this next to Isomorphic. Isomorphic took five billion dollars to build a vertically integrated pharma company on a closed engine, with partner deals that, as we covered yesterday, have about eighty-two million dollars in disclosed upfronts and no reported milestone payments. Boltz says plainly in its announcement that models this powerful should be accessible to every scientist, rather than confined to a single company's proprietary pipeline or locked behind complex, restrictive strategic partnerships. That's a direct shot at the platform-deal model. The economic argument underneath is about timing. A typical A-I discovery partnership pays a small upfront and leaves most of the value in milestones that arrive years later, if ever. A usage model gets paid as the compute is consumed. The trade-off is that Boltz gives up the drug-level upside Isomorphic is betting on.

On the closest comparison, Chai Discovery, which OpenAI backs, raised four hundred million dollars at a three point eight billion dollar valuation in July and sells models to Lilly and Pfizer. Chai has also reportedly built a custom model on Lilly's proprietary data, the same playbook Boltz runs with Pfizer and Bristol. Latent Labs, founded by an AlphaFold alum, sits in the same lane. So Boltz isn't alone in selling models, and it's the smallest-funded of the group.

The skeptic's case has three parts. First, scale. One hundred ten million dollars is small next to Chai's four hundred million or Isomorphic's five billion, and frontier model training costs keep rising. Boltz says the money goes to compute and data, and it'll need both. Second, the open models are a double-edged moat. They built the user base, but anyone, including a competitor or a pharma's internal team, can download them, fine-tune them, and never pay. Boltz's answer is that the paid models are better and the workflow is easier. That's a claim that has to be re-earned with every model generation. Third, the competitors are the customers. Big pharma is building in-house A-I teams, frontier labs have far bigger compute budgets, and Andreessen Horowitz's own memo opens by noting that frontier labs are focused on this fight. And Pfizer is a customer of both Boltz and Chai, so pharma is clearly hedging across suppliers.

So what should Foresite and Calibr take from this? Three things.

First, for Foresite's A-I portfolio, including Xaira, which Foresite Labs co-incubated, the Boltz round is a pricing signal for the model layer. If good structure, affinity and binder design are available on demand to any biotech at token prices, then a general-purpose structure model isn't a moat anymore. The defensible ground moves to proprietary data, wet-lab validation loops, and the molecules themselves. That's an argument for the pipeline strategy Xaira is pursuing, as long as its edge is in data and biology rather than in architecture alone.

Second, for Calibr, this is about access. Calibr is a translational group that runs real drug programs without a frontier-lab compute budget. Open-weight models under a commercial-use license, plus a pay-per-use platform with clear terms on owning what you design, make this kind of capability reachable without signing away targets in a strategic deal. The practical questions to ask any vendor are the ones Boltz highlights: who owns the designed molecules, whether your data trains the vendor's general models, and whether you can fine-tune on your own assay data.

Third, the scorecard for twelve months from now. Watch whether Boltz publishes prospective validation, meaning named targets, hit rates and binding data for BoltzMol-one and BoltzProt-one designs. Watch whether it discloses revenue, or at least an absolute usage number. And watch whether a molecule designed with Boltz enters the clinic under a partner's name. Isomorphic has to prove it can make drugs. Boltz has to prove its tools make other people's drugs better, and that they'll keep paying for it.
