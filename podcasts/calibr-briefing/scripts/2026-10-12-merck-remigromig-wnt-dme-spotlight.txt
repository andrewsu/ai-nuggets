Today's spotlight is Merck's remigromig, and a question that matters well beyond one eye drug: when a new mechanism matches the standard of care on efficacy but shows a safety imbalance, how do you tell whether the problem is the drug or the comparator?

Start with what was shown. On Saturday at the American Academy of Ophthalmology meeting in New Orleans, Donald D'Amico of Weill Cornell presented the full one-year results of Brunello, Merck's pivotal Phase two-b three trial in diabetic macular edema. Nine hundred eighty-four adults were randomized equally to two doses of remigromig, half a milligram or point eight milligrams, or to Roche's ranibizumab, Lucentis, at half a milligram. Everyone got an injection into the eye every four weeks for the first year. The primary endpoint was the change in best-corrected visual acuity at week fifty-two, and the test was non-inferiority.

Both remigromig doses passed, independently. The lower dose gained nine point one letters, the higher dose eight point seven, and Lucentis eleven point eight. Merck has not disclosed the non-inferiority margin, so you cannot judge how much room the trial allowed. The investigator called the two-point-seven to three-point-one letter gap not clinically meaningful. No secondary endpoint showed superiority, including retinal thickness. And the higher dose did not beat the lower one, so there is no dose response to lean on.

Then the number that drove the story. Adverse events related to proliferative diabetic retinopathy occurred in six point seven and six point one percent of patients on the two remigromig doses, versus zero point nine percent on Lucentis. That category is a composite, a footnote in Merck's release spells it out: proliferative retinopathy itself, vitreous hemorrhage, new vessels on the iris or retina, and neovascular glaucoma. Discontinuations for adverse events were four point nine and four point five percent versus zero point nine. On the reassuring side, there was no retinal vasculitis, which matters because this field remembers what that did to Novartis's Beovu, and intraocular inflammation was low and balanced across arms.

The market's read arrived this morning. B-M-O Capital Markets wrote, quote, "We see limited competitive potential for remigromig," with "weaker numerical efficacy, and a less favorable safety profile versus ranibizumab." They added that the imbalances "could limit physician enthusiasm," given the established anti-V-E-G-F drugs. When Merck gave the topline without numbers last month, R-B-C called it mixed and Evercore's Umer Raffat thought the safety language was classic Merck conservatism. With numbers on the table, the skeptics have the better of it for monotherapy.

Now the biology, because this is where the take lives. Every drug that has worked in diabetic macular edema for twenty years suppresses vascular endothelial growth factor. V-E-G-F makes vessels leaky and drives new vessel growth, so blocking it dries the retina. Remigromig comes at the problem from the other side. It is a tetravalent, tri-specific antibody that activates Wnt signaling through Frizzled-four, the receptor that the natural ligand Norrin uses in the retina. The human genetics here are unusually clean. Loss-of-function mutations in Norrin, Frizzled-four or its co-receptors cause Norrie disease and familial exudative vitreoretinopathy, in which retinal vessels form badly and leak. So the pathway is the one that builds and maintains the blood-retina barrier. Remigromig's thesis is repair: instead of suppressing the leak signal, rebuild the seal.

Here is the key analytical point about the safety signal. The control arm is not neutral. Anti-V-E-G-F therapy does not just treat macular edema. It slows diabetic retinopathy itself. In Genentech's RISE and RIDE trials, monthly ranibizumab reduced progression to proliferative disease compared with sham, and the D-R-C-R network's Protocol W showed aflibercept cut the development of proliferative disease in patients who did not yet have edema. So a zero point nine percent rate on monthly Lucentis is a suppressed rate. It is what you get when every patient receives a potent anti-angiogenic every month. D'Amico made exactly this argument in New Orleans: the remigromig rates were better than the sham arms of RISE and RIDE and the laser arms of the VISTA and VIVID trials, and he called the imbalance mechanistically expected. He also said eighty-five percent of the patients who hit a proliferative event still had improved vision at one year.

That is a real argument, but it does not close the question, for two reasons. First, cross-trial comparisons to sham arms from fifteen years ago, enrolled under different standards of diabetes care, are weak evidence. Second, there is a less comfortable mechanistic possibility. Norrin and Frizzled-four signaling is not only a barrier-maintenance pathway. In development it actively drives the growth of the deep retinal vessels. So "remigromig fails to suppress new vessels" and "remigromig modestly promotes new vessels" are both biologically plausible, and the composite endpoint cannot distinguish them. What would distinguish them: the event counts by baseline retinopathy severity, timing of onset, and whether events cluster in patients who were already close to proliferative disease at entry. Merck says additional analyses are underway. Those analyses are the most important thing to watch, more than the topline of the second trial.

Put the two hypotheses side by side and you get the strategic conclusion. Either way, the data say that a Wnt agonist used alone gives up something anti-V-E-G-F therapy provides for free: control of new vessel growth. In a disease where the standard of care is cheap, available as biosimilars, and has twenty years of safety data, a replacement that is three letters worse with six times the proliferative events has no obvious patient. The clinicians' argument for remigromig was never really "replace anti-V-E-G-F." It was the thirty to forty percent of patients with persistent edema despite anti-V-E-G-F. Brunello was not designed to test that population, so the strongest version of the case is still untested.

Which points to combinations, and here the landscape gets interesting. Merck already owns the combination logic in a different molecule. MK-eighty-seven-forty-eight, which it calls Tiespectus and also came from EyeBio, is a bispecific that activates the Tie-two vessel-stabilizing pathway and blocks V-E-G-F at the same time. It is in two pivotal trials in wet macular degeneration and two in diabetic macular edema. That is the same playbook Roche used with Vabysmo, which pairs V-E-G-F blockade with angiopoietin-two inhibition and became the commercial benchmark for adding a stabilizing mechanism on top of V-E-G-F suppression. The direct read-across is Surrozen. Its lead, S-Z-N-eighty-one-forty-one, is a single molecule that is both a Frizzled-four Wnt agonist and a V-E-G-F antagonist, exactly the design the Brunello safety signal argues for. The I-N-D is cleared, the Phase one-b two-a DUET trial is due to dose its first patient this quarter, and the first data are guided for the second half of twenty twenty-seven. Surrozen's stock doubled on Merck's topline last month, which was the market saying the mechanism was validated even if the molecule was not. Saturday's numbers sharpen that: the Wnt mechanism moves vision about as well as the gold standard, and the safety gap is, at least plausibly, exactly what you would close by adding V-E-G-F blockade. Surrozen also has a second molecule, S-Z-N-eighty-one-forty-three, that adds interleukin-six blockade, and Roche's vamikibart data at the same meeting, with an F-D-A decision now expected by July twenty twenty-seven, are a reminder that interleukin-six is a real eye target too.

Some context on the deal. Merck bought EyeBio in twenty twenty-four for one point three billion dollars up front and up to one point seven billion more in milestones. EyeBio's chief executive, David Guyer, co-founded Eyetech, which brought the first anti-V-E-G-F drug, Macugen, to market, so the team behind remigromig is the team that started the anti-V-E-G-F era. B-M-O framed Brunello as a reminder that not every acquired asset becomes a commercial success, while saying the broader Merck thesis is intact, and that is the right size for this. Remigromig is not a Keytruda-cliff problem. It is an option that has repriced from "first new mechanism in twenty years" to "a mechanism that probably needs a partner."

What happens next. BAROLO, a second pivotal trial of similar size and design, is expected within months. A second positive non-inferiority result would let Merck file, but it will not change the positioning unless the proliferative signal shrinks. Year two of Brunello moves patients to personalized dosing intervals, which is the first real test of whether barrier repair is durable, the one thing that could differentiate a drug that does not beat anti-V-E-G-F on vision. A three-month or four-month interval with stable vision would change the commercial story. Monthly dosing would not. And watch whether Merck starts a trial of remigromig on top of anti-V-E-G-F in persistent edema. That would be the clearest sign Merck has read its own data the same way.

The bottom line. Brunello shows that Wnt activation is a genuine, second mechanism for restoring vision in diabetic macular edema, the first one in two decades to get to a pivotal win. It also shows, fairly clearly, that it is not a replacement for V-E-G-F suppression. Whether the excess proliferative events are a lost benefit or an added risk is the open question, and it has an answer sitting in Merck's data. The investment conclusion is less about Merck, for which this is a rounding error, than about the companies that built the combination from the start.
