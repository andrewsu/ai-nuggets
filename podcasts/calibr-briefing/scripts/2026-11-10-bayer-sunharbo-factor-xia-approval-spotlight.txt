Good morning, Travis. Today's spotlight is Sunharbo, Bayer's asundexian, the first Factor eleven-a inhibitor approved in the United States. Back in August we covered milvexian failing in heart attack patients and argued that this class wins only where it has a real gap to fill. Monday's approval puts that argument to a commercial test. Here's what the label says, what the trial does and doesn't show, and how the rest of the class lines up.

Start with the biology, briefly. Most clotting starts when tissue is injured, and the cascade amplifies that signal into a clot. Factor eleven sits on a loop that makes clots bigger and harder to break down. It contributes much less to the initial plug that seals a cut vessel. People born without much Factor eleven have fewer strokes and venous clots, and they mostly don't have a serious bleeding disorder. So the long-standing promise of the class is a blood thinner that separates preventing clots from causing bleeding. Every anticoagulant approved before this one trades one against the other.

Now the label. On Monday evening the F-D-A approved Sunharbo, a fifty-milligram tablet taken once a day, together with antiplatelet therapy, to reduce the risk of stroke in adults after a recent mild-to-moderate non-cardioembolic ischemic stroke, or after a high-risk transient ischemic attack. In plain terms: someone who had a stroke or a mini-stroke from a clot that didn't come from the heart, and who would today go home on aspirin, clopidogrel, or both. The label says outright that the drug is not approved for stroke prevention in atrial fibrillation. That matters, because Bayer's atrial fibrillation trial, OCEANIC-AF, was stopped in twenty twenty-three when asundexian proved less effective than Eliquis. The F-D-A gave the drug fast track and priority review. China approved it first.

The evidence is OCEANIC-STROKE, published in the New England Journal of Medicine this year. Twelve thousand three hundred twenty-seven patients at seven hundred two centers in thirty-seven countries, randomized within seventy-two hours of their symptoms to asundexian or placebo on top of single or dual antiplatelet therapy. Over a median of about fourteen months on treatment, ischemic stroke occurred in six point two percent on drug and eight point four percent on placebo. That's a hazard ratio of zero point seven four, a twenty-six percent relative reduction. STAT's framing is the useful clinical one: treat about fifty-three patients for a year to prevent one recurrent stroke. The composite of cardiovascular death, heart attack or stroke also improved. Major bleeding, the primary safety endpoint, was one point nine percent versus one point seven, a difference that wasn't statistically significant. Overall and serious adverse event rates were the same in both arms.

Two details in the label and the paper explain the drug better than the headline does.

The first is timing. The F-D-A says major bleeding was higher with asundexian in the first ninety days of treatment and in patients over seventy-five, and that gastrointestinal bleeding was more common overall. The paper also shows that the reduction in ischemic stroke over the first ninety days wasn't statistically significant: three point oh versus three point five percent, which stopped formal testing of the lower-ranked secondary endpoints. So the excess bleeding comes early, and the benefit builds up later. That makes sense biologically. In the first weeks after a stroke, patients are often on dual antiplatelet therapy, some have just received a clot-busting drug, and the injured brain is at its most fragile. The label says to wait at least twenty-four hours after thrombolysis. So the scenario Bayer pitched, starting a new drug in the hospital alongside dual antiplatelets, carries more risk than the overall trial result suggests. Over the long run, once a patient is on a single antiplatelet drug, it looks like a safer add-on. I expect neurologists to split on when to start it, and I expect the guideline committees to spend their time on exactly that question.

The second is the number needed to treat. Fifty-three patients for a year is a real benefit for a disease where a second stroke is often worse than the first, but it doesn't make this a statin. The list price is seven hundred forty-eight dollars and fifty cents for thirty days, close to nine thousand dollars a year, for patients who are mostly on generic aspirin and clopidogrel. Bayer's pharma head, Stefan Oelrich, said in August that if the launch has one limiting factor, it will be access. Sebastian Guth, Bayer's U-S president, told BioPharma Dive that stroke neurologists will drive early use and primary care will matter more over time, and that payers already understand the cost of stroke. That will be tested in formulary negotiations, with a fifty-three number needed to treat and a bleeding signal in older patients in the documents.

On the money: pharmaphorum reports analyst peak sales estimates around three billion euros. Bayer's own published forecast is closer to one billion, cut after the atrial fibrillation failure removed the largest indication. For context, Bayer's Xarelto, a Factor ten-a inhibitor sold outside the U-S, brought in seven hundred thirty-eight million euros in the first half of this year, down about forty-three percent on generic competition. Sunharbo becomes Bayer's third growth product alongside Nubeqa in prostate cancer and Kerendia in kidney disease and heart failure. It won't replace Xarelto revenue, but it gives Bayer the first approved drug in a class that every big cardiovascular company has been chasing.

Now the competition, because the next six months are crowded.

The closest rival is Bristol Myers and J&J's milvexian, also an oral Factor eleven-a inhibitor. LIBREXIA-STROKE, a twelve-and-a-half-thousand-patient trial in nearly the same population, reached its primary completion date last month according to ClinicalTrials-dot-gov. It uses twenty-five milligrams twice a day, the same dose that failed in heart attack patients this summer. As we said in August, that's the same total daily amount as asundexian's fifty milligrams once a day. Because the populations overlap so closely, this readout tells us whether OCEANIC-STROKE reflects the class or just one trial. If milvexian matches it, the class has two products, and Bayer's first-mover advantage comes down to a few months and its contracts. If milvexian misses, Bayer has the stroke indication to itself. The larger trial is LIBREXIA-AF, about twenty thousand patients against Eliquis at a much higher dose, a hundred milligrams twice a day, reading out early next year. That's the indication asundexian couldn't win, and the reason Leerink still models two and a half billion dollars in risk-adjusted milvexian sales in twenty thirty-two.

Behind the pills are the long-acting drugs. Novartis paid nine hundred twenty-five million dollars up front for Anthos, in a deal worth up to three point one billion, to get abelacimab, a monthly antibody against Factor eleven. Its Phase 3, LILAC, enrolls atrial fibrillation patients whose doctors consider them unsuitable for any oral anticoagulant, and has a primary completion date at the end of twenty twenty-seven. Regeneron has the broadest program: two Factor eleven antibodies, cenvacibart and a second one called REGN-ninety-nine thirty-three, in at least eight Phase 3 trials across knee replacement, catheter clots, cancer-associated thrombosis, peripheral artery disease and atrial fibrillation, including a fifteen-thousand-patient trial against apixaban. One detail matters most for Bayer: Regeneron registered ROXI-DAISEN, a six-thousand-patient Phase 3 of cenvacibart in secondary stroke prevention, Sunharbo's exact indication, with a planned start date of November twentieth. Further back, City Therapeutics, which just went public, and ADARx are each developing s-i-R-N-A drugs that silence Factor eleven in the liver for months at a time.

That lineup leads to my first takeaway. The August argument holds up. Factor eleven-a inhibition failed against Eliquis in atrial fibrillation and failed on top of potent dual antiplatelet therapy after a heart attack. It succeeded where the comparator was antiplatelet therapy alone and the patients had nothing better. The class wins by filling real gaps, not by replacing the current anticoagulants. Every sponsor's trial choices now reflect that: Novartis and Regeneron are both running trials in atrial fibrillation patients who can't take anticoagulants, and Regeneron is going straight at secondary stroke.

Second, the dosing format may matter more than being first. A daily pill whose bleeding risk is concentrated in the first ninety days is a product you can start, pause before surgery, and stop. A monthly antibody or a twice-yearly s-i-R-N-A is something else: it solves adherence, which is a big problem in secondary prevention, but it can't be switched off when a patient needs emergency surgery or has a bleed, unless the sponsor also develops a reversal agent. Monday's label, with its twenty-four-hour holds around surgery and thrombolysis, shows how often that question will come up. Which format wins depends on whether doctors value adherence over reversibility, and so far nobody has data on that.

Third, for Calibr and for Foresite, this is a clean example of what human genetics can and can't promise. The Factor eleven genetics correctly predicted the safety profile: a large trial added an anticoagulant on top of antiplatelets without a significant increase in major bleeding. They said nothing about which populations had a real gap left to fill. It took about four Phase 3 failures and successes across two companies to map that out. When a target is genetically validated, budget for the trials it takes to find the indication where it actually wins.

That's the spotlight. Bayer has the first approval in the most anticipated cardiovascular class of the decade, with a narrower label, a more expensive product, and an earlier bleeding signal than the twenty-six percent headline suggests. The milvexian stroke readout, likely within months, will show whether the class has one product in this indication or two. Talk soon.
