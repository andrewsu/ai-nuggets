Today's spotlight is Novartis, Avidity, and del-desiran — and the thing worth your time is not that a rare-disease drug failed. It is that this particular drug hit every biological milestone it was supposed to hit, and still lost, which makes it a clean experiment on one of the most expensive assumptions in rare-disease development.

Here is what happened. Yesterday morning Novartis said the Phase three HARBOR study of del-desiran in myotonic dystrophy type one did not achieve statistical significance on its primary endpoint. HARBOR randomized about one hundred fifty patients, double-blind, placebo-controlled, intravenous dosing every eight weeks, fifty-four weeks of treatment. The primary endpoint was video hand opening time. The secondaries were hand grip strength, quantitative muscle testing, the DM1-Activ scale of daily activities, and the ten-meter walk-run test. Novartis reported clinical activity in secondary endpoints and exploratory analyses, said safety was consistent with prior data, and released not one number. It is evaluating the full dataset and will talk to regulators about a path forward. In the same release it reaffirmed five-to-six-percent sales growth guidance through twenty thirty, which is the tell that this was written for the equity market. The New York shares closed around one hundred thirty-eight fifty-one, down roughly ten percent, the worst day since March twenty twenty, about twenty-four billion Swiss francs of market capitalization.

Now the mechanism, because it is the reason the miss is interesting rather than routine. Myotonic dystrophy type one is caused by an expanded C-T-G repeat in the DMPK gene. The pathology is not loss of the DMPK protein — it is that the expanded repeat is transcribed into an RNA that folds into a hairpin and acts as a molecular sponge, sequestering the muscleblind-like family of splicing regulators. Take MBNL out of circulation and splicing goes wrong across dozens of transcripts in many tissues at once. Mis-splice the CLCN1 chloride channel and the muscle membrane cannot repolarize properly, which is myotonia — the delayed relaxation that makes a patient unable to open a clenched fist. Mis-splice the insulin receptor and you get insulin resistance. Mis-splice cardiac channels and you get conduction disease. So this is a single toxic RNA producing a multisystem splicing disease, and the therapeutic logic is beautifully direct: degrade the toxic transcript, MBNL goes back to work, splicing normalizes, symptoms follow.

The reason nobody could execute that logic for twenty years is delivery. Skeletal muscle is roughly forty percent of body mass, poorly vascularized relative to liver, and naked oligonucleotides essentially do not enter it at therapeutic concentrations. Avidity's antibody-oligonucleotide conjugate was the answer to that specific problem: a monoclonal antibody against transferrin receptor one, which muscle expresses abundantly, chemically conjugated to a small interfering RNA against DMPK. The antibody is a delivery vehicle, not a therapeutic. And it worked. The Phase one-two MARINA trial, published in the New England Journal of Medicine, showed dose-dependent siRNA concentrations in muscle biopsies, DMPK messenger RNA knocked down roughly thirty-seven to forty-six percent, and measurable amelioration of mis-splicing, mostly at the two and four milligram per kilogram doses. Exploratory efficacy favored the higher dose, including a day-one-hundred-eighty-three quantitative muscle testing composite of plus three point eight four at four milligrams per kilogram against minus one point zero one on placebo. Then the open-label extension, MARINA-OLE, reported something stronger: not just slowing but reversal of disease progression across video hand opening time, muscle strength and activities of daily living, when compared against the END-DM1 natural history cohort. F-D-A gave it orphan, fast track and breakthrough therapy designations. Japan gave it orphan status. By the time Novartis paid, this looked like the most de-risked asset in muscle biology.

And that phrase — compared against natural history — is where I would put the entire failure. MARINA-OLE was an open-label extension benchmarked to an external cohort. There are two independent ways that flatters a program, and both of them apply with unusual force here. The first is that video hand opening time is a performance-based task the patient repeats at every visit, filmed, in front of staff who know they are on drug. Performance tasks have learning curves. The second is subtler and more important: an external natural-history cohort is not a concurrent control. It cannot capture regression to the mean in a trial population enrolled because they were symptomatic, it cannot capture the trial-participation effect on activity and physiotherapy, and it cannot capture drift in how the measurement is administered between studies. Every one of those pushes the same direction. HARBOR is the first properly randomized, adequately sized read of video hand opening time in this disease, and the answer it gave is that the natural-history-controlled effect did not survive concurrent placebo.

The second-order question is whether the endpoint itself was the wrong choice, and I think there is a real argument that it was chosen for mechanistic proximity rather than clinical importance. Myotonia is the symptom most tightly coupled to the splicing defect you are correcting — CLCN1 mis-splicing to chloride conductance to delayed relaxation is a two-step chain, so if the drug does anything at all, myotonia should move first and fastest. That is genuinely good endpoint reasoning and it is why both companies in this space converged on it. But myotonia is not what most DM1 patients rank as their worst problem. Progressive weakness, fatigue and excessive daytime sleepiness usually outrank it, and those are what the secondary endpoints in HARBOR were measuring. So Novartis is now in the position of saying the secondaries showed activity while the primary failed — which is a legitimate scientific claim and a nearly worthless regulatory one, and the reason they are going to regulators rather than filing.

What survives all of this is the platform, and you should separate the two claims cleanly, because Novartis will. The conjugate delivered oligonucleotide into human skeletal muscle, knocked down its target, and corrected splicing, reproducibly, across studies. That pharmacology is a real asset and it does not go away because a functional endpoint in one indication did not clear. The same release confirmed the F-D-A has granted priority review to delpacibart zotadirsen — del-zota — in Duchenne muscular dystrophy patients amenable to exon forty-four skipping, and that Novartis is going to the agency on delpacibart braxlosiran, del-brax, in facioscapulohumeral muscular dystrophy on the strength of positive Phase one-two biomarker data.

But notice the structural problem that creates. Both of those programs are built on surrogates. Del-zota's accelerated approval case is dystrophin restoration. Del-brax's case is a biomarker package in a disease driven by aberrant DUX4 expression. And HARBOR is now a documented instance, in this exact modality, in this exact tissue, from this exact platform, where robust target engagement plus a favorable functional signal against natural history did not convert to a controlled functional win. That is precisely the risk a reviewer is pricing when they grant accelerated approval on a biomarker, and the DMD field has been arguing about it since Sarepta. I do not think HARBOR blocks del-zota — the exon-skipping precedent in DMD is well established and the review is already underway — but it raises the probability that the confirmatory-evidence conversation gets more demanding, and it makes the del-brax meeting a harder one.

On competitive read-across, Dyne Therapeutics fell twenty-two percent, and I want to be fair about why, because the two situations are not identical. Dyne's DYNE-one-oh-one uses the same route — a transferrin receptor one-directed antibody fragment conjugated to an oligonucleotide — against the same target in the same disease. In its ACHIEVE study, the six point eight milligram per kilogram every-eight-weeks cohort reported a three point three second improvement in video hand opening time versus placebo at six months, alongside substantial DMPK knockdown and splicing correction. That comparison was placebo-controlled, which is a meaningful distinction from MARINA-OLE. What HARBOR does to Dyne is not falsify that number — it makes the sample size the whole argument. A three point three second placebo-adjusted effect in a small dose cohort at six months is exactly the size and setting where effects are systematically overestimated, and Dyne's plan is to file for accelerated approval on a registrational expansion cohort of roughly thirty-two patients plus fifty-six from the long-term extension, on biomarker and functional data. A one-hundred-fifty-patient, fifty-four-week randomized failure on the same endpoint is the strongest available prior on what happens when you scale that up. There is also a live dose hypothesis in Novartis's favor: roughly forty percent DMPK knockdown may simply be below the threshold for functional benefit, in which case the modality is right and the exposure was wrong. Dyne dosing higher is then a virtue rather than a liability. Watch whether Novartis discloses the exposure-response relationship in HARBOR, because that single figure determines whether this is a molecule problem or a target problem.

Then there is the deal, which is what is actually driving the share price. Novartis paid seventy-two dollars a share in cash, about twelve billion dollars, closing February twenty-seventh of this year, after Avidity spun its early-stage precision cardiology programs out into Atrium Therapeutics — one Atrium share for every ten Avidity shares, now trading on the recycled ticker. Barclays carried del-desiran at three point one billion dollars in peak annual sales with a sixty percent probability of success. Jefferies carried it at one point five billion at eighty percent, and Michael Leuchten's note yesterday made two points that matter more than the forecast: that the acquisition came with controversy when it was struck, and that this readout needed to deliver to justify the price — with the follow-on question of whether Novartis can hold mid-single-digit growth through twenty thirty without doing more M-and-A. That question has teeth because Entresto and Cosentyx exclusivity are the reason the growth has to come from somewhere, and because this is the third bad print in eight days: eight rapcabtagene autoleucel CAR-T trials paused after three deaths from immune effector cell-associated hemophagocytic syndrome, pelacarsen failing Lp-little-a HORIZON with eighty percent lipoprotein-little-a lowering and no event reduction, and now HARBOR. The company does have a genuine offset in remibrutinib's Phase three win in relapsing multiple sclerosis, and reaffirming guidance the same morning was the correct move. But three independent failure modes in eight days — a safety halt, a target-validity failure, and an endpoint failure — is not one bad week of luck. It is a portfolio that was assembled on the premise that target engagement plus a plausible surrogate equals clinical benefit.

So, three things to take from this. First, the durable lesson is about controls, not about RNA. A performance-based primary endpoint validated against external natural history is the single most expensive assumption you can carry into Phase three, and twelve billion dollars was paid on a package where that assumption had never been tested against a concurrent placebo arm. If you are looking at any rare-disease asset whose functional evidence comes from an open-label extension benchmarked to a natural-history cohort, this is now the comparison to cite in that diligence conversation. Second, keep platform and indication separate in your own read. Antibody-oligonucleotide conjugation into skeletal muscle is validated pharmacology as of yesterday just as it was the day before — it delivered, knocked down and corrected splicing. What is unvalidated is that correcting splicing at forty percent knockdown, over fifty-four weeks, produces a functional benefit a regulator can see in myotonic dystrophy. Those are different sentences and the market is currently pricing them as one. Third, the specific number to wait for is the HARBOR placebo arm on video hand opening time. If placebo improved, this is an endpoint and trial-conduct story, the secondaries are more believable, and a redesigned trial on a strength or activities-of-daily-living primary is a real if slow option. If placebo declined as expected and the drug simply did not separate, this is a pharmacology story, forty percent knockdown is not enough, and the read-through to Dyne is much worse than a twenty-two-percent day would suggest.
