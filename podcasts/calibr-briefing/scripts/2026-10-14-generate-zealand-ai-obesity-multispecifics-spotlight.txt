Good morning Travis. Today's spotlight is the deal Zealand Pharma and Generate Biomedicines announced after the U-S close on Tuesday. Zealand is a Danish peptide company that has had a bruising month in obesity. Generate is the Flagship generative protein design company that went public in February. On its face it's a modest licensing deal. What makes it worth the time is that it shows what a peptide-native obesity company thinks it's missing, and it puts a current price on A-I protein design as a service. I'll cover the terms, why Zealand needed this, why multispecific proteins are the right place for a design platform to prove itself, and what it says about how the market values these platforms.

Here are the terms. Generate gets thirty million dollars up front for two initial programs. It gets up to thirty-five million more in early preclinical milestones and nearly one point two billion dollars in total milestones, plus tiered royalties on worldwide sales. Zealand has an option to add a third program. Generate runs research through lead-candidate selection using its platform, which pairs generative models with high-throughput lab testing so each design round is checked against real binding and function data. Zealand then takes over clinical development, regulatory filings and worldwide commercialization. The molecules are multispecific proteins for obesity and other metabolic diseases, meaning single molecules built to hit more than one target. Neither company named the targets or gave timelines. Generate's shares rose about five percent in after-hours trading, according to Reuters. I couldn't find any analyst notes on the deal yet, so the analysis that follows is mine and not the Street's.

Now look at it from Zealand's side, because that's where the deal makes the most sense. Zealand has spent twenty-five years making peptides. It invented survodutide, the glucagon and G-L-P-one dual agonist that Boehringer Ingelheim licensed in twenty eleven. It also invented petrelintide, the long-acting amylin analog that Roche partnered last year in a deal worth up to five point three billion dollars. Both reported data in the last two weeks, and investors didn't like either readout. On October first, Boehringer's SYNCHRONIZE-two Phase three showed up to thirteen point one percent weight loss at seventy-six weeks if patients stayed on drug. Counting everyone, the published figure was nine point eight percent, and about eighteen percent of treated patients stopped because of gastrointestinal side effects. A week later, petrelintide's Phase two in patients with type two diabetes gave up to nine point two percent weight loss at twenty-eight weeks. Tolerability was close to placebo, but hemoglobin A-one-C fell only about two-thirds of a point. William Blair said it was disappointed by the magnitude, and now sees petrelintide mainly as a maintenance drug. European Biotechnology reports that Zealand's stock has lost nearly half its value this year.

Zealand had started diversifying before those readouts. Last December it paid the Chinese company O-T-R Therapeutics thirty million dollars up front to find oral small molecules. In January it signed on to use Denmark's Gefion A-I supercomputer, and it set up a research hub in Cambridge, Massachusetts. Generate fills the remaining gap, which is proteins. Zealand's chief scientific officer, Utpal Singh, described the deal as adding proteins to its small-molecule and peptide toolbox and opening new approaches to polypharmacology. Put simply, Zealand is buying a capability it doesn't have, not a specific molecule.

So why multispecific proteins, and why now? Until now, the obesity field has combined mechanisms through peptide co-agonism. Tirzepatide, survodutide and retatrutide each hit two or three related hormone receptors with a single peptide. That works because those receptors are closely related. Peptides can't easily combine unrelated biology, such as blocking one receptor while activating another, or reaching a target on fat or muscle, and they can't easily get to monthly dosing. Antibody-based scaffolds can. The template is Amgen's MariTide. It's an antibody that blocks the G-I-P receptor, with two G-L-P-one agonist peptides attached, and it's given monthly. The Phase two showed average weight loss of roughly twelve to sixteen percent at a year in people without diabetes, and the first Phase three, MARITIME-one, has a primary completion date in January twenty twenty-seven. The rest of the field is moving toward biology that only proteins or R-N-A can reach. Muscle preservation through activin and myostatin antibodies is one example. Another is A-L-K-seven, the fat-tissue receptor behind Roche's R-N-A interference deal with Rona Therapeutics, worth up to about one point two billion dollars and announced the same day. A peptide company that can't build proteins misses out on that next wave.

This is also the part that matters for judging A-I design. In August we looked at Generate's lead antibody, golukibart against T-S-L-P. There the model produced about twenty-fold tighter binding than tezepelumab. But the six-month dosing that the commercial case depends on came from an off-the-shelf half-life mutation, and at the chosen dose the extra affinity turned out to be surplus. Multispecifics are a harder and fairer test. You have to balance the potency of two or three binding arms against receptors that are expressed at very different levels. If one arm is too strong it dominates, and if it's too weak the combination adds nothing. You also need the whole molecule to be manufacturable and stable. That's a constrained optimization across many objectives at once, and it's exactly what generative design says it's better at than iterative engineering. If Generate hits the thirty-five million dollars in early milestones quickly, that will be the first outside evidence that it can.

Now the price. Generate's earlier deals were with Amgen, which paid fifty million dollars up front in twenty twenty-two on a deal reported at up to one point nine billion, and Novartis. In twenty twenty-four Novartis paid fifty million up front, bought fifteen million dollars of equity and promised up to a billion in milestones. This deal has the smallest upfront of the three, for two programs, and nearly all the headline value is in milestones that depend on Zealand's development decisions. That's typical for platform deals right now. G-S-K's deal with Chai Discovery last Friday had no disclosed terms at all. Lilly's deal with Insilico in May was billed at two point seven five billion dollars. Big companies are still paying A-I design shops like research partners with option value attached, not like owners of assets. For Generate, thirty million dollars is a little under half of one quarter's net loss, which was sixty-seven million in the second quarter. The company had four hundred fifty-seven million dollars in cash at the end of June and guides runway into the first half of twenty twenty-eight. Its real value driver is still golukibart's Phase three. What this deal adds is a third major pharma validating the platform, and Generate's first move into metabolic disease.

For the Foresite and Calibr lens, I'd take three things from it. First, this sets a price for design-only partnerships that Xaira and every other generative protein company will be measured against: about fifteen million dollars up front per program, with the partner owning development. Second, obesity's second wave is moving from peptides to proteins and R-N-A, which favors groups that can engineer complex biologics over those that have optimized one peptide class. Third, watch three things. The first is whether Zealand names targets or exercises the third-program option. The second is MARITIME-one in early twenty twenty-seven, which sets the bar any antibody-based obesity drug has to clear. The third is Iambic's I-P-O pricing this week, which is the public market's own vote on what A-I-native drug discovery is worth. That's the spotlight.
