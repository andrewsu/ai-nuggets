Good morning, Travis. It's Sunday, November eighth, 2026. The trade press was mostly dark on Saturday, but two meetings weren't: the American Heart Association's Scientific Sessions in Chicago, and SITC, the cancer immunotherapy meeting in Phoenix. On the list: the full data from the two biggest cardiovascular failures of the year, the F-D-A's own account of the Zolgensma tumors, George Church walking away from an A-I biology startup over a credit dispute, the biotech industry suing to stop Medicare's international pricing pilot, and the best of the immunotherapy data from SITC. Here's what you need to know.

First, and this is today's spotlight. On Saturday at AHA, with simultaneous publication in the New England Journal of Medicine, Novartis and Novo Nordisk presented the full results of two failed Phase 3 outcomes trials. HORIZON tested pelacarsen, Novartis and Ionis' antisense drug against lipoprotein little a, in more than eight thousand three hundred patients followed for nearly five years. It lowered lipoprotein little a by seventy-eight percent, and the hazard ratio for major cardiovascular events was one point oh two. ZEUS tested ziltivekimab, Novo's antibody against interleukin six, in about sixty-four hundred patients with atherosclerosis, kidney disease and inflammation. It cut free interleukin six by seventy-six points and C-reactive protein by sixty-four, and the hazard ratio was point nine nine. Novartis told TCTMD it is discontinuing the entire cardiovascular program for pelacarsen. Both targets came with some of the strongest human genetics in cardiology, and the spotlight takes on what these results do to that kind of validation.

Second, the F-D-A has put its own words on the Zolgensma tumors that were Friday's spotlight. In a safety communication dated Friday, the agency says it has confirmed reports of product-related insertional oncogenesis involving GLI1, a known oncogene. Three of the tumors were GLI1-associated, and in two of them tumor tissue showed Zolgensma's vector D-N-A integrated into the genome, with the construct's promoter landing upstream of or inside GLI1. The tumors were in the central and peripheral nervous system, the spine and the kidney. The F-D-A points to two published integration events in other A-A-V programs, a GLI1 event with FBX-101 and a PLAG1 event with REGENXBIO's RGX-111, and says it is monitoring the risk across similar A-A-V products. It applies the risk to Itvisma, the intrathecal version, too, because it shares the vector and construct, although no tumors have been reported with Itvisma. For now, the F-D-A says the benefits still outweigh the risks, but it is "evaluating whether regulatory action is appropriate," and reviewing whether the label, which calls integration a "theoretical risk," and the long-term follow-up are adequate. A recurrent integration site next to the same oncogene, seen in two different programs, is what turns a theoretical risk into a class question.

Third, an A-I-in-biology credit fight. George Church has resigned as an adviser to Radical Numerics, the startup founded by members of the team behind the Evo genome language models. Endpoints' headline has him calling the company's behavior "sleazy." In October, Radical launched its Omnii-1 model and billed a bacteriophage given to a patient with a drug-resistant infection as the first A-I-designed phage given to a human. The New York Times has since reported that a partner, Tabula Bioscience, did significant parts of the work, including producing the phage cocktail, and that Radical's announcement left Tabula out. The Times added an editors' note to its original story. Church also advises Tabula. Radical's C-E-O, Eric Nguyen, told the Times the two companies worked closely for seven months and that confidentiality terms in their contract kept them from naming the partner. Radical's technical report is not peer-reviewed, and the company has not shared patient details. For anyone underwriting A-I-designed biologics, the lesson is to ask who did the wet-lab work.

Fourth, BIO, Biocom and California Life Sciences sued on Friday in federal court in Northern California to block GLOBE. That's the Medicare model that requires drugmakers to rebate the difference between U-S prices for some Part B drugs and the government-set prices in nineteen other countries. The groups argue that CMS exceeded its authority to test payment models, and cite an analysis finding that just point three percent of Part B beneficiaries would have paid less out of pocket. BIO's chief legal officer, Joe Franklin, said CMS "crossed the limits Congress placed on its authority." STAT's framing is the right one: GLOBE isn't expected to have much financial impact, but it would set a precedent for importing foreign reference prices.

Fifth, SITC. Immunocore presented the first clinical activity from combining two T-cell engagers in a solid tumor: brenetafusp, its PRAME-targeted ImmTAC, plus Kimmtrak, the gp100-targeted one, first-line in metastatic uveal melanoma. In seventeen patients, the response rate was twenty-nine percent, median progression-free survival was eight point one months, and survival was eighty-eight percent at one year and seventy percent at two. Cytokine release syndrome occurred in seventy-seven percent of patients, none of it grade three or four. A randomized Phase 2/3 called DUET-UVEAL is planned. Kimmtrak already extends survival in this disease, so the question is whether adding PRAME deepens that, and only a randomized trial can answer it.

Two more from SITC, both in melanoma that has progressed on checkpoint inhibitors or hasn't seen them. Strand Therapeutics' STX-001, a self-replicating m-R-N-A encoding interleukin twelve that is injected into the tumor, produced a confirmed thirty-three percent response rate, five of fifteen, in the expansion cohort at the hundred-microgram dose in P-D-one-resistant melanoma. It also shrank lesions that weren't injected. Georgina Long of Melanoma Institute Australia said the pattern is what you'd want: immune activation in the injected lesion, then T cells traveling to the rest. And Evaxion's EVX-01, a personalized neoantigen vaccine whose peptides are picked by A-I, given with Keytruda, showed a seventy-five percent response rate in sixteen checkpoint-naive patients. Eighty-nine percent of the neoantigens it chose induced T-cell responses, and the A-I prediction score correlated with how big those responses were. That last point is the interesting one for A-I in discovery, but this is single-arm, mostly a re-presentation of ESMO data, and Keytruda alone does a lot in this setting.

Sixth, back at AHA: Arrowhead pooled its two Phase 3 trials of plozasiran, an A-P-O-C-three s-i-R-N-A, in severe hypertriglyceridemia, about seven hundred sixty patients. Acute pancreatitis fell seventy-eight percent versus placebo, and ninety-one percent in patients with a recent history of it. Glycemic control worsened in fourteen percent versus about nine on placebo, so watch that. Arrowhead also showed first-in-human data on a single dimeric s-i-R-N-A that silences both P-C-S-K-nine and A-P-O-C-three: one dose cut L-D-L by twenty-six to sixty-five percent and triglycerides by fifty-six to seventy-eight. It's tiny, twenty-one patients on drug, but it's the first dual-target molecule of its kind in the clinic.

Looking ahead: AHA runs through Monday, and the late-breakers still to come include CRISPR Therapeutics' CTX310, an in vivo edit of ANGPTL3; Lilly's orforglipron in sleep apnea; and Boehringer's survodutide outcomes trial. Summit and Akeso's ivonescimab decision is due by November fourteenth, Capricor's deramiocel on the twenty-second, and Vertex's povetacicept by the thirtieth. No Foresite portfolio news crossed the wires over the weekend.
