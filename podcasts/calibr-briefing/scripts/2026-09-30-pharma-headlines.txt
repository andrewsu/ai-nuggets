Good morning Travis. Here are your biotech and pharma headlines for Wednesday, September thirtieth, twenty twenty-six. Two European congresses are running at once — the diabetes meeting in Milan and the dermatology congress in Vienna — and between them they produced the two most consequential datasets of the day. Takeda's next-generation T-Y-K two inhibitor beat Bristol Myers' first-generation one in a head-to-head, uniQure lost about a billion dollars in market value on a four-year Huntington's readout, Roche's brain-penetrant B-T-K inhibitor got a priority review in two forms of multiple sclerosis at once, Lilly put full Phase three numbers behind retatrutide, and the biggest artificial intelligence financing of the week came from a hospital billing company. Nine items, then the week ahead.

First, at the European dermatology congress in Vienna this morning, Takeda presented the LATITUDE Atlas study — a head-to-head Phase three of its T-Y-K two inhibitor zasocitinib against deucravacitinib, the Bristol Myers drug sold as Sotyktu, in moderate-to-severe plaque psoriasis. Six hundred and six patients, thirty milligrams of zasocitinib once daily against six milligrams of deucravacitinib once daily, for sixteen weeks. The primary endpoint was complete skin clearance — a PASI one hundred response, meaning the psoriasis area and severity index goes to zero. Zasocitinib was statistically superior on the primary endpoint and on every ranked secondary endpoint, with separation visible by week eight, and more than two and a half times as many patients reached complete clearance at week sixteen. Both drugs are allosteric T-Y-K two inhibitors that bind the regulatory pseudokinase domain rather than the catalytic site, which is how the class avoids hitting J-A-K one, two and three; Takeda's claim for zasocitinib is more than a million-fold selectivity over the other J-A-Ks and, critically, sustained twenty-four-hour target coverage. That last point is the whole thesis — that Sotyktu's ceiling is a potency and coverage problem, not a target problem, and this readout is the first clinical evidence anyone has that it is. Takeda also reported durability, with up to ninety-three percent of week twenty-four responders holding their clearance to week fifty-two in one pivotal study. Zasocitinib is already under F-D-A priority review, accepted earlier this month, and under E-M-A review, with Phase three going in psoriatic arthritis and Phase two in Crohn's, ulcerative colitis, vitiligo and hidradenitis suppurativa. Sotyktu has been the answer to "can an oral match a biologic"; the answer now has a generation number attached to it.

Second, uniQure released four-year data on A-M-T one thirty, its one-time A-A-V gene therapy that delivers a microRNA to knock down huntingtin production, and the stock fell forty percent, to just under twenty-four dollars, erasing roughly a billion dollars of market value. Twelve patients on the high dose at four years showed a forty-four percent slowing of disease progression on the composite clinical scale against an external natural history control — not statistically significant. A second scale focused on daily function showed sixty-one percent slowing. The problem is the comparison with the three-year cut, which uniQure released at the same time: fifteen patients, eighty percent and sixty-seven percent slowing on those same two scales. So the numbers got worse as the follow-up got longer, which is exactly the shape investors fear in a durability story. uniQure's defense is the external control itself — at the forty-eight-month reference point, fifty-three percent of the natural history data was missing, because patients with a neurodegenerative disease drop out of observational studies as they decline, and the ones who stay are the ones doing better. Impute that and the company gets fifty-four and sixty-eight percent. Leerink's analysts wrote that they recognize all the caveats and agree with them, and then said that in Huntington's, slowing of any kind on the functional capacity scale is absolutely unheard of, and that making patients wait for a better-conducted Phase three seems inappropriate. Stifel's Paul Matteis called the four-year data still impressive with some regression, and pointed out that the three-year cut is the one that matters, because that is the data the F-D-A alignment was built on. The application went in late this summer under a rapid review. Leerink has previously modeled a one point seven million dollar price and two and a half billion dollars in twenty-thirty sales.

Third, the F-D-A accepted Genentech's new drug application under priority review for fenebrutinib in both relapsing multiple sclerosis and primary progressive multiple sclerosis — the first B-T-K inhibitor to be filed in both, and if approved, the first high-efficacy oral option in either. Fenebrutinib is non-covalent and reversible, which is the distinguishing feature: most B-T-K inhibitors form a permanent bond, and the reversible design plus central nervous system penetration is what lets it target microglia inside the brain, not just peripheral B cells. That is the mechanistic argument for why it might touch progression and not just relapses. In the two FENhance relapsing trials it cut relapses fifty-one and fifty-eight percent versus teriflunomide, with a consistent positive trend on disability. In FENtrepid, it became the first investigational drug in more than a decade to slow disability progression in a Phase three in primary progressive disease, with a numerical benefit against Ocrevus — Roche's own drug, and still the only approved therapy in that population. Note the safety language carefully: liver enzyme elevations were more frequent with fenebrutinib than with Ocrevus in the progressive study, and Roche disclosed an imbalance in reported deaths across the three pivotal trials, attributed to different timepoints and different causes. With more than twenty-seven hundred patients in the safety database, that is the part the review will turn on.

Fourth, Lilly published the full Triumph-two dataset on retatrutide at the diabetes meeting and in The Lancet. Eleven hundred and fifty-two adults with obesity and type two diabetes; mean weight loss on the top twelve milligram dose was twenty point eight percent at week eighty against four percent on placebo — that part was known from July. What is new is the distribution. Nearly thirty-five percent of patients on the top dose lost at least a quarter of their body weight, fifty-two percent lost at least a fifth, and more than half no longer met the body mass index criterion for obesity. For comparison, tirzepatide moved thirty-four percent of patients past twenty percent weight loss in its Phase three. Retatrutide adds glucagon receptor agonism to the G-I-P and G-L-P-one activity of tirzepatide, and the trade-off is visible in the glucose data: the A-one-C reduction was one point four to one point six percent with no dose response, and about forty percent of patients on the higher doses got into the normal range, against up to fifty-five percent for tirzepatide in an earlier study. In other words, the glucagon arm buys weight and not glycemia. Cardiovascular risk factors moved hard — triglycerides down almost forty percent, systolic blood pressure down almost eleven millimeters of mercury, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein down fifty-eight percent. Filing is planned for the first quarter of next year. And at the same meeting, T-D Cowen flagged twelve-week Phase one data on Hengrui and Kailera's triple agonist that in their words looks better than retatrutide.

Fifth, and on the other side of the obesity field, Zealand and Roche finally showed the primary endpoint from the Zupreme one trial of petrelintide, the amylin analog Roche paid one point six five billion dollars upfront for last year. Weight loss at week twenty-eight was seven point nine percent on the bottom two doses, nine point eight percent on five milligrams — and then it stopped. Seven milligrams gave nine point three percent, nine milligrams gave nine point four. The dose-response curve is flat above the middle dose, which means there is no headroom to chase. Placebo-adjusted, the best result is eight point one percent. Lilly's amylin agonist eloralintide has produced up to twenty point one percent at week forty-eight in Phase two. Roche and Zealand are arguing tolerability instead, and they have a case — nausea at twenty percent against six percent on placebo, but vomiting and diarrhea the same or lower than placebo. B-M-O's analysts have been making the first-line argument for amylins on exactly that basis. Roche starts three Phase three trials today anyway, all monotherapy, in obesity alone, obesity with type two diabetes, and obesity with cardiovascular disease.

Sixth, Beam Therapeutics sued YolTech Therapeutics and its U-S partner Serapha Bio in Massachusetts federal court, alleging that a competing alpha-one antitrypsin deficiency program was built on stolen trade secrets. Beam claims a scientist who worked there from twenty twenty-one to twenty twenty-two secretly founded YolTech in China while still employed, accessed dozens of Beam records she had never opened before — across technical areas outside her own, at night and on weekends — and filed patents for YolTech five months after leaving. Serapha's therapy corrects the same mutation at the same nucleotide as Beam's B-E-A-M three-oh-two. Serapha licensed it from YolTech, raised two hundred thirty million dollars from R-A Capital and R-T-W, hired Kenneth Mills from Regenxbio as chief executive, and plans to start a Phase two-three late this year or early next. Serapha says it categorically refutes the claims. Whatever the merits, this is the first real trade-secret fight of the China in-licensing wave, and every board doing a China deal will now be asked where the molecule came from.

Seventh, a launch worth your attention. ai3Bio came out of stealth with forty-eight million dollars in Series A money, built from the remains of two companies — Corner Therapeutics, founded on Jonathan Kagan's innate immunity work at Harvard and Boston Children's, and Novasenta, a cancer immunology spinout from the University of Pittsburgh. Steven Altschuler, formerly chief executive of Children's Hospital of Philadelphia and a Spark co-founder, is running it. The thesis is a deliberate break from the immune reset consensus: instead of depleting B cells with a cell therapy or a bispecific, ai3Bio wants to delete T-helper seventeen cells, the interleukin-seventeen-producing subset behind a lot of skin and gut disease. Two modalities — a lipid nanoparticle that delivers instructions telling the cell to kill itself, and a depleting antibody. Kagan's line on autoimmune cell therapy is worth repeating: you are eliminating these cells by cracking them open and inducing inflammation in the face of an inflammatory environment, which he calls pouring gasoline onto the fire. U-P-M-C Enterprises and Ziff Capital led. No lead indication named yet.

Eighth, also in Vienna, Egle Therapeutics reported Phase one single-ascending-dose data on E-G-L double-oh-three, an interleukin-two mutein engineered to expand regulatory T cells selectively. Twenty-four healthy volunteers across four dose levels up to five micrograms per kilogram. The drug was well tolerated, produced dose-dependent regulatory T-cell expansion with no amplification of conventional C-D four, C-D eight or natural killer populations, and — the interesting part — the pharmacodynamic markers were consistent with preferential activation of skin-homing effector memory regulatory T cells. That is the selectivity problem the whole low-dose interleukin-two field has been fighting for a decade, and a tissue-homing signal in healthy volunteers is more than most have shown. A multiple-ascending-dose proof-of-concept study in atopic dermatitis starts in the fourth quarter.

Ninth, and today's spotlight. Ortet launched as what it calls a frontier artificial intelligence lab for health, with a five hundred million dollar commitment. It is led by Kyunghyun Cho, who co-developed the gated recurrent unit and the attention mechanism, spent five years building Genentech's lab-in-the-loop molecular design platform after it acquired his company Prescient Design, and walked out in January. The money did not come from a biotech investor. It came from a platform that bought a hospital revenue cycle management company for twelve billion dollars in June. That is the spotlight.

A four-hundred-patient Phase two-b starts this quarter.
