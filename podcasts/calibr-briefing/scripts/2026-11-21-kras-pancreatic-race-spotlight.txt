Welcome to the spotlight, Travis. Today it's the K-RAS race in pancreatic cancer. At the Triple Meeting in Barcelona this week, Revolution Medicines showed what its approved drug can do in first-line disease. A day later Erasca presented a challenger with higher response rates in heavily pretreated patients, and Alterome showed a third approach. Wall Street can't agree whether the challengers matter. Let's go through it.

Start with where the field stands. Rasonque, or daraxonrasib, is an oral drug that binds a chaperone protein, cyclophilin A, inside the cell. That pair then clamps onto RAS in its active, G-T-P-bound state. Because it targets the "on" form of RAS rather than a single mutation, it covers essentially every RAS variant, and more than ninety percent of pancreatic cancers carry a K-RAS mutation. In the Phase three RASolute three-oh-two trial in previously treated disease, median overall survival was thirteen point two months versus six point seven on chemotherapy, close to double. The F-D-A approved it in August for second-line or later metastatic pancreatic cancer. The launch has been among the fastest in oncology: one hundred ninety-eight million dollars in its first quarter, about five thousand patients treated, and Truist projects one point two seven billion dollars in 2027 sales.

The new data are about first-line treatment, the bigger market. Sixty-one previously untreated patients with metastatic disease got Rasonque plus gemcitabine and nab-paclitaxel, the standard chemotherapy pair. With eighteen months of median follow-up, sixty-one percent had a confirmed response, eight percent had a complete response, and ninety percent had disease control. Median progression-free survival was twelve point eight months, and an estimated seventy-seven percent were alive at one year. For context, Truist noted that chemotherapy alone has historically given median overall survival of about eight and a half to eleven months, and the bank had expected progression-free survival of around ten months. The numbers also improved with more follow-up. At AACR in the spring, with forty patients, the response rate was fifty-eight percent and the complete response rate was two and a half percent. Complete responses more than tripled, and BioSpace estimates progression-free survival roughly doubled. Patients were enrolled regardless of RAS genotype, which tells you RevMed plans to compete on the whole disease, not on mutation subtypes. Leerink called tolerability "manageable," with diarrhea, fatigue, and low blood counts the main issues.

The caveat is the usual one. This is a single-arm cohort of sixty-one fit patients, and single-arm pancreatic cancer data have misled the field before. The answer will come from RASolute three-oh-three, a nine-hundred-patient Phase three comparing Rasonque alone, Rasonque plus chemotherapy, and chemotherapy alone, with progression-free and overall survival as co-primary endpoints. The F-D-A gave the combination breakthrough designation in September. Truist expects the readout around 2028, a first-line approval that year, and peak Rasonque sales of fourteen and a half billion dollars.

Now the challenger. Erasca's ERAS-0015 is also a pan-RAS molecular glue that works the same basic way as Rasonque. They're similar enough that Revolution claims patent infringement, which we'll come to, and Erasca argues its drug is better. Erasca in-licensed it from China's Joyo Pharmatech in 2024 for twelve and a half million dollars upfront, so a Chinese-originated molecule costing eight figures is going after a drug analysts value at a possible fourteen billion dollars in peak sales. On Friday Erasca reported its U-S Phase one data. At the thirty-two milligram dose in second-line-or-later pancreatic cancer, the confirmed response rate was forty-four percent in twenty-five patients. Eighty-four percent of those patients were in third line or later, and forty-four percent had G-twelve-R mutations, which are generally less sensitive. In the G-twelve-D and G-twelve-V subtypes, which together make up about seventy percent of pancreatic cancers, the response rate was sixty-nine percent in thirteen patients. The comparison Erasca wants you to make: in the Phase three trial behind Rasonque's approval, about a third of patients responded overall, and only twenty-four percent of those with G-twelve-D did. G-twelve-D is generally seen as Rasonque's weak spot.

Tolerability is Erasca's second argument. At the thirty-two milligram dose, no patient stopped treatment because of side effects, about thirteen percent had a dose interruption, and about seventeen percent had a dose reduction. Rasonque's label lists rash in eighty-six percent of pancreatic cancer patients and mouth sores in fifty-seven percent. Erasca is now moving its first-line pancreatic Phase three up to the first half of 2027 and plans a potentially registration-enabling lung cancer trial in the same period. There are two important caveats. First, the program has had a death. In April Erasca disclosed a grade five pneumonitis in a heavily pretreated patient, which helped send the stock down sharply then. Second, its combination with gemcitabine and nab-paclitaxel is still in dose escalation, with no efficacy data, so in first-line disease Erasca is about two years behind.

The analysts disagree. RBC's Leonid Timashev, who covers Revolution, wrote that ERAS-0015 "could be a formidable competitor in the future, and potentially accelerate the fragmentation of the RAS landscape," with what "may be real differentiation" on both efficacy and safety. TD Cowen's Tyler Van Buren argued the opposite: the data "fail to meaningfully differentiate from Rasonque, especially considering the multi-year commercial head start." Truist, before the release, made what I think is the key point: "ORR alone is unlikely to resolve the competitive debate." Rasonque has a randomized survival benefit across RAS subtypes, and Truist thinks a challenger would ultimately need durable survival data and probably a head-to-head trial. Leerink's Asthika Goonewardene suggested a middle path: if the cleaner safety profile holds up, Erasca could find niches Rasonque can't reach, especially in combinations where toxicities overlap. The stocks reflected the doubt. Erasca fell about four percent on Friday to thirteen dollars and sixty-one cents, and Revolution rose about one percent to one hundred sixty-one dollars.

There's also a legal dispute. In April, Revolution sent Erasca a letter claiming ERAS-0015 is "substantially equivalent" to forms of daraxonrasib covered by its patents and demanding that Erasca stop developing it in the U-S. BioSpace now reports that Revolution has filed patent litigation. Erasca calls the claims without merit. For an asset licensed from China, a freedom-to-operate fight in the U-S is a separate risk from the clinical data.

The third company takes a different approach. Alterome, a private San Diego company, presented first data for ALTA3263, a pan-K-RAS inhibitor that binds both the active and inactive forms of the protein. That's a hedge against the adaptive reactivation that limits drugs targeting only one state. The results were modest. Alone, it produced responses in three of thirteen pancreatic cancer patients, and with the EGFR antibody cetuximab, in five of sixteen colorectal cancer patients, one of them unconfirmed. Diarrhea was common, and a third of combination patients had grade three or higher side effects. Colorectal cancer is the useful test here: tumors there route around a single blocked pathway, and adagrasib plus cetuximab already failed to beat chemotherapy in Phase three. Erasca is also hedging with its own pan-K-RAS inhibitor, ERAS-4001, which it plans to combine with ERAS-0015 in 2027 so it can hit both the active and inactive forms.

So what should Foresite and Calibr take from this? Three things.

First, the bar in RAS-driven pancreatic cancer is now overall survival, and it's rising. A follower's Phase one response rate in a single arm doesn't change that. Rasonque's survival result in RASolute three-oh-two is the comparator for every program, and a first-line approval would make it the backbone too. Anyone developing a RAS drug, whether a molecular glue, a degrader, or a mutant-selective inhibitor, has to decide early whether to compete head-to-head or to differentiate through combinations and tolerability.

Second, tolerability is where real differentiation is likely to come from. Rasonque's rash and mouth sores are manageable as a single agent, but they're a problem when you add chemotherapy, immunotherapy, or EGFR antibodies. That's the case Leerink is making for Erasca, and it's the same reason dual-state inhibitors like Alterome's exist. For a discovery engine, including the A-I platforms you follow, the design goal in a crowded target class is less often "more potent" and more often "combines cleanly."

Third, the China pipeline matters again. A twelve-and-a-half-million-dollar license from Joyo is the most credible current challenger to one of the biggest oncology launches in years. That's the same dynamic the congressional biotech commission warned about on Thursday, and the patent fight shows the legal risk that comes with it.

What to watch: Erasca's first combination efficacy data with chemotherapy in 2027, the start of its first-line Phase three, any ruling in the patent case, and, most important, RASolute three-oh-three around 2028. That trial decides whether Rasonque becomes the backbone of first-line pancreatic cancer treatment.
