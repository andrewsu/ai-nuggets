Welcome to the spotlight, Travis. Today: the F-D-A has rejected ivonescimab. It was the first P-D-one by V-E-G-F bispecific antibody to reach the agency, and the most closely watched drug to come out of China. We've done four spotlights on it this year, most recently two weeks ago, when half of large-cap oncology was lining up to combine its drugs with it. Today's question is narrower. What exactly did the F-D-A refuse, was that refusal consistent with how it has treated this setting before, and what does it tell everyone else building drugs in this class?

Start with what happened. The goal date was Saturday. On Sunday morning, Summit Therapeutics announced a complete response letter for ivonescimab plus platinum chemotherapy in E-G-F-R-mutant non-small cell lung cancer that has progressed on a targeted E-G-F-R inhibitor. The letter itself isn't public yet, so everything we know about its reasoning comes from Summit. According to the company, the agency found that substantial evidence of effectiveness wasn't established without a statistically significant overall survival improvement. It also said the progression-free survival benefit, on its own, doesn't support a positive benefit-risk profile. Summit named no other deficiency. Jefferies says Akeso has confirmed that F-D-A inspections of its Chinese trial sites and its Guangzhou manufacturing plant both passed. So this is a pure efficacy judgment, not a data-integrity or manufacturing problem.

Nor was it a surprise. Summit's own quarterly filing records that the F-D-A had told the company that a statistically significant survival benefit is necessary to support approval in this setting, and that the progression-free survival results may not be enough. Summit filed anyway.

Here's the evidence it filed with. HARMONi randomized four hundred thirty-eight patients to ivonescimab or placebo, each added to carboplatin and pemetrexed. Progression-free survival was unambiguous: six point eight months against four point four, a hazard ratio of zero point five two. But the trial had two primary endpoints, and survival was one of them. At the primary analysis, median survival was sixteen point eight months against fourteen, a hazard ratio of zero point seven nine with a p-value of zero point zero five seven. Every later look has been better. The most recent cut, from June, puts the hazard ratio at zero point seven six with a nominal p of zero point zero one five, and the Western patients now match the Asian ones. But those analyses came after the pre-specified one and spend no alpha. Akeso's chair, Michelle Xia, said as much on a Sunday evening call. She explained that analyses run after the formal one no longer carry the pre-specified statistical power and can't serve as evidence for a key endpoint, and she called that the core regret of the filing. She also called it the most disheartening moment in the company's fourteen-year history. There's a safety side too. Serious adverse events ran twenty-eight percent on ivonescimab against fifteen percent on chemotherapy alone. That's the kind of gap the F-D-A wants offset by a survival benefit.

Now the uncomfortable comparison, which the newsletter Biotech Distilled laid out on Sunday. In September 2024, the F-D-A approved Johnson and Johnson's Rybrevant, amivantamab, with the same chemotherapy, in essentially the same patients. That approval rested on the MARIPOSA-two trial: a progression-free survival hazard ratio of zero point four eight, and a survival hazard ratio of zero point seven three that the F-D-A's own announcement described as not statistically significant at an interim analysis. Summit's press release makes the same point more quietly: no approved regimen in this setting has ever shown a statistically significant survival benefit. So on the surface, the agency accepted one non-significant survival result and rejected another.

The differences are real, though, and they matter for anyone designing a pivotal trial. In MARIPOSA-two, survival was a key secondary endpoint, checked at an interim analysis. In HARMONi, it was one of two primary endpoints, and it missed at its final pre-specified test. A miss on a primary endpoint is a different regulatory fact from an immature secondary. Second, between the two decisions, in August 2025, the F-D-A's oncology center published draft guidance saying overall survival is both an efficacy and a safety endpoint, and that every randomized oncology trial should be designed to assess it. Third, the closest precedent isn't Rybrevant at all. It's patritumab deruxtecan, Daiichi Sankyo and Merck's HER3 antibody-drug conjugate. In its confirmatory trial in this same setting, it met progression-free survival with a survival hazard ratio of zero point nine eight, and its application was withdrawn in May 2025 after discussions with the F-D-A. Put those together and the agency's line in post-E-G-F-R lung cancer looks fairly consistent. Progression-free survival opens the door, and survival is what lets you through it.

What does it cost? Less than Akeso's stock suggests. The Hong Kong shares fell as much as thirty-two percent on Monday, and Nomura downgraded the stock to neutral with a ninety-four Hong Kong dollar target. Jefferies kept its buy rating and a target of two hundred twenty-six. It estimates this indication at about eight thousand accessible U-S patients a year and roughly four hundred million dollars in unadjusted peak sales, against seventeen billion for ivonescimab overall, most of it in first-line lung cancer. Summit's U-S shares hadn't traded on the news yet when we put this together. The commercial loss is small. The narrative loss is bigger. A drug analysts have called a Keytruda killer, and that AstraZeneca backed with a two-billion-dollar equity stake in September, now has no approval anywhere outside China.

That brings us to the China question, which the F-D-A didn't raise but everyone else will. Akeso's China-only trials have all hit on survival. HARMONi-A, in this same post-E-G-F-R setting, had a significant hazard ratio of zero point seven four. HARMONi-two beat pembrolizumab head to head at zero point seven three, and HARMONi-six beat tislelizumab at zero point six six. The one multiregional trial run for the F-D-A is the one that missed. Our read in early November was that much of HARMONi's Western weakness came from immature follow-up, because the Western patients enrolled later than the Asian ones. That still looks right, and it's the practical lesson for any company building a global program on top of Chinese data. If the bridging trial's regions enroll in sequence, the last region's survival curve may not have separated by the time the primary analysis runs. Enroll the regions together, or power survival for the timeline you actually have.

For the rest of the class, Scrip read the letter as a warning, and that's fair. The followers include BioNTech and Bristol's pumitamig, Pfizer and 3SBio's tivunatamig, Merck's version licensed from LaNova, and AbbVie's from RemeGen. Most are designing first-line trials against pembrolizumab, where survival will be the deciding endpoint anyway. What they lose is the hope of a faster first approval in a later-line setting on progression-free survival alone. What they gain, as pharmaphorum noted, is time. Every month ivonescimab spends without a U-S label is a month for the followers to close the gap.

So everything now rides on HARMONi-three. That's sixteen hundred patients randomized to ivonescimab or pembrolizumab, each with chemotherapy, in first-line lung cancer, with squamous and non-squamous cohorts analyzed separately. The data monitoring committee looked at an early squamous interim and let the study continue as planned. Summit expects the squamous primary analysis this quarter, with an early look at survival alongside it, and the non-squamous data in the first half of 2027. If ivonescimab beats pembrolizumab there, Sunday's letter will look like a footnote, as Xia hopes. If it wins on progression-free survival with survival still immature, we'll be back in this exact conversation, in a much larger indication.

Three things to take from this for Calibr and the Foresite portfolio. First, when you make survival a primary endpoint, you're bound by it. A near-miss on a primary endpoint is harder to recover from than an immature secondary, and later analyses don't rescue it. Second, the F-D-A's position is now written down, in the draft guidance and in this letter. In any setting with an approved standard, plan on survival being required, not just reviewed. Third, the things to watch are the letter itself, if the F-D-A posts it to its public database as it has done with others, whether Summit says anything more about the E-G-F-R indication, and the HARMONi-three squamous readout before year-end.
