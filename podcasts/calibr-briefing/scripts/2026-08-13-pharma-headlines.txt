Good morning, Travis. It's Thursday, August thirteenth, and here's your daily pharma and biotech briefing. Six stories today, and then the spotlight goes deep on the first one — Eli Lilly deciding to take the black market for its obesity drug to court.

Story one, and today's lead. Lilly filed six lawsuits on Wednesday against sellers of counterfeit retatrutide — its investigational obesity drug, the triple agonist that hits three gut-hormone receptors at once. The striking part is that retatrutide isn't approved anywhere in the world. It's still in registration-enabling trials, with a filing to the F-D-A expected early next year. And yet Lilly says it has identified more than fourteen thousand websites, ads, and product listings hawking the drug across a hundred-plus countries, much of it labeled "for research use only" to dodge the rules. The defendants are a mix of compounding pharmacies, peptide sellers, and a medical spa, mostly in Texas. We'll unpack what this gray market really tells us in the spotlight.

Story two. Sionna Therapeutics' cystic fibrosis program failed its key mid-stage test, and the stock fell about ninety-two percent — below the company's own cash balance. Sionna's pitch was a differentiated corrector: a small molecule that stabilizes a specific folding domain of the CFTR protein, called N-B-D-1, in a way Vertex's drugs don't. The idea was to layer it on top of Vertex's Trikafta and push efficacy further. But on sweat chloride — the standard readout of CFTR activity — adding Sionna's drug moved the needle only one point, versus the ten-point improvement analysts were looking for. Not statistically significant, not clinically meaningful. Vertex shares rose about seven percent. Analysts called it a clearing event: the last credible near-term threat to Vertex's cystic fibrosis monopoly just evaporated. Sionna says it will preserve capital and lean on an earlier-stage program it licensed from AbbVie.

Story three, a rare win for psychedelics. Definium Therapeutics reported positive results from the first of two Phase 3 trials of its L-S-D pill in generalized anxiety disorder. A single dose cut anxiety scores on the Hamilton scale by nearly twelve points, versus about six for placebo — a large effect that held through twelve weeks, with benefits showing up within days. This is the same compound that already cleared a Phase 3 in a related setting, and it carries an F-D-A breakthrough designation. If the confirmatory trial holds, Definium is on track to bring the first L-S-D-based medicine toward approval — a very different profile from the psilocybin programs also nearing the finish line.

Story four. Vaderis Therapeutics closed an oversubscribed one-hundred-fifty-two-million-dollar Series B and started a global Phase 3 in a rare vascular disease called hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia, or H-H-T — a genetic disorder that causes malformed, bleeding blood vessels and has no approved therapy. Vaderis's drug, engasertib, is an oral allosteric inhibitor of the kinase AKT — the same growth-signaling node implicated in a range of vascular malformations. The round was co-led by Goldman Sachs's life-sciences arm and T-C-G-X, with Perceptive, Omega, and E-Q-T joining. It's a clean example of the money that's still flowing into rare disease with a clear genetic target and a first-mover shot at the whole indication.

Story five, a new company. Boulevard Bio launched out of stealth with sixty-five million dollars from Deerfield, going after B-cell-driven autoimmune disease with multi-specific antibodies. Its lead is a bispecific that blocks two B-cell survival signals, BAFF and APRIL, at once, with early data supporting once-a-quarter dosing in the kidney disease IgA nephropathy. Behind it sits a tri-specific T-cell engager aimed at the immune reset idea — wiping out the misbehaving B cells to reboot the immune system. One of the co-founders is Georg Schett, the physician-scientist who pioneered using CAR-T for lupus. This is squarely in the hottest area of immunology right now.

And story six, on the AI front. A new analysis making the rounds this week put a dollar figure on AI in the clinic: an autonomous clinical-monitoring agent could deliver up to twenty-one million dollars in net value per oncology drug program, mostly by catching data problems earlier and cutting the cost of site monitoring. Take the exact number with a grain of salt, but it's a useful marker of where the AI-in-drug-development conversation is going — from discovery, where most of the attention has been, into the far more expensive business of running trials.
