Good morning Travis. Here are your biotech and pharma headlines for Friday, September eleventh, twenty twenty-six. A major Novartis shareholder went to the press overnight demanding a board shake-up after the worst week that stock has had in years, the F-D-A put a partial clinical hold on the epilepsy program that S-K Biopharmaceuticals licensed two weeks ago, and the agency made its two most important center directors permanent while inventing a deputy commissioner job for artificial intelligence that has never existed before.

First, Novartis, which is also today's spotlight, so I will give you the shape of it and save the analysis. David Samra, managing director at Artisan Partners and a founding partner of its international value group, told Reuters that the board needs to be shaken up. His words: the party is over. He is explicitly not asking for Vas Narasimhan's job — he says Narasimhan has done a very good job running the business and points to more than sixty percent share appreciation since twenty eighteen. What he wants is for Chairman Giovanni Caforio to bring better talent onto the board, to fix how acquisitions get vetted before they are signed, and to rebuild executive compensation around real economic outcomes rather than adjusted metrics that exclude write-downs. The line to hold onto is this one: if you do a twelve-billion-dollar deal and it goes to zero, management needs to be penalised for that. The trigger was two failures this show covered this week — pelacarsen, the lipoprotein-little-a antisense, missing on cardiovascular outcomes, and then del-desiran, the antibody-oligonucleotide conjugate that arrived with the twelve-billion-dollar Avidity acquisition, missing its primary endpoint in myotonic dystrophy. Call it thirty billion dollars of market value in two sessions. Novartis's response was that it continues a disciplined and shareholder-friendly approach to capital allocation and that guidance is unchanged. I will take the governance argument apart in the second episode, including the part of it I think is wrong.

Second, Biohaven. The F-D-A has placed a partial clinical hold on enrollment of new patients in RISE two and RISE three, the pivotal focal epilepsy trials of B-H-V-seven thousand — opakalim — a Kv seven potassium channel activator. Kv seven channels set the resting membrane potential and the slow brake on repetitive firing, so opening them is the most direct pharmacological way to raise the seizure threshold; it is the mechanism of retigabine, which worked and was withdrawn for retinal pigment and skin discoloration. The reason for the hold is not human safety data. A metabolite turned up in rodent toxicology and the agency wants additional nonclinical work to establish whether it is relevant to people. Biohaven's position is that the finding may be rodent-specific. Patients already enrolled keep dosing — more than six hundred of them — and RISE three finished enrolling in June, so that readout is still guided to the second half of this year. RISE two had not finished enrolling, and its December primary completion date is now in doubt. The stock fell more than thirteen percent premarket. Two pieces of context make this sting. Biohaven already took a complete response letter on troriluzole in spinocerebellar ataxia last November and cut research and development spending roughly sixty percent, and B-H-V-seven thousand failed a mid-stage depression trial in December. And on August twenty-sixth, S-K Biopharmaceuticals licensed the Kv seven platform for up to seven hundred ninety-five million dollars — with, per Biohaven, all data disclosed before signing. A rodent metabolite is among the most recoverable holds there is; you answer it with nonclinical work, not a new trial. But it lands on the arm that had not finished enrolling, which is exactly where a delay costs the most.

Third, the F-D-A made its own leadership permanent. Michael Davis is now permanent director of the Center for Drug Evaluation and Research, Karim Mikhail permanent director of the Center for Biologics Evaluation and Research, and Bret Koplow permanent at the Center for Tobacco Products. All three had been acting since mid-May. Davis has said his number one priority is morale and retention, which tells you what the last eighteen months inside those buildings have been like. The genuinely new thing is Jared Seehafer, previously a senior adviser in the commissioner's office, who becomes the agency's first-ever Deputy Commissioner for Technology and Artificial Intelligence. That title did not exist before today. The F-D-A launched its internal large language model, Elsa, in June of last year and deployed agentic models last December for meeting management, pre-market review support and report validation. The practical read for you is that review-side artificial intelligence now has an owner with a reporting line and a budget, which means the frameworks governing how sponsors may use A-I in submissions are about to have a single author rather than emerging center by center.

Fourth, and this is the same Michael Davis: he and three colleagues from the drug center — Teresa Buracchio, Tiffany Farchione and Bernard Fischer — laid out the agency's thinking on psychedelics in the New England Journal of Medicine. The signal is flexibility. They are open to innovative trial designs including non-placebo comparators, they are focused on functional unblinding and on measuring patient expectancy directly rather than pretending it away, and they indicate that dedicated abuse-potential studies may not be necessary where abuse potential is already well characterized by clinical and epidemiological data. This follows July's final guidance, which arrived three years after the draft, and it lands three days before a public hearing on the future therapeutic use of psychedelics on Monday, September fourteenth. Recall that in April the agency handed national priority vouchers to psilocybin in treatment-resistant depression, psilocybin in major depressive disorder, and methylone in post-traumatic stress disorder. Yesterday we covered Compass's fifty-two-week durability data on synthetic psilocybin. This is the regulatory half of that same story, and the thing worth noticing is that the unblinding problem — which is the single hardest methodological obstacle in the field — is being treated as something to measure rather than something to eliminate.

Fifth, Takeda. Andy Plump is retiring as president of research and development in June twenty twenty-seven, after more than ten years in the job and about twelve at the company, with prior stints at Sanofi and Merck. No successor named; he stays until one is found and remains a board member through the transition. His legacy is the narrowing: Takeda went from more than ten modalities to four — small molecules, biologics, antibody-drug conjugates and allogeneic cell therapy — and then exited cell therapy entirely. He also put artificial intelligence through the discovery engine and signed the roughly one-billion-dollar collaboration with Nabla Bio on computational antibody design. This follows Julie Kim taking over as chief executive in June and forty-five hundred job cuts earlier this year, so the read is that the reorganization is complete and the architect is handing over rather than being handed a pink slip.

Sixth, Disc Medicine presented updated Phase two RALLY-M-F data at the Society of Hematologic Oncology meeting yesterday for D-I-S-C-oh-nine-seven-four, an antibody against hemojuvelin, in myelofibrosis patients with anemia. Hemojuvelin is a co-receptor that amplifies bone morphogenetic protein signaling in hepatocytes to drive hepcidin transcription, and hepcidin is the hormone that locks iron inside macrophages and blocks intestinal absorption. In the inflammatory anemia of myelofibrosis, hepcidin is inappropriately high, so iron is present in the body but unavailable to the erythron. Block hemojuvelin, hepcidin falls, iron is released, and erythropoiesis has substrate again — which is a fundamentally different move from stimulating the marrow. With an April data cutoff: half of non-transfusion-dependent patients achieved a mean hemoglobin rise of at least one and a half grams per deciliter sustained twelve weeks or more; seventy-one percent of the low-transfusion-burden group became transfusion independent for at least sixteen weeks; and sixty-seven percent of the high-burden group cut transfusion requirement by at least half. Fatigue scores improved in the two lighter-burden groups, and diarrhea was the only adverse event attributed to the drug in more than one patient. Naseema Gangat presented. Transfusion independence in the low-burden group is the number that matters commercially, because that is the population a J-A-K inhibitor makes worse.

Seventh, BioMarin. Updated Phase three results from the CANOPY-H-C-H-three study of Voxzogo — vosoritide — in hypochondroplasia showed a statistically significant two point three three centimeter improvement in annualized growth velocity over placebo at fifty-two weeks, with consistent gains in standing height, arm span and height Z-score, and no serious treatment-related adverse events. Vosoritide is a C-type natriuretic peptide analogue acting through natriuretic peptide receptor B to brake the over-active F-G-F-R-three-to-M-A-P-kinase signaling that restrains growth-plate chondrocytes; hypochondroplasia is caused by milder F-G-F-R-three gain-of-function variants than achondroplasia, so this is a mechanism extension into an adjacent genotype rather than a new mechanism. BioMarin has submitted the N-D-A and estimates eighty-five hundred to twenty-three thousand affected people in the United States. If approved it would be the first targeted therapy in the indication.

Eighth, BridgeBio Oncology shares slid as the company narrowed its K-R-A-S strategy. On Monday it reported that B-B-O-eight-five-twenty — an oral inhibitor that binds both the active and inactive states of K-R-A-S G-twelve-C, unlike the first generation, which only traps the off state — produced a seventy-five percent response rate at the five-hundred-milligram daily dose in combination with pembrolizumab in patients whose disease had already progressed on a K-R-A-S G-twelve-C inhibitor, fifty-three percent across all dose levels in seventeen patients, and sixty-three percent as monotherapy in twenty-six of forty-one inhibitor-naive patients with a hundred percent disease control rate and no grade three liver enzyme elevations. That last detail is the point of the whole design: dual-state inhibition gets pathway suppression at lower exposure, which is what makes checkpoint combination tolerable. The strategic narrowing is to that second-line-and-beyond combination plus combinations of B-B-O-eleven-eight-eighteen and B-B-O-ten-two-oh-three in colorectal and pancreatic cancer. Three hundred forty-four million dollars of cash into twenty twenty-eight. The market read the focus as a retreat from front line, where Revolution Medicines is building its case.

Ninth, Shionogi's redasemtide missed in the global late-stage Phase two in acute ischemic stroke and the company is weighing the program's future. Redasemtide is a peptide fragment derived from H-M-G-B-one, licensed from StemRIM, and the mechanism is unusual: rather than delivering cells, it mobilizes the patient's own mesenchymal stem cells to injured tissue. A Japanese Phase two had been positive. This is Shionogi's second late-stage disappointment of the year after zatolmilast missed twice in fragile X, and it is another instance of the pattern we keep seeing — a promising single-region study that does not replicate globally.

Tenth, three smaller items worth your attention. Modalis and Exegenesis Bio signed a research collaboration and license agreement on M-D-L-two-oh-one in Duchenne muscular dystrophy. The concept is a mutation-agnostic workaround: instead of repairing or replacing dystrophin, use epigenome editing — catalytically dead Cas nine fused to transcriptional activators, no DNA cutting — to turn up utrophin, the structurally similar protein that can substitute for dystrophin at the sarcolemma. Modalis brings the editing payload and leads the program; Exegenesis brings E-M-C-one-eighty-one, an engineered A-A-V capsid with muscle tropism and liver de-targeting, which is the part that has killed most systemic muscle gene therapy. Separately, Yarrow Bioscience priced a hundred-fifty-million-dollar offering at twenty-six dollars a share, with Jefferies, T-D Cowen and Guggenheim running it, to fund Y-B-one-oh-one, a potential first-in-class antibody against the thyroid stimulating hormone receptor for Graves' disease and thyroid eye disease — going after the receptor itself rather than downstream of it, as teprotumumab does at I-G-F-one receptor. And Breakthrough T-one-D convened about thirty international experts to publish the first international consensus guidance on general-population screening for early-stage type one diabetes, in Diabetologia, endorsed by twenty diabetes organizations: screen islet autoantibodies at ages two to four, rescreen at six to eight and ten to fifteen, confirm positives with a second test. That is the infrastructure question underneath every type one diabetes asset, including the menin program Kura spun into Caspian yesterday — a disease-modifying drug for stage one or two disease is worthless without a screened population to give it to.

Finally, one policy note. A survey of a hundred fifty-seven pharma leaders across sixty-seven companies finds most-favored-nation pricing has changed launch sequencing from arithmetic into strategy — one executive called the policy environment an earthquake that is not going to settle down. Companies are now deciding whether to launch in a given market at all based on what that price does to every other market, and pricing considerations are reaching back into endpoint and indication selection. Insmed delaying a European launch to protect United States price is the cited example.
