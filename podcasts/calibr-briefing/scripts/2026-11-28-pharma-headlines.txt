Friday was a half session for U-S markets and most of the American trade press was still off for the holiday, so this is a short tape with a few real items on it. The biggest move came from Vertex, which shed roughly seven and a half billion dollars of market value in the first full session after the F-D-A pushed back the decision on its kidney drug. That's today's spotlight. Fulcrum Therapeutics is handing shareholders about two hundred seventy-five million dollars in cash before it disappears into a migraine company. Novo Nordisk set a Christmas Eve launch for the Wegovy pill in Sweden, without reimbursement. Sol-Gel will report a pivotal readout in a rare skin cancer syndrome before the open on Monday. Lupin launched generic Januvia in the United States. And Legend Biotech scheduled an investor event at A-S-H to show its first in vivo CAR-T data. Here's what you need to know.

First, the spotlight item. Late Wednesday, Vertex said the F-D-A had extended its review of povetacicept in I-g-A nephropathy by up to three months, to no later than February twenty-eighth. Povetacicept is an engineered fusion protein that traps two cytokines, BAFF and APRIL, which keep alive the B cells that make the abnormal antibody that drives the disease. Late in the review the agency asked Vertex for more information. Vertex answered, the agency classified that answer as a major amendment, and a major amendment buys the reviewers three more months. Friday was the market's first full session to react. Vertex closed down about five point two percent at five hundred thirty-four dollars and fifty-seven cents, after trading as low as a seven percent loss. The analysts who wrote on it kept their ratings. The spotlight gets into why an extension generally reads better than its headline, what the agency plausibly asked for, and what a lost quarter actually costs when a direct competitor is already selling.

Second, Fulcrum. On Friday afternoon Fulcrum Therapeutics declared a special cash dividend of up to about two hundred seventy-five point seven million dollars, or roughly three dollars and sixty-one cents a share, payable to holders of record on December seventh. Fulcrum is the shell in a reverse merger: it was developing small molecules for rare blood disorders, hit a setback, and agreed in August to combine with Slate Medicines, a Raleigh company developing an anti-PACAP-slash-V-I-P antibody for migraine with infrequent subcutaneous dosing. The structure is the point. Slate gets a Nasdaq listing plus a two hundred forty-five million dollar private placement, and Fulcrum's own cash goes back to Fulcrum's holders rather than funding Slate's trials. The dividend is pegged to the amount by which Fulcrum's net cash exceeds twenty point three million dollars at closing, and it includes about seven million dollars from selling Fulcrum's early-stage fetal hemoglobin assets to an undisclosed buyer. Shareholders vote December third, and closing is expected around December eleventh. This is what an orderly wind-down looks like when the balance sheet is worth more than the pipeline, and there have been a lot of them this year.

Third, Novo Nordisk. The company said Friday it will launch the Wegovy pill — oral semaglutide — in Sweden on December twenty-fourth. It will not be reimbursed, so pharmacies set the price, and Novo says the tablet will be priced comparably to the injection. Novo is separately seeking reimbursement from the Swedish authority for a limited obesity population. The pill launched first in the United States in January, and the competitive context is Lilly's oral GLP-one, which has been taking a large share of new patients. A cash-pay launch in a small Nordic market is not a revenue event. It's a pricing experiment: Novo is testing whether an oral at injection-level pricing moves volume at all when the payer is the patient.

Fourth, Sol-Gel. The Israeli dermatology company said Friday it will release top-line results from its pivotal Phase three trial of S-G-T-six-ten at seven a-m Eastern on Monday, with a call at eight. S-G-T-six-ten is patidegib gel, a topical hedgehog pathway inhibitor, being tested to prevent new basal cell carcinoma lesions in adults with Gorlin syndrome — an inherited condition in which patients develop basal cell carcinomas continuously. It has orphan and breakthrough designations. The logic of a topical hedgehog inhibitor is that the oral drugs in this class work but are hard to stay on, because of muscle cramps, taste loss and hair loss. Keeping the drug in the skin is the whole thesis. Monday is a binary.

Fifth, generics. Lupin said Friday it received F-D-A approval for and has launched sitagliptin tablets in the United States — the generic of Merck's Januvia, a D-P-P-four inhibitor for type two diabetes. Lupin cites a reference market around four hundred thirty-nine million dollars. Dr. Reddy's got its own final approval for generic Januvia and Janumet earlier in the week, about six months behind a competitor. Januvia was a multi-billion-dollar franchise at its peak. What's left of it is now being divided among Indian generic makers, and the erosion curve from here is steep.

Sixth, Legend Biotech. The company will host an investor webcast on Sunday evening, December thirteenth, during A-S-H in New Orleans. The C-E-O will host clinicians on new data for CARVYKTI, the B-C-M-A CAR-T it partners with Johnson and Johnson, and for L-B-two-five-oh-one, an investigational C-D-nineteen-slash-C-D-twenty dual-targeting in vivo CAR-T for relapsed or refractory B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma. In vivo CAR-T is the field's current obsession — Pfizer paid three hundred thirty million dollars upfront to get into it earlier this month — and Legend is the rare company that has both the autologous commercial product and an in vivo program to compare it against. The data, not the webcast, is the event.
