Good morning, Travis — this is your biotech and pharma briefing for Thursday, August twentieth, twenty twenty-six. Yesterday delivered the readout the cancer-vaccine field has been waiting on for a decade, and the market response was violent: one of the largest single-day moves a large-cap biotech has had in years. Seven items.

Number one, and today's spotlight: Merck and Moderna said intismeran autogene, their individualized neoantigen therapy, hit the primary endpoint of recurrence-free survival in the Phase three INTerpath-oh-oh-one trial, in combination with Keytruda, in patients with completely resected stage two-B through four melanoma. It also hit the key secondary endpoint of distant metastasis-free survival. Both against Keytruda alone — which is the standard of care in that setting, so this is a win over active therapy, not placebo. One thousand one hundred thirty-seven patients, randomized two to one, and the hit came at a pre-specified interim analysis. This is the first positive Phase three for an individualized neoantigen therapy and the first for any messenger RNA cancer therapy. Moderna's stock went up something like eighty-five to ninety percent, from under sixty-three dollars to a hundred and sixteen and change at the open; Merck was up about nine percent, which on Merck's market cap is a very large number. What the companies did not release is a single efficacy figure — no hazard ratio, no curves, no median. Those are being held for a medical meeting. The spotlight goes deep on what was and wasn't proven, why the manufacturing model is the real question, and what it means that the thing being validated here is an algorithm rather than a molecule.

Number two, the FDA approved Regeneron's garetosmab, brand name Pasatru, for fibrodysplasia ossificans progressiva — the ultra-rare disorder in which muscle, tendon and ligament are progressively replaced by bone. Roughly nine hundred people worldwide are diagnosed; most are wheelchair-bound by thirty and median survival is fifty-six. Garetosmab is a fully human antibody against activin A, and the target came out of Regeneron's own genetics work: activating mutations in the activin A receptor ACVR-one cause the disease, and Regeneron scientists showed activin A is the ligand actually driving the rogue bone. The pivotal OPTIMA trial was sixty-three adults, and the effect size is extraordinary for a rare bone disease — a ninety percent reduction in new heterotopic ossification lesions at the ten-milligram dose and ninety-four percent at three milligrams, by whole-body CT, two lesions versus nineteen on placebo. Clinician-assessed flare-ups dropped eighty-eight percent at the high dose. Two things to hold onto: patient-reported flare-ups were not significantly different, which is a gap worth watching in the label conversation, and the adverse-event profile is unusual — abscess, acne, hair growth, loss of eyebrows, nail infections — the signature of blocking a TGF-beta-family ligand systemically. It's the second approved drug in the disease after Ipsen's palovarotene, and it lands the same week Regeneron killed its anti-CD-three uveitis trial, which is a fair snapshot of that company right now: the genetics engine still works, the clinical bets are noisy.

Number three, on the artificial intelligence side, Enveda cleared Phase one with an oral tablet designed to mimic what exercise does hormonally. The molecule, ENV three-oh-eight, is built around lactoyl-phenylalanine — Lac-Phe — the metabolite Stanford researchers showed in twenty twenty-two rises after exercise and suppresses feeding in mice. The body clears it fast, so the engineering problem was making something drug-like enough for once-daily oral dosing. Eighty-eight healthy volunteers across multiple dose levels, no serious adverse events, no discontinuations, no dose interruptions, and what the company calls an exceptional gastrointestinal profile — which is the whole pitch against incretins, where nausea and vomiting drive most of the dropout. They also saw leptin fall, steepest in the people who started highest, replicating the animal data. The Phase two is the interesting design choice: not head-to-head weight loss, but whether ENV three-oh-eight holds weight off in people coming off a GLP-1. That's the real commercial gap in obesity, and nobody has claimed it. Enveda's platform is machine learning on natural-product metabolomics — mining plant and microbial chemistry for scaffolds — and they've raised about five hundred seventeen million dollars, including two separate hundred-fifty-million rounds last year. This is the first clinical readout that says the platform produces something dosable.

Number four, staying with artificial intelligence: Anthropic published a study in which Claude ran a protein-binder design campaign essentially on its own, and Endpoints' analysis argues the interesting part has nothing to do with proteins. The numbers first: fifteen targets, successful binders against fourteen, hit rates of twenty-two to thirty-five percent depending on whether the system was working one target or many, against a field baseline of ten to fifteen percent. On one competition benchmark it hit forty percent where the competitors managed under four. Adaptyv Bio and Twist Bioscience did the wet-lab validation independently. Critically, Claude didn't invent new methods — it orchestrated the field's existing open-source structure-design, sequence-design and co-folding models, and did the selection and iteration itself. Anthropic's framing is minimal human involvement beyond the initial prompt. The read-through for anyone running a discovery organization is not "the model designs proteins." It's that the scarce skill being automated is judgment about which tool to run next and which candidates to carry forward — the part of the job that has always lived with the senior scientist.

Number five, the amylin field is turning into an argument about molecular design rather than mechanism, and it matters because Novo's CagriSema is likely to become the first approved amylin-containing product later this year, with Lilly's mid-stage data close behind. The design question is receptor selectivity. Cagrilintide hits both the amylin receptor and the calcitonin receptor, and the calcitonin arm carries its own tolerability and safety management burden. Lilly's eloralintide is deliberately selective for the amylin receptor over calcitonin by something like fifty to a hundred fold, and its Phase two produced up to twenty percent weight loss as monotherapy with no incretin component at all. If selectivity is what separates these drugs, then amylin is not one class — it's two — and the second one is a genuine alternative for the large population that cannot tolerate a GLP-1, not just an add-on.

Number six, Biokin said its bispecific antibody-drug conjugate cleared a fourth late-stage China trial, this time in EGFR-mutant non-small cell lung cancer. The molecule, izalontamab brengitecan, targets EGFR and HER-three simultaneously with a topoisomerase-one payload — the bispecific-ADC thesis being that dual targeting improves internalization and coverage of tumors with heterogeneous receptor expression. Bristol Myers holds the ex-China rights through the SystImmune deal, eight hundred million up front and up to eight point four billion in total, and the asset already carries U.S. Breakthrough Therapy designation in previously treated EGFR-mutant lung cancer. Four for four in China is a real signal, and it's also a reminder that the global registrational package for these China-originated assets is still the open question.

Number seven, three quick ones. TScan disclosed that Amgen has exercised its right to terminate their Crohn's disease target-discovery collaboration, effective in November — TScan keeps the thirty million it was paid up front in twenty twenty-three, but over five hundred million in milestones and the royalty stream go away with no termination penalty. The FDA accepted Teva's application for ecopipam in pediatric Tourette syndrome with priority review and a target action date late in the first quarter of twenty twenty-seven; ecopipam is a first-in-class D-one receptor antagonist, which would make it the first Tourette drug with a genuinely new mechanism in over fifty years. And CSL told investors the European revocation of Tavneos is a significant headwind, with about a hundred forty-five million dollars of annual sales at stake. Worth knowing why: the European Commission pulled the marketing authorization under an Article twenty review over data-handling problems in the pivotal ADVOCATE trial — not efficacy, not safety. An approved drug lost a whole region on trial conduct.
