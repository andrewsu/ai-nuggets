First, and this is the spotlight, so I'll be brief here. On Friday morning Acumen Pharmaceuticals reported topline results from ALTITUDE-AD, its Phase 2 trial of sabirnetug in early Alzheimer's disease. The trial missed. No meaningful separation from placebo on the primary cognitive and functional endpoint at eighteen months, at either dose, and no statistical significance on the key secondary endpoints either. Acumen is stopping development of the drug entirely, including the open-label extension, and the company now has no clinical program. The stock closed Friday at twenty-one cents, down from two dollars and twenty-nine cents on Thursday. That's a decline of about ninety-one percent, worse than the eighty percent figure you'll see in the Friday morning coverage, which was quoting the pre-market print. Sixty-six million shares changed hands. I'll go through what the result actually rules out in the spotlight, because it's more specific and more interesting than "another amyloid drug failed."

Second, Geron. The company is terminating IMpactMF, its Phase 3 trial of Rytelo, generic name imetelstat, in intermediate-two and high-risk myelofibrosis in patients who had relapsed on or were refractory to a JAK inhibitor. The independent data monitoring committee looked at the data and concluded the drug was unlikely to show a significant overall survival benefit, and Geron took the recommendation. Three hundred twenty-seven patients had been enrolled across a hundred forty-four sites. Imetelstat is a telomerase inhibitor, which is an unusual mechanism to have gotten this far, and it was approved in 2024 for lower-risk myelodysplastic syndrome in patients who don't respond to or can't take erythropoiesis-stimulating agents. That approval stands. What's gone is the expansion thesis. Geron's remaining myelofibrosis shot is a Phase 1 combination with ruxolitinib in the frontline setting, which is a much longer road, and it's walking into a market where ruxolitinib did five point two billion dollars in 2025. GlobalData had been forecasting one point two billion in Rytelo sales by 2032. Expect that number to come down.

Third, Alnylam, which had the kind of quarter where the revenue line looks fine and the stock falls a quarter anyway. The transthyretin franchise, Amvuttra and Onpattro, booked one point one five billion dollars, up fifty-nine percent year over year. But the shares closed Thursday at a hundred seventy-four dollars, down about twenty-five percent on the day and down fifty-six percent year to date, on a combination of inventory dynamics, pricing pressure, and the proposed three-forty-B update that would affect the money available for drugs including Amvuttra starting January first, 2027. Chief executive Yvonne Greenstreet said directly on the call, quote, we're not satisfied with the pace of our future demand growth, and we need to do better. BMO called it a trifecta of uncertainties while staying constructive on the franchise. Oppenheimer went the other way and said the selloff has made Alnylam a credible takeout, with Sanofi the most likely buyer. The underlying issue is that the cardiomyopathy launch, approved in March 2025, is being contested harder than expected by BridgeBio and Pfizer. A twenty-three billion dollar market cap on a franchise growing fifty-nine percent tells you how much doubt is priced in.

Fourth, policy. The Labor Department finalized its pharmacy benefit manager disclosure rule on Thursday. Self-insured employer plans will have to be shown the rebates a P-B-M negotiates with manufacturers and the spread it keeps when the plan pays more for a drug than the P-B-M reimburses the pharmacy. Disclosure is required at contracting and renewal and every six months after, with an annual audit right, financial penalties for noncompliance, and the possibility that a contract is voided. It takes effect in July 2027. Labor projects twenty-two billion dollars in savings over ten years against roughly a million dollars of first-year compliance cost per P-B-M. The Pharmaceutical Care Management Association called it a solution in search of a problem and argued during the comment period that the department lacks the authority. No lawsuit has been filed yet. I'd expect one. The honest analyst read is that transparency helps at the margin but doesn't fix concentration, because three P-B-Ms still run about eighty percent of U-S prescriptions.

Fifth, two items on the data-and-automation layer under A-I drug discovery. Parse Biosciences, now a QIAGEN company, and Nomic Bio are building something called Cascade, a hundred-million-cell perturbation dataset of the human immune response. The design is the interesting part: a hundred million single-cell R-N-A-seq profiles paired with more than eleven thousand secretome profiles measuring over a thousand secreted proteins, across more than a hundred fifty thousand single and combinatorial cytokine and drug conditions, sampled at multiple timepoints. Pairing transcriptome and secreted proteome on the same perturbation over time is the part that's hard to get anywhere else, and it's aimed squarely at the virtual-cell modeling bottleneck. GSK, Pfizer, Sanofi, Graph Therapeutics and Turbine have signed on as founding partners. Separately, Opentrons raised a forty million dollar Series E led by Avego, with BroadOak participating, for lab robotics and the software layer that lets a model actually run an experiment. Eleven thousand robots deployed in eighty countries. The common thread in both is that the constraint on biological A-I right now is purpose-built perturbation data, not model capacity.

Finally, mark November thirtieth. That's the F-D-A goal date for Vertex's povetacicept in I-g-A nephropathy, filed for accelerated approval. Povetacicept is a T-A-C-I fusion protein that traps both APRIL and BAFF, the two cytokines that drive the B-cell survival and the pathogenic antibody production behind the disease. Because of that mechanism, the infection question is the one to watch, and it got press attention this week ahead of the decision. In the Phase 3 data, upper respiratory infections ran eighteen point three percent on drug versus eleven point four percent on placebo, with no opportunistic infections, no infection-related discontinuations, and serious infections reported at about half a percent in both arms. That's a mild signal on paper. Whether the agency wants it in a boxed warning, a plain warning, or a post-marketing requirement is the real question, and it will set the template for the whole APRIL-and-BAFF class.
