This is your spotlight for Sunday, November eighth, 2026. Two failures, presented back to back on Saturday morning at the American Heart Association meeting in Chicago, and published together in the New England Journal of Medicine. Separately, each is a disappointment. Together, they're a stress test for one of the core assumptions of modern drug discovery: that if human genetics says a target causes disease, a drug against that target will prevent it.

Start with HORIZON, Novartis' trial of pelacarsen. Pelacarsen is an antisense oligonucleotide, licensed from Ionis, that shuts down the liver's production of apolipoprotein little a and therefore of lipoprotein little a, the cholesterol-carrying particle that about one in five people carry at high levels, almost entirely set by their genes. HORIZON enrolled more than eight thousand three hundred patients across forty-two countries, all with a prior heart attack, stroke or other cardiovascular event, and lipoprotein little a above seventy milligrams per deciliter. They got a monthly injection of drug or placebo and were followed for a median of about four point seven years.

On the biomarker, the drug did everything asked of it. Lipoprotein little a fell seventy-eight percent, versus three percent on placebo. It even shaved about thirteen points off L-D-L cholesterol. And outcomes were identical. The primary endpoint, cardiovascular death, heart attack, stroke or urgent revascularization, hit twelve point three percent of the pelacarsen group and twelve point one percent of placebo, a hazard ratio of one point oh two. In the higher-risk group above ninety milligrams per deciliter, the hazard ratio was exactly one. Every secondary endpoint, every sensitivity analysis, every subgroup came out neutral. The study chair, Cleveland Clinic's Steve Nissen, put it plainly: "I cannot avoid the disappointing conclusion: it didn't work." He added, "not even a trend, even in subpopulations."

Then ZEUS, Novo Nordisk's trial of ziltivekimab, a once-monthly antibody against the interleukin-six ligand. The idea was to treat residual inflammatory risk, the risk left over in patients whose cholesterol is already controlled. Sixty-four hundred patients with atherosclerosis, chronic kidney disease, and C-reactive protein of at least two, followed for a median of thirty-three months. Free interleukin six fell seventy-six points versus placebo, C-reactive protein sixty-four. Major cardiovascular events: seventeen point six percent versus seventeen point seven, a hazard ratio of point nine nine. Cardiovascular death, heart attack and stroke were each flat on their own, and the kidney composite trended slightly the wrong way. Safety leaned against the drug: serious infections were twenty point four percent versus seventeen point nine, and serious venous blood clots one point six percent versus point six. Novo had already stopped its two heart failure trials in September, after futility calls. The post-heart-attack trial, ARTEMIS, continues.

So why does this matter beyond two companies? Because both targets were chosen with the best kind of human evidence available, and both trials had flawless target engagement. Lipoprotein little a has decades of Mendelian randomization behind it. People born with low levels have fewer heart attacks, in a dose-dependent way, and the latest A-C-C and AHA guidelines describe a dose-dependent relationship with risk. Interleukin-six signaling has its own genetic support, plus the proof of concept of CANTOS, where blocking interleukin-one beta, upstream of interleukin six, did lower events. The AHA discussant, Karol Watson, summed it up: "even with compelling biology, compelling genetics, everything went right, but cardiovascular events didn't change."

STAT's Matthew Herper used the results this morning to argue for skepticism about A-I hype. His point is that genetics was the last technology that was supposed to raise the odds of success, and here it did "anything but." It's worth taking seriously, because a large share of A-I target discovery runs on the same evidence base: genome-wide association, Mendelian randomization, human knockouts. If a model ranks a target highly because of human genetics, it is inheriting exactly the confidence these two trials just punctured.

But the more useful reading is narrower, and the investigators' explanations show where the gap is. There are three of them.

The first is exposure. Genetics measures a lifetime of low lipoprotein little a, starting at birth. HORIZON gave patients four point seven years of lowering, starting in their late fifties, after they'd already had an event. Nissen: "that's not a lifetime." Paul Ridker, the ZEUS chair at Mass General Brigham, made the same argument for inflammation: ZEUS patients were about a decade older than in CANTOS, with long-standing diabetes, heart failure and advanced kidney disease, and "the horse may already have been out of the barn." Genetic validation tells you a factor causes disease over a lifetime. It does not tell you that lowering it late, in established disease, reverses anything.

The second is what exactly the drug touches. The ZEUS authors themselves note that the genetic evidence points most consistently to variants in the interleukin-six receptor, while ziltivekimab blocks the ligand, not the receptor. Priscilla Hsue, writing in Healio, suggested the field look at receptor-targeting agents. The genetic instrument and the drug are not the same perturbation, and the trial tested the drug.

The third is the most uncomfortable. Donald Lloyd-Jones, a past AHA president, pointed out at the media briefing that cardiology has been "spoiled for decades," because every lipid drug class that worked, from statins to P-C-S-K-nine inhibitors, ultimately works by increasing the number or activity of L-D-L receptors. Pelacarsen lowered L-D-L by thirteen points and still showed nothing, which he said you'd have expected to give some benefit. So he asked whether "tolerability issues or perhaps some toxicity related to this compound" offset it. The tolerability numbers make that a fair question. One in five patients on pelacarsen stopped it because of side effects, versus about seven percent on placebo, flu-like symptoms hit nearly a third, and nineteen patients had anaphylaxis versus none on placebo. Lloyd-Jones also noted that lipoprotein little a still lacks the most important criterion of causality: an intervention that lowers it and lowers disease. HORIZON was the first direct test, and the answer was no.

Now the competitive landscape, starting with lipoprotein little a. Novartis told TCTMD it is discontinuing the pelacarsen cardiovascular program, including the ongoing studies and its managed access program. Nissen called that a good decision and argued the field should move on to small interfering R-N-A drugs, which lower lipoprotein little a further, are dosed far less often, and should be better tolerated. That is the bull case for the survivors: Amgen's olpasiran in OCEAN(a)-Outcomes, Lilly's lepodisiran in ACCLAIM, and Lilly's oral muvalaplin. The bear case is in the numbers. Going into Saturday, BMO had said even a small separation on the primary endpoint, or a signal in the highest-lipoprotein little a patients, would help Amgen and Lilly. Neither showed up. Fierce noted that OCEAN and ACCLAIM require higher entry levels, roughly two hundred and a hundred seventy-five nanomoles per liter, against about a hundred fifty in HORIZON. But HORIZON's median baseline was around two hundred thirty-six, so the populations may be more alike than the cutoffs suggest. Truist's framing back in September still fits: olpasiran is now higher risk and higher reward, not dead. OCEAN reads out in 2028 and ACCLAIM in 2029, and until then the whole class trades on this one null.

On inflammation, the field isn't giving up, it's moving. Ridker's own next steps are earlier, lower-risk patients, or going upstream to the N-L-R-P-three inflammasome, where several companies have oral inhibitors in development. He also said "it remains quite possible that downstream I-L-six inhibition is in fact effective, but that ZEUS targeted the wrong patient population." That is now the thesis Novartis has to carry with pacibekitug, its own anti-interleukin-six antibody, which it advanced into Phase 3 this year arguing exactly that ZEUS picked the wrong patients. And low-dose colchicine, cheap and approved, is still underused.

What should Foresite and Calibr take from this? Three things.

First, separate "this target is causal" from "this drug, in this patient, at this stage, will prevent events." Mendelian randomization answers the first. Only a trial answers the second, and a cardiovascular outcomes trial runs to eight thousand patients and most of a decade. Any program whose case rests on genetics plus a biomarker should be underwritten on the exposure question: how much of the lifetime effect can you plausibly recapture, and starting when?

Second, check that the genetic instrument and the drug perturb the same thing: ligand versus receptor, particle concentration versus particle number, chronic lowering versus intermittent. ZEUS is a clean example of a mismatch that was visible in advance.

Third, for the A-I-in-discovery companies, this is a fair test of what they claim. A model that ranks targets by genetic association would have ranked both of these near the top. The more valuable model would have flagged the ligand-receptor gap, the late-intervention problem, or the tolerability cost. That's a harder problem, and it's where the real differentiation will be.
