Welcome to the spotlight, Travis. Today it's Helus Pharma's Phase three result in depression. It's the cleanest late-stage efficacy number any psychedelic company has produced in the adjunctive setting, and the stock fell on the day anyway. I want to work out whether the market got this wrong, or whether it was reacting to something real that isn't in the headline number.

Start with the molecule. H-L-P-zero-zero-three is deuterated psilocin. Psilocybin, the compound in magic mushrooms, is a prodrug. The body strips off a phosphate group to make psilocin, which is what actually activates the serotonin two-A receptor. Helus doses psilocin directly, so it skips that conversion step, which varies from person to person. It also swaps some hydrogen atoms for deuterium, the heavier isotope, which slows the drug's breakdown. The goal is a more predictable experience and dosing session than with psilocybin itself. The company calls this class novel serotonergic agonists, a phrase chosen to avoid the word psychedelic.

Now the trial. APPROACH enrolled two hundred twenty-two adults with moderate to severe major depression, with baseline M-A-D-R-S scores around thirty-three. All of them had responded inadequately to an antidepressant they kept taking. On top of it, they got two supervised doses of the drug or placebo, three weeks apart. The primary endpoint was the change in M-A-D-R-S at week six. Scores fell nineteen and a half points on drug against eleven point nine on placebo, a difference of seven point six, with a p-value below one in ten thousand and an effect size of about zero point six five. The drug pulled ahead of placebo by day two, with a five-point gap. At week twelve, the key secondary endpoint, the gap was still seven points. Remission at week six was fifty-one percent against eighteen percent, and forty percent against fifteen and a half at week twelve.

Put those numbers next to what doctors use today. When an antidepressant isn't enough, the usual add-on is an atypical antipsychotic like aripiprazole or brexpiprazole. Jefferies puts their placebo-adjusted benefit at two to five points. Those drugs also bring weight gain, metabolic problems and movement disorders, and patients take them every day. Here, two doses produced a larger effect that lasted at least twelve weeks. Before the readout, H-C Wainwright had modeled a four-to-five-point difference, and other analysts set five or six as the bar. Helus cleared every estimate. The University of Toronto psychiatrist Roger McIntyre, quoted in the company's release, called it a new benchmark for adjunctive treatment.

So why did the stock fall? Shares were down as much as about seventeen percent during the day and closed roughly ten and a half percent lower, at about seven ninety-four. The reason was a single sentence in the release: there were three treatment-related serious adverse events. On the call, Helus said two were cases of worsening depression and one was a suicide attempt. Chief medical officer Amir Inamdar gave the details. The patient hadn't responded to the drug. The attempt came fifty-five days after the second dose, about seven weeks after the last exposure. The patient had a prior history of suicide attempts and overdosed on their background antidepressant, which carries its own boxed warning for suicidality. The patient was hospitalized and discharged. Inamdar said the company saw no suicidality signal, and only two patients stopped treatment because of side effects.

The analysts mostly sided with the company. Barclays called the efficacy very strong and said it saw no fundamental reason for the decline. Cantor said a single case doesn't establish a safety signal in this population. Jefferies' Andrew Tsai described safety as fairly clean and kept his Buy rating. Lucid Capital went furthest. It raised its probability of success to seventy percent from forty-five, and blamed the selloff on what it called a significant misunderstanding. The one warning came from H-C Wainwright, which said pivotal efficacy supports the adjunctive thesis, but that safety clarification is essential.

Here's my read. On the suicide attempt itself, the company's argument is reasonable. The timing is weak, the patient's risk factors are clear, and suicidality is part of the disease. The detail that matters more is the label the company chose. Helus itself called all three events treatment-related, and that judgment goes to the F-D-A as part of the safety database. Psychedelic sponsors don't get the benefit of the doubt here. The agency's 2023 draft guidance on psychedelic trials emphasizes safety monitoring and how expectancy shapes results. And in 2024, Lykos' M-D-M-A application was turned down after an advisory committee voted overwhelmingly against it. The concerns were incomplete adverse event capture and trials that were effectively unblinded. Investors remember that.

That brings up the issue analysts didn't talk about much: functional unblinding. People who take a serotonin two-A agonist almost always know it. Helus' placebo arm improved twelve points, which is a normal placebo response in depression. Still, any reviewer will ask how much of the seven-point gap comes from expectancy in patients who could tell which arm they were in. The day-two separation cuts both ways. It fits a fast-acting drug, and it also fits patients who just had an unmistakable experience. Helus will need blinding-integrity data, such as patient and rater guesses about treatment arm, and the second trial will need to replicate the effect. The release didn't say whether that data was collected.

Then there's timing, which may matter more than either issue. APPROACH is the first of two pivotal trials. EMBRACE reads out in the fourth quarter of 2027, and Helus is guiding to a filing in 2028. Meanwhile, Compass Pathways completed its N-D-A for COMP-three-sixty psilocybin in treatment-resistant depression on November ninth. A week before that, the Usona Institute filed its psilocybin for major depression. Both hold Commissioner's National Priority Vouchers, though the fast path behind those vouchers is now in doubt, as we covered Saturday. So the first psilocybin drugs could be approved more than a year before Helus can even file. Helus is betting on a more convenient session, an adjunctive label that matches how depression is actually treated, and an effect size that holds up in a cross-trial comparison.

Spravato, J-and-J's esketamine nasal spray, shows how this market works. It's the closest commercial template: an in-clinic, monitored, restricted-access treatment added on in hard-to-treat depression. It took years to build out the treatment centers and the billing infrastructure, and it now sells more than a billion dollars a year. Whoever launches first in psychedelics will build the clinic network and the reimbursement codes. A later drug with a shorter session could then use that network, or might have to dislodge an incumbent with a similar effect size. There's policy support too. President Trump's April executive order on psychedelic access, and last week's twenty-five million dollars from H-H-S for ibogaine research, show an administration that wants this class to succeed. That support is real, but it doesn't change the review standard, as ivonescimab showed us this weekend.

What would change my view? Three things. First, a full safety readout at a medical meeting, with suicidality scale data for both arms, not just the serious adverse events. Second, any blinding assessment from APPROACH. Third, the EXTEND extension study, which will show how long a response lasts and when patients need another dose. Those two facts drive the commercial model for any intermittent therapy.

For you, there are three takeaways. First, the efficacy is real and large, and in the adjunctive setting it's the best Phase three number in the class. If EMBRACE replicates it, the sponsor's own classification of three serious events is unlikely to sink the program. Second, the market's reaction tells you about the risk premium on this class, not about this drug. After Lykos, investors treat any suicide-related event in a psychedelic trial as a potential review problem, whatever the causality looks like. That matters for any company in the portfolio planning a C-N-S program with a perceptible drug effect: build the blinding-integrity measures and the suicidality monitoring into Phase two, because the agency will ask for them at the end. Third, watch Compass and Usona. Their F-D-A reviews over the next few months will set the label, the monitoring requirements and the reimbursement path that Helus will inherit in 2028.
