Welcome to the spotlight, Travis. Today: Cogent Biosciences' first approval. On Tuesday morning, six days ahead of schedule, the F-D-A approved Ekitna, or bezuclastinib, with sunitinib for adults with advanced gastrointestinal stromal tumor, or G-I-S-T, who have already had imatinib. It's the first new drug in this setting in twenty years. It's also, in Cogent's words, the first G-I-S-T treatment ever to show a significant progression-free survival benefit against an active comparator. Cogent launched the same day. We'll cover the biology, the data, the price, the competitor coming up behind it, and the real question for Cogent shareholders, which is what happens in mastocytosis on December thirtieth.

Start with the biology, because it explains why this worked when three earlier drugs didn't. Most G-I-S-T tumors are driven by mutations in KIT, a receptor tyrosine kinase. Imatinib works well against the most common primary mutations, in exons eleven and nine, and it has been the first-line standard for over two decades. But tumors develop resistance by picking up secondary KIT mutations, and these fall in two places. Some sit in the A-T-P-binding pocket. Others sit in the activation loop, in exons seventeen and eighteen. Sunitinib, the second-line standard since 2006, hits the A-T-P-pocket mutations reasonably well but misses the activation-loop ones. The later-line drugs, regorafenib and then ripretinib, were each approved on response rates under ten percent and about six months of progression-free survival. And in 2021, the INTRIGUE trial tried to move ripretinib up to replace sunitinib in second line, and it failed. The problem is that a single resistant patient often carries several different secondary mutations in different parts of the tumor, and no single drug covered all of them.

Cogent's idea was simple: don't replace sunitinib, add to it. Bezuclastinib is a selective KIT inhibitor designed against the activation-loop mutations, including D-eight-sixteen-V, the same mutation that drives systemic mastocytosis. Put it on top of sunitinib and you cover both resistance regions at once. CEO Andrew Robbins describes it as an umbrella over every well-characterized primary and secondary KIT mutation.

The Phase three PEAK trial tested exactly that. Four hundred thirteen patients who had progressed on or couldn't tolerate imatinib got the combination or sunitinib alone. Median progression-free survival by blinded central review was sixteen and a half months against nine point two, a hazard ratio of zero point five zero. The response rate was forty-six percent against twenty-six, which Cogent's medical team points out is close to what imatinib achieves in first line. Two newer numbers came with the approval. The time to progression on the next line of therapy hasn't been reached on the combination yet, against twenty-one months for sunitinib, which suggests the benefit isn't just pushed downstream. And the average patient has now been on the combination about twenty-three months. Overall survival isn't mature. The label covers every patient after imatinib, with no mutation test required. The independent analyst who writes Biotech Distilled notes that this makes the launch much simpler.

The catch is the liver. The label carries a boxed warning for hepatotoxicity. Liver enzymes rose in forty-three percent of patients on the combination, eight percent at grade three. Most cases appeared in the first four months, and Cogent says about one percent of patients stopped treatment for it and every case resolved. Its argument is that sunitinib already carries its own boxed liver warning, the monitoring language is identical, and oncologists have been doing this monitoring for twenty years, so nothing changes in practice. For G-I-S-T, that's a fair argument. Leerink's Andrew Berens noticed on the call that the liver numbers in the label are lower than those presented at ASCO in June. Cogent attributed the gap to the difference between lab values investigators entered and the ones they recorded as adverse events.

Now the money. On the call, Guggenheim asked about pricing, and Robbins said the wholesale price will be just over forty-nine thousand dollars a month. Cogent counts more than three thousand new U-S patients a year eligible after imatinib, and about twelve thousand diagnoses a year worldwide. At list price, twenty-three months of treatment comes to over a million dollars per patient before discounts. Management cautioned that the first year will look different. Many early patients will be third, fourth or fifth line, people who have already failed sunitinib and want to try the combination, and they will stay on drug for less time. Duration should lengthen as the mix moves toward true second-line patients. Robbins expects net pricing in line with recent oral cancer launches, with some Medicare drag because patients tend to be older. He also made a point about competition. Sunitinib is a generic nobody promotes, so in second line Cogent will have, in his words, near complete share of voice. And one more point for anyone valuing small biotechs: asked about Europe, Robbins said Cogent will file outside the U-S on its own, but carefully, because of the most-favored-nation pricing environment. He said he doesn't want to do anything to damage the U-S business. MFN pricing is now affecting where a small company launches at all.

The market liked it, though not as much as the headline might suggest. Shares closed up seventeen point four percent at thirty-three dollars and one cent, the biggest one-day move in Biotech Distilled's coverage. But that's still about twenty percent below the forty-one fifty-three where the stock traded on August tenth. Published price targets vary widely. H-C Wainwright moved to fifty-six dollars and Wedbush kept fifty-five, but consensus averages quoted by Benzinga and Investing dot com don't agree.

The competition is real, but it's a few years out. In January 2025, GSK paid one billion dollars upfront, and up to one point one five billion, for IDRx, whose drug is now called velzatinib. It's also built to cover the full range of KIT resistance mutations, but as one drug rather than two. Clinical Trials dot gov shows GSK's Phase three of velzatinib against sunitinib after imatinib started in December 2025, aims to enroll four hundred fifty patients, and has a primary completion date of May 2028. The trade newsletter ApexOnco headlined Tuesday's approval as Cogent getting a head start on GSK, and that's the right frame. Cogent has roughly two to three years to become the habit of every G-I-S-T center before a single-pill option with GSK's sales force could arrive. Cogent's own move is to go earlier. Robbins says G-I-S-T experts asked for a first-line trial against imatinib after ASCO, and Cogent is designing one, which he admits will be long and large.

Here's what the market is really watching. Bezuclastinib has a second approval decision on December thirtieth, in nonadvanced systemic mastocytosis, and a third in advanced mastocytosis next June. Mastocytosis is the bigger commercial prize, and the incumbent there is Ayvakit, or avapritinib, which Sanofi now owns through its roughly nine and a half billion dollar purchase of Blueprint Medicines last year. So the company that agreed to pay forty-one billion dollars for Insmed the same morning is the one with the most riding on Cogent's December label. Nonadvanced mastocytosis patients are younger, often treated for symptoms rather than survival, and stay on drug for years. A boxed liver warning would weigh much more in that population than in a metastatic cancer.

There are good reasons to think the G-I-S-T warning won't carry over. In G-I-S-T the warning comes from a combination with a drug that's already toxic to the liver. In mastocytosis, bezuclastinib is given alone and, in Cogent's SUMMIT trial, at a much lower dose. Cogent also filed separate brand names, and Robbins stressed that Ekitna will be used exclusively for G-I-S-T. That looks designed to keep the two labels apart. But when J-P Morgan asked directly whether the boxed warning had any read-through to mastocytosis, Robbins declined: today, he said, is a day to celebrate G-I-S-T. Biotech Distilled's summary is a fair one. The approval removes the manufacturing-inspection risk, but the liver warning now shapes the December decision. Until the mastocytosis label is out, Cogent's valuation turns on that one question.

So what's the read for Foresite and Calibr? Three things. First, PEAK is a lesson in trial design. Cogent didn't try to beat the incumbent head to head, which is how ripretinib failed. It added its drug to the incumbent, aimed at the specific resistance the incumbent leaves open. That made the active-comparator win much more likely, and because the partner drug is an unpromoted generic, Cogent faces almost no marketing competition. Any Calibr or portfolio asset designed against a known resistance mechanism of a generic standard of care should be thinking about this design from the start. Second, rare-oncology pricing is holding. Forty-nine thousand dollars a month for a combination add-on, launched the day of approval, shows that a randomized win against an active comparator still supports premium pricing. But the MFN comment shows the ex-U-S part of the valuation is getting harder to bank on. Third, watch the label segregation. A single molecule with a different brand and dose for each indication, deliberately kept apart, is a tactic worth tracking, and December thirtieth will show whether the F-D-A goes along with it.

That's the spotlight. Have a good Thanksgiving, Travis.
