Good morning Travis. Here are your biotech and pharma headlines for Saturday, September twelfth, twenty twenty-six. The F-D-A pushed Exelixis's first approval decision for its next-generation kinase inhibitor back three months, and in the same two-week stretch a Taiwanese specialty pharma walked a different salt of cabozantinib straight past the patents Exelixis had just finished defending in federal court. Disc Medicine put up polycythemia vera data that three analyst desks called competitive but not best-in-class. And Britain's cost watchdog told Gilead that twice-yearly H-I-V prevention is not value for money.

First, Exelixis, which is also today's spotlight, so I will give you the shape here and save the analysis. On Thursday the F-D-A notified the company that it is extending the review of the new drug application for zanzalintinib in combination with atezolizumab — Roche's Tecentriq — in previously treated metastatic colorectal cancer. Zanzalintinib is Exelixis's successor to cabozantinib: the same multi-kinase pharmacology aimed at vascular endothelial growth factor receptor two, M-E-T, and the T-A-M family kinases A-X-L and M-E-R, but engineered with a shorter half-life so it is easier to combine with a checkpoint inhibitor. Exelixis had submitted updated safety and efficacy data in response to an agency information request, and the F-D-A classified that submission as a major amendment, which automatically adds three months to the clock. The action date moves from December third of this year to March third, twenty twenty-seven. The stock gave up two to three and a half percent. Andy Hsieh at William Blair kept an Outperform rating and wrote that while he was surprised by the three-month delay, the risk of an outright rejection remains low, given that the combination produced an overall survival benefit across the intent-to-treat population in the Phase three STELLAR three-oh-three study. He floated two plausible reasons for the information request: that regulators want additional data comparing the hundred-milligram dose of zanzalintinib against sixty milligrams, and that they want clearer evidence on what zanzalintinib and Tecentriq each contribute on their own. Then on Friday, Handa Pharmaceuticals of Taiwan announced that the F-D-A has granted final approval to OMCAZIO, a laurylsulfate salt of cabozantinib cleared under the five-oh-five-b-two pathway. That second item is the one I think is underpriced, and it is where the spotlight goes.

Second, Disc Medicine. At the Society of Hematologic Oncology meeting in Houston, Disc reported the first data from the Phase two RESTORE-P-V trial of D-I-S-C-thirty-four-oh-five, a monoclonal antibody against T-M-P-R-S-S-six, in polycythemia vera. The mechanism is worth holding onto because it is the mirror image of the Disc program I covered yesterday. T-M-P-R-S-S-six is a liver serine protease that cleaves hemojuvelin and thereby switches hepcidin production off. Block it, hepcidin rises, iron gets locked in macrophages and absorption falls, and erythropoiesis runs out of substrate — which is exactly what you want in polycythemia vera, where the problem is too many red cells. Yesterday's item, D-I-S-C-oh-nine-seven-four in myelofibrosis anemia, is an antibody against hemojuvelin itself, pushing hepcidin the other direction. Disc is running both ends of the same axis. In thirteen patients who completed twenty-six weeks, more than sixty percent were phlebotomy-free, with blood draws falling from four at baseline to zero point six, and eleven of thirteen reported symptom improvement, ninety-two percent with less fatigue. Injection-site reactions were low-rate and self-limited. Jefferies told clients the data "look competitive" with Mimrylo — that is rusfertide, the hepcidin mimetic from Takeda and Protagonist that the F-D-A approved late last month — but added that they still think Silence Therapeutics' divesiran has shown best-in-class performance with a lighter dosing burden. B-M-O Capital said they were impressed by the phlebotomy reductions and hematocrit control. William Blair agreed that Silence is the one to beat, pointing at the SANRECO Phase two readout last month, where the small interfering R-N-A against the same T-M-P-R-S-S-six target produced an eighty-eight percent clinical response rate against nineteen percent on placebo. The useful number in the William Blair note is the price: Mimrylo lists at two hundred eighteen thousand four hundred dollars a year, which they read as clear room for pricing upside for everything that follows it. More RESTORE-P-V data by year end, more SANRECO at the hematology meeting in December.

Third, Karyopharm bought itself a month. The company reached an agreement with lenders, noteholders and royalty investors to defer a fifteen point eight million dollar debt payment that was due Thursday, pushing it to October fifteenth, in exchange for issuing twenty million dollars of convertible stock. R-B-C Capital Markets said the deal at least buys the company time, while noting they would have preferred something with longer-term relief. Karyopharm is waiting on F-D-A feedback on a supplemental application for Xpovio — selinexor, the exportin-one inhibitor that traps tumor suppressor proteins in the nucleus — in myelofibrosis. That is the asset that has to carry the balance sheet, and a one-month extension is not a financing.

Fourth, Evommune's E-V-O-seven-fifty-six missed a second Phase two-b in three months. The drug is an oral antagonist of M-R-G-P-R-X-two, the mast cell receptor that drives I-g-E-independent degranulation — the pathway behind pseudo-allergic reactions and a genuinely attractive target for itch. It failed in chronic spontaneous urticaria in June, and now it has missed both primary and secondary endpoints at every dose in a hundred twenty-one-patient atopic dermatitis trial. The company will not advance it in atopic dermatitis and is redirecting there to E-V-O-three-oh-one, an interleukin-eighteen binding protein fusion, while keeping E-V-O-seven-fifty-six alive in migraine prophylaxis, where tolerability was clean. Evommune has lost more than forty percent of its value since last year's I-P-O. Two clean misses at two doses in two indications is usually the target telling you something, not the molecule.

Fifth, Britain. NICE issued draft guidance recommending against N-H-S reimbursement of lenacapavir — Gilead's capsid inhibitor, sold as Yeytuo in the twice-yearly pre-exposure prophylaxis formulation — saying the available evidence does not suggest it is value for money in this population. The committee acknowledged that lenacapavir reduces H-I-V risk more than the oral options, but pointed to the breadth of existing choices including GSK's long-acting cabotegravir, and to uncertainties in how well the trial population reflects the U-K and in the economic model's assumptions about who would actually take up prophylaxis. Richard Angell of the Terrence Higgins Trust called himself profoundly disappointed and said the most exciting innovation in H-I-V prevention for years is being kicked into the long grass. Daniel Fluskey at the National AIDS Trust framed it more precisely as a failure to agree an affordable price. A second committee meeting is set for November third, which is where this usually gets resolved with a confidential discount. The signal for you is not that NICE said no — it is that a twice-yearly injectable with the strongest efficacy data in its category can still lose a value argument to a cheap daily pill plus an existing long-acting competitor. Dosing convenience does not price itself.

Sixth, Daiichi Sankyo won the last piece of a decade-old mess. The Tokyo District Court dismissed all claims in a two hundred three point three billion yen damages suit — roughly one point three billion dollars — brought by a unit of Malaysia's I-H-H Healthcare over Daiichi's stalled bid for India's Fortis Healthcare. That is the long tail of the Ranbaxy acquisition, and it is now off the balance sheet.

Seventh, AbbVie extended its migraine franchise into an indication that has never had an approved drug. The Phase three LUNA study of atogepant — Qulipta, an oral small-molecule antagonist of the C-G-R-P receptor — met its primary endpoint and all eight ranked secondary endpoints at p less than point oh-oh-oh-one in four hundred sixty-eight women with menstrual migraine. Dosing was short-course: seven consecutive days starting three days before menses, across three cycles. The primary result was a mean reduction of one point two migraine days in the perimenstrual window versus zero point four on placebo. That is a modest absolute number, but the comparison is against nothing — there is no approved therapy for menstrual migraine — and the intermittent dosing schedule is the commercially interesting part, because it converts a chronic preventive into a predictable, cyclical prescription. Submissions worldwide are planned.

Eighth, on the computational side, Schrödinger has spun out Tectora Therapeutics with fifty-five million dollars, co-founded with New Enterprise Associates and R-A Capital. Schrödinger contributed two early-stage oral small molecules, S-D-G-R-forty-five-ninety-four and S-D-G-R-eighty-one-thirty-nine, in exchange for equity plus milestones and royalties, and Tectora will keep using the physics-based platform to push them to clinical candidates in immunology and inflammation. This is Schrödinger continuing to convert software into equity rather than selling compute — the same structure that produced Nimbus and Morphic. Worth watching as a template, because it is the model most of the A-I-native discovery companies will eventually need: you cannot sustain a platform valuation on service revenue, and you cannot run twelve clinical programs yourself.

Ninth, financing. Frazier Life Sciences closed more than one point one billion dollars for a fund aimed at small and mid-cap public biotechs — the same vehicle family that held Verona Pharma before Merck paid ten billion dollars for it and Carmot before Roche paid two point seven billion. R-A Capital has already participated in four biotech financings this month. Janus Henderson is the year's most prolific backer of reverse mergers, which have gone from disparaged to so crowded that deal advisers are now asking whether there are enough decent shell companies to meet demand. And Encoded closed a two hundred seventy-five million dollar Series F led by G-V, Arch and R-T-W for E-T-X-one-oh-one in Dravet syndrome. Capital is available; it is just going to assets with data and to public equities at a discount, not to the seed stage.

Finally, a policy number worth filing. A new report finds that no biosimilar is in development for roughly ninety percent of the biologic products losing exclusivity by twenty thirty-four — more than a hundred ten biologics coming off patent, about ten percent with a biosimilar program behind them. The estimated cost is two hundred thirty-two billion dollars in missed savings over the next decade, against fifty-six billion dollars biosimilars have actually delivered since twenty fifteen. That gap is the direct consequence of how expensive and legally hostile biosimilar entry has become, and it is the strongest argument anyone has made this year that the biologics cliff will simply not arrive on schedule.

Looking ahead: the F-D-A's psychedelics hearing is Monday, Ultragenyx has U-X-one-eleven on the nineteenth, Merck's Winrevair label update on the twenty-first, zilurgisertib on the twenty-sixth, Caspian's menin data at the diabetes meeting on the twenty-ninth, Scholar Rock's apitegromab on the thirtieth, Bristol Myers' POETYK S-L-E-one around October nineteenth, NICE's second look at lenacapavir on November third, and now zanzalintinib on March third.
