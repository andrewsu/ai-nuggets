First, and it's the spotlight: yesterday the F-D-A's cell, tissue, and gene therapy advisory committee voted ten to three that the efficacy data on Replimune's R-P-one are evaluable and clinically meaningful. That is a remarkable turn for a drug the agency has rejected twice. R-P-one is an oncolytic virus injected directly into tumors and given together with Opdivo, for melanoma that has stopped responding to checkpoint immunotherapy. Here's the piece that makes this the story of the week — this is the exact same committee that, one day earlier, voted nine to three against Capricor's Duchenne cell therapy. Same panel, back-to-back days, opposite answers. Replimune's stock jumped roughly a hundred and twenty-seven percent after hours, and the F-D-A's decision is due August second. I'll walk through why the panel found this evidence believable when the agency's own scientists did not, and what the contrast with Capricor tells you about where the evidence bar really sits.

Second, a rough day for one of the marquee names in R-N-A interference. Alnylam's stock fell as much as twenty-nine percent Thursday. The headline product, Amvuttra — that's vutrisiran, a small interfering R-N-A that silences the gene for transthyretin, used in transthyretin amyloidosis — crossed a billion dollars in quarterly sales for the first time, but it still came in below what analysts wanted, and the company trimmed its full-year sales guidance. The reason matters more than the miss: management said the surge of second-line heart-disease patients who had been waiting for a new therapy has now largely been cleared, so growth is normalizing. When the best-selling R-N-A-i drug on the market resets expectations, the whole silencing field takes note.

Third, on the big-pharma side, Bristol Myers Squibb delivered a clean Q2 beat and raised its full-year outlook. Revenue came in just under thirteen billion dollars for the quarter, up six percent, and adjusted earnings of two dollars and four cents a share blew past the roughly dollar-sixty analysts expected. The company lifted full-year revenue guidance to about forty-nine to fifty billion dollars, driven by the blood thinner Eliquis alongside newer growth drivers — Camzyos, its cardiac myosin inhibitor for hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, and the anemia drug Reblozyl. It's a reminder that even with the Eliquis and Opdivo patent cliffs looming, the newer portfolio is carrying more weight than the market had priced in.

Xaira Therapeutics — the roughly one-billion-dollar A-I drug-discovery company incubated by ARCH Venture Partners and Foresite Labs, and run by Marc Tessier-Lavigne — is stepping out of stealth.
