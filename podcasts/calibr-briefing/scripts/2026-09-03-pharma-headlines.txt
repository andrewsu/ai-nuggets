Good morning Travis. Here are your biotech and pharma headlines for Thursday, September third, twenty twenty-six.

Two days, two Phase three failures, and the second one is worse than the first. Alumis lost fifty-eight percent on Tuesday in lupus. Last night Ultragenyx lost forty-five percent in Angelman syndrome, and unlike the lupus miss there is no subgroup to fall back on and no ambiguity in the readout. Set against that, a genuinely good day for Novartis in multiple sclerosis, a first-of-its-kind celiac result from Teva, and a gene therapy filing that took a year of regulatory whiplash to get to. Ten items, then the week ahead.

First, and today's spotlight. Ultragenyx reported results from Aspire, the Phase three study of apazunersen — the drug you know as G-T-X one-oh-two — in Angelman syndrome, and the trial missed on everything. Angelman syndrome is caused by loss of the maternal copy of the U-B-E-three-A gene; the paternal copy is present in neurons but silenced by an antisense transcript. The therapeutic idea, and it is a beautiful one, is to inject an antisense oligonucleotide into the spinal fluid that degrades that silencing transcript and lets the healthy paternal copy switch back on. Aspire enrolled roughly a hundred twenty-nine children aged four to seventeen with a confirmed full maternal deletion, randomized one-to-one against a sham lumbar puncture over forty-eight weeks, with three monthly eight-milligram loading doses and then quarterly maintenance up to fourteen milligrams. The primary endpoint was change in the Bayley four cognitive raw score. The key secondary was net response on a multi-domain responder index spanning cognition, receptive communication, behavior, gross motor function and sleep. The company's own words: there were no differences between the treated and control groups that could support efficacy in the Bayley cognition raw scores, nor in the responder index looking at net response or at the mean change in any of the five individual domains. Safety was consistent with Phase one-two. That is a complete null across six pre-specified measures — not a near miss, not a powering problem. Chief executive Emil Kakkis said he was disappointed, and pointedly, disappointed for a patient community that had invested so much in early-stage research. The company will decide on the program's disposition and implement what it calls significant expense reductions. Shares closed the after-hours session at fourteen dollars sixty-seven, down forty-four point seven percent, on a hundred forty times average volume — about one point one four billion dollars of value gone, leaving a market capitalization of one point four five billion against twenty twenty-six revenue guidance of seven hundred thirty to seven hundred sixty million dollars. Evercore ISI cut to In Line with a sixteen-dollar target; Baird and J-P-Morgan also downgraded. The spotlight takes apart why the Phase one-two data looked so convincing and why that was the actual failure, and what it means for Ionis.

Second, the good news of the day, and it is substantial. Novartis reported topline results from REMODEL one and REMODEL two, twin Phase three trials of remibrutinib in relapsing multiple sclerosis. Remibrutinib is a covalent oral Bruton's tyrosine kinase inhibitor, already approved as Rhapsido in chronic spontaneous urticaria in the U-S last September and in Europe in April. Roughly two thousand patients with recent disease activity, randomized one-to-one against teriflunomide. Both trials hit the primary endpoint of annualized relapse rate and beat teriflunomide on all key secondaries within each trial, including M-R-I lesion reduction. In a preplanned pooled analysis it showed a positive trend on three-month confirmed disability progression and reached nominal significance on six-month confirmed disability progression. The critical detail is the safety line: no liver safety signal, no cases meeting Hy's law, across a program spanning more than forty-five hundred participants in neurologic and immune indications. That sentence is doing an enormous amount of work, because this class has failed in exactly two ways and remibrutinib just avoided both. Merck K-G-a-A's evobrutinib ran the same design — twin Phase threes against teriflunomide in relapsing disease — and failed to beat it on relapses, then picked up drug-induced liver injury cases and a partial clinical hold, and the company dropped it. Sanofi's tolebrutinib did the harder thing and hit on disability progression, cutting six-month confirmed progression thirty-one percent versus placebo in secondary progressive disease, but the F-D-A issued a complete response letter — the redacted version came out in January — citing a serious risk of severe liver injury it judged un-mitigable by the proposed risk management strategy: six Hy's law cases in roughly twenty-seven hundred treated patients, one liver transplant and death. Europe approved it in June anyway. So the state of the class going into yesterday was one drug that could not beat teriflunomide and one that could not clear F-D-A on hepatotoxicity. Novartis has now beaten teriflunomide with no Hy's law cases at all. The strategic frame is Roche: this is an oral pill going after the disability-progression territory that Ocrevus owns by infusion, and Novartis needs a successor to Kesimpta. Full data comes as a late-breaker at MSToronto, October twenty-first through twenty-third.

Third, uniQure has submitted its biologics license application for ifezuntirgene inilparvovec, formerly A-M-T one-thirty, seeking accelerated approval in Huntington's disease, and simultaneously filed a marketing authorization application with the U-K's M-H-R-A. This is a one-time A-A-V gene therapy delivered by M-R-I-guided infusion directly into the striatum, expressing a micro-R-N-A that knocks down huntingtin messenger R-N-A. The application rests on three-year data from the Phase one-two studies compared against a propensity-score-matched external control drawn from the Enroll-H-D natural history database, and a four-year analysis is promised before the end of this quarter. It is the first Huntington's candidate to hold both Breakthrough Therapy and R-M-A-T designations. Worth noting what a year it has been: uniQure spent it in public disagreement with the agency over exactly this external-control design. And worth noting the tension with our lead story — an external natural-history control is precisely the evidentiary structure that just detonated in Angelman. The difference uniQure will argue is that huntingtin knockdown is directly measurable and Huntington's progression is relentless and quantifiable, whereas pediatric neurodevelopment is not. That argument is now doing more work than it was a week ago.

Fourth, a first in celiac disease. Teva reported positive topline results from a Phase two-a study of T-E-V four-oh-eight, an anti-interleukin-fifteen monoclonal antibody, in fifty patients already on a gluten-free diet who then underwent a gluten challenge. A single subcutaneous dose produced a statistically significant and, in the company's characterization, clinically meaningful reduction in gluten-induced intestinal damage versus placebo, with lower gastrointestinal symptom scores on the celiac disease symptom diary and no safety signals. Interleukin fifteen is the cytokine that drives the intraepithelial lymphocyte expansion and enterocyte killing that produces villous atrophy — it sits downstream of gluten recognition, which is why blocking it should work regardless of which peptide triggered the response. There is no approved drug for celiac disease. The precedent here is Amgen's A-M-G seven-fourteen, licensed to Provention Bio as P-R-V zero-fifteen and now inside Sanofi: in a sixty-three-patient Phase two-a gluten challenge it cut symptom scores and intraepithelial lymphocyte counts by about thirty-eight percent each, and it went into a Phase two-b in gluten-free-diet non-responsive disease. So the mechanism has been de-risked once before and never converted. What is new in Teva's version is the single dose, and Teva is calling four-oh-eight a pipeline-in-a-product, meaning it intends to run it into multiple interleukin-fifteen-driven indications. Fast Track came in May of last year. Fifty patients and a challenge model is a long way from a registrational package, but a single dose moving a histologic endpoint is a real result.

Fifth, an F-D-A governance story with teeth. Endpoints reports that Asha Das, director of the biologics center's office of clinical evaluation, overruled her own review team to grant accelerated approval to Replimune's R-P-one, now marketed as Tudriqev, in advanced melanoma. We covered that approval in early August as the third act of a drama that included two rejections and an advisory committee that sided with the company against F-D-A scientists. What is new is the confirmation that the review team never came around — the approval was a management override. For anyone modeling regulatory risk, that is a different fact pattern than "the agency was persuaded," and it makes the confirmatory Phase three, reading out in twenty twenty-seven, load-bearing in a way an ordinary accelerated approval is not.

Sixth and seventh, two related items on where trials get run. The F-D-A is signaling a tougher line on foreign trial data as Chinese trial volume surges: more bioresearch monitoring inspectors at its China office and other global offices, remote assessments and information collection requests as supplementary tools, and heightened scrutiny of early feasibility and Phase one work conducted where the agency doubts informed consent was freely given. The House Appropriations Committee has separately approved nonbinding language that would bar acceptance of clinical data from China, Russia, Iran or North Korea. Against that backdrop, two pediatric deaths in Chinese gene-editing trials have reignited a technical argument that matters well beyond China. Both deaths — a six-year-old girl who died of thrombotic microangiopathy seven days after a C-R-I-S-P-R base editor for a nonfatal neurodevelopmental disorder, and a boy who died of acute respiratory distress syndrome in HuidaGene's H-G three-oh-two Duchenne trial — followed very high doses of adeno-associated virus. Most genetic-medicine experts quoted point at the viral load, not the editing. The Duchenne trial had no independent data safety monitoring board, and used a Chinese pathway that lets hospitals initiate studies without regulatory oversight. The disclosure came roughly twelve months late. For anyone with an in-vivo editing program, the read is that delivery dose, not the nuclease, is the safety-limiting variable, and that the sector's regulatory latitude is about to narrow.

Eighth, another domino in the Tavneos withdrawal. The M-H-R-A has barred Amgen's avacopan — the oral C-five-a receptor antagonist for A-N-C-A-associated vasculitis — from use in any new U-K patient as of September first, and intends to revoke U-K approval on March first, twenty twenty-seven, on the grounds that unreliable data from the pivotal trial can no longer demonstrate effectiveness. That follows the European Commission revoking marketing status last month. In the U-S, C-D-E-R proposed withdrawal on April twenty-seventh, saying the drug has not been shown to be effective and that the approval application contained untrue statements of material fact; Amgen submitted data on July twenty-third requesting a hearing. This is a rare case of a drug being unwound on data integrity rather than a new safety finding, and it is worth watching as a template.

Ninth, your platform item. Typewriter Therapeutics came out of stealth with a fifty-six-million-dollar Series A led by A-N Venture Partners and R-A Capital, with ANRI, Gemseki and the S-B-I U-S Gateway Fund. The technology is target-primed reverse transcription, built on the R-two retrotransposon — a natural gene-insertion machine. The therapy is two R-N-As: a messenger R-N-A encoding the R-two protein, and a second R-N-A carrying the therapeutic gene flanked by sequences that recruit that protein. The two assemble inside the cell, insert the gene at a specific site, and then the machinery degrades along with the messenger R-N-A, leaving only the insert. No D-N-A, no nuclease, no double-strand break, and in principle no persistent editing enzyme. Initial focus is in vivo C-A-R-T and genetic liver disease. Put this alongside ArsenalBio's pivot yesterday and the Novartis and Bristol holds from Monday — the entire in vivo cell-engineering field is now competing on the delivery-and-dose-control axis rather than on receptor design.

Tenth, the capital backdrop for the A-I-native side of the field. A-R-C-H Venture Partners is targeting a three-billion-dollar raise for its latest fund. A-R-C-H co-led Xaira's one-billion-dollar launch alongside Foresite Labs, and a fund that size is what keeps the thousand-million-dollar platform bet available as a category. On the A-I-in-discovery beat itself there was no major news overnight — the last two days gave us BigHat's first A-I-designed molecule into humans and Ono's option deal on Aitia's causal platform, and today is quiet. But I would flag that today's lead story is a genuinely important negative result for anyone building on target certainty. Angelman syndrome is about as solved as human genetics gets: one gene, a known imprinting mechanism, an unambiguous molecular fix. Knowing the target perfectly bought nothing. Whatever the failure turns out to be — insufficient reactivation, or reactivation in a brain too far along to reorganize — the lesson is that the hard part was never the target.
