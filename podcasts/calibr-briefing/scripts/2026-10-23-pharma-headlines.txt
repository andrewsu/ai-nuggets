Good morning, Travis. It's Friday, October 23rd, 2026, and the European Society for Medical Oncology congress opens today in Madrid. The biggest data of the week was supposed to stay embargoed until tomorrow afternoon. It didn't. Here's what you need to know.

First, and this is today's spotlight, so I'll keep it short here. Merck and Moderna were forced to release the full Phase 3 results for intismeran autogene yesterday afternoon, after the data were posted online in error and then pulled. Intismeran is the individualized mRNA neoantigen therapy formerly known as mRNA-4157, given on top of Keytruda in patients whose melanoma has been completely removed by surgery. It worked. Recurrence-free survival improved with a hazard ratio of zero point five eight, a forty-two percent reduction in the risk of recurrence or death across eleven hundred and thirty-seven patients. That is the first positive Phase 3 for an individualized neoantigen therapy, and the first for any mRNA cancer therapy. Moderna's stock fell about twenty-one percent. Merck fell about one percent. I'll explain that apparent contradiction in detail in the spotlight, because the explanation is more interesting than the headline.

Second, AbbVie reported Phase 3 results for lutikizumab in hidradenitis suppurativa, and this one is a genuine miss dressed as a win. Lutikizumab is a dual-variable-domain antibody that blocks both interleukin-1 alpha and interleukin-1 beta. In the roughly fourteen-hundred-patient INTREPID trial it hit its primary endpoint, with thirty-one point four percent of treated patients achieving the target reduction in abscess and nodule count at week sixteen, against twenty-three point four percent on placebo. That is an eight-point placebo-adjusted difference. The problem is the benchmark. UCB's Bimzelx, the current leader in this indication, delivers about twenty points placebo-adjusted. Merck's anti-TL1A antibody tulisokibart showed about twenty-five points in mid-stage work. And AbbVie's own Phase 2 data had suggested twenty-eight. Guggenheim called the placebo-adjusted rate disappointing and said monotherapy uptake will likely be limited, though the data could still support approval. Leerink was similarly negative. BMO took the other side, arguing the result at least validates interleukin-1 alpha-beta blockade as a mechanism in this disease. The read-through hit a neighbor hard: Avalo Therapeutics, which is developing the interleukin-1 beta antagonist abdakibart, fell more than thirty-six percent. Leerink called that selloff overdone, and I'd agree, since a dual-blocker underperforming doesn't cleanly indict a single-target follower. AbbVie plans thirty-six-week extension data next year and a possible filing in the second half of 2027, and is already planning combinations with two of its internal assets.

Third, Biohaven shares jumped about twenty-five percent after the FDA accepted the resubmitted application for troriluzole in spinocerebellar ataxia. This is the second time around. The agency issued a complete response letter in November 2025, and the core objection was evidentiary rather than safety-related: reviewers concluded effectiveness had not been established and that the key study, which used an external real-world control arm, did not qualify as an adequate and well-controlled investigation. The resubmission adds supportive analyses rather than a new randomized trial, and the missing-data problem that worried reviewers is substantial, with year-three ataxia ratings unavailable for fifty-two percent of treated subjects and seventy-nine percent of the external controls. The FDA has set a goal date in the first quarter of 2027 and, notably, plans to convene the Peripheral and Central Nervous System advisory committee. An adcomm on a resubmission after a complete response letter is not routine, and for a rare neurodegenerative disease with no approved therapy it will be a genuine test of how much weight external control arms can carry.

Fourth, Roche's quarterly pipeline update quietly killed an asset it paid a lot of money for. Among eight discontinuations, Roche dropped RG6540, the off-the-shelf CD19-by-CD20 CAR T that came with its acquisition of Poseida Therapeutics in January 2025 for up to one and a half billion dollars. The Phase 1 programs in lupus and multiple sclerosis were withdrawn and the B-cell malignancy study terminated. Chief executive Thomas Schinecker was careful to separate the molecule from the thesis, saying Roche still believes in CD19-by-CD20 CAR Ts and has stopped working on that particular molecule, with others he described as even better, though Roche hasn't named them or put them on the public pipeline. The centerpiece of the Poseida deal, the allogeneic BCMA CAR T for myeloma, is still running, but topline data aren't expected until 2029. Taken together with Caribou's decision earlier this month to halt both of its allogeneic CAR T programs for lack of financing, the off-the-shelf cell therapy field is consolidating around fewer, better-capitalized shots on goal.

Fifth, staying with ESMO, Merck will present three late-stage trials of sacituzumab tirumotecan, the TROP2 antibody-drug conjugate it licensed from Kelun, including the Phase 3 OptiTROP-Lung06 study in non-small cell lung cancer. A Merck vice president framed the molecule as sitting in a Goldilocks zone on payload release kinetics, which is the central engineering problem in this class: release too fast and you get systemic toxicity, too slow and you never reach a tumoricidal intracellular concentration. Given how much trouble the TROP2 ADC field has had converting response rates into survival, the Phase 3 data are the thing to watch, not the framing.

Sixth, a market datapoint worth holding onto. Thirty-one biotechs have gone public in 2026 so far, with four more on file. For context, that's eight in 2025, nineteen in 2024, thirteen in 2023, and seventeen in 2022. The window is genuinely open, not just reopening. This year also produced the largest biotech IPO on record, Parabilis Medicines at six hundred and seventy million dollars, ahead of Kailera at six hundred and twenty-five million and ADARx at four hundred and forty-six million. October alone has already delivered four debuts, including the AI-driven drug developer Iambic Therapeutics, which we covered last week. Still on file are Lycia Therapeutics, seeking about two hundred and twenty-eight million for its targeted protein degradation pipeline, Aspen Neuroscience with its autologous stem cell therapy for Parkinson's disease, the bispecific antibody developer Bambusa, and Rezera with its NLRP3 inhibitor.

Seventh, Ferring is cutting jobs in what it describes as a realignment behind strategic priorities, adding to a steady drumbeat of restructurings this year. EyePoint cut fifty-three percent of its workforce on the nineteenth after a Phase 3 miss in wet age-related macular degeneration, and Enlivex has cut sixty percent.

And looking at the weekend ahead, the Presidential Symposia run today through Sunday afternoon in Madrid. Beyond the Merck and Moderna readout, watch for Roche's divarasib in KRAS G12C-mutant lung cancer from the Krascendo-1 trial, Akeso's ivonescimab with overall survival data in first-line biliary tract cancer, the final analysis of adjuvant pembrolizumab in kidney cancer, and updated survival for capivasertib in prostate cancer. One caveat on all of it: most of these trials have already been declared positive in topline releases, so the question at ESMO is never whether they worked, it's the shape of the curves and what the subgroups show.
