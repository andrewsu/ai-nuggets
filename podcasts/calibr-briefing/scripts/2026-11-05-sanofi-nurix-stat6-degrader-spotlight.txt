Today's spotlight is a small program that got cut, and it tells you a lot about one of the most crowded targets in immunology. On Tuesday after the close, Nurix Therapeutics said Sanofi is stopping the first-in-human Phase 1 trial of S-A-R-four-four-eight-two-seven-two, an oral degrader of STAT-six, and ending the program. Nurix gave no reason. Its shares fell as much as thirty-eight percent after hours and were down more than forty percent in early trading Wednesday. The real question isn't about Nurix. It's whether this says something about STAT-six as a target, weeks before Kymera reads out the Phase 2b that will set the value of the whole class.

Start with why STAT-six matters. It's the transcription factor that sits directly under the I-L-four and I-L-thirteen receptors. When those cytokines signal, STAT-six switches on the type two inflammation program behind atopic dermatitis and asthma. That's the same pathway Dupixent blocks from the outside, by binding the I-L-four receptor alpha chain. So a pill that shuts off STAT-six could, in principle, give you Dupixent-like biology without an injection, and without the broad immune effects of a JAK inhibitor, which hits many cytokines upstream.

For decades, the problem was that STAT-six couldn't be drugged. The part you'd want to block, the S-H-two domain that docks onto the activated receptor, is large, flat and featureless, and nobody could find a selective small molecule that bound it tightly enough. Degraders changed that. A degrader only has to grab the protein somewhere and hold it next to an E-three ligase so the cell destroys it. That's why STAT-six went from undruggable to one of the hottest targets in immunology in about two years.

Now the deal history. Sanofi signed with Nurix in December 2019, with fifty-five million dollars upfront and a headline value around two and a half billion across multiple targets, and added twenty-two million a year later to expand it. Nurix brought its platform, DEL-AI: DNA-encoded libraries screened by the billions, with machine learning on top to pick and optimize hits. In June 2025, Sanofi exercised its option on the STAT-six program for fifteen million dollars. At the time, Nurix's chief scientific officer said the candidate, then called N-X-thirty-nine-eleven, gave anti-inflammatory efficacy in animal models "equivalent to a STAT6 gene knockout." That brought Nurix to a hundred twenty-seven million received, with another four hundred sixty-five million in STAT-six milestones still possible. A ten million dollar payment followed when the Phase 1 started this summer. The rest, about four hundred fifty-five million, is now gone, along with any royalties.

So what happened? Sanofi's release said nothing, but its investor relations team told Leerink the decision was based on, quote, "emerging nonclinical findings that did not warrant continued development in atopic dermatitis." Sanofi also told Leerink it still sees STAT-six as "an important pathway in immunology," and that this "particular molecule will not be able to move forward." Nonclinical means animal or lab data, not patients. The program had already cleared the toxicology needed to start Phase 1, so my inference, which no one has confirmed, is that this came from the longer-duration animal studies that run alongside early human trials, because you need them before dosing patients for months. If that's right, it's a molecule problem found on schedule, not a surprise from human data.

Analysts mostly took that at face value. Truist wrote that "initial discussions have pointed to molecule-specific attributes of SAR448272 rather than a target-related issue," and called the after-hours drop "an overreaction," since it gave the STAT-six program no value in its model. Leerink's David Risinger wrote, "We thus do not view this termination as a negative read-through to the broader STAT6 class." Jefferies' Roger Song said it doesn't touch Nurix's core value driver, which is bexobrutideg, the B-T-K degrader that Roche paid seven hundred million dollars upfront for in June and that is now in a Phase 3 in chronic lymphocytic leukemia. RBC's Brian Abrahams was the most candid about what surprised people: "We thought initial preclinical properties looked reasonably similar" to Kymera's drug.

Oppenheimer gave the full list of explanations, and it's worth taking each one seriously. The first is a degrader safety signal. The second is that Sanofi has another STAT-six program it likes better. The third is strategy: last month Sanofi paid Regeneron a billion dollars upfront for four long-acting next-generation antibodies, including a long-acting I-L-thirteen and an I-L-thirteen by I-L-four bispecific, as it searches for Dupixent's successor ahead of the 2031 U-S patent expiry.

The second point is the one I'd weight most. Sanofi didn't make one STAT-six bet. It made two. The other is with Recludix, a private San Diego company, on R-E-X-eighty-seven-fifty-six, now called S-A-R-four-four-eight-seven-five-five. That's a classical small-molecule inhibitor that actually binds the S-H-two domain, the thing degraders were supposed to make unnecessary. It started dosing in a Phase 1 in January, which paid Recludix a twenty million dollar milestone, and Sanofi still lists it in its third-quarter pipeline. So Sanofi is keeping the inhibitor and dropping the degrader on the same target. If the finding were about what happens when you remove STAT-six from the body, the inhibitor would face the same question. That Sanofi kept going with it is the best evidence that this is about the molecule. Sanofi has done this before: last year it dropped Kymera's I-R-A-K-four degrader K-T-four-seventy-four after Phase 2 and moved to Kymera's next-generation molecule, keeping the target and changing the compound.

There's a mechanistic reason to believe molecule-specific problems are common in this modality. A degrader is two molecules joined together: one end binds the target, the other binds the ligase, often cereblon. Each end brings its own off-target liabilities, and the ligase end can pull in other proteins and degrade them too. Two degraders against the same target can look alike in a potency assay and behave quite differently in a monkey. That's exactly the gap RBC's comment points to. "Similar preclinical properties" usually means potency, selectivity and pharmacokinetics, not chronic toxicology.

That's also the AI lesson here. Nurix's DEL-AI found chemical matter for a target nobody could drug, and the molecule reached the clinic. But what ended it was animal toxicology, which no discovery engine predicts well for a bifunctional molecule. AI speeds up getting to a candidate. It hasn't yet changed how often candidates die in toxicology.

Now the competitive landscape, which got simpler overnight. Kymera's K-T-six-twenty-one, a cereblon-based oral STAT-six degrader, is the clear leader. In its Phase 1b in atopic dermatitis, it cut STAT-six by a median ninety-four percent in skin and ninety-eight percent in blood, and reduced eczema severity scores by sixty-three percent and peak itch by about forty percent. That was a small early study, though. The real test is BROADEN2: about two hundred patients with moderate to severe atopic dermatitis, three doses against placebo, sixteen weeks, with eczema severity as the primary endpoint. It finished enrolling nearly six months early, and Kymera has guided to data by year end. Behind Kymera are the Sanofi–Recludix inhibitor in Phase 1, Gilead's oral STAT-six program, which it licensed from LEO Pharma in January 2025 for two hundred fifty million dollars upfront and up to about one point seven billion total, and patent filings from Pfizer and others.

So Sanofi stepping back leaves Kymera with one less degrader competitor, though a pharma that owns Dupixent still has an inhibitor in the race. The read-through to Kymera is mixed. If you believe Sanofi, there's no class problem, and a big rival just dropped out. But until BROADEN2 reports, nobody outside Sanofi can rule out that a STAT-six degrader liability shows up only with longer exposure, and Kymera is the one carrying that question into a placebo-controlled trial.

For Nurix, the cash position cushions the hit: one point one billion dollars as of the end of August, enough to fund operations into the second half of 2029. Sanofi still holds a license to a second transcription-factor program with an undisclosed target, and Nurix said in 2025 that its STAT-six work included additional discovery-stage assets. Nurix also kept an option to co-develop and co-promote in the U-S after proof of concept, so it's worth asking whether Nurix can get STAT-six rights back, and which backup molecules would come with them. Nurix hasn't said.

Here's what to watch. First, Kymera's BROADEN2 by year end, which now carries the weight of the whole class. Second, whether Sanofi or Nurix ever describes the nonclinical finding. If it's tied to the ligase-binding half, every degrader using the same ligase will get questions. Third, whether Sanofi moves the Recludix inhibitor into Phase 2, which would confirm its interest in STAT-six is intact. Fourth, any deal involving the rights Nurix may get back.

The takeaway: a target can be validated and still not fit a particular molecule. Sanofi kept its inhibitor and dropped its degrader on the same target in the same year. That tells you the bet on STAT-six is still on. What's in question is whether degrading STAT-six is the right way to hit it, and Kymera's data, due by year end, is where we find out.
