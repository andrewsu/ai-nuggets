This is your spotlight for Friday, October ninth. Today: argenx's Sjögren's trial was stopped for futility, and what that says about which autoimmune diseases you can treat just by lowering antibodies.

Here's what happened. Before the market opened on Thursday, argenx said it is ending UNITY, its Phase three study of subcutaneous efgartigimod, sold as Vyvgart Hytrulo, in adults with moderate-to-severe Sjögren's disease. An independent data monitoring committee looked at an interim analysis and concluded the trial cannot meet its primary endpoint, change from baseline in systemic disease activity on the clinESSDAI scale at week forty-eight. Safety was clean. The company didn't release any efficacy numbers. It told William Blair that the futility look included more than five hundred of the six hundred thirty patients enrolled, which suggests the analysis came well before the planned week-forty-eight readout, a result nobody expected until the second half of twenty twenty-seven. U-S shares fell about fourteen percent, roughly a hundred thirty dollars, to around seven hundred ninety-seven. That still leaves argenx worth about fifty billion dollars, more than Biogen.

Why the market cared. Vyvgart made about a billion and a half dollars in the second quarter alone, and the argenx story has always been one mechanism, many diseases. Sjögren's was the biggest unclaimed one. Patients' immune systems attack the glands that make tears and saliva, and many also have fatigue, joint pain and disease in other organs. No therapy is approved to change its course. William Blair had modeled one point four billion dollars in peak Sjögren's sales and called this the next potential blockbuster expansion opportunity. Analysts' overall Vyvgart peak estimates, eleven to seventeen billion dollars, mostly excluded Sjögren's, so near-term numbers barely move. What investors sold was the idea that the label keeps growing one disease after another.

The analyst reaction was measured. H.C. Wainwright's Douglas Tsao called it a good-news, bad-news day and a surprising setback, but kept his buy rating and a twelve hundred two dollar target. William Blair's Matt Phipps wrote that, given argenx's premium valuation, Phase three setbacks have historically caused sharp pullbacks that ended up being buying opportunities. Baird cut its target to eight hundred twenty-three dollars and warned that the abrupt stop would hurt sentiment.

Now the main point. When we covered argenx's myositis win in August, we said every FcRn trial tests one specific hypothesis: is this disease driven by pathogenic I-g-G? Efgartigimod is an antibody fragment that occupies the neonatal Fc receptor, the receptor that rescues I-g-G from degradation and gives it its three-week half-life. Block it and all I-g-G drops, autoantibodies included, while B cells, T cells and complement are left alone. In myasthenia gravis, where the antibody against the acetylcholine receptor clearly causes the disease, that works. In myositis it worked in the subtypes with well-defined autoantibodies. We called Sjögren's the real test of how far the mechanism travels, because there the antibodies' role is much murkier.

UNITY was designed to give the hypothesis its best shot. Every patient had to test positive for anti-Ro antibodies, the hallmark autoantibody of Sjögren's, and have at least moderate systemic disease. Lowering I-g-G in those patients for most of a year apparently didn't move systemic disease activity enough.

Compare the drugs that have worked in Sjögren's. Novartis' ianalumab, an antibody against the B-cell activating factor receptor that both depletes B cells and cuts their survival signal, won two Phase three trials. Amgen's dazodalibep blocks C-D-forty ligand, the signal T cells use to activate B cells, and its Phase three was positive last month. RemeGen and Vor Bio's telitacicept, which neutralizes the B-cell growth factors BLyS and APRIL, won a Phase three in China. All three act upstream, on the B cells and the T-cell help that drive them, and on the clusters of immune cells that build up inside the salivary glands. None of them depends on lowering circulating antibody. The simplest reading of Thursday's result is that in Sjögren's, anti-Ro is mostly a marker of a disease run by cells in the tissue, not the cause of the damage. If you remove the antibody and leave the cells in place, the disease continues.

There are two fair caveats. First, Sjögren's trials are hard to win. William Blair pointed to high placebo response rates, and argenx's chief medical officer called it one of the most heterogeneous and complicated diseases in immunology. Until argenx releases the data, we can't tell whether efgartigimod had a small effect that was swamped by placebo or no effect at all. Second, this was always shakier than the stock implied. The Phase two RHO study had only thirty-four patients. It showed a strong composite response, forty-five percent versus eleven on placebo, but William Blair notes that on the share of patients improving by at least three points on the main disease activity scale, it showed no difference from placebo. A small trial that wins on a composite but not on the standard scale isn't strong support for a Phase three on that standard scale.

Next, what it means for the rest of the class. Johnson and Johnson's nipocalimab, another FcRn blocker, had a positive Phase two in Sjögren's called DAHLIAS, and its Phase three, DAFFODIL, is still running. J and J's exploratory analyses found the best responses in patients with the highest autoantibody levels. That's the obvious argument for why a selected population might do better, and it's also the kind of subgroup argument that often fails in Phase three. Immunovant has its own Sjögren's study of its FcRn blocker I-M-V-T fourteen-oh-two. Immunovant shares fell about six percent on Thursday, to around thirty dollars, and its parent Roivant fell about two. Wainwright said it is now more cautious about the Immunovant study, which follows a late-September miss for fourteen-oh-two in cutaneous lupus that ended that program. For the FcRn class, it's now two expansion misses in three weeks in diseases where the antibody's role was debatable.

On the same day, though, someone paid real money for I-g-G lowering. Biohaven licensed its I-g-G degrader portfolio, led by BHV-thirteen-hundred, to Japan's Ono Pharmaceutical for Japan, South Korea, Taiwan and Southeast Asia: eighty million dollars up front, twenty million more on a milestone expected within a year, and royalties of about twenty percent. BHV-thirteen-hundred is a small molecule that sends I-g-G to be destroyed rather than blocking its recycling, and it spares the I-g-G-three subclass. Its Phase three is in Graves' disease, where an antibody against the thyroid-stimulating hormone receptor directly drives the disease. In a small Phase one-b, that autoantibody fell more than eighty percent by week twelve and thyroid hormones normalized. That's a good illustration of the lesson. Antibody-lowering drugs work in diseases where the antibody does the damage, such as myasthenia, C-I-D-P, Graves' and parts of myositis. They struggle where the antibody is just a sign of something else going on. The companies that choose indications on that basis, rather than by listing every disease with an autoantibody, will spend their Phase three money better.

The second announcement Thursday shows how argenx is adapting. FB102, from the two point two billion dollar Forte Biosciences acquisition announced in July, met its primary endpoint in a Phase two celiac study. FB102 blocks C-D-one-twenty-two, the beta chain shared by the interleukin-two and interleukin-fifteen receptors. Interleukin-fifteen is central to celiac damage: it arms the lymphocytes in the gut lining that destroy the villi when a patient eats gluten. The trial enrolled a hundred twenty-six patients who had been symptom-free on a strict gluten-free diet, gave them an eight-week gluten challenge, and measured how well the gut lining held up, the ratio of villus height to crypt depth on biopsy. FB102 beat placebo with a p-value of about zero point zero two. Immune-cell density in the gut lining and symptoms moved the same way. TD Securities' Yaron Werber, who models nearly a billion dollars in peak celiac sales, wrote that the result derisks a second franchise rather than just adding another Vyvgart indication. One caveat: a gluten challenge is a controlled test of whether the drug protects against a known exposure. It's not the same as showing benefit in everyday patients who get accidental exposures. Phase three will need to show that, and no one has defined that path in celiac yet.

Taken together, argenx learned something important about the limits of its main mechanism on the same morning it showed it has a second mechanism. It has had expansion failures before, in immune thrombocytopenia and pemphigus in twenty twenty-three and thyroid eye disease last year, and the stock recovered each time. This one is bigger because Sjögren's was the largest indication on the list, and because it suggests the right question for a new indication isn't "is there an autoantibody?" but "does the autoantibody cause the disease?"

What to watch. First, the full UNITY data after database lock, especially the placebo response and whether any anti-Ro-high subgroup moved. Second, J and J's DAFFODIL Phase three, now the deciding test for the FcRn class in Sjögren's. Third, ianalumab's U-S filing and dazodalibep's second Phase three, which will decide who owns this market. Fourth, argenx's next Phase three readout, empasiprubart in multifocal motor neuropathy, which blocks the complement protein C-two rather than FcRn, and full FB102 data at a medical meeting. That's your spotlight.
