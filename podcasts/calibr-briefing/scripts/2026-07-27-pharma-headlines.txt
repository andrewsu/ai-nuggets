It was a quiet weekend, so the real action is what's coming Wednesday — the single most consequential regulatory event of the year for one small company. Five things today, and the spotlight goes deep on Replimune.

First, and it's the spotlight, Replimune. This Wednesday, the F-D-A's cell and gene therapy advisory committee meets to vote on R-P-one, an oncolytic virus therapy for advanced melanoma, with the agency's own decision due August second. What makes this a genuine cliffhanger is that R-P-one has already been rejected twice, and Replimune has said plainly in its filings that the drug's development isn't viable without a timely approval. I'll walk through what the therapy is, why the F-D-A keeps balking, and how the vote is likely to break, in the spotlight.

Second, AstraZeneca picked up a European approval worth watching for how it changes the timing of treatment, not just the treatment. The drug is Etcamah, generic name camizestrant, a next-generation oral pill in a class called selective estrogen receptor degraders — it doesn't just block the estrogen receptor that drives most breast cancers, it tears it down. The approval is for first-line E-R-positive advanced breast cancer, combined with a C-D-K four-six inhibitor, and the clever part is the trigger. Instead of waiting for a scan to show the tumor is growing, doctors monitor the blood for an emerging resistance mutation called E-S-R-one, and switch the patient onto Etcamah the moment it shows up. In the pivotal trial, that approach cut the risk of progression or death by fifty-six percent and nearly doubled the time before the cancer advanced. It's the first drug of its kind cleared to act on a blood signal before the disease visibly worsens.

Third, Johnson and Johnson put up what may be the best data yet for bispecific antibodies in multiple myeloma. In a trial called MonumenTAL-six, J-and-J combined two of its bispecifics — Talvey and Tecvayli, each of which grabs a T-cell with one arm and a myeloma cell with the other, aimed at two different tumor targets — together with an older drug, pomalidomide, in patients earlier in the course of their disease. The combination cut the risk of the cancer progressing or the patient dying by eighty-nine percent, and the risk of death alone by sixty-two percent, against the standard of care. Those are the strongest numbers anyone has posted for bispecifics in this setting, and they push these drugs from a last resort toward earlier, frontline use.

Fourth, the obesity fight between the two giants got nastier. Novo Nordisk asked a federal court for a preliminary injunction to force Eli Lilly to pull a set of national advertisements Novo calls misleading. The heart of the complaint is that Lilly's ads stack the highest approved doses of its drugs, Zepbound and Mounjaro, against lower doses of Novo's Wegovy and Ozempic — all of them in the G-L-P-one class — while leaving out Novo's newer high-dose data. Lilly says its ads are truthful. The read for you is that with both companies' injectables now in a genuine street fight for share, the competition has moved from the clinic to the courtroom, and that's usually the sign that the easy growth is over.

And fifth, a signal from the A-I side of drug development. Formation Bio, one of the A-I-native drug developers, hired Michael Ehlers as its chief scientific officer and head of research and development. Ehlers is not a lightweight — he ran R-and-D at Biogen, led neuroscience research at Pfizer, and founded a string of biotechs as a venture partner. The interesting part isn't the hire itself, it's the pattern. The A-I-first companies are no longer selling the algorithm alone. They're buying the seasoned drug-hunter's judgment to sit on top of the platform, because the hard part was never generating molecules, it's picking the right target and running the trial that proves it. That's a maturation signal for the whole space.
