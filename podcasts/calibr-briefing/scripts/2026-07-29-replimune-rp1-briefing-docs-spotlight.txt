Let's go deeper on Replimune, because tomorrow's advisory committee meeting is one of those events where the specific drug almost matters less than the precedent it sets. I gave you the setup on this a couple of days ago. Today the F-D-A's own briefing documents are public, and they reframe the whole thing.

Start with what the drug is. R-P-one is an oncolytic virus. Replimune took a herpes simplex virus — the cold-sore virus — and engineered it so that instead of causing disease it selectively infects and bursts tumor cells. A clinician injects it directly into a melanoma lesion. The theory is elegant: the virus lyses the tumor from the inside, spills tumor antigens into the surrounding tissue, and sets off an immune response that — and this is the crucial promise — travels through the body to attack tumors the needle never touched. Replimune pairs it with Bristol Myers Squibb's Opdivo, a P-D-one checkpoint inhibitor, in patients whose melanoma has already stopped responding to that class of drug. That's a population with real unmet need and few good options left.

On paper, the data looked like a winner. The pivotal study, called IGNYTE, enrolled about a hundred and forty patients, and Replimune reported an objective response rate near thirty-four percent, with roughly fifteen percent of patients seeing their tumors disappear entirely. They reported a median duration of response around two years and a median overall survival of almost thirty-three months — genuinely striking numbers for late-line melanoma. So why is the F-D-A's staff so hostile?

The answer is one word that appears over and over in the briefing documents: interpretable. Or rather, not interpretable. IGNYTE was a single-arm study — everyone got the drug, there was no comparator group. And the agency's reviewers made three arguments that stack on top of each other. First, without a control arm and without a reliable historical benchmark, they say it simply cannot be determined whether R-P-one is responsible for what was observed, versus the Opdivo, versus the natural variability of the disease. Second — and this is the technical heart of it — they argue the way responses were assessed confounds the results and inflates them. When the agency ran its own analysis, that thirty-four percent response rate fell to under sixteen, and the two-year duration of response shrank to about fourteen months. That's not a rounding error; that's the agency saying the headline number is roughly double what the data actually support. And third, they made the point that because the drug is injected locally, the entire question of whether the benefit is systemic — whether it's really reaching distant tumors — is exactly what a single-arm study cannot answer.

When those documents posted, Replimune's stock fell about twenty-eight percent in a day. B-M-O Capital's read was blunt: the briefing documents read negatively, and the panel skews toward a negative vote, even if some practicing oncologists might argue for approval on the strength of the long-term survival curve.

Now here's the piece of context that makes this more than a one-company story. This is Replimune's third try. The F-D-A has already turned R-P-one down twice. Those earlier rejections were widely blamed on Vinay Prasad, who ran the F-D-A's biologics center and was a famous, vocal skeptic of single-arm trials and accelerated approvals. Prasad stepped down in April. A lot of investors read his departure as the cloud lifting — as the agency pivoting toward the more flexible, industry-friendly posture the new administration had been signaling. And yet here we are: the briefing documents are, if anything, harsher than before, and they're coming from the career review staff, not from one departed official. That's the real lesson. The skepticism about single-arm data isn't one person's ideology you can simply wait out. It's baked into how the agency's scientists think about evidence.

And that's why you should care about tomorrow's vote even if you never touch an oncolytic virus. A huge number of small oncology companies — and plenty of the accelerated-approval strategies across this industry — are built on exactly this bet: run a single-arm study, show a striking response rate in a desperate population, and get to market fast on the promise of a confirmatory trial later. R-P-one is now the clean test case for whether that bet still pays in the current F-D-A. If the panel votes yes, it tells every one of those companies that a compelling response rate and a plausible mechanism can still carry the day. If it votes no — and the briefing documents are pointing that way — the message is that a single-arm response rate, however good it looks, isn't enough when the agency can't verify attribution and can't rule out that the assessment itself inflated the number. That raises the evidentiary bar for a whole category of programs and, frankly, makes randomized data look a lot less optional.

A couple of things to keep in mind as it plays out. The advisory committee's vote is exactly that — advisory. The F-D-A doesn't have to follow it, and its own decision deadline is August second, just days away. But it is genuinely rare for the agency to approve over the objection of both its own reviewers and an outside panel, so a negative vote tomorrow would put R-P-one in a deep hole. The most likely path back for Replimune, if it comes to that, is the one the agency has been signaling all along: a proper randomized, controlled confirmatory trial that finally settles the attribution question the single-arm study left open.

So watch tomorrow. The narrow question is whether one melanoma drug reaches patients. The wide question — the one that should be on your radar for the portfolio — is how much evidentiary rope the post-Prasad F-D-A is actually willing to give single-arm accelerated approvals. Tomorrow's vote is the best read we're going to get. Talk soon.
