Good morning Travis. Here are your biotech and pharma headlines for Saturday, August twenty-ninth, twenty twenty-six.

Yesterday the individualized cancer vaccine story got its second act, and it is not the one the market was pricing. Nine days after Moderna and Merck won the first Phase three ever run on a neoantigen therapy, BioNTech and Genentech killed theirs in colorectal cancer for futility. Around that, two approvals — one of them a genuinely new mechanism in hematology — a heavy European Society of Cardiology hotline slate that delivered one clean win, one durability update and one autopsy, Novartis walking straight into the trial that just failed for Novo, another billion-dollar China antibody-drug conjugate deal, and an A-I drug discovery company filing Chapter eleven. Nine items, then the week ahead.

First, and today's spotlight. BioNTech and Genentech terminated the Phase two trial of autogene cevumeran in resected colorectal cancer. The independent data safety monitoring board recommended stopping for futility, citing a numerical imbalance in overall survival between the arms and concluding that continuing was unlikely to change the efficacy outcome. The trial had actually crossed its futility boundary back in October of last year, at which point the board judged the data insufficiently mature and let it run. This time it did not. BioNTech was explicit that the decision was not driven by safety and that no new safety signals were identified. Shares fell about seven and a half percent to a hundred three dollars and ten cents. Moderna, which had nothing to do with the trial, fell about six percent in sympathy — which tells you the market read this as a statement about the modality rather than about one company. B-M-O Capital Markets called it a clear setback and said it raises the question of whether vaccine-induced immune responses can prevent recurrence without concurrent checkpoint inhibition or chemotherapy. That question is exactly right, and it is the whole spotlight this morning, because the trial that failed and the trial that succeeded differ on three design axes and the failure is far more informative than the headline suggests.

Second, the approval with the biggest revenue attached. The F-D-A approved Eli Lilly's Mounjaro, tirzepatide, to reduce the risk of cardiovascular death, heart attack and stroke in adults with type two diabetes and established cardiovascular disease. It is the first dual G-I-P and G-L-P-one receptor agonist to carry a cardiovascular risk reduction indication. The evidence is SURPASS-C-V-O-T: thirteen thousand patients across thirty countries, more than four and a half years, and — this is the part worth noting — the comparator was not placebo. It was Lilly's own Trulicity, dulaglutide, a G-L-P-one with an established cardiovascular benefit. That is an active-controlled outcomes trial, which is a much harder and much more expensive thing to run, and tirzepatide came in eight percent better on three-point major adverse cardiovascular events on top of an already-active control. The strategic point is that this closes the last label gap with Novo Nordisk. Semaglutide has had cardiovascular indications on both the diabetes and obesity sides for a while; Lilly now has the diabetes side, and the dual-agonist mechanism gets its first outcomes-level validation rather than just a weight and glucose story.

Third, and the more interesting approval scientifically. The F-D-A approved Mimrylo, rusfertide, from Takeda and Protagonist Therapeutics, for adults with polycythemia vera. This is the first hepcidin mimetic ever approved for anything. Polycythemia vera is a myeloproliferative neoplasm — almost always JAK-two driven — in which the marrow overproduces red cells, and the standard of care has for decades been therapeutic phlebotomy, which is to say bloodletting, plus cytoreduction. Rusfertide is a peptide that mimics hepcidin, the master hormone regulating iron availability. By restricting iron export from macrophages and enterocytes, it starves erythropoiesis of substrate rather than suppressing the clone. That is a genuinely different lever — it does not touch the driver mutation at all, it removes the raw material. The pivotal VERIFY trial hit its primary endpoint with seventy-six point nine percent of patients on rusfertide plus standard of care achieving clinical response at weeks twenty through thirty-two, against thirty-two point nine percent on placebo plus standard of care. On the business side, watch the structure: Protagonist discovered the molecule and ran it through Phase three, then elected to opt out in April, handing regulatory and commercial responsibility to Takeda. The approval triggers two hundred seventy-five million dollars to Protagonist — a two hundred million opt-out fee plus a seventy-five million approval milestone — with up to eight hundred seventy-five million more in milestones and tiered royalties running from fourteen to twenty-nine percent of worldwide net sales. A discovery company taking a fourteen-to-twenty-nine percent royalty on a first-in-class mechanism it invented, with none of the commercial build, is about as clean an outcome as that model produces.

Fourth, from the European Society of Cardiology congress in Munich, the cleanest positive of the meeting. Cytokinetics presented ACACIA-H-C-M in a hotline session and published simultaneously in the New England Journal of Medicine. Aficamten, brand name Myqorzo, is a cardiac myosin inhibitor — it reduces the number of actin-myosin cross-bridges and therefore hypercontractility, which is the core pathophysiology of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. It is already approved in the United States, the European Union and China for the obstructive form. ACACIA tested the non-obstructive form, which is the harder indication because there is no outflow gradient to knock down and therefore no convenient surrogate. At thirty-six weeks the drug separated on all five efficacy metrics at p less than zero point zero zero one, with fifty-three percent of aficamten patients showing a clinical response on three or more outcome measures against thirteen percent on placebo. The safety signal to track is ejection fraction: left ventricular ejection fraction below fifty percent occurred in ten percent of aficamten patients versus one percent on placebo, though interruptions for ejection fraction below forty percent were rare. That is the known class liability — you are deliberately reducing contractility — and ten versus one percent is the number the label conversation will be about. Cytokinetics expects to file the supplemental application in the fourth quarter.

Fifth, staying at the congress, the durability update. CRISPR Therapeutics presented one-year Phase one-a data on C-T-X three-ten, an in vivo CRISPR-Cas nine therapy that knocks out A-N-G-P-T-L three in the liver, with simultaneous publication in the New England Journal. Fifteen participants, single ascending intravenous doses. At the top dose, mean reduction from baseline was seventy-nine percent for A-N-G-P-T-L three with a maximum of eighty-nine percent, forty-eight percent for triglycerides, and fifty-three percent for L-D-L cholesterol — all sustained through a full year after one infusion. No dose-limiting toxicities, no treatment-related serious adverse events, no Grade three or higher transaminase changes; three grade-two infusion reactions, all resolved. Chief Medical Officer Naimish Patel framed the point correctly: the news is not the depth of the knockdown, it is the persistence. Luke Laffin of the Cleveland Clinic put the clinical case in one line — for high-risk patients the challenge is usually not starting therapy, it is staying on it. That is the entire thesis for one-shot cardiovascular gene editing, and a year of durability is the first real evidence for it. The Phase one-b is running a fixed flat dose, with a severe hypertriglyceridemia update expected in the second half of this year.

Sixth, the autopsy at the same meeting. AstraZeneca and Ionis presented the full CARDIO-T-T-Ransform dataset and published it in the New England Journal. You will remember the topline missed back in July and we spotlighted it on the tenth. The detail now available is worse than the topline implied. Over fourteen hundred patients, a hundred forty weeks: twenty-nine percent of eplontersen patients had a cardiovascular event or died versus thirty-two percent on placebo, not statistically significant. Break it apart and the combination story falls over — in patients already on tafamidis, Pfizer's stabilizer, the hazard ratio was one point one four, meaning numerically worse. Monotherapy looked considerably better, around a twenty-nine percent improvement. Stifel's Paul Matteis: this result makes it hard for us to see a path towards filing or approval. The read-through is the thing to hold onto. Alnylam is down roughly thirty percent since the topline, and the design question now aimed at its next-generation candidate nucresiran is that it, too, permits background stabilizer use. The field spent two years assuming silencer plus stabilizer was additive. That assumption is now the open question, not the premise.

Seventh, Novartis is going anyway. At the same congress Novartis said it will push pacibekitug, its anti-interleukin-six antibody, into Phase three for cardiovascular risk reduction — despite Novo Nordisk's ziltivekimab having missed its primary endpoint in the ZEUS trial, which we spotlighted on August first. Recall the shape of that failure: ziltivekimab hit its target cleanly, driving down free interleukin-six and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein exactly as designed, and the biology did not convert into fewer major adverse cardiovascular events in patients with atherosclerosis and chronic kidney disease. Novartis paid one point four billion dollars for Tourmaline Bio to get pacibekitug, and its Phase two data showed hs-C-R-P reductions of eighty-five to eighty-six percent — that is target engagement of the same kind that did not save Novo. So the bet is not on the mechanism working better; it is on population selection and endpoint design working better. Whether that is conviction or sunk cost is the thing to watch, and a Phase three in this space is a very expensive way to find out.

Eighth, the deal. Genentech is paying DualityBio of Shanghai forty-five million dollars upfront with more than a billion in development, regulatory and commercial milestones plus tiered royalties, for the D-U-P-A-C platform. The technical premise is specific and worth understanding: rather than a new target, this is a new payload class aimed at resistance to topoisomerase-one inhibitors, which are the payload underneath the entire current generation of antibody-drug conjugates — Enhertu, Trodelvy, Datroway. Those payloads are effective and patients relapse on them, so the field's next constraint is not finding antigens, it is what you hang off the linker. The molecules also have a short half-life and clear quickly, which is a therapeutic-index play. DualityBio runs discovery through Phase one-a and Genentech takes it from there. This is Roche's second Asia deal in a week — the other being up to two point three billion dollars to Hanmi for a lean-mass-preserving obesity candidate, which was our spotlight on the twenty-fifth — and it follows five hundred seventy million to MediLink in January. The China licensing pipeline is now a structural feature of large-cap R and D, not an opportunistic one.

Ninth, three quick notes, and the first is the cautionary one. BioXcel Therapeutics filed for Chapter eleven and agreed to sell its assets to Teva, which is the stalking-horse bidder at fifty-seven and a half million dollars upfront plus sixty-seven and a half million in milestones, up to a hundred twenty-five million total. The asset is Igalmi, dexmedetomidine sublingual film, for agitation in schizophrenia and bipolar disorder, with an at-home administration decision pending on November fourteenth. BioXcel marketed itself as an A-I-derived drug discovery company, and this is the shape that story takes when the science works and the commercial model does not — Igalmi is approved and on the market, and the company still could not fund itself to the next label. Second, on the A-I side with better news, ProFound Therapeutics, the Flagship company, took an investment commitment of up to thirty-five million dollars from the Gates Foundation — twenty million upfront, fifteen million on experimental milestones — to apply its platform and agentic A-I tooling to placental tissue and serum from women with preeclampsia and eclampsia, hunting previously undetected proteins as targets and biomarkers. Preeclampsia is one of the few large indications where the target space is genuinely unmapped, so a proteomics-first approach is well matched to it. And third, a correction to yesterday's number: Moderna's convertible note offering was upsized. It priced at two point six billion dollars, not the two billion announced, which only reinforces the point — the convertible window is wide open for anyone with a catalyst behind them.

Now the week ahead. The European Society of Cardiology congress runs through Monday in Munich, so there is more hotline data coming this weekend. The European Respiratory Society congress opens September fifth and runs through the ninth — that is where Generate Biomedicines' three already-public posters on golukibart get their official presentation. On the regulatory calendar, Ultragenyx's Sanfilippo A gene therapy has a September nineteenth action date, Scholar Rock's apitegromab is September thirtieth, BioXcel's at-home Igalmi decision is November fourteenth if the bankruptcy process leaves anyone to receive it, and Capricor's deramiocel remains pushed to November twenty-second. Cytokinetics files for non-obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy in the fourth quarter.
