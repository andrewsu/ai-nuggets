Good morning Travis. Here are your biotech and pharma headlines for Friday, September eighteenth, twenty twenty-six. Today is a rare disease day at the top. The F-D-A approved the first treatment ever for Sanfilippo syndrome type A, and it did something in that approval that matters well beyond one ultra-rare disease — that is where the spotlight goes. Bayer also got a first-in-thirty-years kidney approval, Roche read out a confirmatory trial for a T-cell engager and signed a billion-dollar bispecific deal, Novo signed its third platform deal in a week, and Bristol Myers handed a degrader conjugate back to its partner. Eleven items, then the week ahead.

First, and today's spotlight. The F-D-A granted standard full approval to Fayuvi, also known as U-X one-eleven, from Ultragenyx, for pediatric patients with mucopolysaccharidosis type three-A, better known as Sanfilippo syndrome type A. It is the first approved treatment for that disease, full stop. Sanfilippo type A is caused by deficiency of the sulfamidase enzyme, which leaves heparan sulfate to accumulate in cells and destroy the developing brain; children lose cognition, language and motor function, and median life expectancy is about fifteen years. Fayuvi is a single intravenous infusion of an A-A-V nine vector carrying a working copy of the S-G-S-H gene. The approval came two days ahead of the September nineteenth action date, and thirteen months after a complete response letter that was purely about manufacturing — chemistry, controls and facility inspection findings, with no clinical issues raised. Ultragenyx set the U-S list price at three point nine five million dollars, received a priority review voucher, and expects product shipping to qualified treatment centers within thirty to sixty days. The stock rose about thirteen percent. The reason this is the spotlight is the evidence the agency accepted: seventeen treated children against an external natural-history cohort of twenty-seven, on a cognitive raw score — and the company had asked for accelerated approval on a biomarker and got a full approval on the clinical endpoint instead. That has consequences for everyone benchmarking against natural history right now.

Second, Bayer got a genuine first. The F-D-A approved Kerendia, generic name finerenone, for adults with chronic kidney disease associated with type one diabetes. Finerenone is a nonsteroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist, and this is its third indication after type two diabetic kidney disease and heart failure. It is the first new therapeutic advance for kidney disease in type one diabetes in more than thirty years, which tells you how completely that population has been left to A-C-E inhibitors and angiotensin receptor blockers. The basis is the Phase three FINE-ONE trial in two hundred forty-two adults on standard of care: about a twenty-five percent placebo-adjusted reduction in the urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio over six months, roughly twenty-two percent by month three and twenty-eight percent by month six. Note what the label rests on — albuminuria, a surrogate expected to translate into slower decline in estimated glomerular filtration rate and less end-stage kidney disease, not a hard outcome. Overall adverse events were essentially identical to placebo, forty-seven percent versus forty-nine, but hyperkalemia ran ten point one percent against three point three on placebo, which is the same tradeoff nephrologists already manage with this class.

Third, Roche put a confirmatory win on the board for a T-cell engager. The Phase three CELESTIMO study of Lunsumio, mosunetuzumab, in combination with lenalidomide met its primary endpoint of progression-free survival in relapsed or refractory follicular lymphoma. The comparator is the interesting part. Mosunetuzumab is a C-D twenty by C-D three bispecific, and the control arm was rituximab plus lenalidomide — so the engager replaced the anti-C-D twenty antibody in the standard combination rather than being stacked on top of it. Overall survival was immature at this interim analysis and no new safety signals appeared. This is the study Roche needed to convert Lunsumio's accelerated approval in third-line-and-later monotherapy into full approval, and to earn a second-line-and-later indication. Data go to health authorities and to an upcoming medical meeting.

Fourth, Novo signed its third platform deal in barely a week. Novo and fellow Danish company Orbis Medicines will collaborate on multiple macrocycles against undisclosed cardiometabolic targets, with Orbis eligible for as much as one point four billion dollars in upfront and downstream payments plus royalties. Macrocyclic peptides are the current best answer to the oral biologic problem, and the problem is concrete: the highest-dose Wegovy pill contains twenty times the highest injectable dose because most of the active ingredient is destroyed before it reaches the bloodstream. Orbis designs its molecules with a computational platform it calls n-Gen, aimed at compounds that are both orally bioavailable and able to hit hard intracellular and extracellular targets. It launched in January twenty twenty-five with ninety-three million dollars and Lilly among its backers. Merck validated the category commercially earlier this year with Lipfendra. Worth noting for your own book: Foresite is in this same lane through Pinnacle Medicines, which raised an oversubscribed eighty-nine million dollar Series B in March on an oral peptide platform.

Fifth, Roche also bought optionality in immunology bispecifics. It is paying Dualitas Therapeutics thirty-six and a half million dollars up front, with up to a billion in milestones and royalties, to screen more than three hundred thousand potential bispecific antibody combinations for immune disease targets. Dualitas is not building conventional bispecifics that bridge two cells. Its molecules use what it calls a proximity engager to force two proteins on the same cell into contact, creating effects neither target produces alone. Roche gets to pick a limited number of programs and Dualitas keeps whatever Roche passes over. Co-founder Forbes Huang says each of the company's own three programs beat commercial and clinical benchmarks head-to-head in preclinical work; the lead, D-T-X one-oh-two in rheumatoid arthritis, reaches the clinic in twenty twenty-seven. Dualitas launched last year with sixty-five million from Qiming and Versant.

Sixth, a modality test failed. Bristol Myers Squibb returned O-R-M six-one-five-one to Orum Therapeutics after reviewing Phase one data. That molecule is a degrader-antibody conjugate — an anti-C-D thirty-three antibody delivering a G-S-P-T one degrader instead of a cytotoxic payload — in acute myeloid leukemia and high-risk myelodysplastic syndromes. Bristol Myers paid one hundred million up front for it in twenty twenty-three; eighty million in milestone payments now will not be paid, and Orum's shares hit their daily downside limit in Seoul. The company still has its own clinical-stage degrader conjugates, so the read is narrower than the headline — one payload against one antigen in a hard disease — but it is the first real clinical verdict on the class from a large partner.

Seventh, the capital markets are wide open and the structures are getting creative. Electra Therapeutics priced a three hundred fifty million dollar I-P-O, upsized from the terms it set on Monday, making it the twenty-first biotech to go public this year; eleven of those have raised three hundred million or more, matching all of twenty twenty-one and beating the previous four years combined. Electra's ipsoprubart is a pan-S-I-R-P antibody in a registrational trial in secondary hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, a disease with no approved therapy; it trades today as E-T-R-A. Separately, Lisata Therapeutics is acquiring Marea Therapeutics alongside a two hundred twenty-five million dollar private placement from R-A Capital, Third Rock, Forbion, Sofinnova, Perceptive, venBio and others — Marea holders end up with about sixty percent of the company and legacy Lisata shareholders with two point four. Marea brings M-A-R oh-oh-one, an antibody against A-N-G-P-T-L four that augments lipoprotein lipase activity, in Phase two-b for severe hypertriglyceridemia, and M-A-R oh-oh-two, an allosteric growth hormone receptor antagonist in acromegaly; both read out in the fourth quarter of twenty twenty-seven. And North Immunology is going public through a reverse merger with Aethlon Medical plus a one hundred eighty million dollar placement from Bain Capital, Janus Henderson and Deep Track. Its N-O-R one-oh-one targets interleukin thirteen, like Dupixent, but adds interleukin eighteen; Phase one-a starts in the first quarter. That is roughly two dozen reverse mergers this year, and the buyers are now good companies rather than distressed ones.

Eighth, a mixed and important day for Xenon. The company submitted its N-D-A for azetukalner in focal seizures, built on the Phase two-b X-TOLE and Phase three X-TOLE-two trials, where all four doses significantly cut monthly seizure frequency, with more than fifteen hundred patient-years of safety data behind it. Azetukalner is a K-v seven potassium channel opener, the same mechanism the field has been trying to rehabilitate since retigabine. In the same release, Xenon voluntarily paused new enrollment in its major depressive disorder and bipolar depression studies after analyzing neuropsychiatric adverse events — events it says are consistent with the drug's known profile but that had not appeared in the earlier X-NOVA study in depression. The epilepsy trials are unaffected. X-NOVA-two is about eighty percent enrolled toward four hundred fifty patients, still powered on H-A-M-D seventeen, and will be completed and unblinded, with topline now in the first quarter of twenty twenty-seven. Put this next to the F-D-A's partial enrollment hold on Biohaven's K-v seven program two weeks ago and the pattern to watch is the mechanism's tolerability ceiling outside epilepsy, not either company's execution.

Ninth, a Parkinson's licensing deal with an unusually thin front end. S-K Biopharmaceuticals licensed global rights to one-S-T one-oh-four from fellow Korean company one-S-T Biotherapeutics, an oral small molecule that inhibits both L-R-R-K two and c-Abl. Total potential value is three hundred fifteen million dollars, but the upfront is one point eight million with another near-term one point eight million and a two point two million dollar equity investment. Hitting both a kinase implicated in genetic Parkinson's and a stress kinase tied to alpha-synuclein pathology is a reasonable thesis; the economics say the data are early.

Tenth, a launch worth thirty seconds. G-O-A Therapeutics came out of stealth with fifteen and a half million dollars and a candidate, G-O-A twenty-six, for acute alcohol intoxication and poisoning — an engineered enzyme that accelerates ethanol metabolism paired with a chemical scavenger that sequesters acetaldehyde, so you do not simply trade ethanol for a worse metabolite. In I-N-D-enabling pig studies after an oral ethanol dose, blood alcohol was sixty-one percent lower than untreated controls at twenty minutes. The pitch is a rescue agent for emergency departments, and the data were presented at the trauma surgery meeting in Dallas.

Eleventh, market context. The Federal Reserve raised rates a quarter point on Wednesday. The X-B-I sold off on the announcement and is down more than three percent over five days, but it is up more than twenty-six percent year to date at around one hundred fifty-five dollars. B-M-O Capital Markets argues higher rates now drive stock selection rather than sector-wide de-rating, because the M-and-A bid is structural — large pharma facing loss of exclusivity needs differentiated assets. Their caveat is the one that matters for early-stage financing: the discrimination falls hardest on companies without a near-term catalyst or a balance sheet.
