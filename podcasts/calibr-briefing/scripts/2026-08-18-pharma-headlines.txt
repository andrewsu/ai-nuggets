Good morning, Travis — this is your biotech and pharma briefing for Tuesday, August eighteenth, twenty twenty-six. Yesterday was one of those days where the tape did all the work: a sixteen-percent winner, a seventy-percent loser, and a big pharma that killed a late-stage program and declared two wins in the same news cycle. Six items.

Number one, and today's spotlight: argenx said its FcRn blocker Vyvgart Hytrulo — efgartigimod — passed a Phase 3 test in autoimmune myositis, and the stock jumped almost seventeen percent, adding something on the order of nine billion dollars of market value in a day. The headline number is a fifteen-point advantage on the Total Improvement Score at one year versus placebo, with separation showing up as early as week four and holding through a protocol-mandated steroid taper. The part that matters most: the trial hit in immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy, a subtype with no approved therapy at all, and it's the first Phase 3 ever to do so. There's a caveat in the fine print about the dermatomyositis subtype and about polymyositis, which argenx is now dropping. I'll take all of that apart in the spotlight.

Number two, the mirror image: EyePoint's Duravyu missed the primary endpoint in LUGANO, its first pivotal trial in wet age-related macular degeneration, and the stock fell about seventy percent. Duravyu is vorolanib — a small-molecule inhibitor of the VEGF receptor tyrosine kinases — delivered from a bioerodible intravitreal insert, and the whole pitch is duration: fewer injections than Regeneron and Bayer's Eylea. It didn't clear the bar on best-corrected visual acuity against on-label aflibercept. Management's explanation is that nine of two hundred eleven patients, about four percent, lost fifteen letters or more for reasons unrelated to their macular degeneration, with no matching cases in the control arm — an asymmetry they argue distorted the mean. Investors aren't waiting to find out. The second pivotal, LUCIA, reads out in the fourth quarter, and a filing next year depends entirely on it. The sustained-delivery thesis in retina keeps breaking on the same rock: Eylea's efficacy bar is high and the comparator arm is very well run.

Number three: AstraZeneca had a split decision in lung cancer, all in one morning. It discontinued the Phase 3 eVOLVE-Lung02 trial of volrustomig — its PD-1 by CTLA-4 bispecific — in first-line metastatic non-small cell lung cancer with PD-L1 expression under fifty percent, after the independent monitoring committee concluded the combination was unlikely to hit either progression-free or overall survival in the PD-L1-negative primary analysis population. No new safety signals; the cervical, head and neck, and mesothelioma trials continue. On the same day it announced two wins: Enhertu, the HER2 antibody-drug conjugate partnered with Daiichi Sankyo, beat Keytruda plus chemotherapy on progression-free survival in first-line HER2-mutant non-squamous lung cancer — the first HER2-directed drug to do that — and Orpathys plus Tagrisso beat platinum chemotherapy on both progression-free and overall survival in MET-driven, EGFR-mutant disease. The read-through: the checkpoint-bispecific bet keeps getting harder to make, while the targeted and ADC franchises compound.

Number four, and it belongs right next to that: Summit and Akeso's ivonescimab got its third China approval — first-line squamous non-small cell lung cancer with chemotherapy — but the label the regulator posted alongside it contained the surprise. It showed the progression-free survival benefit in the HARMONi-6 trial degrading with longer follow-up. Ivonescimab is the PD-1 by VEGF bispecific that basically created this whole category, and the concern Leerink raised is that the ongoing global HARMONi-3 trial, meant to support U.S. accelerated approval, may not show a substantial enough benefit. Their language was increased pessimism, and their view is that the FDA's bar for accelerated approval on progression-free survival in first-line lung cancer is high. They pushed their squamous launch estimate out two full years, to the fourth quarter of twenty twenty-nine. Summit closed down four percent, and with six hundred ninety million in cash — under a year of runway — the November FDA decision in EGFR-mutant disease now carries a lot of weight. One nuance worth holding onto: a benefit that's large early and shrinks later is exactly what you'd expect if the VEGF arm is doing more of the work than the PD-1 arm.

Number five, this morning's freshest deal: Leo Pharma is licensing dersimelagon from Japan's Tanabe Pharma, paying up to four hundred thirty-five million dollars in upfront and near-term payments. Dersimelagon is an oral, small-molecule agonist of the melanocortin-1 receptor — it drives melanin production in the skin — and it's for erythropoietic protoporphyria and X-linked protoporphyria, rare disorders where sunlight exposure causes severe, burning pain. It cleared its global Phase 3, the INSPIRE study, earlier this year and is under FDA review. For Leo, a dermatology company, it's a clean fit; for the field, another data point that oral small molecules keep winning rare-disease licensing dollars against injectables.

Number six, two quick ones on the platform and cost side. Eli Lilly signed a global collaboration and license with OmniAb for an ion-channel discovery program — up to three hundred seventy million in milestones plus tiered royalties, target and modality undisclosed. Ion channels are among the hardest targets to raise antibodies against, and OmniAb has built a screening franchise there; the deal let it raise year-end cash guidance to roughly fifty million. And on the other side of the ledger, Sanofi cut two hundred twenty-nine employees at Blueprint Medicines, the company it acquired last year — routine post-acquisition consolidation, but the integration bill always comes due.

That's your Tuesday briefing. The spotlight goes deep on argenx: what the myositis result actually proves about autoantibody biology, why the subtype breakdown is more interesting than the headline, and what it means for the four-way race to own the FcRn class. Have a good one.
