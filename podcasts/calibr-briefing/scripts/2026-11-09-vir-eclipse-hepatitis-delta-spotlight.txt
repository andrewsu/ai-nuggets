This is your Calibr spotlight for Monday, November ninth, 2026. Today: Vir Biotechnology's first pivotal win in hepatitis delta. ECLIPSE 1 drove the virus to undetectable levels in nearly four out of five patients. I'll cover why the drug design works, what the numbers do and don't prove, and how a market that had no approved drug in the U-S six months ago now has three companies competing for it.

Start with the disease, because the biology explains the drug. Hepatitis delta is a satellite virus. It has its own small R-N-A genome and can copy it inside a liver cell, but it can't make its own envelope. To leave one cell and infect the next, it borrows hepatitis B's surface antigen as its coat. So it only exists in people who already have hepatitis B, roughly five percent of the quarter-billion people chronically infected worldwide. In those people it's the worst form of viral hepatitis, moving to cirrhosis, liver failure and liver cancer faster than hepatitis B alone. The standard hepatitis B pills don't touch it, because they block an enzyme the delta virus never uses.

That dependence gives you three ways to attack it. You can block entry, which is what Gilead's Hepcludex, bulevirtide, does by blocking NTCP, the receptor both viruses use to get into liver cells. It's a daily injection. Europe approved it in twenty twenty, and the U-S gave it accelerated approval this May. You can mop up the coat with an antibody against surface antigen, which is Mirum's brelovitug. Or you can stop the coat from being made at all. Vir's combination does the last two at once. Elebsiran is an s-i-R-N-A, licensed from Alnylam and delivered to the liver, that degrades hepatitis B's R-N-A and so cuts production of surface antigen. Tobevibart is an antibody against surface antigen. It blocks entry, and its Fc region is engineered to help the immune system clear antigen complexes, with Xencor's half-life extension so it can be dosed monthly. The logic is simple: starve the virus of its coat, and catch whatever coat is still made.

Now the trial. ECLIPSE 1 randomized a hundred twenty-four adults two to one, to monthly elebsiran plus tobevibart or to delayed treatment that started at week twelve. It ran in the U-S and other regions where bulevirtide is hard to get. About fifty-five percent of patients had compensated cirrhosis. The primary endpoint was strict: undetectable delta virus R-N-A plus a normal A-L-T, the liver enzyme, at week forty-eight. Forty-four percent of treated patients hit it, versus zero in the delayed arm, measured at week twelve. Seventy-nine percent reached undetectable virus, eighty-five percent of the cirrhotic patients and seventy-three percent of the rest. Every single patient had a virologic response, meaning undetectable virus or at least a hundredfold drop. And ninety-five percent got their surface antigen below ten international units per milliliter, which is the number to remember. Safety was clean: one grade three event, a drop in neutrophils that resolved in fifteen days, and no treatment interruptions or discontinuations. The most common side effect was flu-like symptoms, mostly at the first dose and gone within a day or two.

Nancy Reau of Rush University, who presented the data, put the clinical bar this way: "the goal is not simply to reduce the amount of virus, but to drive it to levels where it is no longer detectable since ongoing H-D-V replication drives liver disease progression." That's the right lens for these results, and it's why undetectable virus is the number that separates Vir from what came before.

Here's how to calibrate it. In Hepcludex's pivotal trial, as HCPLive summarized it, about twenty percent of patients had undetectable virus at week forty-eight. Vir reports seventy-nine. Hepcludex was approved on a composite with a looser viral bar, at least a hundredfold drop or undetectable, plus a normal A-L-T, with responses in the mid to high forties. Vir hit forty-four percent on a stricter composite that requires undetectable virus. Mirum's AZURE-1, which read out in late September, reported fifty-six percent on its weekly dose and forty-five on the monthly dose, but on the looser composite, and at week twenty-four rather than forty-eight. These are cross-trial comparisons, in different populations, with different timepoints. HCPLive also cautions that ECLIPSE 1 was open-label against no treatment, not against an active drug. Still, what's distinctive about the Vir data is how deep the viral suppression goes, not the composite.

Three caveats. First, the primary comparison is lopsided by design: forty-eight weeks of treatment against twelve weeks of nothing. That's fine for showing the drug works, since untreated delta virus doesn't clear itself, but the zero in the control arm says little. Second, the gap between seventy-nine percent undetectable and fifty-three percent with a normal A-L-T is the question I'd ask on this morning's call. If the virus is gone and the liver enzyme hasn't normalized in a quarter of patients, is that lag, fatty liver or alcohol, or a cost of the immune clearance the antibody is built to drive? Regulators care about the composite, and so will physicians. Third, every A-L-T and fibrosis result here is a surrogate. Nobody has shown yet that these drugs reduce liver cancer or death, though the biology makes it likely.

The more interesting data came out alongside. In the Phase 2 SOLSTICE trial, all twenty-eight patients who finished ninety-six weeks of the combination had undetectable virus. Liver stiffness, a non-invasive stand-in for fibrosis, improved or held steady in twenty-four of twenty-seven. Then there's an exploratory piece. Patients who stayed undetectable for ninety-six weeks could choose to stop treatment. Ten did, seven of them cirrhotic. All ten were still undetectable twelve weeks off drug, seven of them at twenty-four weeks, and one at forty-eight, with no viral rebound and no A-L-T flares. Tarik Asselah of Hôpital Beaujon in Paris said the potency "provides optimism for the potential of elebsiran and tobevibart to offer finite treatment," and Vir's chief medical officer, David Liu, went further and said "cure." Be careful here. That's ten self-selected patients, only one past a year off therapy. But if it holds, it changes the product: a defined course of treatment rather than an injection for life. And that's where the ninety-five percent with surface antigen below ten matters. Surface antigen loss is what functional cure of hepatitis B looks like. An entry inhibitor like Hepcludex doesn't touch surface antigen production, so a regimen that drives it toward zero is in a different category.

Now the race. Gilead has the only approved U-S drug and a European franchise six years old, but it's a daily injection with modest rates of undetectable virus. Mirum bought brelovitug in its six-hundred-twenty-million-dollar Bluejay acquisition and is ahead on timing. Its second Phase 3, AZURE-4, reads out by the end of this year, and Mirum plans to file in the first half of twenty twenty-seven, with a possible launch late that year. Vir files in mid-twenty twenty-seven, after ECLIPSE 2 and 3 read out in the first quarter. When Mirum read out in September, Leerink called brelovitug commercially differentiated in a nascent market. It pointed to Mirum's enrollment of sicker patients and its low rate of flu-like symptoms as its edges over Vir's combination. Vir has now answered the first point, with more than half its patients cirrhotic and better results in them. The flu-like symptoms are real but mostly a first-dose event. Vir's chief executive, Marianne De Backer, is going for "best-in-disease." The analyst notes will come after the eight-thirty call this morning, and I'd watch for what they make of the composite and of the finite-treatment data.

The trial that will settle it is ECLIPSE 3, which puts Vir's combination head-to-head against bulevirtide in patients who haven't had it. ECLIPSE 2 tests switching patients whose virus wasn't suppressed on bulevirtide. If both read out cleanly in the first quarter, Vir goes to the F-D-A with head-to-head data against the incumbent. How big is the market? Mirum estimates about fifteen thousand U-S patients are diagnosed with delta and under care, and about two hundred thirty thousand potentially eligible across the U-S and Europe. Most patients are undiagnosed, because testing for delta in hepatitis B patients is still patchy. This is a rare-disease launch built on screening. Vir has already licensed Europe, Australia and New Zealand to Norgine, so the U-S is where Vir keeps the economics. It has about nine hundred million dollars in cash as of September, plus ninety million upfront from a T-cell engager deal with Gilead, which is a reasonable balance sheet to take into a filing and launch.

What should Foresite and Calibr take from this? Three things. First, combining modalities is where the depth came from. Pairing an Alnylam-delivered silencer with an engineered antibody against the same protein is a design pattern that travels to other diseases. Second, after yesterday's discussion of genetics-validated targets that failed outcome trials, it's worth noticing why this one worked. For an infectious disease the target is the pathogen itself, so causality isn't in doubt. The hard part was depth and durability, and that's an engineering problem that platforms solve. Third, for a company that a few years ago was best known for a COVID antibody, Vir has rebuilt itself around two programs: this one and its masked T-cell engagers. If ECLIPSE 2 and 3 hold, hepatitis delta funds the oncology bet, and that's the clearest turnaround story in infectious disease biotech right now.
