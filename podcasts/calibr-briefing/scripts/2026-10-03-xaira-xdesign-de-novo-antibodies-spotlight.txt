Today's spotlight is Xaira Therapeutics, which on Friday published the first real technical account of what its molecule-design model can do, along with the first look at two programs from its pipeline. This matters to you for three reasons. Xaira launched in April twenty twenty-four with more than a billion dollars committed, co-led by ARCH Venture Partners and Foresite Labs, so this is portfolio news. David Baker is a co-founder, so the provenance runs straight back to the Institute for Protein Design. And the specific claim being made is the one that antibody discovery groups — Calibr's included — actually care about. Not that a model can generate a binder. That a model can generate a molecule you can put on a development path, in one pass, against a target that defeated conventional discovery.

Let me set up the framing the Xaira design team uses, because it is unusually disciplined for a company blog post. They start by conceding the state of the field. Since the first demonstration that you can design antibodies de novo with real epitope specificity and therapeutic affinity, several groups have reported designed binders with good hit rates and good affinities — but in many cases against targets and epitopes that were already well characterized and already well served by traditional methods. That is a fair description of most of the published work, including some of Xaira's academic lineage. So they propose a two-part bar. For de novo design to meaningfully supplement or replace traditional antibody discovery, it has to do at least one of two things. It has to be faster or cheaper than traditional approaches on a real therapeutic target — can one design pass give you a fully developable lead against the epitope you chose? Or it has to work where traditional approaches fail outright — can design succeed on a target that has already resisted both conventional methods and earlier computational ones?

They then take one program for each question. XA-1 is the speed case. XA-4 is the failure case. And the vocabulary that holds both together is a term Xaira has been building toward in earlier posts: the progressable binder. Not a binder. A molecule that is simultaneously human-like, potent, selective, cross-reactive into the surrogate species you need for toxicology, and developable — meaning it survives the panel of assays that predict whether you can actually manufacture and formulate the thing. The model class they describe, which they call Vega, is trained to optimize for all of those at once rather than to maximize binding and let the rest be somebody else's problem. That distinction is the whole argument, because binding and developability trade off against each other, and the history of antibody engineering is largely the history of paying for binding with downstream pain.

Now the first case. XA-1 is Xaira's lead oncology program. The requirement was narrow: bind a precision-constrained epitope that is selective over closely related family members, cross-react with the homologous protein in the surrogate species, land inside a specific affinity window — not simply as tight as possible — and carry all the other progressable properties. They designed a hundred eighty-two single-domain antibody sequences, V-H-H format, de novo against that chosen epitope, and ran them through surface plasmon resonance against the human and surrogate proteins, then through a battery of developability assays — polyspecificity, heparin and cation exchange binding, hydrophobic interaction chromatography, a baculovirus particle assay, and thermal stability.

Initial screening gave twenty-five hits, eighteen confirmed binders after full quality control — a ten percent binder rate — and nine with high-quality kinetic fits. The best molecule bound the human target at seventeen nanomolar and the cynomolgus version at eighty-three nanomolar, passed every developability criterion, and scored high on humanness. One additional optimization round brought the surrogate cross-reactivity up, and that produced the preclinical lead.

The number Xaira is selling is the clock. Three weeks of wet-lab work from designs to a progressable binder. Seven weeks from D-N-A synthesis to lead molecule, including that optimization round. For context, the sequence this replaces — hit-to-lead campaigns, affinity maturation, humanization, then developability triage and reformatting — is routinely six to twelve months, and humanization alone can eat a quarter. There is also a quieter data point in their first figure that I think is more informative than the seven weeks: the Vega model roughly triples the rate of human-like V-H-H sequences that satisfy every developability criterion, compared with a baseline version of the same model trained without Xaira's proprietary data. That is the clearest thing in the post about where the advantage comes from. It is not the architecture. It is the proprietary assay data that lets the model navigate the binding-versus-developability trade-off.

Be honest about the caveats on this one, though. Xaira is explicit that the seven weeks was enabled by precursor work already being in place: the antigen and reagents were made, and crucially they had already decided which epitope they wanted. Picking the epitope is often the hard scientific judgment, and the model did not make it. They also note that an earlier version of X-Design had already produced binders to this target — those just needed more optimization. So XA-1 is a genuine acceleration result, but it is an acceleration on the tractable end of the problem, and there is no head-to-head conventional arm run alongside it.

The second case is the one that would change how you plan a portfolio. XA-4 is a G-protein-coupled receptor, and it is the hard kind. Heavy post-translational modification. A small, flexible extracellular region. No clear structural understanding of which epitope maps to which function. No preexisting broadly antagonizing antibodies to copy from. And the requirement was not just a binder but an antagonist against the human receptor that also cross-reacts with both cynomolgus and mouse.

What makes this a real experiment is that Xaira ran the conventional arm, and it failed. Two campaigns of naive V-H-H library screening, four rounds of selection each, produced putative hits that enriched in the library and then failed to bind when they were produced clonally — which is the classic enrichment artifact. A llama immunization campaign against the same target produced no functional binders at all. Earlier versions of their own model had also failed on it. So the baseline here is not a slower method. It is zero.

The design approach was different in kind from the XA-1 campaign, and this is the methodological point worth taking away. Because they did not know the functional epitope or the relevant conformation, they did not pick one. They modeled conformational flexibility across multiple candidate epitopes, probed the target first with designed minibinders, and then used the model to generate a library of sixty thousand V-H-H designs — the explicit purpose being to interrogate many conformations and many epitopes in a single campaign. They panned that library against the receptor displayed in nanodiscs, and called hits with an enrichment model across selection rounds rather than by raw enrichment, which is presumably the fix for exactly the artifact that sank the naive library.

From that they picked ninety-two candidates, confirmed seven as binders by flow cytometry on receptor-overexpressing cells against null controls, and found six that were functional inhibitors in a beta-arrestin reporter assay against the natural ligand. The best one gives better than ninety-six percent maximum inhibition at a potency of a hundred eighty-three nanomolar, binds antigen-positive human cells at thirty-two nanomolar, cross-reacts with mouse and cynomolgus, and has a clean developability and humanness profile. It is now in lead optimization.

Hold two things in your head about that result. The first: a hundred eighty-three nanomolar is modest potency, and seven binders out of ninety-two is not a high hit rate. If you read this as "AI designs drug," you will be disappointed. The second, which I think is the correct reading: the conventional comparator produced nothing, twice, by two different methods, and complete functional antagonism with built-in rodent and primate cross-reactivity is a legitimate starting molecule. The design step did not replace the campaign — there is still a sixty-thousand-member library and a panning workflow in there. What it replaced was the diversity source. Instead of sampling an immune repertoire and hoping it contains something, you are sampling a hypothesis space you constructed on purpose, structured around the conformations you think matter.

For calibration, they also show the easy version: against CXCR4 and the apelin receptor APJ, both of which have known structures and accessible epitopes, a single plate of ninety-one designs each gave thirty-two and twenty-six hits — thirty-five and twenty-nine percent hit rates — with best potencies essentially matching the positive controls, roughly thirteen nanomolar against CXCR4 versus eight point six for the control, and eight point four against APJ versus eight point eight. So the model is strong where structure is known, and the XA-4 workflow is what they reach for when it is not.

Now the read-through. Three things.

First, and this is Xaira's own framing: if it holds, the pipeline stops being selected by tractability. Their line is that programs can now be driven by the therapeutic potential and biological understanding of the target rather than by whether the target is amenable to antibody discovery. Every antibody group in the industry, Calibr included, maintains an informal list of targets that were abandoned not because the biology was wrong but because nothing bound them usefully. A method with a credible shot at that list changes what is worth putting back on the board — and G-protein-coupled receptors are the biggest single entry on it, with a large fraction of them still unaddressed by biologics.

Second, the species cross-reactivity point deserves more attention than it will get. In both campaigns, mouse and cynomolgus cross-reactivity was a design objective, not something discovered afterward. Anyone who has watched a program stall because the lead does not bind the tox species, forcing you into a surrogate molecule and a second manufacturing campaign, knows what that is worth. That is a schedule and budget lever independent of any claim about speed of discovery.

Third, the format. These are V-H-H single domains, which is exactly the building block you want if the molecule's destination is a multispecific — a T-cell engager arm, a targeting domain on a conditionally active construct, a CAR binder. A method that reliably produces developable, humanized, species-cross-reactive single domains against epitopes you specify is, functionally, a parts supplier for every modality Calibr builds. The interesting question is not whether Xaira's two molecules work in the clinic. It is whether the capability is specific to Xaira's proprietary data, or whether this is the beginning of binder generation becoming a commodity input.

And that question connects directly to yesterday's spotlight. Sanofi paid Regeneron a billion dollars up front for four antibodies against twenty-year-old targets, which I argued was a price on format rather than on biology. Put the two stories side by side. If de novo design keeps moving in this direction, format gets cheaper too — and the durable value migrates to the things a model does not do: which target, which epitope, and whether modulating it actually changes the disease. Xaira told you as much, in their own caveat. Having progressable antibodies to these two programs does not guarantee their ultimate success.

Two honest limitations before we close. Neither target is disclosed, so nothing here is independently checkable — you are taking the company's word on the difficulty of XA-4 and the constraints on XA-1. And Xaira says a systematic assessment of the model is coming in a future post, which is an acknowledgment that this is two case studies, not a benchmark. Watch for that assessment, and watch for whether XA-1 and XA-4 actually file to go into humans. Those are the two events that would turn this from a very good engineering demonstration into the thing the company was funded to be.
