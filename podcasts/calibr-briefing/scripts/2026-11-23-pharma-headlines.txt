Novartis says Leqvio, its twice-yearly cholesterol siRNA, has met its primary endpoint in a cardiovascular outcomes trial. Lilly is buying a Cambridge, U-K Huntington's startup for up to seven hundred twenty-five million dollars. A short seller has gone after Abivax's ulcerative colitis drug a few weeks before the company plans to file it with the F-D-A, and that's today's spotlight. And the Novartis C-E-O, who sits on Anthropic's board, says A-I won't cure cancer within ten years. Here's what you need to know.

First, the biggest clinical news of the morning. Novartis announced at seven a-m Basel time that ORION-four, the cardiovascular outcomes trial for Leqvio, or inclisiran, met its primary endpoint. Inclisiran is a small interfering R-N-A that silences PCSK9 in the liver. Novartis licensed it from Alnylam. Less PCSK9 means more L-D-L receptors on liver cells, so more L-D-L cholesterol is cleared from the blood. Its selling point is dosing: an injection in the clinic, a second at three months, then one every six months. The trial was led by the University of Oxford with the TIMI Study Group and randomized more than sixteen thousand people with established atherosclerotic disease to inclisiran or placebo on top of standard therapy. The primary endpoint was a composite of coronary death, heart attack, ischemic stroke and urgent coronary revascularization. Novartis calls the reduction statistically significant and clinically meaningful but gave no number: no hazard ratio, no event counts, and nothing yet on cardiovascular death, which is a secondary endpoint. Full results are due in the first half of 2027. Why it matters: the PCSK9 antibodies, Amgen's Repatha and Sanofi and Regeneron's Praluent, have had outcomes data for years. Leqvio has been sold on L-D-L lowering alone, and that gap has been the standard objection from cardiologists and payers. Three days ago, at its investor day, Novartis raised its Leqvio peak sales forecast to five billion dollars. This result backs that forecast, but how much depends on the size of the risk reduction, and we don't have it yet. A second outcomes trial, VICTORION-two PREVENT, reads out in 2027. Merck's oral PCSK9 inhibitor, enlicitide, is the next competitor, and its own outcomes trial is still running.

Second, a deal. LoQus twenty-three Therapeutics, a preclinical company in Cambridge, England, says Lilly has acquired it for up to seven hundred twenty-five million dollars in cash. That's an undisclosed upfront plus development and regulatory milestones. The drug is LQT-twenty-three, an oral allosteric inhibitor of MSH3, a DNA mismatch-repair protein. The biology is one of the more interesting threads in neurodegeneration. In Huntington's, the C-A-G repeat in the huntingtin gene keeps lengthening in neurons over a person's life, and that somatic expansion seems to set when symptoms start. Genetic studies of what modifies age of onset keep pointing to mismatch-repair genes, MSH3 among them. The idea is that blocking MSH3 slows the expansion, which should slow the disease. LQT-twenty-three blocked expansion in cell and animal models, and a clinical trial application is expected by the end of the year. So Lilly is paying up to seven hundred twenty-five million dollars for an asset that hasn't been tested in people. The company was seeded by SV Health's Dementia Discovery Fund. Novartis Venture Fund led a seed extension, and Forbion led a thirty-five million pound Series A in October 2024. Endpoints counts this as Lilly's fifteenth acquisition this year. LoQus says the same approach could work in other repeat-expansion diseases, including myotonic dystrophy type one.

Third, Abivax, which is today's spotlight. On Friday, Scorpion Capital published a two-hundred-ninety-seven-page short report titled "A Biotech Fraud for the Ages." It goes after obefazimod, Abivax's once-daily oral drug for ulcerative colitis. Abivax says obefazimod works by boosting an anti-inflammatory microRNA, miR-one-twenty-four. Scorpion claims the drug works through nonspecific toxicity instead, and that its own lab tests suggest it could be a photocarcinogen, meaning it might cause skin cancer when exposed to light. The U-S shares closed down about nine percent at seventy-three dollars and twenty cents, after falling as much as twenty-two percent intraday, on roughly ten times normal volume. CNBC says the Paris shares fell seventeen percent. After the close, Abivax said the report contained "numerous factually inaccurate and misleading statements." It also pointed out that Scorpion is short the stock and said it would start covering on publication. The shares were up about ten percent in Paris this morning. Abivax still plans to file with the F-D-A by the end of the year. In the spotlight we go through which allegations hold up and which don't.

Fourth, A-I in drug discovery. Last week the research heads at seven big pharmas told Reuters that A-I is cutting early discovery time. Now the Novartis C-E-O is pushing back on the bigger claims. Vas Narasimhan told the Financial Times that A-I will not cure cancer in five to ten years. That matters because he has sat on Anthropic's board since April, and Anthropic C-E-O Dario Amodei said during last week's I-P-O that A-I could cure cancer and Alzheimer's in that window. According to India Today's write-up of the interview, Narasimhan said tech companies treat medicine like an engineering problem, when science understands only about five percent of what happens in the body. He also said long animal and human trials aren't going away. His research president, Fiona Marshall, has warned that many A-I-generated ideas fail even in basic cell assays. Big pharma's position is consistent: it's happy to use A-I for discovery, and it's skeptical that A-I gets around clinical trials. Expect to hear it often while Anthropic and Isomorphic are raising money on the bigger promise.

Fifth, a company to watch over the holidays. BioSpace profiles Praxis Precision Medicines, which has two F-D-A decision dates coming. The first is on or before December twenty-seventh, for relutrigine, a sodium channel blocker that targets the persistent sodium current, in S-C-N-two-A and S-C-N-eight-A developmental and epileptic encephalopathies. In its pivotal study, fifty-one patients had a fifty-three percent placebo-adjusted reduction in seizures over sixteen weeks. The F-D-A pushed the date back three months after Praxis submitted extra sensitivity analyses, but it hasn't asked for another trial. The second is ulixacaltamide in essential tremor, due on or before January twenty-ninth. Chief executive Marcio Souza calls the F-D-A's new rare disease framework "fantastic," but says he isn't yet sure there's enough alignment to make it a reality.
