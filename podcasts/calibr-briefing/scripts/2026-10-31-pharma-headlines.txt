Good morning, Travis. It's Saturday, October thirty-first, 2026. Yesterday's tape was earnings-driven, and the single most consequential disclosure was buried in a slide deck rather than a press release: Gilead has a next-generation CAR-T program on partial clinical hold. Today's briefing covers that, a regulatory delay at Agios, AstraZeneca's restructuring, Sanofi on European pricing, a new rare disease company spun out of a Jazz acquisition, and a European filing accepted in desmoid tumors. Here's what you need to know.

First, and this is the spotlight, so I'll keep it short here. On Gilead's third-quarter call, the company disclosed that two trials, PALISADES-1 and PALISADES-2, have been placed on partial clinical hold. Both test Kite seven-fifty-three, a next-generation CAR-T cell therapy, in second-line and third-line-plus relapsed or refractory diffuse large B-cell lymphoma. The reason is grade three and grade four ICANS, the neurotoxicity syndrome that CAR-T cells cause, in a small number of patients. Gilead says it noticed a safety difference between the Phase 1 and the Phase 2 portions, paused voluntarily, and then aligned with the agency on a partial hold. Kite's head of cell therapy said there were no deaths and no grade five events. More on why this one matters in the spotlight.

Second, Agios. The company announced that its F-D-A decision date for mitapivat in sickle cell disease has moved from November first to February first, 2027. On Tuesday's briefing I flagged that decision as due this Sunday, so this is the shoe dropping. The mechanics matter more than the delay. On October ninth the agency sent an information request asking whether mitapivat needs a Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy in sickle cell. Agios responded with updated safety data, which it says shows no new cases of hepatocellular injury in sickle cell patients. The agency classified that response as a major amendment, which triggers an automatic three-month extension. So the delay is a procedural consequence of Agios answering a question, not a new safety finding. Mitapivat is a pyruvate kinase activator, already on the market as Pyrukynd. Agios volunteering hepatocellular injury data in its response tells you what the agency is circling. The stock fell more than fifteen percent. The company says it remains launch-ready on its existing hematology infrastructure.

Third, AstraZeneca. Third-quarter revenue was sixteen point two billion dollars, up seven percent, and full-year guidance was reaffirmed. The news was the restructuring. The company launched a program it calls Reshaping for Sustainable Growth: up to two billion dollars in one-time restructuring costs to deliver one point five billion in annualized pre-tax run-rate benefits, essentially complete by the end of 2029, with the savings reinvested principally in the pipeline. Note the shape of that. AstraZeneca is not taking the money out, it is moving the money from everywhere else into R and D, against what it describes as more than a hundred eighty pipeline projects. Endpoints reports that the reorganization could also mean trimming trials and shifting some clinical work to emerging markets. This follows GSK's savings program, which is the pattern worth watching: two large-cap European pharmas restructuring the commercial and support base to protect development spend. Two Phase 3 discontinuations also showed up in the same document. AWAKE, testing Ultomiris in kidney transplant recipients at risk of delayed graft function, was stopped on an independent monitoring committee recommendation for futility, with no new safety concerns. And eVOLVE-Lung02, testing the PD-1-slash-CTLA-4 bispecific volrustomig in first-line non-small-cell lung cancer with low PD-L1, was discontinued in August for futility. The other volrustomig Phase 3s continue.

Fourth, Sanofi, on pricing. Endpoints reports that Sanofi's chief financial officer says the company intends to keep launching new medicines across Europe under the Trump administration's most-favored-nation framework, and may seek higher European prices rather than withhold launches. That is the opposite of the threat several of Sanofi's peers have made this year, which was to delay or skip European launches rather than let a low European price travel back to the United States. If the industry converges on Sanofi's approach, the arbitrage closes from the European side rather than the American one, and the burden lands on European payers. Separately, management said it has no plans for a major overhaul as the Dupixent patent cliff approaches, which is a notable thing to have to say out loud.

Fifth, a new company. Setara Biosciences launched with a ninety million dollar Series A from Deerfield Management, Canaan, and Jazz Pharmaceuticals. The provenance is the interesting part. Jazz acquired Actio Biosciences in August for eight hundred twenty million dollars cash up front and up to one point three billion in total, and what Jazz actually wanted was a single molecule, ABS-twelve-thirty, a blocker of a malfunctioning potassium channel for KCNT1-related epilepsy. At signing, Jazz agreed to spin the rest of the pipeline back out and keep a minority stake, and on Thursday that pipeline became Setara. David Goldstein, the human geneticist who founded Actio, is chief executive. The lead programs are ion channel drugs, including one in Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease. The structural point for your purposes is that the disposition of the non-lead assets was negotiated into the deal documents at signing rather than left to the acquirer's discretion afterward, and the seller's team got the assets back with the buyer on the cap table.

Sixth, Immunome. The European Medicines Agency validated the marketing authorization application for varegacestat in adults with desmoid tumors, which starts the centralized review clock. Varegacestat is an oral, once-daily gamma secretase inhibitor. The filing rests on the Phase 3 RINGSIDE trial, a hundred fifty-six patients randomized against placebo, which cut the risk of progression or death by eighty-four percent, a hazard ratio of zero point one six. Objective response rate was fifty-six percent versus nine percent on placebo by blinded central review, and median best change in tumor volume was minus eighty-three percent versus plus eleven percent on placebo. Diarrhea hit eighty-two percent of treated patients, but ninety-five percent of all adverse events were grade one or two. The U.S. decision date is April twenty-eighth, 2027. Immunome is run by Clay Siegall, formerly of Seagen, and the company is otherwise an antibody-drug conjugate shop, so varegacestat is the near-term commercial engine for a pipeline that is mostly early A-D-Cs.
