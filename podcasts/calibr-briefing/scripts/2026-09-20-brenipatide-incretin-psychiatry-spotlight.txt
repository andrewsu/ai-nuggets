Today's spotlight is on Eli Lilly's brenipatide, and on the specific scientific objection Lilly spent Friday at Psych Congress trying to close.

Start with the money, because it explains the urgency. Mounjaro and Zepbound — the two brands of tirzepatide — did eighteen point six billion and nine point one billion dollars respectively in the first half of twenty twenty-six. Twenty-seven point six billion combined, against total Lilly revenue of forty-two point eight billion. Roughly sixty-five percent of the company is one molecule hitting two receptors. Every large pharma in that position faces the same question, which is what the second act looks like when the first act commoditizes. Lilly's answer, laid out across three posters in New Orleans on Friday, is that the incretin receptors are not fundamentally metabolic targets. They are central nervous system targets that happen to also regulate appetite.

Brenipatide, internally L-Y three five three seven oh three one, is a dual agonist of the glucagon-like peptide-one and glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide receptors — the same pair as tirzepatide. What is different is what it was engineered toward: central nervous system and inflammatory pathways, including the receptors implicated in reward and addiction circuitry, rather than peripheral metabolic ones. Robert Nicholson, who runs Lilly's global neuroscience medical affairs for psychiatry and substance use, told the trade press that the longer half-life is a large part of why the company sees a distinct profile against currently available incretins.

Now the Phase one poster, because the design is more revealing than the results. The trial enrolled more than two hundred participants, and it was split into three parts. Part A was the obvious one — body mass index twenty-seven to forty-five, ninety-six on drug, twelve on placebo. Part B was body mass index twenty-two to twenty-six-point-nine, sixty-four on drug, eight on placebo. Part C was that same normal-to-overweight band in Japanese and Chinese participants, twenty-four on drug and eight on placebo. Lilly deliberately built two thirds of the study's arms around people who are not obese. Nothing in a conventional incretin development program requires that. It only makes sense if the indication you are designing for has nothing to do with weight.

The pharmacokinetics came out where they needed to: mean half-life between nine point oh eight and twelve point five days, the longer end at the top four-and-a-half-milligram dose. Nicholson's framing is that this gives durability of exposure beyond the weekly dosing interval — so weekly maintenance dosing has real headroom rather than sitting right at the edge. Safety was clean on the headline measures. No deaths, no serious adverse events, and only four participants, two point two percent, discontinued for a treatment-emergent adverse event. Gastrointestinal events ran twenty-five percent in both the brenipatide and placebo arms — identical, which is not what you usually see with this class, and Nicholson attributes it to a narrower peak-to-trough ratio than existing incretins. One signal does stand out and deserves tracking: dysesthesia-related events in fifteen point two percent of treated participants and zero on placebo. In a chronic psychiatric population, an abnormal-sensation signal at that rate is the kind of thing that shows up later as a discontinuation driver, and it went unremarked in the coverage.

Here is why the normal-weight arms matter, and it is the whole analytical point of this episode. Every positive randomized result for an incretin in addiction so far has been generated in patients with obesity. The strongest of them is the Lancet trial published this spring — one hundred eight treatment-seeking patients with moderate-to-severe alcohol use disorder and comorbid obesity, twenty-six weeks of once-weekly semaglutide at two point four milligrams on top of standard cognitive behavioral therapy. Heavy drinking days in the prior thirty fell by about twelve against about eight on placebo, from a baseline of seventeen. Number needed to treat came out at four point three, against seven or worse for the three drugs currently approved for alcohol use disorder. That is a real result. But when the Science Media Centre canvassed experts, the caveat was unanimous and specific. Marie Spreckley at Cambridge said flatly that including only individuals with obesity limits generalizability. Matt Field at Sheffield went further, pointing out that a significant minority of patients with addictions are underweight, and asking whether the benefit extends to them at all.

That is the objection. If the anti-craving effect of an incretin is downstream of weight loss and metabolic change, then it is an obesity drug with a side benefit, the addressable population is the overlap of two conditions, and the mechanism is not what the field has been claiming. If the effect is a direct central action on reward circuitry, it is a new class of psychiatric medicine and the population is everyone. Lilly's Phase one poster is the direct answer to that question — it establishes that you can dose this molecule safely across the whole body-mass-index range, including normal weight, which is the precondition for running the trial that settles the argument.

And they are running it. Three Phase three trials, all registered and recruiting. RENEW-A-L-C-one enrolls eleven hundred patients with moderate-to-severe alcohol use disorder over fifty-six weeks; it started last October and reads out around April twenty twenty-eight. RENEW-A-L-C-two enrolls another eleven hundred in hazardous drinking. RENEW-M-D-D-one enrolls a thousand patients across fourteen countries; it began in February and completes in February twenty twenty-eight. No Phase two in either indication. Nicholson's justification for going straight to Phase three was the size of the populations and the depth of the unmet need — roughly twenty-one million American adults with a major depressive episode annually, and twenty-eight million with an alcohol use disorder, against three approved drugs, all generic, all built around abstinence.

Three design details are worth holding onto. First, the registered inclusion criteria for the alcohol trials contain no body mass index floor. They exclude other substance use disorders, recent suicidality, and — importantly — advanced liver fibrosis, cirrhosis and acute alcohol-associated hepatitis. So the sickest alcohol-use-disorder patients, the ones who drive the cost argument, are not in the trial. That will shape the label and the payer conversation.

Second, the depression trial is a relapse-prevention design, not an acute antidepressant trial. Patients are already stable on standard-of-care medication, brenipatide goes on top, and the primary endpoint is time to relapse. That is a much easier design to power than head-to-head antidepressant efficacy, and a correspondingly narrower claim. Note also one of the secondary endpoints: percent change in body weight specifically in patients with body mass index over twenty-five who are on an atypical antipsychotic. Antipsychotic-induced weight gain is a second commercial door, and Lilly has propped it open.

Third, the registered primary endpoint for the alcohol trials is change in drinking patterns by the Timeline Followback method — which is a measurement instrument, not a specified endpoint. Watch that. Altimmune, when it reported positive Phase two data in July, named its two key secondaries precisely: zero heavy drinking days, and a two-level reduction in World Health Organization risk drinking levels. Those are the two endpoints the F-D-A has recognized as registrational. Lilly has not said in the registry which derived endpoint it will use, and in a field where the regulator has been explicit about what it will accept, that ambiguity is worth a question.

The competitive picture is more crowded than Lilly's scale suggests. Altimmune's RECLAIM Phase two hit its heavy-drinking-days primary with pemvidutide, a G-L-P-one and glucagon dual agonist, in late July, and hit both registrational secondaries — so a small-cap got to positive randomized alcohol data with a dual agonist before Lilly. Baseline Therapeutics launched in January purely to run an incretin in alcohol use disorder and plans two Phase threes of its own. Novo Nordisk, which owns the molecule with the best published evidence, has been conspicuously quiet; its Phase two in alcohol-related liver damage completed earlier this year with results unreleased, and as of last year the company said it had no plans to study semaglutide in further conditions. If Lilly is right about the mechanism, that will look like one of the more expensive silences in the industry.

Lorenzo Leggio at the National Institutes of Health, who is probably the most-cited voice in this field, has been consistent about where things stand: the observational data are large and suggestive, the small randomized trials are encouraging, the large randomized trials are the part that is still missing, and nothing worrying has turned up on safety in these patients so far. He describes himself as very optimistic, and adds that the investment itself is a win for a set of conditions that have been neglected for decades.

Here is my read. Brenipatide is in thirteen Phase two and Phase three studies simultaneously — alcohol, tobacco, opioids, depression, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, plus asthma, irritable bowel syndrome and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease on the immunology side. No pharmaceutical company runs a molecule that broadly on the strength of a Phase one unless it believes it has a platform mechanism rather than a drug. The thing to watch is not the depression trial, which is designed conservatively enough to be likely to succeed and narrow enough to matter less. It is RENEW-A-L-C-one, because it is the first adequately powered trial of an incretin in alcohol use disorder that is not restricted to patients with obesity. April twenty twenty-eight is when the field finds out whether it has been looking at a central mechanism or a metabolic epiphenomenon — and whether the largest franchise in pharmaceuticals has a second act in the brain.
