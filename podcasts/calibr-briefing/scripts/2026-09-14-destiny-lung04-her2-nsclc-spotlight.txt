Travis, today's spotlight is DESTINY-Lung-oh-four, and I want to spend the time on it because it is the rare readout where the press release headline and the actual dataset point in opposite directions, and where the competitive context — set five days ago by an F-D-A action nobody connected to this trial — decides what the result is worth.

Start with what is genuinely new. HER-two-mutant non-small cell lung cancer is a small, distinct population: activating mutations in the HER-two tyrosine kinase domain, mostly exon twenty insertions with a minority in exon nineteen, found in two to four percent of non-squamous disease. It concentrates in younger patients, women, and never-smokers, and it carries an unusually high rate of brain metastasis. Until very recently there was no targeted first-line option at all. These patients got platinum-pemetrexed plus pembrolizumab like everyone else, and they did poorly on it, because an oncogene-driven, low-mutational-burden tumor in a never-smoker is close to the worst-case setting for a checkpoint inhibitor. Progression inside a year was the norm.

DESTINY-Lung-oh-four randomized four hundred fifty-four of those patients one to one, across Asia, Europe and North America, stratified by smoking history and brain metastases, to Enhertu at five point four milligrams per kilogram or to that chemo-immunotherapy standard. Enhertu is trastuzumab deruxtecan, the antibody-drug conjugate Daiichi Sankyo developed and partnered with AstraZeneca: a trastuzumab backbone carrying a topoisomerase-one inhibitor payload at a drug-to-antibody ratio of about eight, with a cleavable linker and a membrane-permeable payload that produces a bystander effect in neighboring cells. It is already approved in more than eighty countries for HER-two-mutant lung cancer after prior therapy, on DESTINY-Lung-oh-two and oh-five, and it is second-line standard of care. This trial was the move to first line.

It worked on its primary endpoint and it was not close. Median progression-free survival by blinded independent review was fourteen point three months against eight point three, hazard ratio zero point six three, confidence interval zero point five zero to zero point seven nine, p less than zero point zero zero zero one. Response rate seventy percent against forty-four point five. Median duration of response thirteen point four months against nine point seven. Time to second progression, which is the endpoint that tests whether you have merely borrowed benefit from the next line, was twenty-two point seven months against seventeen point three — also favoring Enhertu. Julia Rotow at Dana-Farber, the lead investigator, has the appropriate quote: seventy percent responding, fourteen point three months of progression-free survival, potential to become an important new first-line option. Susan Galbraith at AstraZeneca called it the first Phase three to demonstrate superior progression-free survival versus the global first-line standard in this population, which is true.

Now the part the headline does not carry. Interim overall survival, at forty-six point nine percent maturity, is twenty-nine point three months on Enhertu against thirty-three point one months on chemo-immunotherapy. Hazard ratio one point one five, confidence interval zero point eight eight to one point five two. No formal hypothesis testing, because the trial is not powered to test overall survival at this point and the boundary has not been reached. But that is a point estimate pointing the wrong way, with almost half the events in, in a trial whose progression-free survival hazard ratio was zero point six three. You very rarely see a gap of that size between a progression endpoint and a survival estimate in an oncogene-driven population.

AstraZeneca and Daiichi give an explanation, and it is a real one. Subsequent therapy was badly imbalanced: forty-eight percent of patients in the control arm went on to receive a HER-two-directed therapy after progression, against twenty-three point three percent of patients in the Enhertu arm — because the Enhertu arm had already had the HER-two drug. Meanwhile only twenty-three point eight percent of the Enhertu arm received subsequent immunotherapy plus chemotherapy. So the control arm got Enhertu second-line, and the Enhertu arm largely did not get the chemo-immunotherapy it skipped. That is a legitimate confound and it is exactly what you would predict in a crossover-permissive design.

But sit with what that explanation actually concedes. It says: giving Enhertu first and chemo-immunotherapy second produces no survival advantage over giving chemo-immunotherapy first and Enhertu second — and may, on the current estimate, be slightly worse. Which is the status quo. The clinical value of moving an agent earlier in a sequence is precisely that the sequence beats the alternative sequence on survival. If it does not, what you have bought is a better first progression-free interval, a higher response rate, and a longer time on your most toxic drug. That is not nothing — response rate and time to progression matter to patients, especially symptomatic ones with brain disease — but it is a quality-of-life-and-control argument, not a survival argument, and it has to be priced against the toxicity.

Which brings me to the second number. Interstitial lung disease and pneumonitis, adjudicated, occurred in twenty point eight percent of the Enhertu arm. Grade one in three point one percent, grade two in thirteen point three, grade three in two point two, grade four in zero point four, and grade five — fatal — in one point eight percent. Four patients died of drug-induced lung injury. Enhertu carries a boxed warning for this and the rate in HER-two-mutant lung cancer has always run higher than in breast cancer; DESTINY-Lung-oh-two at this dose was in the low teens. Twenty point eight percent is the highest figure I have seen attached to the five point four milligram dose in a randomized setting. Overall grade three or higher treatment-related adverse events were actually comparable between arms, thirty-four point one percent versus thirty-three point six — the ADC is not broadly more toxic. The toxicity is specific, it is pulmonary, and it is occurring in a lung cancer population, roughly a fifth of whom will get some grade of it and about one in fifty-five of whom will die of it.

Here is where the competitive context decides the answer. On February twenty-sixth this year the F-D-A granted accelerated approval to Boehringer Ingelheim's zongertinib, sold as Hernexeos, for treatment-naive HER-two-mutant non-squamous disease, on Beamion LUNG-one: a seventy-six percent response rate, sixty-four percent of responders out past six months, forty-four percent past twelve. Then on September ninth — five days ago, and we covered it on Thursday — the agency granted accelerated approval to Bayer's sevabertinib, sold as Hyrnuo, in the same first-line setting, on sixty-nine treatment-naive patients in SOHO-oh-one: seventy-five percent response rate, seventy-three percent of responders past six months, thirty-eight percent past twelve. Both are oral, once or twice daily, small-molecule HER-two inhibitors with selectivity for mutant over wild-type E-G-F-R, which is what spares them the classic rash-and-diarrhea profile. Neither carries anything like a twenty percent pneumonitis rate.

So the first-line HER-two-mutant market that Enhertu is entering already has two approved oral targeted therapies in it. The trade is now explicit. Enhertu brings randomized Phase three evidence, a seventy percent response rate, fourteen point three months of progression-free survival, and a control arm — chemo-immunotherapy — that no one thinks is the right comparator anymore, because it was the standard when this trial enrolled and it is not the standard now. The T-K-Is bring single-arm accelerated approvals, response rates in the mid-seventies that are indistinguishable from Enhertu's, no randomized evidence at all, oral administration, and a tolerability profile that lets a patient stay on drug. The honest framing is that DESTINY-Lung-oh-four won a randomized trial against a comparator that has already been displaced, and now has to compete against two drugs it never tested itself against.

What I would watch. The first thing is SOHO-oh-two, Bayer's Phase three confirmatory trial of sevabertinib against standard of care in treatment-naive patients — that is the trial that converts the accelerated approvals into real evidence, and it is the one that will eventually be read alongside DESTINY-Lung-oh-four. The second is the final overall survival analysis here. If that hazard ratio of one point one five drifts toward one as events accumulate, the subsequent-therapy explanation holds and Enhertu is a legitimate first-line option for the patient who needs a response now — high tumor burden, symptomatic, brain metastases, where a seventy percent response rate and intracranial activity are worth the pulmonary risk. If it stays above one, this becomes a case study, and a consequential one, about first-line positioning of ADCs generally.

Because that is the read-through, and it is why this matters beyond a two-to-four-percent population. The industry's dominant oncology strategy right now is to take an antibody-drug conjugate that works in later lines and move it earlier, on the theory that more benefit accrues where there is more tumor left to kill and fewer resistance mechanisms in place. DESTINY-Lung-oh-four is now the second result this month arguing that the theory is harder to execute than it looks. The first was EVOKE-oh-three, presented in Seoul this weekend, where Gilead's T-ROP-two ADC plus pembrolizumab added nothing at all to pembrolizumab alone in first-line P-D-L-one-high disease and was discontinued. The mechanism of failure is different in each case, but the shape is the same: in the first line you are no longer competing against exhausted patients and weak options. You are competing against the best available therapy in a fitter population, your cumulative toxicity has more time to express itself, and any survival advantage you might have had gets handed back the moment your drug becomes available as second-line therapy to the control arm. An ADC that is a clear winner in the third line can be a coin flip in the first. Daiichi and AstraZeneca have a dozen programs riding on the earlier-is-better thesis, and this is the first one where the survival curve has publicly declined to cooperate.
