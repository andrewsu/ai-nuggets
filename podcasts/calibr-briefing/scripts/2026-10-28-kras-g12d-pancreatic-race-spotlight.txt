Welcome to the spotlight, Travis. Today it's the KRAS G-twelve-D race in pancreatic cancer, which got a second serious entrant at ESMO on Monday. Incyte presented Phase 1 data for its oral G-twelve-D inhibitor, zecdarasib, and the numbers are in the same neighborhood as Revolution Medicines' zoldonrasib. For two years this has looked like Revolution Medicines' field. It no longer does. Let me take you through what was shown, what the comparison actually supports, and where the real differentiation is likely to come from, because it probably isn't response rate.

Start with why G-twelve-D matters more than G-twelve-C ever did in this disease. KRAS is mutated in roughly ninety percent of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma. G-twelve-D alone accounts for about forty percent of cases, and about fifteen percent of colorectal cancer. G-twelve-C, the variant that sotorasib and adagrasib drug, is only a few percent of pancreatic cancer. So the chemistry that made G-twelve-C tractable — a covalent warhead reacting with the cysteine that the mutation installs — was solving a small slice of the problem. G-twelve-D replaces glycine with aspartate. There's no cysteine to grab, the aspartate side chain is charged, and the surface is shallow. Getting selective, orally bioavailable inhibition of G-twelve-D over wild-type KRAS was the hard medicinal chemistry problem in this field, and the fact that two companies have now solved it well enough to run Phase 3 trials is the larger story here.

Both solved it with the same conceptual move: a tri-complex or chaperone-assisted approach, using cyclophilin A as a presenting protein so the drug and the chaperone together make a composite surface that grips the mutant. Revolution Medicines pioneered that with zoldonrasib, which is a covalent inhibitor of the active, GTP-bound, or ON state. Incyte describes zecdarasib as an ON/OFF inhibitor, meaning it engages the mutant in both nucleotide states. On paper that should give you more complete pathway suppression. Whether it does in patients is not something either dataset can tell you yet.

Now the data. Incyte's Phase 1, data cutoff July 6th, fifty-four patients with metastatic G-twelve-D-mutant pancreatic cancer, drug at twelve hundred milligrams once daily, on top of one of the two standard chemotherapy backbones. In the twenty-seven patients on modified FOLFIRINOX, the release reports objective response rates of eighty-nine and eighty-one percent — two numbers, one confirmed and one counting responses still awaiting confirmation, and the release doesn't make clear which is which, so treat the lower one as the safer figure. Disease control was one hundred percent, and ninety percent of patients were progression-free at six months. In the twenty-six patients on gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel, the response rates were sixty-nine and fifty-eight percent, disease control ninety-six percent, and six-month progression-free survival eighty percent.

Revolution Medicines showed its first-line combination data at ESMO GI in July: response rate eighty-two percent with modified FOLFIRINOX, sixty-one percent with gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel. RBC's read is that Incyte "appears at least competitive on response, particularly with mFOLFIRINOX." BMO called Incyte a "firm contender" and put one point four billion dollars on the pancreatic opportunity.

Here's where I'd push back on the frame. These are cross-trial comparisons of single-arm cohorts of twenty-six and twenty-seven patients, run at different times, with different enrollment criteria and different investigators choosing which patients got which chemotherapy backbone. The gap between eighty-nine and eighty-two percent is four or five patients. In cohorts this size, that is noise. What the comparison does establish — and this is the genuinely useful conclusion — is that neither drug is obviously inferior, which means the differentiation will be settled somewhere other than response rate.

So where? Three places, in rough order of how much they'll matter.

The first is duration, and the first real signal is the six-month progression-free number. Ninety percent progression-free at six months on the FOLFIRINOX combination is a strong figure for first-line metastatic pancreatic cancer, where historical median progression-free survival on chemotherapy alone runs around six to seven months. But a six-month landmark in a Phase 1 cohort is a snapshot with no comparator arm and favorable selection baked in. Incyte's Phase 3, DAWN three-oh-three, is running now in first-line metastatic disease against investigator's choice of both chemotherapy backbones, with topline expected in twenty twenty-eight. Revolution Medicines' RASolute three-oh-five is the equivalent trial. Those two readouts, not these cohorts, are the comparison.

The second is tolerability, and this is already visible. Incyte's gastrointestinal toxicity is substantial — diarrhea in sixty-eight percent, nausea sixty-four, vomiting fifty-seven, neutropenia fifty-seven on the FOLFIRINOX arm. Incyte reports no treatment-related discontinuations of its drug and says the events were mostly grade one or two and improved on treatment, which is the right thing to report and a reassuring one. But stacking a drug with that GI profile on FOLFIRINOX, already one of the harsher regimens in oncology, in a disease where patients are frequently cachectic, is a real-world delivery problem that doesn't show up in a response rate. Pablo Cagnoni, who runs research and development at Incyte, noted at ESMO that its drug has a longer duration of exposure than zoldonrasib — which means more time for benefit and more time for toxicity. Analysts flagged that as a small but potentially important differentiator, and I'd agree it cuts both ways.

The third, and the one I'd actually watch, is where G-twelve-D lands in the treatment paradigm — which RBC also identified as the crux. Revolution Medicines has a structural advantage that has nothing to do with zoldonrasib. Its multi-selective RAS inhibitor daraxonrasib, branded Rasonque, was approved in August for previously treated metastatic pancreatic cancer on the strength of RASolute three-oh-two — median overall survival thirteen point two months against six point seven on chemotherapy. That approval came more than six months early through the FDA's national priority voucher pilot. So Revolution Medicines is already in the clinic, with a commercial field force, with prescribers who have used a RAS inhibitor in this disease. And it is running RASolute three-oh-nine, zoldonrasib plus daraxonrasib. The thesis there is that you hit the G-twelve-D allele hard and cover the RAS pathway broadly at the same time, which is a coherent answer to the resistance that single-allele inhibitors have shown in every other RAS setting.

Incyte's answer to that is combinations with other people's drugs. It announced two new Phase 1b collaborations alongside this data: with Bristol Myers Squibb on navlimetostat, an MTA-cooperative PRMT-five inhibitor, and with Synnovation on a PARP-one inhibitor. Both are reasonable — PRMT-five in particular exploits MTAP deletion, which co-occurs with KRAS mutation in pancreatic cancer at meaningful frequency. But they're earlier, and they're partnered rather than owned.

Two other things from the same release worth holding. Incyte's colorectal data: thirty-six percent response rate and one hundred percent disease control with cetuximab in twenty-two heavily pretreated patients, median progression-free survival seven point two months, with a Phase 3 planned for the first half of twenty twenty-seven. G-twelve-D colorectal is a smaller population but a less crowded one, and if the pancreatic Phase 3s come in close, the colorectal franchise could be the thing that separates these two companies commercially. And Incyte's other asset, a T-G-F-beta-receptor-two-by-P-D-one bispecific from the Merus platform now inside Genmab, posted a seventy-one percent response rate with FOLFOX and bevacizumab in first-line microsatellite-stable colorectal cancer. Microsatellite-stable colorectal has been immunologically cold and resistant to checkpoint blockade for a decade. That number, in ninety-four patients across cohorts, deserves its own session.

The read-through for you. Three things. First, the hardest part of the G-twelve-D problem — selective oral drugging of a shallow, charged, non-cysteine surface via a chaperone-presented composite interface — is now solved twice, independently. That's a validated template, and the same logic applies to any target where the mutant surface is undruggable alone but becomes a pocket when a presenting protein is recruited. Second, a first-to-market RAS inhibitor with an approval, a commercial footprint, and an in-house combination partner is a more durable position than a better response rate in a Phase 1 cohort — relevant to how any Foresite oncology asset should think about sequencing a franchise rather than optimizing a single molecule. And third, for Calibr, the tolerability point generalizes: in pancreatic cancer the limiting factor is increasingly what the patient can absorb on top of chemotherapy, not what the molecule does to the target. Any program whose value proposition is being added to a harsh backbone needs a therapeutic-index story from the start, not after the Phase 1.
