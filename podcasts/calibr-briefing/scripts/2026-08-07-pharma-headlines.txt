Good morning, Travis. It's Friday, August seventh, twenty twenty-six, and here's your biotech and pharma briefing. Earnings season is winding down, but the regulatory calendar delivered the story of the week, and the financing window is still wide open. Five things.

First, and it's today's spotlight: Replimune won accelerated approval for its oncolytic virus. We have been tracking this one for two weeks, and yesterday it landed. The FDA approved Tudriqev — vusolimogene oderparepvec — in combination with Bristol Myers Squibb's nivolumab, the anti-P-D-one antibody sold as Opdivo, for advanced melanoma that has progressed on prior P-D-one therapy. This is a genuine reversal: the drug had been rejected twice, and the agency's own reviewers called the pivotal data hard to interpret, before the advisory committee voted ten to three in favor on July thirtieth. It is only the second oncolytic virus ever approved in the United States, and the company set the list price at four hundred fifty thousand dollars per course. I'll go deep in the spotlight on why this one matters well beyond melanoma.

Second, another first-in-class approval, and this one opens a new field in sleep. The FDA approved Takeda's oveporexton, branded Orzeyful, for narcolepsy type one. It's the first orexin receptor two agonist ever cleared — meaning it treats the actual cause of the disease, the loss of orexin signaling that regulates wakefulness, rather than just papering over the daytime sleepiness and cataplexy. That validates an entire emerging drug class, and it puts a spotlight on the companies chasing the same orexin-agonist mechanism, including Alkermes and Centessa. Launch waits on D-E-A scheduling.

Third, a deal in eye disease. Tarsus Pharmaceuticals — the company behind the eyelid-mite drug Xdemvy — agreed to acquire privately held Alkeus for up to eight hundred million dollars, about four hundred fifty million up front and up to three hundred fifty million in milestones. The prize is gildeuretinol, an oral, once-daily small molecule in phase three for Stargardt disease, an inherited retinal disorder with no approved treatment. It's a clean strategic pivot: Tarsus is using its eyelid-disease cash flow to buy its way from the front of the eye to the back of it. The phase three reads out in twenty twenty-nine, so this is a patient bet on the retina franchise, not a near-term catalyst.

Fourth, the biotech I-P-O window is not just open, it's hot. Braveheart Bio priced an upsized offering at eighteen dollars a share, raising about three hundred eighty-three million, and the stock popped roughly two-thirds on its debut under the ticker B-R-V-E. Braveheart's lead drug is an oral cardiac myosin inhibitor licensed from China's Hengrui, aimed at hypertrophic cardiomyopathy — squarely a competitor to Bristol Myers' Camzyos, with a phase three in obstructive disease planned this year. That's the third big upsized biotech I-P-O in a week, after Attovia and Apnimed. For your portfolio, the read is that public appetite for new issues is real again — and notably, that a Chinese-licensed asset is now a credible way to build a U-S public company.

Fifth, and this is in your A-I lane: Recursion reported the quarter, and the headline wasn't the financials — it was Genentech. Under their Roche and Genentech collaboration, Genentech exercised its first validated-target option, moving an A-I-discovered neuroscience target into an early discovery program. That's an outside pharma partner putting money behind a target that Recursion's platform found and validated — the kind of external validation the whole computational-discovery sector has been waiting for. Recursion also detailed three agentic A-I systems now running in-house, claiming its design agent cut a four-hour analysis to thirty minutes. Revenue was thin and the loss was wide, but the Genentech option is the signal that matters.
