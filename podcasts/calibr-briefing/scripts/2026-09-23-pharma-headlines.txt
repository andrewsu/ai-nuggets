Good morning Travis. Here are your biotech and pharma headlines for Wednesday, September twenty-third, twenty twenty-six. Tuesday was a heavy readout day rather than a deal day. Five Phase three trials reported out, two more readouts landed in kidney disease and two in obesity and diabetes, and the biggest of them produced the strangest market reaction of the week — a company met every endpoint in two pivotal trials and lost thirteen percent of its value before lunch. That is today's spotlight. Around it, Ionis had the best twenty-four hours it has had all year, the incretin field got two more datasets, and there were two artificial intelligence items worth watching. Ten items, then the week ahead.

First, and today's spotlight. Celldex reported topline results from EMBARQ-CSU-one and EMBARQ-CSU-two, two Phase three trials of barzolvolimab in chronic spontaneous urticaria — chronic hives — in patients who stay symptomatic on antihistamines. Barzolvolimab is a humanized antibody against the KIT receptor, which mast cells need to survive; the drug does not block a mediator, it removes the cell that makes the mediators. Both trials hit the primary endpoint and every key secondary endpoint at both doses. Weekly urticaria activity score fell about twenty points from a baseline around thirty, against roughly eleven on placebo. Complete response — no itch, no hives at all — was reached by forty-two to forty-six percent of treated patients at twelve weeks against nine to thirteen percent on placebo, and it deepened to between forty-five and fifty-four percent at twenty-four weeks. It worked in patients refractory to omalizumab, and among patients with angioedema at baseline, sixty-two to seventy-four percent had it fully resolve against about thirty-four percent on placebo. Cantor Fitzgerald's Kristen Kluska wrote that she would pound the table that this is the best Phase three dataset yet seen across all pivotal studies in the disease, and models four point eight billion dollars in peak sales. The stock fell thirteen percent anyway, and the reason is in the safety table — which was in the slide deck but not in the press release. We will take that apart in the spotlight.

Second, Ionis. On Tuesday morning Otsuka and Ionis reported that the Phase three FUSION trial of ulefnersen hit its primary endpoint in FUS-ALS — the form of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis caused by mutations in the fused-in-sarcoma gene. The primary endpoint was a joint rank analysis combining time to death or permanent ventilation, time to rescue intervention, and change in the ALS functional rating scale out to day five hundred five. Secondaries favored the drug too, including a statistically significant reduction in serum neurofilament light chain, the axonal damage biomarker. This is the first placebo-controlled trial in FUS-ALS ever to succeed, and only the second time an antisense oligonucleotide has changed the course of a genetic ALS, after tofersen in the SOD-one form. FUS-ALS is rare but it is the most common cause of juvenile ALS and it moves fast in young people. The ALS Association put out a statement the same day worth noting for how the program started: the drug was originally called jacifusen, after Jaci Hermstad, a young woman with the disease whose family pushed Neil Shneider's group at Columbia to make it. When the early clinical work ran out of money, the ALS Association and Project ALS funded it. One patient in that expanded access program regained the ability to walk and breathe unassisted. Otsuka and Ionis are going to the F-D-A to discuss expedited pathways.

Third, and this is overnight news out of Basel. Roche announced positive prespecified interim results from the Phase three IMAgINATION study of sefaxersen in I-g-A nephropathy. Sefaxersen is a liver-directed antisense oligonucleotide that suppresses production of complement factor B — which means it shuts down the alternative complement pathway at the source rather than intercepting it downstream. Four hundred fifty-nine patients randomized one to one, once-monthly subcutaneous dosing designed for self-administration, and the primary endpoint of proteinuria reduction at thirty-seven weeks was met with what Roche calls best-in-class potential. No effect size disclosed — that is coming at a medical meeting. The study stays blinded to the real endpoint, which is estimated glomerular filtration rate at week one hundred five. Here is the thread: Roche licensed sefaxersen from Ionis. So Ionis has now had a first-in-disease F-D-A approval this month with Zanvastro in Alexander disease, a partner's Phase three win in I-g-A nephropathy, and its own Phase three win in FUS-ALS, all inside three weeks. That is the antisense platform delivering across three completely unrelated organ systems, which is the argument the company has been making for a decade.

Fourth, staying in the kidney. Vertex reported the Phase two-three AMPLIFED study of inaxaplin, its A-P-O-L-one inhibitor, in A-P-O-L-one-mediated kidney disease. In the first cohort, twenty-three patients with modest proteinuria, the forty-five milligram dose on top of standard of care cut urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio by forty-two point seven percent at week thirteen, with a forty-four point seven percent drop in protein-to-creatinine ratio. B-M-O Capital Markets called it best-in-class potential. The caveat everyone flagged is the size — forty-one patients total across both cohorts. The strategic point is that Vertex is building a renal franchise on two fronts at once: this, and a pending F-D-A decision on povetacicept in I-g-A nephropathy. Put the Roche and Vertex items next to each other and I-g-A nephropathy is now the most crowded rare-kidney market in the industry, with Novartis's Fabhalta already approved, Vera's Trutakna holding a confirmatory Phase three win, Vertex awaiting a decision, and Roche's antisense coming behind them.

Fifth, two incretin datasets on the same day. Viking Therapeutics reported the maintenance portion of its Phase two trial of V-K twenty-seven thirty-five, a dual G-L-P-one and G-I-P agonist. One hundred eighty people with obesity went through twenty-one weeks of weekly induction dosing up to twenty-two and a half milligrams, losing sixteen to nineteen percent of body weight, and were then switched to every-other-week or monthly injections for another twelve weeks. On every-other-week dosing they kept ninety percent of the weight they had lost; on monthly, eighty-five percent. Leerink's Thomas Smith called the data stellar. The stock rose as much as thirty-six percent. The importance is not the depth of weight loss, it is that the field has been assuming chronic weekly dosing forever, and this says the maintenance phase may be a different and much lighter regimen. Separately, Roche put out Phase two data on enicepatide, formerly C-T three eighty-eight, in type two diabetes with overweight or obesity. At the top titrated dose of twenty-four milligrams, hemoglobin A-one-C fell two point six five percentage points from a baseline of eight point one, ninety percent of that cohort got under six point five percent, and sixty-two percent reached outright normoglycemia below five point seven. Weight loss was fifteen point five percent at forty-eight weeks with no visible plateau, and discontinuation was two percent. Those are very strong numbers for an asset Roche bought with Carmot and is generally considered to be running third in.

Sixth, Amgen reported the first of two Phase three trials of dazodalibep in Sjogren's disease, and it hit. Statistically significant and clinically meaningful improvement on the standard disease activity index at forty-eight weeks, with separation as early as week four. Dazodalibep blocks the C-D-forty to C-D-forty-ligand costimulatory axis, and the specific worry with that mechanism historically has been thrombosis; Amgen says there was no imbalance in clots or opportunistic infections. No numbers yet. There is no approved disease-modifying therapy in Sjogren's at all. Leerink's David Risinger said the detail will determine positioning against Novartis's ianalumab; William Blair's Matt Phipps said he had considered the program relatively high risk and was encouraged, but pointed out that the two Phase three trials enroll distinct populations and Amgen needs both. The second, in high-symptom-burden patients, reads out in the fourth quarter. The asset came to Amgen through Horizon, which got it from Viela Bio, which was spun out of AstraZeneca — it has been passed along three times before finally working.

Seventh, and this one is a direct sequel to yesterday's spotlight. One day after Telix announced it was buying I-T-M, the company that supplies Novartis with lutetium one-seventy-seven, Novartis licensed a preclinical radioligand therapy from BoomRay Pharmaceuticals of Suzhou for up to nine hundred million dollars — upfront, milestones and royalties, worldwide rights including China. The target, the isotope and the indication were all undisclosed. Novartis is also putting more than six hundred million yuan into radioligand manufacturing capacity in China due to come online by the end of this year. The read is straightforward: Novartis just watched a competitor buy its isotope supplier, and it is buying optionality on both molecules and supply outside that relationship.

Eighth, the artificial intelligence deal of the day, and it is squarely in your wheelhouse. Boehringer Ingelheim signed a multi-year research and option agreement with Envisagenics worth more than one billion dollars in potential payments — upfront, research funding, option fees, milestones and royalties. Envisagenics runs SpliceCore, a machine learning platform that mines alternative R-N-A splicing in tumors to find neoantigen-like surface targets that healthy tissue does not express. What makes this one interesting rather than routine is the modality list: the collaboration will validate targets for antibody-drug conjugates, T-cell engagers and multispecific antibodies. That is a target-discovery deal priced on the assumption that the binder engineering is the solved part and the target is the bottleneck, which is a reasonable read of where the T-cell engager field actually is. Boehringer takes exclusive license options on selected targets at the end of the research program.

Twenty-five biotechs have listed this year, five of them this month, the most since twenty twenty-one. For Iambic, coming twenty-four hours after the Enchant version three release and the AbbVie collaboration, the sequencing is not accidental: model, pharma contract, filing.

Tenth, a regulatory setback worth flagging because of what it says about the agency's mood on surrogate endpoints. Compass Therapeutics — not Compass Pathways, a different company — said the F-D-A has recommended it run an entirely new trial demonstrating an overall survival benefit for tovecimig, its bispecific, in advanced biliary tract cancer before filing. The COMPANION-zero-zero-two trial had hit on progression-free survival, four point seven months versus two point six, but missed on overall survival. Compass says it will file the B-L-A anyway, on the argument that the agency recommended a study rather than requiring one. Leerink notes this is the same situation Summit is in, and that a new trial would cost roughly three years. The stock fell nearly thirty percent to a dollar twenty-nine and is down seventy-five percent this year.
