Today's spotlight is Merck and Moderna's intismeran autogene, and the question I want to answer is a narrow one: why did the first successful Phase 3 trial of an individualized cancer vaccine in history knock twenty-one percent off Moderna's market capitalization in a single session?

Start with what was actually released. Yesterday afternoon, Merck and Moderna put out the full results of INTerpath-001 about forty-eight hours ahead of schedule, because the data had been posted online in error and then pulled. The trial was supposed to debut tomorrow in Madrid as the first late-breaking abstract of the opening Presidential Symposium at ESMO. Instead it came out as a press release on a Thursday afternoon, into a market that had been trading on the expectation for months.

The design. Eleven hundred and thirty-seven patients with stage two-B through stage four cutaneous melanoma, all of it completely resected, none of them having had prior systemic therapy. Randomized two-to-one to either intismeran plus pembrolizumab, or placebo plus pembrolizumab. Intismeran is one milligram every three weeks for up to nine doses; pembrolizumab is the standard four hundred milligrams every six weeks for up to nine cycles. The comparator here matters: this is not vaccine against nothing, it's vaccine on top of the existing adjuvant standard of care, which is itself an effective therapy. Beating adjuvant pembrolizumab is a high bar.

The intervention itself is worth describing precisely, because the mechanism explains the result. For each patient, the companies sequence tumor DNA alongside normal DNA, identify the mutations unique to that person's cancer, computationally rank which of those mutations are likely to generate peptides that their particular set of HLA molecules will present on the cell surface, and then synthesize a single mRNA encoding up to thirty-four of those selected neoantigens. That ranking step is a machine-learning problem, and it is the step on which the entire therapeutic class stands or falls. Every patient receives a different drug. There is no batch, no inventory, no lot release in the conventional sense.

Now the numbers. In the intention-to-treat population at a median follow-up of twenty-four point six months, recurrence-free survival improved with a hazard ratio of zero point five eight, confidence interval zero point four five to zero point seven five. That's a forty-two percent reduction in the risk of recurrence or death. Distant metastasis-free survival, the key secondary endpoint, came in at a hazard ratio of zero point six two, confidence interval zero point four five to zero point eight five, p equals zero point zero zero two. In absolute terms, two-year recurrence-free survival was eighty-two point two percent against seventy percent, and two-year distant metastasis-free survival was eighty-eight point five percent against eighty point two percent. Safety was unremarkable for this class: treatment-related adverse events in ninety-five point eight percent of the combination arm versus ninety percent on pembrolizumab alone, and grade three through five events in fourteen point two percent versus thirteen point four percent. Overall survival is immature with a favorable trend.

So why the selloff? Because the market was not comparing zero point five eight to one point zero. It was comparing zero point five eight to zero point five one.

Zero point five one is the recurrence-free survival hazard ratio from KEYNOTE-942, the randomized Phase 2b study of the same combination in the same setting, which enrolled about a hundred and fifty-seven patients. That study also reported a fifty-nine percent reduction in the risk of distant metastasis or death, against thirty-eight percent in the Phase 3. RBC said investors had been looking for something between zero point five zero and zero point five nine. BMO's verdict was that the results were not enough to drive confidence amidst elevated expectations. William Blair's Myles Minter called it a sell-the-news reaction. Mizuho's Jared Holz took the other side, saying he didn't think the data were bad at all and that he preferred Merck as the cleaner exposure. Merck's own Jane Healy pushed back directly: this, she said, is not a subtle benefit.

Here is where I think the market analysis is weak, and it comes down to a single feature of the data that got almost no attention yesterday. The hazard ratio is not constant over time. In a sensitivity analysis the companies describe as prespecified, splitting the curves at six months, the two arms are essentially identical for the first six months — a hazard ratio of roughly one point zero, no separation at all. After six months, the hazard ratio is zero point four two, confidence interval zero point three zero to zero point five eight. A fifty-eight percent reduction.

That is not a statistical curiosity. That is the mechanism showing up in the survival curve. Think about what has to happen before this drug can possibly work. The tumor has to be resected and sequenced. Neoantigens have to be called and ranked. A bespoke mRNA has to be synthesized, released, and shipped. The patient then needs multiple doses, three weeks apart, to prime and expand a neoantigen-specific T-cell population large enough to matter. You cannot compress that into weeks. During that entire lead-in, the patient is receiving pembrolizumab and nothing else of consequence — they are, functionally, in the control arm. An intention-to-treat hazard ratio computed from day zero necessarily averages a period when the drug cannot work with a period when it does, and reports the blend as if it were a single number.

The same logic explains the other number that moved yesterday. In the modified intention-to-treat analysis — restricted to patients who actually received the therapy — recurrence-free survival improves to a hazard ratio of zero point five one and distant metastasis-free survival to zero point five five. That is exactly the Phase 2b figure. The gap between zero point five eight and zero point five one is the cost of the patients who were randomized but never got dosed, because their disease recurred during manufacturing or their product failed. That gap is a manufacturing and logistics number, not a biology number.

I want to be careful not to oversell this, because the argument cuts both ways. A piecewise analysis that improves the result is exactly the kind of analysis that invites scrutiny, and landmark analyses conditioned on surviving to six months can introduce their own selection. Intention-to-treat is the primary endpoint for good reasons, and regulators will price the therapy on zero point five eight, not zero point four two. If you are a payer, the intention-to-treat number is the one that describes what happens when you start a patient down this path, manufacturing failures and all. The honest reading is that zero point five eight is the real-world number and zero point four two is the biological ceiling, and the distance between them is an engineering problem rather than a pharmacological one.

Which brings me to the part that should actually concern you about the commercial case, and it isn't the hazard ratio at all. RBC made the sharpest point: approval looks likely in high-risk melanoma, but commercial success is a separate question, and the relevant competitor may not be adjuvant pembrolizumab. The field has been moving toward neoadjuvant immunotherapy — treating before surgery rather than after — which produces large benefits, costs essentially nothing incremental, and gives you a pathologic response readout that tells you whether it worked. An individualized therapy with a six-to-eight-week bespoke manufacturing cycle, a price tag that will not be modest, and a benefit that by construction cannot begin for six months has to justify itself against that. BMO also warned specifically against extrapolating melanoma results to other tumors, which matters a great deal given that the INTerpath program now spans nine Phase 2 and Phase 3 trials, including three ongoing Phase 3 studies in non-small cell lung cancer, plus pancreatic and gastric work. Melanoma is the friendliest possible setting for this approach: high mutational burden, high neoantigen load, established immunotherapy sensitivity. Lung is harder, and pancreatic is much harder.

So the summary I'd give you is this. The scientific result is real and it is a first — individualized neoantigen therapy works, the ranking algorithms are picking real targets, and an mRNA cancer therapy has now cleared Phase 3. The shape of the hazard function is consistent with the mechanism rather than with noise, and the modified intention-to-treat result reproduces the Phase 2b estimate almost exactly, which is reassuring about the biology and pointed about the logistics. What yesterday actually repriced was not the science. It was the premium that had accumulated in Moderna's stock, which even after a twenty-one percent drop is up more than five hundred percent year to date. The data cleared the scientific bar and missed the expectations bar, and those are different bars. The one to watch now is overall survival, and the lung cancer readouts, because that is where we learn whether this is a melanoma result or a platform.
