Today's spotlight is Replimune, because on Wednesday this company faces the kind of moment that either makes it or ends it, and the science underneath is a genuinely interesting argument about what counts as proof. Let me walk through what's happening, what the drug actually is, why the F-D-A has already said no twice, what changed to earn a third hearing, and how I'd think about the odds and the read-through.

Start with the event. This Wednesday, July twenty-ninth, the F-D-A's cell, tissue, and gene therapy advisory committee will meet to review Replimune's lead drug, R-P-one — vusolimogene oderparepvec — and vote on whether the evidence supports approval. The agency's own decision is due three days later, on August second. That committee vote isn't binding, but after two rejections it's the whole ballgame, because Replimune has stated plainly in its filings that without a timely accelerated approval, R-P-one's development is not viable. The company ended its fiscal year with about two hundred sixty-nine million dollars in cash, down from close to half a billion the year before, and a runway that reaches only into early next year. This is as binary as biotech gets.

So what is R-P-one. It's an oncolytic immunotherapy, which is a mouthful for a simple, elegant idea. You take a herpes simplex virus — the cold-sore virus — and you engineer it so it can no longer cause disease, but it can still infect and replicate inside tumor cells and burst them open. Replimune's version is souped up two ways. It carries a gene for G-M-C-S-F, an immune-signaling molecule that calls immune cells to the wreckage, and it carries a fusogenic protein that makes infected tumor cells fuse together and die in a way that's especially visible to the immune system. The idea is that the virus doesn't just kill the tumor it's injected into — it turns that tumor into a training camp that teaches the immune system to hunt the same cancer elsewhere in the body. It's given by injection directly into the tumor, and it's paired with nivolumab, the anti-P-D-one checkpoint inhibitor sold as Opdivo, which takes the brakes off the T-cells the virus just recruited.

The setting is advanced melanoma in patients who have already progressed on anti-P-D-one therapy — people whose checkpoint drugs stopped working. That's a real hole in the treatment map. Once melanoma outruns checkpoint inhibitors, the options thin out fast, so a drug that can re-sensitize these patients would matter. And the data Replimune has are not nothing. In its single-arm trial, called IGNYTE, R-P-one plus nivolumab produced an objective response in about thirty-four percent of these patients, and — the number that kept this program alive through two rejections — those responses lasted a median of roughly two years, with median overall survival near thirty-three months. Three years out, about forty-eight percent of all treated patients were still alive, and among those who responded, about eighty-four percent. For a post-checkpoint melanoma population, those are striking survival curves.

So why has the F-D-A said no twice — first in July of last year, again this past April? This is the crux, and it isn't about safety. It's about attribution. The trial was single-arm — everyone got R-P-one plus nivolumab, and there was no control group getting nivolumab alone. And here's the problem: nivolumab by itself has some activity even in previously treated patients. So when a third of patients respond, the agency's question is, how much of that is the virus, and how much is the checkpoint drug you gave alongside it? That's the contribution-of-components problem, and a single-arm trial structurally cannot answer it. The F-D-A also flagged how responses were measured — when you inject a tumor and it shrinks, cleanly separating a true body-wide immune effect from local shrinkage takes care — and the fact that the randomized, controlled confirmatory trial, called IGNYTE-three, is only a fraction enrolled, so the definitive answer is still years away. In its April rejection the agency was blunt: the package was insufficient to conclude substantial evidence of effectiveness. Former Commissioner Marty Makary publicly defended that scientific call.

So what changed to earn a third look? Two things, and this is where it gets interesting for how you read the F-D-A right now. The narrow answer is that Replimune and the agency had what the company describes as a more collaborative dialogue, and the F-D-A accepted the resubmission as a complete response and scheduled the advisory committee. The broader answer is that this reconsideration is happening during a stretch of real turnover at the top of the F-D-A and its biologics center. The same survival data that were called insufficient in April are now getting a public hearing, and nothing in the underlying evidence changed — the trial is still single-arm, the contribution question is still unanswered. So what's being tested Wednesday is partly the drug and partly the agency's own evidentiary bar for accelerated approval in a cancer with high unmet need. That's why this meeting is being watched well beyond Replimune's own shareholders.

On the competitive landscape, R-P-one isn't inventing the category. Amgen's Imlygic — talimogene laherparepvec — was the first oncolytic immunotherapy approved, back in twenty-fifteen, and it's also an engineered herpes virus carrying G-M-C-S-F. But Imlygic never became a big product; the injection logistics and a modest body-wide effect held it back, and that history hangs over R-P-one as the cautionary case. On the other side, the post-checkpoint melanoma space now has a recent competitor that got approved on a single-arm trial — Iovance's Amtagvi, a personalized cell therapy grown from a patient's own tumor-infiltrating T-cells. So the F-D-A has already blessed a single-arm melanoma product in this exact setting, and that's precisely the precedent Replimune will lean on Wednesday.

So here's how I'd frame it for you, Travis. Watch three things. First, the briefing documents, which post before the meeting — the F-D-A's own reviewers lay out their concerns there, and the tone of that document usually telegraphs the vote better than anything the company says. Second, watch how the panel handles the contribution-of-components argument, because that's the hinge. If they decide the survival data are compelling enough that the attribution question can wait for the confirmatory trial, R-P-one gets through; if they insist that question be answered first, it doesn't. And third, watch the read-through to everyone else, because a yes here signals the accelerated-approval door is open again for single-arm data in serious cancers, and a no signals the bar just went back up — and that matters for every small oncology company betting the company on a fast, uncontrolled trial. For Replimune specifically, this is genuinely a coin flip on the firm's existence. For the field, it's a referee's ruling on how much proof is enough when patients are out of options. That's your spotlight. Talk soon.
