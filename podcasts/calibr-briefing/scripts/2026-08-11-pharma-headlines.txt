Good morning, Travis. Here are your biotech and pharma headlines for Tuesday, August eleventh. Yesterday's big pre-open readout landed, and it landed well, so today's briefing leads with it and the spotlight goes deep on it. Five items.

First, and leading today, AbCellera. The readout we flagged yesterday came in, and it beat the bar cleanly. AbCellera reported positive top-line Phase 2 data for A-B-C-L six-three-five, its antibody for moderate-to-severe hot flashes due to menopause, and the market's verdict was immediate — the stock jumped about a third. The numbers are the story. A single subcutaneous dose cut the frequency of moderate-to-severe hot flashes by eighty-three percent at four weeks, versus thirty-three percent for placebo — a placebo-adjusted reduction of more than five events a day, highly statistically significant. Severity fell by fifty-eight percent versus twelve percent, and patients slept better. Safety looked clean — no serious adverse events, no discontinuations. Why this matters beyond one menopause drug: the drug hits the neurokinin-three receptor, the same validated target behind Astellas' daily oral Veozah, fezolinetant, and Bayer's elinzanetant — but AbCellera did it with a long-acting antibody from a single shot, and it is the company's first wholly owned clinical asset. That is the make-or-break test of its pivot from AI antibody-discovery engine to actual drug developer, and it is today's spotlight.

Second, deal news in neuroscience. Jazz Pharmaceuticals agreed to acquire privately held Actio Biosciences for eight hundred twenty million dollars up front, plus up to five hundred million in milestones — roughly one-point-three billion dollars all in. What Jazz is buying is Actio's lead asset, a first-in-class small-molecule inhibitor of the K-C-N-T-one ion channel, a precision therapy for a rare genetic developmental and epileptic encephalopathy that affects roughly twenty-five hundred patients in the U.S. The logic is a clean fit — Jazz already leans on neuroscience with Xywav and the epilepsy drug Epidiolex, and K-C-N-T-one is a genetically defined, targetable channelopathy. The deal is expected to close in the fourth quarter.

Third, a supply story worth watching if you touch rare disease. Sanofi is running short on two of its Pompe disease treatments — the older Myozyme and the newer Nexviazyme — after the FDA flagged manufacturing problems at its plant in Waterford, Ireland. The company has warned patient groups and physicians in the U.S. and Europe of a bottleneck in final manufacturing and quality-control issues, and it has signaled that normal supply could be months away. For a life-sustaining enzyme-replacement therapy in an ultra-rare disease, a multi-month gap is a genuine patient-safety and reputational problem, not just an earnings footnote.

Fourth, a hard reset at aTyr Pharma. The company is cutting roughly sixty percent of its workforce and concentrating everything on efzofitimod, its first-in-class immunomodulator, in interstitial lung disease. Efzofitimod targets neuropilin-two to damp down inflammation, and after a disappointing pivotal in pulmonary sarcoidosis the company is now awaiting FDA comments, expected by the end of this month, on the protocol for a new Phase 3 in that indication, while a Phase 2 in scleroderma-associated lung disease reads out early next year. It is the classic small-cap survival move — shrink the burn, extend the runway into late 2028, and bet the company on one mechanism.

And fifth, some market color. The FDA's approval engine is running hot — twenty-nine novel drugs cleared so far this year, well ahead of the twenty-one at this point in 2025 — and the biotech IPO window has stayed open, with drug-startup listings topping six billion dollars year-to-date. The read for the portfolio is simple: capital and regulatory momentum are both cooperating right now, which is exactly the environment in which clean clinical readouts, like AbCellera's this morning, get rewarded fast.
