Good morning, Travis. It's Wednesday, November eleventh, 2026. Tuesday was a genetic medicine day: a splicing drug that nearly doubled a company's stock, a sickle cell readout that didn't move one at all, a second Phase three win in hepatitis delta, a new pill for a rare joint tumor, a smart ring company buying its way into clinical A-I, and AstraZeneca buying a vaccine platform it had been renting for three years. Here's what you need to know.

First, and this is today's spotlight. PepGen said Tuesday that six patients with myotonic dystrophy type one, given four monthly infusions of its oligonucleotide, showed forty-six and a half percent correction of the splicing defect that causes the disease, against about seven percent on placebo. Myotonic dystrophy is driven by a repeat expansion whose R-N-A traps the proteins that control splicing, so hundreds of transcripts get assembled wrong. Alongside the biomarker, the company reported a nearly four-second improvement in how long it takes patients to open a clenched fist, plus gains in grip strength and walking speed. Shares rose more than ninety percent. The context is everything: five days earlier Novartis said del-desiran, the drug at the center of its roughly twelve-billion-dollar Avidity purchase, missed the primary endpoint of a Phase three on that same hand-opening measure. The spotlight digs into what six patients can and can't tell you, and why the partial clinical hold PepGen is still operating under matters more than the headline number.

Second, base editing's first pivotal win, and a strange market reaction. Beam Therapeutics reported that risto-cel met the primary endpoint of its BEACON study in sickle cell disease: at least twelve consecutive months free of severe pain crises, independently adjudicated. The therapy is the patient's own blood stem cells, edited at the promoters of the two fetal hemoglobin genes so that a repressor called BCL11A can no longer sit there and switch them off. Single letters changed, no double-strand cut. Forty-nine patients have been treated, thirty-one had completed the required follow-up at the October cutoff, and all secondary endpoints were met as well. Beam plans to file as early as year-end, which would be the first marketing application for a base-edited medicine anywhere. The stock fell as much as ten percent. H-C Wainwright's explanation is the right one: Beam disclosed that the endpoint was met but not what fraction of patients met it, and not the safety detail. The full dataset goes to a medical meeting in 2027, with an earlier cutoff presented at the hematology meeting next month, where Wainwright says it will be looking for fetal hemoglobin above sixty percent and sickle hemoglobin below forty. Worth remembering what Beam would be walking into. Casgevy and Lyfgenia are both approved and both launched slowly — five patients treated with Casgevy in the second half of 2024, sixty-four over 2025, two hundred nineteen million dollars in sales through September of this year. Beam's pitch is a manufacturing one: most of its patients completed stem cell collection in one or two cycles, where competing therapies have needed up to six, and collection has been the practical bottleneck keeping patients out of these treatments entirely.

Third, hepatitis delta gets a third horse. Mirum said brelovitug, an antibody against the hepatitis B surface antigen, met the primary endpoint of AZURE-four, the second of two Phase threes. In a hundred thirteen treatment-naive patients, fifty-nine percent on the weekly dose and forty-four percent on the monthly dose hit the combined endpoint of virologic response plus normalized liver enzymes at twenty-four weeks, against zero percent in the delayed-treatment arm. Fifty-three percent of the population had cirrhosis. The company will file in the first half of 2027 and is guiding to a launch in the fourth quarter. That is a direct answer to Vir, whose ECLIPSE one data we covered Monday, and to Gilead's already-approved Hepcludex. Three different mechanisms — an entry inhibitor, a surface-antigen antibody plus siRNA combination, and an antibody alone — all converging on a disease that had nothing a few years ago.

Fourth, an approval in sarcoma. The F-D-A approved pimicotinib, brand name Pimjetmi, sold by EMD Serono, for adults with symptomatic tenosynovial giant cell tumor where surgery would cost the patient function. It's a selective inhibitor of C-S-F-one receptor, the growth factor axis that drives this tumor. In the MANEUVER trial the response rate was fifty-six percent against three percent on placebo, with seventy-seven percent of responders still responding at a year, and significant gains in joint range of motion and pain. This is the third drug in the class after Turalio and Romvimza, and the selling point is selectivity rather than novelty — the surgeon who led the trial framed it as a better therapeutic index and, more interestingly, as a reason to stop treating diffuse disease as a surgical problem. The asset came out of China: Abbisko discovered it, Merck K-G-a-A paid eighty-five million dollars to exercise global rights after the Phase three, and China approved it last December. The review ran through Project Orbis with five other regulators in parallel.

Fifth, consumer hardware buys clinical A-I. Oura, the smart ring maker that pulled its I-P-O plans a month ago citing market conditions, is paying six hundred twenty-five million dollars in cash for Counsel Health, which runs a chatbot that triages a user's health question and then hands off to a clinician by telehealth. Counsel had disclosed thirty-six million dollars in funding. The logic is that a continuous stream of physiological data is worth much more if there's a clinical endpoint attached to it. Watch where that pressure lands: it's the same bet that OpenEvidence, Abridge and Epic are making from the clinician side, and the same week STAT reported that MDCalc launched a tool that strips patient identifiers out of queries to medical chatbots rather than trying to broker privacy agreements around them.

Sixth, AstraZeneca acquired Omniose, a preclinical vaccine company out of Saint Louis, after a three-year exclusive research collaboration. Terms weren't disclosed. Omniose's technology makes the sugar and the carrier protein of a conjugate vaccine inside a single cell and lets an enzyme link them, instead of coupling them chemically afterward. The platform goes into AstraZeneca's infectious disease unit to support preclinical programs against bacterial pathogens. Note the pattern, which is the same one argenx ran with Unnatural Products on Monday: collaborate for years, then buy the platform outright.

Seventh, cost cuts. Coherus Oncology is cutting thirty percent of its staff — roughly forty-four people off a base of a hundred forty-seven — and narrowing to a single lead indication, esophageal cancer, for its C-C-R-eight antibody tagmokitug combined with toripalimab. That combination produced a seventy-five percent confirmed response rate in front-line esophageal squamous cell carcinoma in a Phase one-b last month, regardless of P-D-L-one status, though only eight patients were evaluable at the time. Management says the reorganization takes six million dollars a quarter out of the burn. It's the company's second thirty percent cut in under two years. Separately, Vertex is eliminating seventy-eight jobs at Crinetics, the San Diego company it bought for ten billion dollars, and Gyre is cutting after its three-hundred-million-dollar Cullgen acquisition.

Eighth, briefly. Altimmune has an end-of-Phase-two meeting with the F-D-A next month on a Phase three design in alcohol use disorder for pemvidutide, its glucagon and G-L-P-one dual agonist; William Blair called the thirty-eight percent drop in a direct blood biomarker of alcohol consumption, against a six percent rise on placebo, the strongest validation in the program. Dutch competition regulators opened an investigation into whether Biogen abused its position by reserving a required safety blood test for patients on its own Tysabri and withholding it from patients on Sandoz's biosimilar. And in Washington, the incoming Democratic committee chairs sent letters demanding that drugmakers disclose the full terms of the most-favored-nation pricing deals they struck with the administration, which sets up the oversight fight for January.

Looking ahead: Summit and Akeso's ivonescimab decision is due by Friday, Capricor's deramiocel on the twenty-second, and Vertex's povetacicept by the thirtieth. Beam's hematology meeting presentation is December twelfth. We didn't see any Foresite portfolio news in Tuesday's wires.
