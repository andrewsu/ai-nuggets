Good morning Travis. Here are your biotech and pharma headlines for Monday, September twenty-eighth, twenty twenty-six, covering Friday after the close and the weekend. The lead is a withdrawal. Merck and Daiichi Sankyo pulled their filing for ifinatamab deruxtecan in small cell lung cancer after the agency told them the data would not support accelerated approval — the second of the three antibody-drug conjugates in that four-billion-dollar collaboration to fail to reach the market. That is today's spotlight. Around it, Roche got the first approval in seventy years for a childhood kidney disease, Bristol Myers finally showed the full myeloma dataset behind an approval it already has, an alpha-biased interleukin-two bispecific widened its lung cancer program, and a schizophrenia trial is still running everywhere except the United States. Seven items, then the week ahead.

First, and today's spotlight. On Friday afternoon, Daiichi Sankyo and Merck voluntarily withdrew the biologics license application for ifinatamab deruxtecan in adults with extensive-stage small cell lung cancer who progressed on or after platinum chemotherapy. The reason given is unusually blunt for a press release: discussions with the agency established that the data, including the IDeate-Lung one Phase two trial, do not satisfy the requirements for accelerated approval. This had priority review and an action date of October tenth. Ifinatamab deruxtecan is a B-seven-H-three directed conjugate carrying a topoisomerase-one inhibitor payload, and there is still no approved B-seven-H-three medicine in any tumor type anywhere. What makes this worth eight minutes is not the setback itself but what it says about where the accelerated approval bar now sits, and the fact that the target was validated this year — by GSK and Hansoh, not by Daiichi.

Second, an approval that is genuinely a first. On Friday the agency approved Roche's Gazyva, obinutuzumab, to reduce relapse risk in adults and children two years and older with frequently relapsing or steroid-dependent childhood-onset idiopathic nephrotic syndrome who are in complete remission. Levi Garraway at Genentech called it the first approved treatment option for idiopathic nephrotic syndrome in seventy years, and that is close to literally true — the disease has been managed with steroids and off-label immunosuppressants since the nineteen fifties. The basis is INShore, a randomized open-label Phase three in eighty-five patients aged two to twenty-five, comparing two doses of obinutuzumab at days one and fifteen, repeated at weeks twenty-four and twenty-six, against mycophenolate mofetil for fifty-two weeks. Ninety-five point five percent of the obinutuzumab arm were in sustained complete remission at week fifty-two versus seventy-three point two percent on mycophenolate, a seventy-five percent reduction in the risk of relapse or death, four relapses versus twenty-three, and a meaningful cut in cumulative steroid exposure. Sustained remission at week seventy-six favored obinutuzumab numerically but did not reach significance, which is the number to keep an eye on — this is a two-dose-course therapy and the durability question is whether you are re-dosing these children annually for life. Safety was worse than the comparator: grade three or higher events in twenty-five percent versus seven point three, infusion reactions in thirty-six percent, plus the class warnings on hepatitis B reactivation and progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy. The strategic read matters more than the numbers. Obinutuzumab is a type two glycoengineered anti-C-D-twenty antibody, and Roche is systematically converting it from an oncology asset into a general-purpose B-cell depletion franchise in immune-mediated disease: lupus nephritis last October, this on Friday, primary membranous nephropathy under review, systemic lupus under review here and already authorized in Britain as of last week. For anyone building a C-D-nineteen C-A-R T-cell or a T-cell engager for autoimmune disease, the comparator you have to beat keeps getting approved, indication by indication, with fifty-two-week randomized data behind it.

Third, Bristol Myers put the full dataset behind Zenbexus on the table. At the International Myeloma Society meeting in Glasgow on Thursday, with simultaneous publication in The Lancet Oncology, Bristol Myers presented the first full look at EXCALIBER, the Phase three of iberdomide plus daratumumab and dexamethasone against daratumumab, bortezomib and dexamethasone in relapsed or refractory myeloma. Four hundred twenty patients, median follow-up fifteen point seven months. The minimal residual disease negative complete response rate — the primary endpoint — was forty-one point one percent versus twenty point seven, essentially doubled. Overall response was eighty-eight point nine versus seventy-six point one, and complete response or better was forty-five point nine versus twenty-four point nine. Sagar Lonial at Emory made the case that early deep responses track with long-term control, and the benefit held in patients refractory to lenalidomide, which is the population that actually needs a new immunomodulatory option. Iberdomide is a cereblon E-three ligase modulator, a CELMoD — it degrades Ikaros and Aiolos more completely than lenalidomide does, and it works in cells that have stopped responding to lenalidomide. Now the catch, and it is a large one. Grade three or four neutropenia occurred in eighty-four point three percent of the iberdomide arm versus eleven point three percent of the comparator. Grade three or four infections were thirty-nine point two versus twenty-one point six, with fatal infections at two percent versus one point five. Discontinuation for neutropenia was only one percent because it was managed with growth factor and dose interruptions in the first two cycles, but this is a regimen that requires a practice equipped to manage profound neutropenia, not a community convenience play. Against that, peripheral sensory neuropathy was twelve point three percent versus forty-two point six with the bortezomib arm, which is the trade most patients would take. The reason to hold this next to today's spotlight: this drug got accelerated approval on August thirteenth on the strength of a minimal residual disease endpoint, the first myeloma therapy ever to win on that primary endpoint, with progression-free survival in the eight-hundred-patient confirmatory cohort still pending. Same agency, same month, two very different answers about what a surrogate endpoint is worth — and the difference is that Bristol Myers ran a randomized trial and Daiichi ran a single-arm one.

Fourth, and this one runs straight through your interests. Innovent announced this morning that it is expanding the global Phase three MarsLight eleven trial of I-B-I three-sixty-three into non-squamous non-small cell lung cancer. The trial was squamous-only; it now splits into two substudies, both testing the drug as monotherapy against docetaxel in patients who have progressed after chemotherapy and immunotherapy. I-B-I three-sixty-three is an alpha-biased interleukin-two slash P-D-one bispecific fusion protein, co-developed globally with Takeda — it blocks the P-D-one axis and delivers interleukin-two signaling with engineered bias away from the alpha receptor subunit, which is the design choice meant to avoid the vascular leak and regulatory T-cell expansion that killed high-dose interleukin-two as a therapy. It carries two fast track designations here and three breakthrough designations in China. The interesting part of today's news is not the expansion itself but the comparator: going head-to-head against docetaxel in post-immunotherapy lung cancer is the graveyard where a long line of interleukin-two engineering programs has been buried. If an alpha-biased construct clears docetaxel in a randomized global trial, that settles a decade-long argument about whether the not-alpha design thesis actually converts into clinical benefit.

Fifth, a clinical hold that is not going away. Newron said Friday that the agency's hold on United States enrollment in the Phase three ENIGMA-TRS two study of evenamide remains in place, while enrollment continues to expand in Europe, Asia, Latin America and Canada. The company expects at least six hundred patients enrolled by mid-October across twenty countries. Evenamide is a glutamate modulator — it works through voltage-gated sodium channels to damp excess glutamate release — and it is being developed as an add-on to existing antipsychotics in treatment-resistant schizophrenia, a population with essentially one option, clozapine, and no approved adjunct. Newron's chief medical officer says they will keep working with the agency constructively. Read that as a company that has decided the trial is worth finishing without the United States, which is a real strategic choice and one worth noting — a registrational package with no American sites is a harder conversation at the end, not an easier one.

Sixth, a rare disease filing. Pharming said Friday that the agency accepted with priority review a supplemental application for lower doses of Joenja, leniolisib, in children four and older weighing between thirteen and twenty-seven kilograms with activated P-I-three-K-delta syndrome. Target action date is January thirtieth. Leniolisib is a selective P-I-three-K-delta inhibitor and the only approved therapy for the syndrome, which is an inborn error of immunity where the delta isoform is constitutively overactive. This follows the recent approval in four-to-eleven-year-olds above twenty-seven kilograms, so the company is walking the label down the weight curve one band at a time. Small in dollars, but a clean example of how a rare-disease franchise actually gets built after the first approval.

Seventh, briefly. Akeso dosed the first patient in a global Phase three of cadonilimab, its P-D-one by C-T-L-A-four bispecific, in advanced gastric cancer. Cadonilimab is already approved in China in gastric and cervical cancer; this is the global registrational push, and it is the same pattern we have been tracking all month — Chinese bispecifics that cleared domestic approval now running the trials that would give them a Western label on their own rather than through an out-license.
