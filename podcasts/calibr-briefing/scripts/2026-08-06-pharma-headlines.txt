Good morning, Travis. It's Thursday, August sixth, twenty twenty-six, and here's your biotech and pharma briefing. Earnings season is still setting the agenda, and today it drew a sharp line down the middle of the obesity market. Five things.

First, Novo Nordisk. On paper it was a beat — adjusted earnings of about ninety-five cents a share against expectations near seventy-eight, on revenue of roughly twelve billion dollars. And yet the stock fell about six percent. The problem was the newest product: the oral version of Wegovy, the semaglutide pill, stumbled out of the gate on wholesaler inventory destocking, even though by prescription volume it was the strongest G-L-P-one launch Novo has ever run. Add a cautious full-year sales outlook — flat to down mid-single digits on a constant-currency basis — and the market decided execution, not the science, is Novo's problem. There was a bright spot in the pipeline: CagriSema, the combination of the amylin analog cagrilintide with semaglutide, posted up to fourteen percent weight loss in type-two diabetes. But the read of the day was that Novo is still the number-two story in its own category.

Second, and this is the other half of that story: Eli Lilly. Lilly did the opposite — a clean beat and a raise. Revenue came in near twenty-three billion dollars, earnings of eight dollars thirty-eight, and worldwide sales of tirzepatide, the dual G-I-P and G-L-P-one agonist sold as Mounjaro and Zepbound, jumped ninety-one percent to almost ten billion dollars in the quarter. Lilly raised full-year revenue guidance to between eighty-five and eighty-seven billion, and committed another four-and-a-half billion dollars of Indiana manufacturing capacity specifically for its next wave — orforglipron, the oral small-molecule G-L-P-one now branded Foundayo, and retatrutide, the triple agonist hitting G-I-P, G-L-P-one and glucagon. The stock rose about four percent. Put Novo and Lilly side by side this morning and the gap in the metabolic race is widening, not narrowing.

Third, and it's today's spotlight: Revolution Medicines reported the quarter, and it is the most important thing on your desk. The F-D-A accepted the company's first-ever New Drug Application — for daraxonrasib, its multi-selective RAS-ON inhibitor, in previously treated metastatic pancreatic cancer — and selected it for the Commissioner's new National Priority Voucher pilot for accelerated review. Europe opened a phased review, Switzerland granted orphan status, and the expanded-access program put the drug in front of physicians for more than two thousand patients in roughly three weeks. This is the closest anyone has come to cracking mutant-RAS as a broad franchise. I'll go deep on what that means in the spotlight.

Fourth, two quick follow-ups on stories we've covered this week, both now resolved. The F-D-A did approve Moderna's messenger-R-N-A flu vaccine, mFLUSIVA — the first messenger-R-N-A influenza shot ever licensed in the U-S — cleared for adults fifty and up, with the sixty-five-plus indication riding on a confirmatory trial. That was yesterday's spotlight, and it landed. And the roughly four-hundred-billion-dollar AstraZeneca–Bristol Myers merger talk that we broke down Tuesday? A senior source told Reuters the two companies never actually held discussions. AstraZeneca recovered its slide, Bristol Myers gave back its pop, and the largest-pharma-deal-ever headline is, for now, dead.

Fifth, another marker that the biotech financing window is genuinely open. Attovia Therapeutics priced an upsized initial offering at seventeen dollars a share, raising about two hundred and eighty-nine million, and began trading under the ticker A-T-T-O. Attovia's lead program is an antibody it calls an Attobody, targeting I-L-thirty-one — the itch cytokine — for chronic pruritic diseases, with a phase-two program planned for next year. That's the second upsized biotech I-P-O in two days, after Apnimed yesterday. After a long drought, the appetite for new biotech issues is worth tracking as a sentiment gauge for your portfolio.
