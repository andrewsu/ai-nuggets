Good morning Travis. Here are your biotech and pharma headlines for Thursday, October fifteenth, twenty twenty-six, covering Wednesday. The A-I drug discovery class got its public-market test: Iambic priced its I-P-O and starts trading today, and that is the spotlight. Around it, Verastem put up a K-RAS G-twelve-D dataset aimed straight at Revolution Medicines' weak spot, Arrowhead showed an under-the-skin R-N-A drug can knock down tau in the brain, Gilead bought into Vir's masked T cell engagers, and United Therapeutics and Liquidia ended their patent war. First, today's spotlight. Iambic Therapeutics, the San Diego A-I drug discovery company, priced its initial public offering Wednesday night. It sold nine point three seven five million shares at sixteen dollars, the midpoint of its fifteen-to-seventeen-dollar range, for a hundred fifty million dollars in gross proceeds. It trades on Nasdaq this morning. Private investors had put in four hundred sixty-two million dollars since twenty nineteen. The lead drug is I-A-M-thirteen-sixty-three, an oral tyrosine kinase inhibitor of H-E-R-two, with a K-I-F-eighteen-A inhibitor and a C-D-K-two-and-four program behind it. The spotlight covers what the prospectus actually shows and what the pricing says about investor appetite for A-I-native biotechs.

Second, Verastem. Its oral drug V-S-seventy-three-seventy-five, licensed from China's GenFleet, selectively inhibits the G-twelve-D mutant of K-RAS. It binds both the active, on state and the inactive, off state of the protein. In the Phase one-two TARGET-D trial, four of eight second-line metastatic pancreatic cancer patients at nine hundred milligrams had a confirmed response, and the confirmed response rate was thirty-six percent in fourteen third- and fourth-line patients. In lung cancer at six hundred milligrams, the confirmed response rate was forty-three percent. Only two percent of patients stopped for side effects, which were mostly low-grade nausea, vomiting and diarrhea. The timing is pointed. Last week the F-D-A's review documents for Revolution Medicines' Rasonque, the pan-RAS on-state inhibitor approved in August, showed a twenty-four percent response rate in the G-twelve-D subgroup, which was forty-three percent of the pancreatic trial. Mizuho wrote that this "reinforces our view that an allele-specific agent should come first for G-twelve-D patients, with pan-RAS reserved for later lines or combinations." Verastem says the F-D-A has signed off on a first-line pancreatic Phase three, starting by year end. Keep in mind the sample sizes: the pancreatic responses come from eight patients and fourteen patients.

Third, Arrowhead's ARO-MAPT, a small interfering R-N-A against the tau gene. It is given as an injection under the skin and uses a delivery chemistry built to cross the blood-brain barrier. In healthy volunteers, two doses of twelve point eight milligrams per kilogram two weeks apart cut tau in cerebrospinal fluid by an average of seventy-three percent at day one hundred five, and by as much as eighty-five percent in some subjects. The lower doses gave forty and sixty-nine percent. Side effects were mild. Stifel's James Condulis said the data "look compelling" and arguably better than other brain-penetrant approaches, while cautioning that tau is still not a proven Alzheimer's target. The comparison is Biogen's tau antisense drug diranersen, which has to be injected into the spinal fluid and whose Phase two missed its primary endpoint at the Alzheimer's conference this summer. So the delivery result is the clearer win here. If a quarterly injection under the skin gets that much knockdown, the brain-delivery field for oligonucleotides shifts, even while tau itself stays unproven. Next come patients with mild Alzheimer's, and trials are planned in frontotemporal dementia and progressive supranuclear palsy.

Fourth, Gilead licensed three dual-masked T cell engagers for solid tumors from Vir Biotechnology. The masks come from Vir's PRO-XTEN technology, which keeps the drug inert until tumor proteases clip the masks off, to spare healthy tissue. Vir gets sixty-five million dollars in cash, a twenty-five-million-dollar equity investment and forty-five million in near-term preclinical milestones, with up to one point seven four billion more in milestones. It's Vir's second platform deal this year after Astellas. H-C Wainwright called it "strategic and financial validation rather than a basis to increase clinical probabilities of success," because no targets or new clinical data were disclosed. The real test is the Phase one readout of VIR-fifty-eight-eighteen in H-E-R-two tumors, due before year end.

Fifth, United Therapeutics and Liquidia settled all six of their legal fights. A September thirtieth Delaware ruling had found that Liquidia's Yutrepia, an inhaled dry-powder treprostinil, infringed a United Therapeutics patent covering pulmonary hypertension with interstitial lung disease. Liquidia will pay net amounts totaling three hundred eighty point seven million dollars. That is a hundred seventy-five million within thirty business days and two installments of seventy-two point seven million by April twenty twenty-seven. It also filed to remove that indication, which covers more than sixty thousand U-S patients, from the Yutrepia label, and keeps pulmonary arterial hypertension. Liquidia jumped about forty-two percent after hours because the worst case, a market withdrawal, is gone. But the bill eats about ninety-six percent of its three hundred ninety-six million dollars in cash, and the stock is still about forty-six percent below where it was before the ruling.

Sixth, two F-D-A actions with the same theme. The agency launched a Rare Disease Framework Initiative, with a white paper promising disease-by-disease evidence standards that cover everything from pre-I-N-D meetings through manufacturing review. There's a public meeting on October twenty-eighth and no deadline for final deliverables. Endpoints read it as favoring consistency over flexibility. Then, nearly three months before its January sixth deadline, the F-D-A expanded ImmunityBio's Anktiva, an I-L-fifteen superagonist given with B-C-G, to B-C-G-unresponsive bladder cancer with papillary-only tumors. The approval rested on extrapolating efficacy from the carcinoma in situ cohort, not on the single-arm papillary data. That's a concrete example of how the agency is willing to reason across subtypes.

Seventh, a big private round in endocrinology. Oxford's OMass Therapeutics raised three hundred ten million dollars in a Series C co-led by Bain Capital Life Sciences, Forbion and Novo Holdings. Its lead drug, OMS-sixteen-twenty, is an antagonist of the M-C-two receptor, which is the A-C-T-H receptor on the adrenal gland. It's built to stay on the receptor longer, so it holds up as A-C-T-H levels rise in congenital adrenal hyperplasia. It's heading through Phase two in that disease and in Cushing's. Endpoints framed it as a chase of Neurocrine's Crenessity, which blocks the C-R-F-one receptor one step upstream, and of Crinetics' M-C-two-R antagonist atumelnant, now owned by Vertex. OMass also has an S-L-C-fifteen-A-four inhibitor for lupus heading into the clinic.

Eighth, a San Diego item with Scripps roots. Vividion Therapeutics, the chemoproteomics company built on Scripps science and now an independently run Bayer subsidiary, said this morning it has dosed the first patient in a randomized Phase two of VVD-two-fourteen. That drug is a covalent inhibitor of the Werner helicase, W-R-N, which is synthetic lethal in mismatch-repair-deficient cancers. The trial tests it with Keytruda against Keytruda alone in first-line M-S-I-high colorectal cancer. Moving a W-R-N inhibitor into the frontline is the first real bet that the mechanism adds to checkpoint blockade, not just salvages patients after it.
