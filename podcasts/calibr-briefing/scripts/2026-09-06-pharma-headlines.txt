First, the big one. Novartis announced that the Phase three L-P little a HORIZON trial of pelacarsen did not meet its primary endpoint. Pelacarsen is an antisense oligonucleotide, discovered at Ionis and licensed to Novartis in twenty nineteen, that shuts down hepatic production of apolipoprotein little a — the protein that, bolted onto an apoB particle by a disulfide bond, makes lipoprotein little a. Elevated lipoprotein little a is inherited, roughly ninety percent genetically determined, unmovable by diet or exercise, and present in about one in five people worldwide. It has been considered a causal risk factor for atherosclerotic disease for two decades on the strength of genetics. There has never been a drug approved to lower it, and until Friday there had never been an outcomes trial to test whether lowering it helps. The trial randomized eight thousand three hundred twenty-three patients with established cardiovascular disease and lipoprotein little a at or above seventy milligrams per deciliter, one-to-one, to eighty milligrams of pelacarsen subcutaneously every month or matching placebo, on top of optimized guideline-directed care. Nine hundred ninety-three adjudicated events, nine hundred two sites, minimum follow-up two and a half years, first patient dosed in December twenty nineteen. Novartis says the drug lowered lipoprotein little a in the range previously observed, and that safety was acceptable. It just did not reduce the composite of cardiovascular death, non-fatal myocardial infarction, non-fatal stroke and urgent coronary revascularization. Ionis chief executive Brett Monia called the result disappointing but said it provides clarity that will meaningfully inform future cardiovascular care. Novartis chief medical officer Shreeram Aradhye said these are not the results we hoped for. William Blair had modeled six billion dollars in peak U-S sales. The full dataset goes to an upcoming medical congress, and given the calendar the American Heart Association meeting in November is the obvious venue.

Second, and this is why the miss matters well beyond two companies. There are currently about forty-six thousand patients enrolled in Phase three cardiovascular outcomes trials of lipoprotein little a lowering agents, and every one of them just got repriced. Amgen has olpasiran, a small interfering R-N-A, in a seven thousand two hundred ninety-seven patient secondary prevention trial reading out in twenty twenty-eight, plus an eleven thousand patient primary prevention trial that runs to twenty thirty-one. Lilly has lepodisiran, also an siRNA, in the seventeen thousand three hundred patient ACCLAIM trial due in twenty twenty-nine — the single largest of the group. And Lilly started a ten thousand four hundred fifty patient Phase three of muvalaplin, an oral small molecule that blocks assembly of the particle rather than silencing its message, on September second. That is two days before pelacarsen failed. William Blair's Myles Minter wrote Friday that his team sees, in his words, meaningful risk to the remaining trials, while allowing that deeper lipoprotein little a inhibition may still be an opportunity — Amgen's drug has cleared ninety-five percent lowering in mid-stage work versus pelacarsen's eighty. Jefferies' Dennis Ding said his team will be looking hard at any correlation between baseline levels, the magnitude of the drop, and clinical benefit. Neither of those is a bullish note.

Third, an item that speaks directly to Friday's other story. Argo Biopharmaceutical presented updated Phase two data at the Bradykinin Symposium in Berlin on B-W two-oh-eight-oh-five, a small interfering R-N-A against plasma prekallikrein for hereditary angioedema. Twenty-five patients, open label. Three hundred milligrams dosed once every twenty-four weeks cut the average monthly attack rate by ninety-six percent. Six hundred milligrams every twenty-four weeks gave eighty-three percent, three hundred milligrams every twelve weeks gave ninety-three, and between half and three-quarters of patients were attack-free from day twenty-nine onward depending on dose. Mostly mild adverse events, mostly injection site reactions, no discontinuations. Hold that against yesterday's spotlight: ADARx is going public with onvuzosiran, an siRNA against the same target, in Phase three, and Intellia just borrowed four hundred million dollars against a one-time CRISPR knockout of the same gene. Takeda's Takhzyro, the incumbent, is dosed twice monthly. Twice a year is a different product.

Fourth, Climb Bio reported first-in-human data on C-L-Y-M one-sixteen, an antibody against A Proliferation-Inducing Ligand — APRIL — for I-g-A nephropathy. Forty-six healthy volunteers, single and multiple ascending dose. A single three hundred twenty milligram dose suppressed free APRIL by more than ninety percent, with sixty to seventy-five percent reductions in immunoglobulin A, galactose-deficient I-g-A one, and I-g-M, all sustained through twelve weeks. No dose-limiting toxicities, no hypogammaglobulinemia. The design point is that this is a sweeper antibody — engineered for pH-dependent release so it hands APRIL to the lysosome and recycles, degrading the target rather than merely parking on it. Chief executive Aoife Brennan is claiming best-in-class on depth and duration with quarterly dosing. APRIL is a validated target here; the competition is Otsuka's sibeprenlimab and Vera's atacicept, both dosed far more often.

Fifth, at the European Respiratory Society congress, which opened yesterday, Mabwell presented Phase one-b two-a results for nine-M-W nineteen-eleven, the first Chinese-developed monoclonal antibody against S-T two, the I-L thirty-three receptor. Eighty former smokers with moderate-to-severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, four dose levels intravenously every four weeks, most with blood eosinophils at or below three hundred. Annualized moderate-to-severe exacerbations fell twenty-four percent at six hundred milligrams and eighty-one percent at nine hundred; severe exacerbations fell forty-seven and one hundred percent. Those are small numbers in a small trial and the dose-response is steep enough to be suspicious, but the interesting part is the population — this is the low-eosinophil C-O-P-D patient that dupilumab does not serve, and it is the same thesis Roche is testing with astegolimab and Regeneron and Sanofi with itepekimab. Phase two-b enrollment is complete, Phase three around the end of this year, and the F-D-A has cleared a U-S Phase two-a.

Sixth, on the policy side, A-R-P-A-H committed up to a hundred twenty-five million dollars to a program called GIVE — Genetic Medicines and Individualized Manufacturing for Everyone. The goal is a distributed, automated network for making individualized R-N-A therapies outside major academic centers, replacing what the agency calls a slow, costly, centralized model. The named teams include Centillion Biosciences on fill-finish and quality control, H-D-T Bio on chip-based R-N-A production, Massachusetts General on continuous manufacturing, and Waterfall Scientific on automation. F-D-A is engaged to co-develop the regulatory framework. Read this against last year's roughly five hundred million dollars of federal cuts to m-R-N-A vaccine work: the money did not leave R-N-A, it moved from prophylaxis to bespoke manufacturing.
