This is your spotlight for Wednesday, October twenty-first. Today: Rapport Therapeutics, whose epilepsy drug just passed a Phase 2 trial in bipolar mania, and why the market's reaction, a twenty-five percent jump before the open that was almost gone by the close, is a reasonable reading of the data.

Start with the molecule, because the mechanism is the whole thesis. AMPA receptors carry most fast excitatory signaling in the brain. They don't work alone: they come with helper proteins, called TARPs, that shape how they behave, and different brain regions use different TARPs. TARP gamma eight is concentrated in the hippocampus and cortex and is largely absent from the hindbrain and cerebellum. RAP two nineteen is a negative allosteric modulator, a dimmer rather than an off switch, that acts only on AMPA receptors paired with gamma eight. The idea is to calm overactive circuits in the forebrain without affecting the brain regions that cause dizziness, sedation and loss of coordination. The comparison is perampanel, the approved AMPA blocker for epilepsy, which acts on all AMPA receptors. Perampanel causes a lot of dizziness and carries a boxed warning for aggression and hostility.

The program has a pharma origin. The chemistry came from Johnson and Johnson's neuroscience group, and Rapport was founded in twenty twenty-two by Third Rock Ventures and J and J's venture arm to develop it. The first proof point came in September twenty twenty-five, when a small Phase 2a trial in drug-resistant focal epilepsy cut seizures by close to seventy-eight percent and the stock nearly tripled. Two pivotal epilepsy trials, called FOCUS one and FOCUS two, are now enrolling. Bipolar mania was the first test of the "pipeline in a product" pitch, the claim that one well-placed mechanism can support several indications.

Here's what Tuesday's trial showed. It enrolled two hundred fifty-three adults hospitalized with acute mania from bipolar one disorder, after they had stopped their other bipolar medications. Patients got a low dose of RAP two nineteen, zero point seven five milligrams a day, or placebo for three weeks. On the Young Mania Rating Scale, which runs from zero to sixty, the drug group improved by fifteen point four points and the placebo group by twelve point three. The difference of three point one points had a p-value of point zero four five. The secondary endpoint, a clinician's overall rating of mania severity, also reached significance, at point zero two four. The gap over placebo grew each week, from just under one point at week one to two point two at week two to three point one at week three, so the effect was still building when the trial ended. All eleven items on the scale favored the drug. The ones that reached nominal significance were irritability, disruptive and aggressive behavior, thought content, and sexual interest, which are the symptoms that usually put a manic patient in the hospital.

Safety is the core of the commercial pitch. Overall side-effect rates were about the same in both arms, slightly over half of patients, and none were severe or serious. The side effects that drive patients off current mania drugs, which are sedation, akathisia, movement problems and dizziness on standing, were essentially absent. Somnolence was actually less common on drug than on placebo. The most common side effect was agitation, in about seventeen percent on drug versus nine percent on placebo. It usually happened in the first week and lasted around two days, and investigators judged most cases unrelated to the drug. CEO Abraham Ceesay framed it this way: side effects drive discontinuation, discontinuation drives relapse, and RAP two nineteen could break that cycle.

Analysts were positive but restrained. Stifel's Paul Matteis said expectations had been low and called the result a "clear positive," but noted that three point one points barely reached significance and sits at the low end of the three-to-four-point range considered competitive with today's drugs. Jefferies' Andrew Tsai called the safety "clean," said efficacy was in line with the two-to-six-point improvements seen with approved bipolar drugs, and estimated that bipolar mania could add more than a billion dollars to peak sales. He also said the result should encourage management to test bipolar depression next. Raymond James argued that in this disease the drug patients will keep taking often wins, and that tolerability could make RAP two nineteen commercially relevant even without better efficacy. William Blair went furthest and called the asset multi-blockbuster.

The stock tells a more measured story. Shares traded near thirty-six dollars before the open, then dropped as low as twenty-six seventy-three during the day, below Monday's close, and finished at twenty-nine thirty-seven, up just under three percent. The fifty-two-week high is about fifty-three dollars. A company worth roughly one point four billion dollars, with four hundred thirty-six million in cash and funding into the second half of twenty twenty-nine, got almost no credit for a second positive Phase 2. Investors are still valuing Rapport mainly on the epilepsy pivotal trials.

Here's my read of the details, starting with something the press release leaves out. On the patient-flow slide in Rapport's investor deck, twenty-four point six percent of patients on drug stopped treatment early, versus eleven point eight percent on placebo, which is about double. Almost all of that gap came from patients withdrawing consent: twenty-one on drug versus six on placebo. Only four drug patients stopped because of an adverse event. The reasons listed for withdrawal include personal choice, family reasons, five drug patients and no placebo patients who wanted to leave the inpatient unit, and a few who said their symptoms were worsening or wanted care elsewhere. Ninety-five of a hundred twenty-six drug patients finished, against a hundred twelve of a hundred twenty-seven on placebo. This does not overturn the result. Manic inpatients leave trials for many reasons, and the statistical model uses every patient's data up to the point they dropped out. But that model assumes dropouts are random, and when one arm loses patients at twice the rate of the other, that assumption carries weight. It also bears directly on the company's main argument. The pitch is that patients will stay on this drug, yet in a three-week supervised hospital study, twice as many patients on drug as on placebo didn't finish. Expect that question to dominate the full presentation and the FDA end-of-phase-two meeting.

The second thing worth noticing is that Rapport's Phase 3 plan suggests the dose was too low. The deck lists three changes. The dose rises to one point two five milligrams, which the company's modeling says reaches target receptor occupancy faster and higher by day twenty-one. The trials will add sites outside the US, in regions the deck says have historically produced strong signals in neuropsychiatry trials. And the protocol will be adjusted based on what they learned. Put together with a drug effect still rising at week three and a placebo response of twelve points, this reads like a company that thinks it under-dosed a mechanism that works, and is building Phase 3 to get past the high placebo response that sinks many psychiatry trials. If that's right, Phase 3 should show a larger effect. If it's wrong, the higher dose is where any agitation or dizziness signal would show up.

On competition: acute mania today is treated with atypical antipsychotics, lithium and valproate, often in combination, and most of them work in part by reducing glutamate signaling. Their downsides are metabolic effects, sedation, movement disorders, and in lithium's case, blood-level monitoring. Rapport's Gary Sachs, a bipolar specialist at Massachusetts General Hospital, said what's encouraging is what wasn't seen: no somnolence, akathisia or movement problems. If RAP two nineteen holds that profile at the higher dose, it doesn't need to beat olanzapine on efficacy. It needs to match the low end of the class and be the drug patients keep taking. The open question is whether the trial's dropout data supports that.

There's also a broader lesson. This is now the second indication where choosing a target by its location in the brain, rather than by receptor type alone, has produced a clean safety profile in patients. For anyone building neuroscience programs around region-restricted receptor subunits, Rapport is the best public evidence so far that the strategy works in the clinic.

What to watch: long-term open-label epilepsy data in the fourth quarter of this year. The FDA end-of-phase-two meeting on mania in the first quarter of twenty twenty-seven, with two Phase 3 trials starting in the second half. A Phase 3 in generalized tonic-clonic seizures in the first half of twenty twenty-seven. Pharmacokinetic data on a long-acting injectable version in the third quarter of twenty twenty-seven, which would matter a lot for adherence in bipolar disorder. And the full Phase 2 presentation, including eight weeks of follow-up safety data, at an upcoming medical meeting. When that comes, look first at the reasons for discontinuation.
