A big drugmaker pre-committing to give away its next H-I-V-prevention pill before it's even approved, another company fighting to keep a product on the market after its pivotal data unraveled, the obesity talent wars claiming another executive, and a labor market that's easing but not finished. Merck's H-I-V move gets the spotlight.

First, and it's today's spotlight, Merck. The company laid out how it intends to get alimatravir — an investigational, once-monthly oral pill to prevent H-I-V — to the parts of the world that carry most of the disease burden, and it did something unusual in the timing. Before the drug is approved anywhere, while it's still running Phase 3 trials, Merck signed seven royalty-free licensing deals with generic manufacturers, three in Africa and four in India, covering a hundred and twenty-nine low- and middle-income countries. That's essentially the whole map of where new H-I-V infections happen. Royalty-free means the generic makers owe Merck nothing, so they can price at cost. This gets the deep dive because both halves are interesting, the drug and the strategy. A once-monthly pill sits in a genuinely useful gap between daily pills and twice-a-year injections, and pre-committing to generic access before launch is quickly becoming the new template for H-I-V prevention.

Second, Amgen is fighting to keep a drug on the market, and the backstory is ugly. The drug is Tavneos, generic name avacopan, an oral treatment for a rare, severe autoimmune inflammation of the small blood vessels called A-N-C-A-associated vasculitis. It works by blocking the C-five-a receptor, a piece of the complement arm of the immune system, and Amgen got it through its purchase of ChemoCentryx. The problem is that in April, the F-D-A's drug-review center proposed withdrawing the drug's approval, after it emerged that the pivotal trial's data had apparently been manipulated — outcomes altered for several patients, some investigators unblinded — and the New England Journal of Medicine took the rare step of retracting the original paper. This past week, Amgen pushed back, submitting new data and analyses to the F-D-A and formally requesting a hearing to preserve access. Watch this one, because it's an unusual case of a regulator trying to claw back an approval over data integrity, and how it lands will set a precedent for how the agency handles tainted trials.

Third, the obesity talent wars claimed another name. Verdiva Bio, a startup developing once-weekly oral obesity drugs, hired Steve Marso as chief medical officer, along with a clinical-development colleague. What makes it notable is where Marso came from. He was chief medical officer at Metsera, the obesity biotech that Pfizer bought last year, and he's an interventional cardiologist who co-authored two of the landmark New England Journal papers that helped establish the cardiovascular benefits of Novo's semaglutide. The read is that as the injectable obesity market matures, the money and the talent are chasing the next frontier, convenient oral drugs, and the people who built the first wave are the prize. Verdiva has key readouts coming, and it just stocked up on people who know how to run them.

And fourth, a quick read on the labor market. The biotech layoff picture is easing compared with the brutal stretch of the last two years, but it hasn't stopped. Amgen cut around forty jobs this week in what it called organizational alignment, and in Australia, Clinuvel Pharmaceuticals is letting go of up to a fifth of its workforce as it moves its headquarters to the United States. The signal for the sector is a two-speed market. Funding has rebounded to its best first half since twenty twenty-two, but it's flowing to later-stage, de-risked companies, while the smaller, single-product players are still trimming to survive.
