Good morning Travis. Here are your biotech and pharma headlines for Thursday, October twenty-second, twenty twenty-six. Wednesday delivered a first-in-class approval, a pivotal sweep in an eye disease, an acquisition by OpenAI, and new signals on China policy. Seven items, then the week ahead.

First, the big one. The FDA approved Hibsago, GSK and Ionis's antisense oligonucleotide bepirovirsen, for chronic hepatitis B. It is the first antisense drug ever approved in hepatitis B, and the first finite-course regimen to deliver what the field calls functional cure, meaning the virus stays undetectable after you stop all treatment. You already know the efficacy from the B-Well trials we covered back in May: nineteen percent functional cure against zero on placebo. What is new is the label, and the label is more interesting than the approval. That is today's spotlight.

Second, Roivant keeps compounding. Its subsidiary Priovant reported that brepocitinib, a dual TYK2 and JAK1 inhibitor, won both of its pivotal trials in non-infectious uveitis, an inflammatory eye disease that can end in blindness. In the Clarity one and Clarity two studies, patients on brepocitinib went a median of more than forty-eight weeks before treatment failure, against twelve weeks on placebo. They also went forty weeks without needing immunosuppressive steroids, and the drug hit every secondary endpoint, including resolution of macular edema. Priovant plans a supplemental application in the first half of next year. Remember the backstory here: Pfizer handed brepocitinib to Priovant and took a twenty-five percent stake in the company, and the drug already won its first approval in late August as Lisraya in dermatomyositis. Stifel's Alex Thompson called it a potential best-in-disease medicine and said this validates a broader multi-indication franchise across rare and specialty markets. Cantor Fitzgerald's Prakhar Agrawal put a five-billion-dollar peak-sales number on uveitis alone. The caveat is the usual one: the comparison to AbbVie's Humira is cross-trial, not head to head.

Third, on your artificial intelligence beat, OpenAI made an acquisition. It bought Orchestra Bio, a San Francisco company whose platform coordinates drug development programs, and is folding the team into Rosalind, OpenAI's life sciences stack that already includes a reasoning model for biology and chemistry and a research workbench. Terms were not disclosed; Orchestra had raised roughly twenty million dollars. Note carefully what this is and is not. Orchestra's agents do not design molecules. They monitor studies, track timelines and budgets, and flag knock-on effects, so that when a manufacturing batch fails, the system tells you which trial starts just slipped and what your options are. OpenAI is buying the operations layer of drug development, not the discovery layer, which is a different bet from the one Xaira or Iambic are making, and it sits alongside OpenAI's existing partnerships with Novo Nordisk and Moderna. Separately, Alcon paid five hundred fifty million dollars upfront for Neurotech Pharmaceuticals, picking up Encelto, an encapsulated cell therapy implanted in the eye and the only FDA-approved treatment for macular telangiectasia type two.

Fourth, AstraZeneca and Amgen presented full Phase 3 results for Tezspire in eosinophilic esophagitis at United European Gastroenterology Week. Tezspire is tezepelumab, the antibody against TSLP, the alarmin cytokine sitting at the very top of the allergic inflammatory cascade. In the CROSSING trial, histologic remission at week twenty-four was sixty-five percent on the lower dose and sixty-nine percent on the higher dose, against three and a half percent on placebo. That is an unusually wide separation. Swallowing difficulty scores improved by twenty-two to twenty-four points from baseline by week fifty-two, and the companies reported significant total endoscopic remission at a year. This is the asthma franchise extending into the gut, and it lands in a market where Sanofi and Regeneron's Dupixent got there first.

Fifth, two pieces of the China picture moved in opposite directions. Endpoints reports the Treasury Department is expected to apply tougher scrutiny to China biotech deals in forthcoming outbound-investment rules, despite months of industry lobbying for a lighter touch. Meanwhile, new data from the investment bank Locust Walk reframes whether the United States is actually losing. China's share of global licensing deal value has climbed to fifty-eight percent this year, while the US share fell from fifty-six percent in twenty twenty-four to twenty-six percent now. But American biotechs had a record year in mergers and acquisitions: a hundred seventy-three billion dollars through the third quarter, above the hundred fifty-one billion for all of twenty twenty-five, and ninety-one percent of that value went to US-headquartered companies against one percent for Chinese firms. Locust Walk's chief executive Geoff Meyerson's explanation is the useful part. Chinese biotechs treat licensing as a financing event, comparable to a venture round, because venture capital has collapsed there and acquisition exits are structurally hard. American companies would never license out their lead asset, because they can raise, go public, or sell the whole company. His other point is one to keep: Chinese sellers accept bigger back-end milestones and care less about royalties, which is why the headline biobucks numbers keep inflating. Upfront payments were ten percent of total deal value for US sellers and six percent for Chinese ones, and as Meyerson put it, in the vast majority of deals you never receive all the milestones.

Sixth, the IPO window held open for another one. Rezera, the company that was NodThera until it rebranded last month, filed publicly for a Nasdaq offering of up to a hundred million dollars under the ticker R Z R A. The money backs ruvonoflast, an oral inhibitor of NLRP3, the inflammasome sensor that triggers interleukin one beta release, with a Phase 3 planned in peripheral artery disease starting next year. Novo Holdings and Blue Owl Capital each own about twenty-four percent going in. Worth noting the lane has a recent price: Lilly agreed to acquire Ventyx Therapeutics for one point two billion dollars earlier this year, largely for NLRP3.
