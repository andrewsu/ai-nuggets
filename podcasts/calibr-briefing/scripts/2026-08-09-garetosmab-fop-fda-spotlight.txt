Today's deep dive is on a drug that came back from the dead, and a disease most people have never heard of. The company is Regeneron, the drug is garetosmab, and sometime this month the F-D-A is expected to decide whether it becomes a treatment for fibrodysplasia ossificans progressiva. If that name doesn't ring a bell, here's the shorthand that does: it's the disease that turns muscle into bone.

Let me start with the disease, because you can't understand why this approval matters without it. Fibrodysplasia ossificans progressiva — F-O-P — is one of the rarest and most devastating conditions in medicine. It affects only about nine hundred people worldwide. It's caused by a single gain-of-function mutation in a receptor called A-C-V-R-1, and that one change rewires the body's bone-building machinery. In people with F-O-P, soft tissue — muscle, tendon, ligament — progressively ossifies. The body builds a second skeleton on top of the real one, bridging joints, freezing the shoulders, the hips, the jaw. It's episodic and cumulative: painful flare-ups, often triggered by injury, leave behind new bone that never goes away. And there's a cruel catch — trauma makes it worse, so the very act of a biopsy or surgery to remove the bone triggers more of it. Most patients are locked into a fixed position by their thirties or forties, and it shortens life.

Now the mechanism, because this is where garetosmab is elegant. That mutated A-C-V-R-1 receptor does something it's not supposed to do: it responds to a signaling protein called Activin A. In healthy people, Activin A doesn't switch this receptor on — but the F-O-P mutation flips Activin A from a bystander into the trigger that drives the abnormal bone formation. Garetosmab is a monoclonal antibody that binds and neutralizes Activin A. So rather than working somewhere downstream, it removes the actual driving signal at the very top of the cascade. It's a clean, rational piece of drug design — go after the ligand that the broken receptor is listening to.

Here's why this is a comeback story and not just a launch story. Back in 2020, garetosmab was in a phase 2 trial called LUMINA-1, and the efficacy looked genuinely exciting — a large drop in new bone lesions and in flare-ups. But then, in the open-label extension where everyone was getting the drug, there were five deaths among forty-four patients. Regeneron paused dosing. The company's analysis concluded the deaths were unlikely to be related to garetosmab, but couldn't fully rule it out — and for a moment it looked like the program might not survive. In a disease with nine hundred patients and no antibody in sight, that was a real possibility.

Instead, Regeneron pushed into a phase 3 trial called OPTIMA, and in September of last year it read out — and it read out clean. Sixty-three adults, fifty-six weeks, two doses tested against placebo. The lower dose cut new bone lesions by ninety-four percent; the higher dose by ninety percent. And by lesion volume — the total amount of abnormal new bone — both doses reduced it by more than ninety-nine percent. Essentially, garetosmab came close to switching off new heterotopic ossification. And critically, this time the safety profile was manageable, with no deaths in the trial. That's the arc: a program that nearly died on a safety signal came back with a pivotal trial that was both overwhelmingly effective and clean. The F-D-A accepted the application under priority review in February, with a decision due this month.

So why does an approval for a nine-hundred-patient disease matter beyond those families? Two reasons.

The first is the standard of care it would reset. There is exactly one approved F-O-P drug today: Ipsen's Sohonos, generic name palovarotene, an oral retinoic-acid-receptor agonist approved in the U-S in 2023. And Sohonos is a deeply compromised option. It carries a boxed warning for birth defects, and — most damaging — it causes premature closure of the growth plates in children. In trials, that growth arrest hit about thirty-five percent of patients under fourteen. So the one approved drug is effectively off-limits, or heavily restricted, in exactly the young, still-growing patients who have the most disease ahead of them, and it's been contentious enough that European regulators declined to approve it. Against that backdrop, a mechanistically direct antibody that neutralizes the disease's actual driver, with ninety-plus-percent efficacy, isn't just another option — it's potentially a new first-line standard. The one caveat is that OPTIMA was run in adults, so the initial label will likely be adult-only, and the pediatric question — where the need is greatest — will be the next chapter.

The second reason is what it says about Regeneron's science. Garetosmab came out of the same human-genetics-plus-antibody engine that produced the company's bigger drugs. Finding that Activin A is the actionable node in F-O-P, and then building an antibody to it, is a proof point for going from a genetic cause straight to a targeted biologic. Commercially, an ultra-orphan drug for nine hundred patients will never move Regeneron's top line — this is priced and sized like the ultra-rare product it is. The value is strategic and reputational: it validates the Activin A biology, which reaches into other conditions, and it's a rare-disease win for a company whose growth narrative right now is really about eye disease, immunology, and oncology.

Now the part to actually watch, because with garetosmab the label is the story. Given the 2020 deaths — even deemed unlikely related — the F-D-A's framing will matter enormously: whether there's a boxed warning, a formal risk-management program, and which dose gets approved. And it's worth noting the lower dose, three milligrams per kilogram, was numerically the better performer in OPTIMA while delivering less drug exposure — exactly the efficacy-with-a-lower-dose profile regulators like when safety is the sensitivity. So don't just watch for a yes or no this month; watch the label. In ultra-rare disease, where a single trial of sixty-three patients carries the whole approval, the wording around safety is what determines how freely doctors actually reach for the drug.

The bottom line: garetosmab is a rational antibody against the precise molecular driver of a horrific disease. It survived a near-fatal safety scare to post a near-definitive phase 3, and it's about to challenge a flawed incumbent for a standard of care that badly needs replacing. For nine hundred families it could be the first real answer. For the rest of us, it's a clean case study in how modern target biology — and a company willing not to walk away after a setback — turns an untreatable genetic disease into a treatable one. Watch for the decision, and the label, this month.
