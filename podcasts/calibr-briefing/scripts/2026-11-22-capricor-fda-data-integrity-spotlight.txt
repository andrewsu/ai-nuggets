Welcome to the spotlight. Today it's Capricor, and specifically the F-D-A's decision to explain in public why it rejected deramiocel for Duchenne muscular dystrophy a second time. Most complete response letters are private, and the public only hears the company's summary. This time, on the same evening as the letter, the F-D-A posted its own statement on its biologics website saying the pivotal trial's data were unreliable and couldn't be fixed by reanalysis. That makes this one of the clearest signals yet of how the post-Makary F-D-A plans to use the flexibility it keeps promising rare disease sponsors.

Start with the drug. Deramiocel is an allogeneic cell therapy made from cardiosphere-derived cells, a population of donor heart cells. Capricor's model is that the cells don't engraft. Instead they release exosomes that push macrophages from an inflammatory state toward a healing, anti-fibrotic one. The company first developed it for Duchenne cardiomyopathy, the heart failure that is now the leading cause of death in the disease, and later for preserving upper-limb function in older, mostly non-ambulatory patients.

Now what happened Friday. Capricor got the letter on November twentieth, two days before the goal date, and put out a release just before eight p-m Eastern. The release said the F-D-A found the data insufficient to establish substantial evidence of effectiveness, plus "certain other clinical deficiencies." Chief executive Linda Marbán said Capricor stands behind "the rigor of our clinical program and the integrity of our data." The F-D-A's statement went further. It said the agency's Bioresearch Monitoring program, which inspects how trials are conducted, found serious deficiencies in Study HOPE-three, the Phase three trial. Its three named findings were unauthorized post hoc changes to the statistical analysis plan, violations of blinding procedures, and other protocol violations that, in its words, "individually and collectively, compromise the reliability of the study data." The key sentence is that these problems render the data "unreliable and incapable of post hoc remediation." The agency added that even if the question of clinical meaningfulness "could theoretically be addressed," the data can't support an approval. It also issued a separate regulatory responsibility letter tied to the inspection, which Capricor can answer.

The stock closed Friday at seven fifty-nine and traded around a dollar sixty-three after hours, down roughly eighty percent. Capricor had about one hundred ninety-nine million dollars at the end of September, which it says lasts at least twelve months, and it's reviewing costs to stretch that. It cut about ten percent of staff in September and had been expanding its San Diego manufacturing plant ahead of a launch. Sell-side notes weren't out by Sunday morning. The first chance to hear management take questions is Monday's call.

To see why the F-D-A said "cannot be remediated," you have to go back through a long and contentious review. The first rejection, in July 2025, was for cardiomyopathy, based on a small trial plus external natural-history data, and it also cited manufacturing issues. That letter came after an advisory committee planned by the review office was canceled by Vinay Prasad, then head of the biologics center. Capricor said it was blindsided. Then in December 2025, Capricor said HOPE-three hit its primary endpoint, a measure of upper-limb performance, in one hundred six patients, and its secondary endpoint, left ventricular ejection fraction.

The F-D-A's briefing documents for the July 2026 advisory committee told a different story. Reviewers wrote that HOPE-three "did not meet its pre-specified primary and secondary efficacy endpoints." They said that after the randomized, double-blind phase ended, while patients were in the open-label extension, the statistical analysis plan was changed, "generating at least two additional versions." The changes covered the primary and key secondary endpoint definitions, the analysis methods, and how missing data were handled. Capricor said its results are governed by version three point oh of the plan, finalized before unblinding, and that the F-D-A was using an unsigned, obsolete internal draft, version one point one. Marbán said the agency had refused to meet with the company about it.

The blinding problem is more specific, and anyone running a placebo-controlled trial of a cell therapy should note it. Hypersensitivity reactions occurred in forty-two percent of patients on deramiocel versus fifteen percent on placebo. The reviewers wrote that a side effect profile that distinctive "raises the possibility that treatment assignment could be inferred even under formal blinding conditions." The outcome measure, upper-limb performance, depends on patient effort. If families and assessors can guess who got the active drug, an effort-dependent endpoint becomes hard to trust. On the same day as the advisory committee, the HOPE-three paper came out in The Lancet with results that conflicted with Capricor's earlier claims, and the company had to file a clarification. The committee voted nine to three that the evidence didn't support effectiveness in cardiomyopathy. Members were more open on upper-limb function, so Capricor switched the requested indication, added two-year open-label data, and got a three-month extension to November twenty-second. More than two dozen trial investigators wrote to the agency that a rejection would signal "complete inflexibility." The F-D-A's answer on Friday was that it considered all of it, and that open-label extension data can't rescue a randomized trial whose conduct it no longer trusts.

So why does this matter beyond one company? Three reasons.

First, look at what the F-D-A was flexible on and what it wasn't. Since Marty Makary and Vinay Prasad left, the agency has reversed several hard-line decisions, and in October it launched a Rare Disease Framework promising evidence standards fitted to small populations. A patient advocate and Capricor consultant who lives with Duchenne wrote in BioSpace this month that deramiocel would be that framework's first real test. Friday's statement draws the line clearly: "flexibility in regulatory approach cannot come at the cost of the foundational requirement that the data underlying an approval decision be reliable." So the agency will bend on how much evidence a rare disease sponsor needs, but not on whether that evidence was collected cleanly. It also stressed that career scientists made the call, which rules out the political explanation that hung over the 2025 rejection.

Second, the F-D-A is now willing to make the reasons public. It posted rejection letters in bulk last year, but this is different: the explanation went up the same night, before the company's call, and it contradicted the company's account. For investors, that means you may now hear the agency's side of a contested review directly rather than depending on management's summary, at least when the issue is how a trial was run. For sponsors, a fight over a statistical analysis plan can become a public finding that your data can't be trusted.

Third, the patients. HOPE-three enrolled older, mostly non-ambulatory boys and young men, and that group has the fewest options. Sarepta's gene therapy Elevidys was pulled back from non-walking patients after deaths from liver failure in 2025. The exon-skipping oligonucleotides only cover certain mutations, and the steroid and H-DAC inhibitor options don't target the heart. That's why advocacy groups pushed hard for deramiocel. Parent Project Muscular Dystrophy called the decision "devastating," but noted that a rejection letter "does not necessarily mean" deramiocel can never be approved. It's also asking what happens to patients now getting the drug through trials and access programs. Realistically, data the agency calls unfixable means a new randomized trial. Marbán said on the third-quarter call that if the drug were rejected, Capricor would decide whether another trial could establish efficacy. A new controlled study in non-ambulatory Duchenne would take years, Capricor has one year of cash, and its Europe and Japan filings would rest on the same HOPE-three dataset.

What should Foresite and Calibr take from this? Three things.

One, treat version control of the statistical analysis plan as a regulatory exposure, not just paperwork. Sign one plan and lock it before unblinding, and keep a dated record of every draft. If the F-D-A asks for all your drafts, as it did here, an unsigned early version can end up defining your trial.

Two, plan for functional unblinding at the design stage. Cell therapies, bispecifics and anything with frequent infusion reactions give themselves away. If the primary endpoint depends on patient effort or clinician judgment, build in blinded central raters and assessors walled off from adverse event data, and decide before the trial how you'll test whether blinding held. A side effect gap of forty-two percent against fifteen is enough to undermine a pivotal trial.

Three, for portfolio diligence on rare disease companies, the regulatory question has changed. It used to be whether this F-D-A would be flexible. Now it's whether the trial was run cleanly enough to deserve that flexibility. A single pivotal study with a mid-course analysis change and an effort-dependent endpoint is where that risk sits.

Monday's call will show whether Capricor contests the inspection findings in its response to the regulatory responsibility letter or moves toward a new trial. Either way, Friday put on record what the agency will and won't be flexible about. That's the spotlight.
