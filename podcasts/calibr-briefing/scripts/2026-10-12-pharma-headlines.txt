Good morning Travis. Here are your biotech and pharma headlines for Monday, October twelfth, twenty twenty-six. The trade press was dark over the weekend, but three medical meetings were not: the American Academy of Ophthalmology in New Orleans, the Heart Failure Society of America in Phoenix, and the American Society for Bone and Mineral Research in Boston. Most of today's news came out of those rooms, plus one Friday approval that got buried under the I-P-O news. The lead, and today's spotlight, is Merck's full year-one data on remigromig, the eye drug it paid one point three billion dollars up front to get. Seven items, then two readouts landing this morning.

First, Merck. Last month Merck said remigromig was non-inferior to Roche's Lucentis in diabetic macular edema, without giving numbers, and promised the full data at the eye meeting. Saturday it delivered. Remigromig is not an anti-V-E-G-F drug. It is a tri-specific antibody that switches on Wnt signaling through the Frizzled-four receptor, the pathway that builds and maintains the blood-retina barrier. In the nine-hundred-eighty-four-patient Brunello trial, the two remigromig doses gained nine point one and eight point seven letters of vision at one year, against eleven point eight for monthly Lucentis. Both cleared the non-inferiority margin, which Merck did not disclose, and no secondary endpoint showed superiority. The safety numbers are the problem: events tied to proliferative diabetic retinopathy, the stage where fragile new vessels grow on the retina, hit six point seven and six point one percent on remigromig versus zero point nine percent on Lucentis, and discontinuations for adverse events ran about five times higher. B-M-O wrote that it sees "limited competitive potential," with weaker numerical efficacy and a less favorable safety profile. The spotlight takes apart whether that safety signal is the drug or the comparator, and why it points toward combinations.

Second, AstraZeneca's rare disease unit, Alexion, took a public bruising on its successor to Strensiq. Efzimfotase alfa is a next-generation enzyme replacement for hypophosphatasia, the inherited bone disease caused by deficient alkaline phosphatase. At the bone meeting on Sunday, Alexion showed full data from HICKORY, the Phase three in adolescents and adults, which missed its primary endpoint last spring. The six-minute walk improved by seventeen meters over placebo, with a p-value of zero point one seven. The breakdown is the story. In the sixty patients whose disease started in childhood, the gain was about thirty-six meters. In the sixty-four with adult-onset disease, it was minus three meters, because the placebo group in that subgroup did better than anyone expected. Endpoints' headline was that AstraZeneca blames trial design. The pediatric trial, MULBERRY, was clean, and the F-D-A is reviewing the drug under priority review with a decision expected in the first half of twenty twenty-seven. The practical read is that the label will probably follow pediatric onset. That leaves adult-onset disease open, and BioMarin bought an oral program in August that targets the substrate that builds up rather than replacing the enzyme.

Third, a Friday approval that deserves more attention than it got. The F-D-A approved pritelivir, to be sold as Hovilpri, for herpes simplex lesions in immunocompromised adults that have not responded to acyclovir, valacyclovir or famciclovir. Those drugs are nucleoside analogues that need the viral thymidine kinase to activate them, and resistance usually comes from mutations in that enzyme. Pritelivir is a helicase-primase inhibitor that blocks viral DNA unwinding directly, so thymidine kinase mutations do not matter. It is the first new herpes antiviral mechanism approved in nearly thirty years. In the pivotal PRIOH-one trial, sixty-three percent of patients had complete lesion healing by day twenty-eight, versus thirty-four percent on investigator's choice, which for these patients often means intravenous drugs like foscarnet, with real kidney toxicity. Adverse reactions were less than half as frequent. The drug came from AiCuris, the German company Asahi Kasei bought in April. Asahi Kasei has guided to about fifteen thousand addressable U-S patients and more than four hundred million dollars in peak sales.

Fourth, the most interesting pharmacology of the weekend came from Cardurion at the heart failure meeting. Tovinontrine is an oral inhibitor of phosphodiesterase nine, the enzyme that breaks down cyclic G-M-P inside heart muscle cells, so it amplifies natriuretic peptide signaling. It is the same pathway Entresto boosts, from a different angle. In five hundred twenty-nine patients with reduced ejection fraction, already on heavy background therapy including Entresto and S-G-L-T-two inhibitors, the two-and-a-half and twenty-five milligram doses cut N-T-pro-B-N-P at twelve weeks, and the fifty milligram dose did nothing. Cardurion's explanation is a counter-regulatory feedback loop when cyclic G-M-P gets too high. The awkward part is that its separate preserved-ejection-fraction trial, two hundred seventy-seven patients, tested only the fifty milligram dose, and was flat. So the program has proof of concept in one form of heart failure, a re-do in the other, and an inverse dose response that any cyclic-nucleotide program should take as a warning to dose-range the bottom of the curve.

Fifth, a busy weekend for BridgeBio across two meetings. At the heart failure meeting, an independent analysis of electronic health records from Epic's COSMOS database, two thousand six hundred eighty-four propensity-matched patients with transthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy, found acoramidis associated with an eighteen percent lower risk of a composite of death, hospitalization, new atrial fibrillation and diuretic escalation compared with Pfizer's tafamidis, and about twenty-seven percent fewer heart failure hospitalizations. It is observational and cannot settle the question, but it is the second real-world dataset pointing the same direction, and BridgeBio is plainly building the case for acoramidis as the stabilizer of choice. At the bone meeting, BridgeBio showed encaleret, its calcium-sensing receptor antagonist, restoring parathyroid hormone to at least the lower limit of normal in ninety-one percent of patients with autosomal dominant hypocalcemia type one, versus none on standard care. The application has priority review with a May eighth, twenty twenty-seven action date, and BridgeBio dosed the first patient in its registrational trial in chronic hypoparathyroidism, which is the much larger market.

Sixth, more from the eye meeting. Roche showed one-year data for vamikibart, an antibody against interleukin-six injected into the eye, in uveitic macular edema, the swelling that is the leading cause of vision loss in uveitis. Gains in vision and retinal thickness held through fifty-two weeks, about two-thirds of eligible patients needed no retreatment after week sixteen, and the F-D-A has accepted the application with a decision expected by July twenty twenty-seven. One caution: Roche's own language on the primary endpoint is "numerically higher," which is not the same as statistically significant in both trials. If approved, it would be the first targeted non-steroid treatment for the disease, and it is a validation point for interleukin-six as an eye target, which matters for the spotlight.

Seventh, two quick regulatory notes. Leads Biolabs, the Hong Kong-listed Nanjing company, received a second F-D-A Fast Track designation for opamtistomig, a bispecific that blocks P-D-L-one and stimulates the T-cell co-activator four-one-B-B, this time in first-line biliary tract cancer, granted nine days after submission. And InnoCare cleared a Chinese I-N-D for a long-acting bispecific against T-L-one-A and interleukin-twenty-three, the two most validated targets in inflammatory bowel disease, in one molecule.

Two readouts land this morning, both at eight-thirty Eastern, after this briefing was recorded, so here is the bar rather than the result. First Tracks Biotherapeutics, the drug-development company AnaptysBio spun out in April, reports the first cohort of its Phase one-b gluten-challenge study of A-N-B-zero-thirty-three, an antibody against C-D-one-twenty-two, in celiac disease. That is the same target as F-B-one-oh-two, which argenx's Forte unit took to a Phase two win last Thursday in a hundred twenty-six patients. The question is whether First Tracks protects the gut lining on biopsy in a smaller cohort, or merely trends. And Mirum reports topline Phase three EXPAND data for Livmarli, its bile acid transport inhibitor, in itch from ultra-rare cholestatic liver diseases including biliary atresia, the label expansion that would push the drug well beyond Alagille syndrome and P-F-I-C.

For the rest of the week: City Therapeutics and the A-I-native drug developer Iambic are both expected to price their I-P-Os, and TRexBio's offering closes on the thirteenth. Iambic is the one to watch for this show: an A-I-native small-molecule company asking public investors to underwrite a clinical-stage pipeline. That is your Monday briefing.
