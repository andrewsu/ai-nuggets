This is your Calibr spotlight for Wednesday, November eighteenth. Today: Isomorphic Labs' five billion dollar Series C, the largest private financing biotech has ever seen, for a company that hasn't yet named a drug.

Start with the facts. On Tuesday, Isomorphic announced a five billion dollar Series C, co-led by Greenoaks and Sequoia Capital. Andreessen Horowitz and several other new investors joined. Alphabet, which owns three-quarters or more of the company according to U-K filings, invested again, and so did every existing backer: Thrive Capital, MGX, G-V, CapitalG, Temasek and the U-K's Sovereign A-I Fund. The company says the round was oversubscribed. It didn't disclose a valuation, but Bloomberg reported on November fourth that the talks valued it at about fifty billion dollars, up from forty billion or more in October.

The pace is the story. Isomorphic took its first outside money, six hundred million dollars, in March 2025. In May of this year it raised two point one billion, then the second-largest biotech round ever. Six months later comes five billion, which beats Altos Labs' three billion dollar launch in 2022. That's about seven point seven billion dollars in twenty months. For scale, European Biotechnology points out that this one round is larger than all the venture money raised by the entire U-K biotech sector in the first nine months of 2026, about two point eight billion pounds against roughly three point eight billion pounds for Isomorphic alone.

So what is the money for? Isomorphic spun out of Google DeepMind in 2021 to build on AlphaFold, and its product is IsoDDE, the Isomorphic Drug Design Engine. That's a set of models that predict protein structures, how drugs bind to them, how tightly they bind, and where new binding pockets might be. In February the company published a technical report. It claimed more than twice AlphaFold three's accuracy on hard protein-ligand cases, better antibody-antigen prediction, and binding-affinity estimates that match physics-based methods. It released no code, no weights and no training details, and it doesn't sell access. Its president, Max Jaderberg, said at Bloomberg Tech this month, "We're not selling access to the models, we're not selling tokens or API access." Outsiders get IsoDDE only by partnering with Isomorphic.

The new money goes three ways: more foundational biology models, a bigger internal and partnered pipeline, and something new, Isomorphic's own translational and clinical development infrastructure. Hassabis told Bloomberg the company has more than twenty programs under way, and that it plans to go beyond small molecules into "pretty much all modalities," including biologics. His stated ambition is "a platform to do hundreds of drugs a year." Sequoia's Konstantine Buhler told Bloomberg that what convinced his firm was that Isomorphic has found scaling laws, meaning its models keep getting better as you add computing power and data. Greenoaks' Neil Mehta called drug discovery "an optimization challenge of almost infinite scale." And with Jensen Huang's exit from Nvidia last week rattling A-I companies about chip supply, Hassabis made a point of saying Isomorphic has "very secure compute pipelines." Google designs its own chips.

Now the skeptics' case, which is mostly about what hasn't been disclosed. Isomorphic has named no drug candidate, no target and no I-N-D. The only pipeline details are two therapeutic areas, oncology and immunology, and a claim of "positive preclinical results." The clinical timeline has also slipped. Hassabis once said there would be A-I-designed drugs in trials by the end of 2025. At Davos in January he moved that to the end of 2026. That's now six weeks away, and on Tuesday he wouldn't give Bloomberg a new date. He said only that the company is on track with its regulatory submissions and will have more to announce in 2027. Earlier this month Jaderberg described the programs as "deep in preclinical development."

The best public evidence is a September post by Jaderberg about an unnamed "Target D." IsoDDE searched chemical space for two to four days, a handful of molecules were made, and one produced a working dose-response curve. Isomorphic compared that with a published molecule it estimates took three to five years to find. But there was no potency figure, no selectivity data, and no animal results. BiopharmaTrend's Andrii Buvailo laid out the debate in a piece this month, quoting the analyst Melinda Chu: "Isomorphic is slower than traditional R-and-D, and with two billion dollars. No IND in five years." She predicted no trial in 2026, and possibly not in 2027. She also pointed to Insilico Medicine, which took its lung fibrosis drug rentosertib from target to Phase one in under thirty months and has since started Phase three.

The money tells the same story. In January 2024, Lilly paid forty-five million dollars upfront with up to one point seven billion in milestones, and Novartis paid thirty-seven and a half million upfront with up to one point two billion. Novartis expanded its deal last year, and J-and-J signed in January on undisclosed terms. Public upfronts total about eighty-two and a half million dollars, and BiopharmaTrend found no reported milestone payment. A fifty billion dollar valuation is roughly six hundred times the disclosed upfronts. McKinsey's numbers show this is how A-I deals work across the industry: drugmakers pay about two percent of the headline value upfront and tie sixty to ninety percent to clinical and commercial milestones that, for Isomorphic, haven't happened yet.

The optimists have answers. Pharma partners who have seen the internal data are expanding their deals, not walking away. The IsoDDE benchmarks are public numbers that can be checked. And five years without an I-N-D is less damning than it sounds. The industry average from first hit to development candidate is about three years before safety studies even start, and Isomorphic spent its early years building the platform, including co-developing AlphaFold three, rather than running programs. In effect, the round is a bet that the engine compounds, that drug number twenty comes much faster than drug number one.

Now the competition. Chai Discovery, which OpenAI backs, raised four hundred million dollars at a three point eight billion dollar valuation in July. Xaira, which Foresite Labs co-incubated with ARCH, launched with more than a billion dollars in 2024. And Iambic, now public, reported its first public-company update earlier this week: its brain-penetrant HER2 drug shrank tumors in thirty-six percent of heavily pretreated patients. So the field has split. The smaller companies have human data. Isomorphic has the capital and the AlphaFold pedigree but no patients dosed. Across the sector, drug-discovery startups have raised a record twenty-three point two billion dollars this year, according to Dealroom figures cited by CNBC. The U-K's BioIndustry Association warns that megadeals like this inflate the headline numbers and mask how hard it still is for early-stage companies to raise.

What should Foresite and Calibr take from this? Three things.

First, the valuation benchmark for A-I-first drug design is now set by a company without clinical data. That helps Xaira and its peers on paper. But it also shows where capital is concentrating: on a few platforms with big-tech balance sheets and their own compute, not across the field. Expect investors to ask every A-I platform company how it competes for chips, talent and pharma partnerships against one with seven point seven billion dollars.

Second, frontier models are going closed. AlphaFold three's weights came out under non-commercial terms. IsoDDE will not come out at all. For groups like Calibr that build on public structure-prediction tools, the gap between open models and the best proprietary ones is likely to widen, and pharma will get the frontier through partnerships with Isomorphic, not through software licenses. Isomorphic is also building its own clinical team, so over time it will compete for programs and people, not just partner.

Third, the scorecard is simple, and it's coming in 2027. Watch for the first named target and I-N-D, the first reported milestone payment from Lilly or Novartis, and any results showing that IsoDDE predicted something in advance that held up in the lab. If those arrive on time, five billion dollars will look early, not excessive. If the first trial slips again, this round becomes the high-water mark for pricing A-I drug discovery before any proof that it works in patients.
