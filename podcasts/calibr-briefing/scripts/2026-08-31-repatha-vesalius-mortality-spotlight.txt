Good morning Travis. Today's spotlight: Repatha, VESALIUS-C-V, and the distance between a mortality signal and a mortality claim.

Here is what Amgen put out at three-eighteen this morning Eastern time. In a late-breaking session in Munich, with simultaneous publication in Circulation, the company presented a prespecified mortality analysis of VESALIUS-C-V and headlined it as follows: Repatha reduces the risk of death in patients at high risk for a first heart attack or stroke. Twenty percent relative reduction in death from any cause. More than twelve thousand patients. Separation of the curves beginning at about a year and a half. Jay Bradner, Amgen's head of R and D, framed it as lowering L-D-L cholesterol doing more than preventing events — it may also reduce the risk of dying.

That is a very large claim in a very large market, and it is worth taking apart carefully, because the underlying data are better than the skeptical read and weaker than the press release.

Start with what the trial was, because the design decisions are the whole story. VESALIUS-C-V enrolled twelve thousand two hundred fifty-seven patients at seven hundred seventy-four sites in thirty-three countries, run by the TIMI Study Group with Amgen as sponsor. The entry criteria are the important part: men fifty to seventy-nine, women fifty-five to seventy-nine, with an L-D-L cholesterol of at least ninety, or non-H-D-L of at least a hundred twenty, or apolipoprotein B of at least eighty, already on stable optimized lipid-lowering therapy — seventy-two percent on a high-intensity statin. And critically, no prior heart attack and no prior stroke. Two-thirds had documented atherosclerosis somewhere — coronary, cerebrovascular or peripheral — without having had a major event. The other third had high-risk diabetes with no qualifying atherosclerosis at all. Median baseline L-D-L was a hundred twenty-two. Randomized one-to-one to evolocumab a hundred forty milligrams every two weeks or placebo, and in the lipid substudy the drug arm sat at forty-five milligrams per deciliter at forty-eight weeks against a hundred nine on placebo.

Then the design decision that matters most: the trial was built to run a median of at least four and a half years. It delivered four point six. Compare that to FOURIER, the original evolocumab outcomes trial, at about two point two years, or ODYSSEY OUTCOMES with alirocumab at about two point eight. The TIMI group explicitly designed for duration because the benefit of L-D-L lowering lags — the statin literature is unambiguous that the effect size grows with time on drug, and that fatal endpoints in particular only emerge after several years. That single choice is why this trial has anything to say about mortality at all, and it is the most transferable lesson in the entire dataset.

The primary results, published in the New England Journal in January, were clean. The three-point composite of coronary death, heart attack or ischemic stroke: three hundred thirty-six events on evolocumab against four hundred forty-three on placebo, five-year Kaplan-Meier estimates of six point two versus eight point zero percent, hazard ratio zero point seven five, p less than zero point zero zero one. The four-point composite adding ischemia-driven revascularization: thirteen point four versus sixteen point two percent, hazard ratio zero point eight one. Heart attack alone: two point seven versus four point one percent, hazard ratio zero point six four — a thirty-six percent reduction, and the single largest effect in the trial. No safety difference of any kind.

Now the mortality numbers, which is what today is about. Death from any cause occurred in four hundred thirty-four patients on evolocumab and five hundred thirty-nine on placebo — five-year estimates of seven point nine versus nine point seven percent, hazard ratio zero point eight zero, confidence interval zero point seven zero to zero point nine one. Death from cardiovascular causes: a hundred fifty-six versus a hundred ninety-five, two point eight versus three point six percent, hazard ratio zero point seven nine, interval zero point six four to zero point nine eight. Both intervals exclude one. Both look like real effects.

And here is the catch, which the New England Journal paper states outright and the press release does not. The trial used a prespecified hierarchical testing sequence, meaning secondary endpoints are tested in a fixed order and the moment one fails, everything below it stops being confirmatory. The sequence broke at death from coronary heart disease — a hundred five events versus a hundred seventeen, hazard ratio zero point eight nine, p equals zero point three nine. Cardiovascular death and all-cause death both sit below that step. The paper's own words are that the hierarchical testing plan precluded formal hypothesis testing and conclusions may not be drawn, and that the confidence intervals for untested endpoints were not adjusted for multiplicity and should not be used to infer treatment effect. So Amgen's twenty percent is arithmetically correct, prespecified in the sense that the analysis was planned, and statistically uncertified. Those are three different things and the press release collapses them into one.

Now the piece of arithmetic nobody is discussing, and it cuts against the claim. There were a hundred five fewer deaths from any cause in the evolocumab arm. There were thirty-nine fewer cardiovascular deaths. That means roughly two-thirds of the all-cause mortality difference sits in non-cardiovascular deaths — cancer, infection, everything else — where lowering L-D-L cholesterol has no plausible mechanism. When the total-mortality effect is substantially larger than the cause-specific effect that is supposed to be generating it, the most likely explanation is chance in a play of a large number of endpoints. That is exactly the kind of pattern a testing hierarchy exists to protect you from, and it is exactly the endpoint the hierarchy declined to certify.

So why do I still think the mortality direction is probably real? Three reasons, and they are not the press release's reasons.

The first is that the endpoint that did pass, and passed convincingly, contains the fatal events. The composite of cardiovascular death, heart attack or ischemic stroke came in at a hazard ratio of zero point seven three, a twenty-seven percent reduction, formally significant. Fatal cardiovascular events are in that number.

The second is the calibration argument, and it is the strongest thing in the paper. The investigators ran the Cholesterol Treatment Trialists' endpoint definitions and observed a thirty-five percent lower risk of major coronary events. Given the achieved L-D-L difference, the statin meta-regression predicts thirty-one percent. Observed and predicted essentially agree. That means evolocumab is behaving exactly like the L-D-L hypothesis says a drug lowering L-D-L by that amount for that long should behave — no more and no less. And statins, given enough follow-up, do reduce mortality. If this drug sits on the statin line for events, the prior that it sits on the statin line for deaths is reasonable.

The third is external consistency: Mendelian randomization on P-C-S-K-nine variants shows lower coronary death, FOURIER's open-label extension showed a cardiovascular death effect, and ODYSSEY OUTCOMES showed an all-cause death effect with alirocumab. None of that proves anything. Together with a four-point-six-year randomized dataset, it is a defensible position.

There is a second paper worth your attention that came out of the congress and is getting less coverage. In the Journal of the American College of Cardiology, the TIMI group published a prespecified total-events analysis — first and subsequent events rather than time-to-first. Among these patients there were one thousand six hundred fifty-four first four-point events and one thousand one hundred seven subsequent ones, sixty-seven percent more. Evolocumab cut first events nineteen percent, subsequent events twenty-five percent, total events twenty percent. Projected over five years, that is thirty-one first and twenty-four subsequent events prevented per thousand patients — fifty-five total. Marc Sabatine's line is that counting subsequent events almost doubles the number of events prevented. For a commercial and payer audience this is more consequential than the mortality headline, because cost-effectiveness in prevention is driven by total healthcare events avoided, not by a time-to-first hazard ratio, and the standard analysis has been systematically undercounting the denominator of value.

Now the competitive picture, which is where this actually lands for you. Repatha's problem has never been evidence — it is a fifteen-year-old molecule with fifty-one trials and nine million patients behind it. Its problem was access. The F-D-A broadened the label to high-risk primary prevention in August of last year; the European Commission expanded it this month on the strength of VESALIUS-C-V. Amgen has been selling direct at two hundred thirty-nine dollars a month, roughly sixty percent below U-S list. And the threat is not another injectable antibody. It is Merck's Lipfendra, enlicitide decanoate, the oral P-C-S-K-nine macrocycle we spotlighted on July seventeenth, at three hundred fifteen dollars for thirty days — under four thousand a year — approved on L-D-L lowering alone, with its outcomes trial, CORALreef Outcomes, still running. It is Novartis's Leqvio, inclisiran, twice-yearly, with VICTORION-one PREVENT testing exactly this primary-prevention population. And behind those, one-shot base editing and C-R-I-S-P-R approaches aimed at the same pathway.

Against an oral that is easier, cheaper, and mechanistically equivalent, an antibody's only durable moat is outcomes evidence the oral does not have yet. That is precisely why Amgen is pushing a mortality claim rather than a MACE claim. A twenty-five percent reduction in events is a reason to prescribe something; a twenty percent reduction in death is a reason to write it into a guideline and a formulary as the referenced agent. The claim is doing strategic work, and understanding that it rests on an uncertified endpoint tells you how hard it will be to defend when a guideline committee reads the same hierarchy language I just read you.

One last frame, from the same congress. The REACT initiative imaged nearly seventeen thousand adults aged eighteen to seventy with no known cardiovascular disease and found silent atherosclerosis in fifty-seven percent of them — one in thirteen in their twenties, nine in ten in their sixties. A viewpoint in the Canadian Journal of Cardiology last week asked, straightforwardly, which patient is a VESALIUS-C-V patient. The primary-versus-secondary prevention dichotomy that has organized cardiovascular drug economics for thirty years is dissolving, and what replaces it is a continuous risk gradient across a population that is enormous. In that world the binding constraint is never efficacy. It is which subset of a hundred million people gets a biologic, and at what price, and the answer is going to be settled by health-economic modeling in which total events prevented and mortality are the two highest-leverage inputs. Amgen knows this. Today's announcement is an argument aimed directly at that model.

So the bottom line. The mortality effect is directionally credible and mechanistically consistent, and it is not established — the trial's own statistical plan says so, and the split between total and cardiovascular deaths is a reason for genuine caution rather than a technicality. The more durable result from this congress is the total-events analysis, which raises the measured value of the drug by roughly eighty percent without any new patients. And the strategic point is that Amgen is spending its best evidence to build a wall against an oral competitor that will win on convenience and price the moment it has outcomes data of its own. Watch the guideline language, watch VICTORION-one PREVENT and CORALreef Outcomes, and if you are designing anything in cardiometabolic prevention, note that the bar VESALIUS-C-V just set is four and a half years of follow-up with a total-events analysis — not two years and a first-event hazard ratio. Talk soon.
