Good morning Travis. Today's spotlight: milvexian, LIBREXIA A-C-S, and what a fourteen-thousand-patient null result actually tells you about factor eleven-a inhibition.

Let me start with the numbers, because they are unusually clean and the cleanliness is the point. Fourteen thousand one hundred ninety-four patients, eight hundred ninety-three sites, forty-four countries. All of them within seven days of an acute coronary syndrome, all of them with at least two additional risk factors for a recurrent ischemic event, ninety-two percent of them having had a stent placed for the index event. Mean age sixty-three, seventy-eight percent men, fifty-seven percent presenting with a S-T-elevation heart attack, forty-three percent diabetic. Randomized to milvexian twenty-five milligrams twice daily or matching placebo, on top of whatever antiplatelet regimen the investigator judged appropriate. Median follow-up twelve point two months. Cardiovascular death, heart attack or ischemic stroke: three hundred eighty-four events on milvexian, five point four percent, versus three hundred sixty-five on placebo, five point one percent. Hazard ratio one point zero five, confidence interval zero point nine one to one point two one, p equals zero point five zero. No difference in any individual component. No difference in all-cause mortality. The independent monitoring committee had already recommended stopping for futility last November; the final analysis confirmed it. Presented yesterday in Munich by Gabriel Steg of Hôpital Bichat, published simultaneously in the New England Journal of Medicine, funded by Janssen and Bristol Myers Squibb.

Now, why anyone cared in the first place, because this is one of the few drug classes in cardiovascular medicine that was built on a genuine human genetic argument rather than a mouse. Every anticoagulant we have ever approved trades thrombosis against bleeding, because the pathway that makes pathological clots is largely the same pathway that stops you bleeding when you cut yourself. Factor eleven is the one place in the coagulation cascade where nature appears to have separated those two things. People born with severe factor eleven deficiency — hemophilia C, common in Ashkenazi populations — have a markedly reduced rate of ischemic stroke and venous thrombosis, and yet they mostly do not have a bleeding disorder in the ordinary sense. They bleed at mucosal surfaces after trauma or surgery, and otherwise live normal lives. Elevated factor eleven runs the other way, raising thrombotic risk. So the human experiment has already been done, in both directions, and it says factor eleven amplifies thrombus formation but is largely dispensable for hemostasis. If you could drug it, you would get a blood thinner that does not cause bleeding. That is the single most valuable unclaimed asset in cardiovascular medicine, and it is why Bristol Myers, Bayer, Novartis and Merck all have programs.

So hold on to that, because the most important result in this trial is not the efficacy failure. It is the bleeding. The primary safety endpoint — intracranial, vision-threatening intraocular, or fatal bleeding — occurred in zero point three percent of patients in both arms. Hazard ratio one point zero four. No increase in any other major bleeding definition either: not the standardized major bleeding scales, not the non-surgical major bleeding definition. The only thing that moved was clinically relevant minor bleeding, five point two versus four point five percent, and that confidence interval crosses one. And remember the context: ninety-five percent of these patients were on dual antiplatelet therapy at baseline, sixty percent of them on the potent P2Y-twelve inhibitors, and eighty-six percent were still on dual therapy a year later. You added a third antithrombotic on top of that, in fourteen thousand people, and you did not move serious bleeding at all. The genetic hypothesis survived its largest test. It just did not pay.

Which brings us to the interesting question, the one Steg spent his discussion on: why no benefit? He offered three possibilities and I think they are worth taking in order, because they are not equally likely.

The first is that the dose was too low. This is the obvious defense and it is partially blocked by the trial's own data. Trough clotting time — the activated partial thromboplastin time, which is the readout for intrinsic-pathway inhibition — was prolonged about one point six fold at four weeks on milvexian versus essentially unchanged on placebo. The drug was on board and doing exactly what a factor eleven-a inhibitor does. Steg added the sharper point: asundexian, Bayer's competing oral factor eleven-a inhibitor, is dosed at fifty milligrams once daily, which is the same daily milligram load as twenty-five twice daily, and at that dose it worked in secondary stroke prevention. So "not enough drug" is not a comfortable explanation.

The second is timing. Dual-pathway inhibition — adding low-dose anticoagulation to antiplatelet therapy — may only be useful in the first few months after an acute coronary syndrome, when the culprit plaque is still healing and the coronary bed is still thrombogenic, and may add nothing over the following year. That is testable and this trial cannot test it, because it enrolled everyone at seven days and followed them for a year.

The third is the one I find most persuasive, and it is a competitive argument rather than a biological one. The trials that established the value of adding low-dose anticoagulation after an acute coronary syndrome — the ones with rivaroxaban — were run in an era when background antiplatelet therapy was aspirin, or aspirin plus clopidogrel. This trial ran in an era of near-universal potent dual antiplatelet therapy on top of a revascularized artery. Marc Sabatine of the TIMI Study Group, who discussed the trial, made exactly this point: ninety-two percent revascularized, prolonged and potent dual antiplatelet therapy in most patients, and in that setting there may simply be no residual thrombotic events left for a third agent to prevent. He also said the confidence intervals were tight enough that it is unlikely a clinically meaningful benefit was missed. And then he asked the question that actually matters for everything downstream: was milvexian at this dose incredibly safe, or was it simply not inhibiting factor eleven enough? Because those two readings look identical in a trial that showed neither benefit nor harm. His conclusion — and he is the most careful person in this field on this exact point — is that the optimal degree of factor eleven inhibition required in any given population remains undefined.

So let me put the class scorecard on the table, because the pattern is legible now in a way it was not eighteen months ago. Bayer's asundexian failed against apixaban in atrial fibrillation and was stopped for inferior efficacy. Bayer's asundexian then succeeded in secondary stroke prevention — OCEANIC-STROKE, over twelve thousand patients, a twenty-six percent reduction in ischemic stroke with no increase in major bleeding, now under F-D-A priority review, and the first successful phase three of any factor eleven-a inhibitor. Milvexian now fails in acute coronary syndrome. Novartis, which bought Anthos for this, has abelacimab — an antibody rather than a small molecule, given monthly — which in phase two produced a dramatic reduction in bleeding versus rivaroxaban in atrial fibrillation, and is now in a phase three specifically in patients with atrial fibrillation whom physicians have deemed unsuitable for anticoagulation at all.

Read those four results together and the through-line is not about vessels or tissue beds. It is about what the drug has to beat. Where the standard of care is already potent — apixaban in atrial fibrillation, aggressive dual antiplatelet therapy on a stented coronary — factor eleven inhibition does not add. Where there is an actual gap — patients on antiplatelets alone after a non-cardioembolic stroke, patients whose bleeding risk has excluded them from anticoagulation entirely — it wins, and it wins on the safety axis. That is a real and defensible commercial position. It is just a much smaller one than "replace the direct oral anticoagulants."

Which brings me to the money, and to why I would not write this program off on yesterday's data. Bristol Myers has two products, Eliquis and Opdivo, that together are roughly half its revenue and both lose exclusivity later this decade. Milvexian is the designated Eliquis successor. The trial that carries that value is LIBREXIA A-F: fifteen thousand five hundred patients, milvexian versus apixaban, powered for noninferiority on stroke and systemic embolism and for superiority on major bleeding. And the dose in that trial is one hundred milligrams twice daily. That is four times the dose that just failed. So the efficacy failure at twenty-five milligrams tells you very little about efficacy at one hundred, and — this cuts the other way — the pristine bleeding profile at twenty-five milligrams tells you very little about bleeding at one hundred. The atrial fibrillation trial is genuinely undetermined by yesterday's result in both directions, which is not what the market usually assumes when a phase three fails. The readout has already slipped once, to the first quarter of twenty twenty-seven, on slow event accrual — which management framed, plausibly, as the drug preventing the strokes it needs to count. LIBREXIA Stroke, the third trial, does use twenty-five milligrams twice daily, and there the ACS result is more directly relevant, though the population is the one where asundexian already succeeded. Steg's own framing was that the safety data from A-C-S are the reassuring input to the two ongoing trials, and that those trials differ in population, endpoints, background therapy and disease pathology. He is right, but the safety reassurance transfers only for the stroke trial, since that is the one sharing the dose.

The last thing worth saying is about how to update, because there is a general lesson here that applies well beyond anticoagulation and directly to how you underwrite genetically validated targets. The factor eleven human genetics were, and remain, about as good as target validation gets: loss of function protects against thrombosis, gain of function causes it, and the loss-of-function humans are not bleeding to death. That package told you the target is causal and that inhibiting it is safe. What it could not tell you — what no genetics can tell you — is whether there is any therapeutic room left once you account for what patients are already receiving. Fourteen thousand patients were spent establishing that in a modern, stented, potently antiplatelet-treated coronary population, there is not. The binding constraint on this class was never the biology. It was the standard of care, and the genetics were silent on that.

Two other things carried in this dataset that are worth noting. About twenty percent of patients discontinued assigned treatment, which is high but not disqualifying for a futility-stopped trial. And the investigators explicitly acknowledged that too few patients were on antiplatelet monotherapy to test whether milvexian would have shown benefit against a lighter background — which is the one hypothesis this trial most wanted to test and structurally could not. If a sponsor wants to revisit dual-pathway inhibition after an acute coronary syndrome, that is the trial: short duration, single antiplatelet agent, and probably a higher dose.

That's the spotlight. Bristol Myers loses the smallest of the three LIBREXIA indications and keeps the one that matters, at a dose this trial did not test, with a bleeding profile this trial makes more encouraging and less informative than it first appears. Talk soon.
