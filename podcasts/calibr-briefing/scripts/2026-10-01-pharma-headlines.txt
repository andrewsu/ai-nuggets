The single biggest item landed at four-thirty this morning Eastern time, and it is a deal: Sanofi is paying Regeneron a billion dollars in cash, up front, for four early-stage immunology antibodies — one of them in Phase one, three of them still preclinical. That is today's spotlight. Ten items, then the quick ones and the week ahead.

First, the deal. Sanofi and Regeneron expanded the alliance that produced Dupixent, adding four next-generation, long-acting antibodies that Regeneron invented. Sanofi pays a billion dollars up front with up to seven billion more in development, regulatory and commercial milestones, the two companies split development and commercialization costs, and they split any future profits fifty-fifty worldwide. Regeneron leads research and development; Sanofi leads global commercialization — the same division of labor as the original. The Dupixent profit split itself does not change. The assets are a long-acting interleukin-thirteen antibody now in a Phase one trial in atopic dermatitis, a long-acting interleukin-four by interleukin-thirteen bispecific, and long-acting antibodies against interleukin-four and the interleukin-four receptor alpha subunit — those three expected to enter the clinic in twenty twenty-seven. Regeneron also takes an option on Sanofi's lunsekimig, a bispecific nanobody against T-S-L-P and interleukin-thirteen, exercisable when its Phase three trials in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease finish. And the two companies settled the litigation Regeneron filed against Sanofi in twenty twenty-four. Belén Garijo, five months into the chief executive job, called it a deliberate first step in igniting Sanofi's innovation engine. Jefferies told clients that while the assets are early-stage, the expansion should be viewed positively, because it signals the new chief executive's proactive focus on the most important yet addressable uncertainties investors face. Sanofi shares rose two percent. Much more in the spotlight.

Second, Lilly's combination of its amylin receptor agonist eloralintide with Zepbound — tirzepatide, its approved G-I-P and G-L-P-one agonist — produced twenty-three point three percent average weight loss at forty-eight weeks in adults who have both obesity or overweight and type two diabetes. Three hundred sixty-seven patients, the combination at several doses against each drug alone against placebo. At the top combination dose, nine milligrams of eloralintide plus fifteen milligrams of tirzepatide, average loss was fifty-four point one pounds. Tirzepatide alone managed thirty-four point four pounds; eloralintide alone at six milligrams, twenty-eight point six. For context, Lilly's own triple agonist retatrutide delivered twenty point eight percent at week eighty in the same comorbid population, and tirzepatide historically loses efficacy when diabetes is present. Citi's analysts called twenty-three point three percent comparable to tirzepatide in patients without type two diabetes, which they said is notable given the attenuation you normally see, and comfortably above their seventeen percent bar. The catch is tolerability: adverse events were more frequent in the combination arm than with either drug alone, mostly gastrointestinal, and discontinuations ran as high as twenty-seven percent. Citi's read is that starting both components simultaneously amplified the problem and that optimized Phase three titration could preserve the efficacy while improving adherence. Phase three starts in the fourth quarter.

Third, and read this against the second item. Boehringer Ingelheim and Zealand reported full Phase three results for survodutide, their glucagon and G-L-P-one dual agonist, in adults with obesity and type two diabetes — seventy-six weeks, up to thirteen point one percent average weight loss on the efficacy estimand against three point one percent on placebo, published simultaneously in the New England Journal of Medicine. Hemoglobin A-one-C fell up to one point two one percent against essentially nothing on placebo, and up to twenty-nine point five percent of treated patients returned to a normal glucose range against four percent. A magnetic resonance substudy in the companion trial showed up to a thirty-four percent relative reduction in visceral fat against twelve percent. Those are good numbers in isolation. They are also third place: thirteen point one percent at seventy-six weeks against retatrutide's twenty point eight at eighty and the eloralintide combination's twenty-three point three at forty-eight. And note who had the worse week. Zealand is the originator of survodutide and of petrelintide, the amylin analog Roche paid one point six five billion dollars up front for — and petrelintide's flat dose-response landed Tuesday. Two Zealand-originated assets, forty-eight hours, both trailing Lilly.

Fourth, staying in the same mechanism neighborhood as today's spotlight. AbbVie presented Phase two part B data as a late breaker at the European dermatology congress for zumilokibart, the half-life-extended interleukin-thirteen antibody it bought when it acquired Apogee Therapeutics for ten point nine billion dollars in June. Three hundred forty-six patients in the sixteen-week induction period; all three dose regimens hit the primary endpoint on E-A-S-I seventy-five response, all three beat placebo on deeper skin endpoints and on itch, and the mid-dose regimen has been selected for Phase three. The antibody is engineered to a clinical half-life of roughly seventy-seven days, which is the entire point — it opens the door to dosing every three months or even every six. Hold that number next to the fact that Sanofi this morning paid a billion dollars up front for a long-acting interleukin-thirteen antibody that is in Phase one.

Fifth, Bristol Myers amended the long-term safety extension of its Phase three Aloft trial of admilparant in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis to monitor liver injury more closely, after what a spokesperson called a limited number of liver-related events including the death of one participant from liver injury. The amendment went to European Union sites in May and reclassified liver injury as a newly classified important potential risk. Most events were described as mild, transient, or consistent with hepatic adaptation; the fatality was multi-organ failure with liver damage, immune destruction of red cells and widespread clotting, in a patient with underlying lung disorders, and the company says it is not even clear whether that patient received admilparant rather than placebo. Independent monitors have reviewed unblinded data repeatedly and recommended continuing. The reason this moved stocks — Bristol Myers and Contineum Therapeutics both fell sharply late Tuesday before rebounding — is that admilparant is an L-P-A-one receptor antagonist, and the class has a liver history: Bristol Myers killed an earlier molecule over liver toxicity, and Amgen shelved one it inherited from Horizon. R-B-C's Brian Abrahams wrote that the news is likely to reaffirm historical concerns around liver toxicity for the class even though there are too many variables to conclude whether there is a true safety signal. Leerink's Roanna Ruiz was blunter: it may not be a drug-related issue, let alone a class issue. Stifel's Paul Matteis noted Contineum's molecule is designed differently and is being tested at doses as low as ten milligrams a day. Full Aloft results are expected by year end.

Sixth, Merck reported detail behind a Phase two-b win for tulisokibart, the anti-T-L-one-A antibody it acquired with Prometheus Biosciences for ten point eight billion dollars, in moderate-to-severe hidradenitis suppurativa. A hundred forty-nine patients across three dose arms and placebo. At week sixteen, seventy-two percent of the high-dose group, sixty-four percent of the mid-dose and fifty-two percent of the low-dose reached a fifty percent reduction in abscesses and inflammatory nodules; the stricter seventy-five percent threshold was met by forty-one, forty and twenty-nine percent against fifteen percent on placebo. Phase three is coming. T-L-one-A sits upstream of the T-helper-one, T-helper-two and T-helper-seventeen axes at once and is the field's cleanest shot at the inflammation-plus-fibroblast loop that produces the tunneling and scarring in this disease — which matters because the tulisokibart record has been mixed, a Phase three win in ulcerative colitis in June and a Phase two failure in scleroderma-associated lung disease in August. The indication is crowded: Incyte's oral J-A-K-one inhibitor povorcitinib has two Phase three wins, U-C-B's Bimzelx, Novartis's Cosentyx and off-patent Humira are all approved, and Avalo recently reported a Phase two win with an interleukin-one-beta antibody.

Seventh, Amgen published the data behind its Phase two win for daxdilimab in discoid lupus. Seventy-two patients, two doses. The low dose produced a six-point placebo-adjusted drop on the cutaneous lupus disease area and severity index, the high dose five point seven — and a six-point drop is roughly a halving of affected area and severity. Sixty-one point seven and sixty-two point one percent of low- and high-dose patients achieved at least a fifty percent reduction against twenty-three point seven on placebo, and sixty point three and fifty-one point six percent got to clear or almost clear on the clinician scale against eight point nine percent. No serious adverse events, no deaths. Daxdilimab targets immunoglobulin-like transcript seven to deplete plasmacytoid dendritic cells, the interferon factory. Two things make this interesting: the dose-response is flat to inverted, which is the expected shape when you are depleting a cell population and the depletion saturates; and this molecule already failed in systemic lupus under Horizon in twenty twenty-three, while Amgen's twenty-seven point eight billion dollar Horizon acquisition was being challenged by the Federal Trade Commission. Amgen says it is taking steps toward registrational development.

Eighth, on the artificial intelligence file, the most interesting thing published this week is the pushback. Anthropic announced on September twenty-third that Claude had identified an enzyme system with properties it called reminiscent of C-R-I-S-P-R — roughly nine hundred fifty agents running in parallel for twenty-one and a half hours, screening genomic databases for unusual reverse transcriptases, narrowing more than two hundred thousand candidates down to a family the company calls array-associated reverse transcriptases, found in jumbo phages, carrying a long tandem repeat array beside the enzyme. Wired canvassed the field. Fyodor Urnov at Berkeley complimented Anthropic for telling the world. Stanford's Le Cong was sharper: the experiments are still in the queue, the P-R is already live. Gladstone's Seth Shipman does not think this is a new C-R-I-S-P-R system — he reads it as plausibly a retron — but says the finding is still interesting, and his line is the one to keep: the novel thing is how they found it, not what it is. Mining novel reverse transcriptases normally takes months by hand; a day is genuinely impressive. The deflating detail is that this same reverse transcriptase was already described by Jason Gill's group at Texas A and M in a twenty twenty-one paper on jumbo phages — what is new is the repeat array around it. Gill's framing: these models are better at finding patterns than a person staring at it with their eyeballs, and a person could do all this, but you would have to already have the hypothesis in mind. One wet-lab experiment appears in the technical report, which is not peer reviewed, and nobody yet knows whether the system cuts D-N-A at all.

Ninth, and a cleaner piece of applied artificial intelligence in the same week. Ginkgo Datapoints and Apheris named the founding members of an Antibody Developability Consortium — AbbVie, argenx, Lundbeck and Takeda. Each contributes proprietary antibody sequences, the remainder comes from public sources, and the target is ten thousand antibodies characterized in one standardized wet-lab campaign. Ginkgo then trains a foundation model for developability inside Apheris's federated environment and ships it into each member's own environment for fine-tuning on private data, so members can benchmark against the full dataset without exposing their sequences to each other. Charlotte Deane at Oxford and Peter Tessier at Michigan provide independent oversight; members get the initial dataset in early twenty twenty-seven. AbbVie's Athena Hadjixenofontos made the point that matters: developability models have always been trained on convenience datasets, and this one is designed for machine learning from the start. That is the unglamorous bottleneck — not which target, but whether the molecule can be made — and note that AbbVie, which just paid ten point nine billion dollars for a half-life-extended antibody, is a founding member.

Tenth, on policy. The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services finalized the GLOBE model, which ties a portion of Medicare Part B drug payment to prices in nineteen reference countries as a mandatory five-year test starting in twenty twenty-seven. The headline is how much smaller it got. The proposed rule projected eight point four billion dollars in net savings on the Original Medicare side alone; the final rule projects four hundred forty million in overall Part B net savings across the seven-year payment period. The narrowing came from carve-outs added in response to comments — orphan-only drugs, biosimilars and their reference biologics once a biosimilar is on the U-S market, plasma-derived products, and certain cell and gene therapies. For portfolio purposes, that carve-out list is the actual news: international reference pricing now explicitly exempts the categories most of this industry is moving toward.

And eleventh, because it is genuinely novel. Varda Space Industries closed a two hundred fifty-one million dollar Series D led by Lux Capital and Natural Capital, with Founders Fund, Khosla, Caffeinated and General Catalyst participating, bringing total capital raised to five hundred ninety-eight million. The thesis is that microgravity lets you crystallize active pharmaceutical ingredients into forms gravity makes impossible on Earth — a formulation play, not a discovery play. Six reentry missions completed since twenty twenty-three, more than a dozen more planned through twenty twenty-eight. Treat it as a long-dated option that now has a real launch cadence behind it, which is more than this idea has ever had.
