Good morning, Travis. It's Tuesday, November twenty-fourth, 2026. Sanofi has agreed to buy Insmed for forty-one billion dollars in cash, the biggest biopharma acquisition in three years, and that's today's spotlight. Kymera's oral STAT-six degrader posted Phase two-b eczema data that look like an injectable biologic. Medicare published its third round of negotiated prices. The F-D-A approved two drugs ahead of schedule. And Arcus stopped its Phase three pancreatic cancer trial. Here's what you need to know.

First, the deal. Sanofi announced this morning that it will acquire Insmed for one hundred seventy-five dollars a share, all cash, about forty-one billion dollars in total. That's a seventeen and a half percent premium to Monday's close, and forty-six percent above where Insmed traded on November eighteenth, the day before Bloomberg reported the talks. Sanofi gets Brinsupri, or brensocatib, the oral D-P-P-one inhibitor that blocks activation of neutrophil proteases in bronchiectasis and has already passed a billion dollars in sales. It also gets Arikayce, the inhaled liposomal amikacin for refractory M-A-C lung disease, and T-P-I-P, an inhaled treprostinil prodrug in Phase three for pulmonary hypertension and fibrotic lung disease. Sanofi is paying with debt and cash on hand and expects to close in the first half of 2027. It says the deal adds to earnings per share by the end of 2028. Sanofi shares were up about six percent in Paris this morning. When the rumor broke last Thursday, they fell three percent. In the spotlight we look at what the price says, what the analysts think, and what it means for early-stage dealmaking.

Second, the most important clinical readout of the week for immunology. Kymera reported Phase two-b results for KT-six-twenty-one, a once-daily oral degrader of STAT-six. STAT-six is the transcription factor that relays I-L-four and I-L-thirteen signaling, the same pathway Dupixent blocks with an antibody. In BROADEN-two, two hundred forty-four patients with moderate to severe atopic dermatitis got one of three doses or placebo for sixteen weeks. All three doses met the primary and secondary endpoints. On the mid dose, eczema severity scores fell seventy-two percent, against twenty-seven percent on placebo, a placebo-adjusted difference of forty-five points. The placebo-adjusted EASI-seventy-five response was forty points, and itch improved from the first week. A third of patients had already had a biologic. Kymera says the profile is at least comparable to approved biologics. That's a cross-trial comparison, so treat it with care, but the stock closed up thirty percent at one hundred fifty dollars and thirty-seven cents. Phase three is planned by mid-2027, and asthma data are due late next year. One connection to the spotlight: this month Sanofi dropped the STAT-six degrader it had licensed from Nurix. So on the same day Sanofi spent forty-one billion dollars on life after Dupixent, a small-molecule pill aimed at Dupixent's own pathway looked like it works.

Third, drug pricing. Late Monday, C-M-S announced negotiated Medicare prices for thirteen drugs, starting January first, 2028. This is the first round to include Part B drugs given in the doctor's office. C-M-S puts the net savings at nine point two billion dollars, or forty-five percent, against 2025 spending. Read the footnote, though: once you count the Part D manufacturer discount program, the saving drops to about five and a half billion dollars, or thirty-three percent. Three of the original fifteen drugs got no negotiated price. Xeljanz and Xolair were dropped because a generic and a biosimilar reached the market, which is how the law is meant to work. Botox is out for now under a court order in AbbVie's lawsuit against H-H-S. Four companies took C-M-S's written final offer, and in five other cases C-M-S accepted the company's counteroffer. That brings the program to thirty-seven drugs.

Fourth, two approvals, both early. On Monday the F-D-A approved Roche's Wilserdly, or giredestrant, an oral selective estrogen receptor degrader, as adjuvant therapy for intermediate to high risk E-R-positive, HER-two-negative early breast cancer. It came a week ahead of the November thirtieth goal date. In the lidERA trial of more than four thousand one hundred patients, giredestrant cut the risk of invasive recurrence or death by thirty percent against tamoxifen or an aromatase inhibitor, with a hazard ratio of zero point seven zero. Overall survival is immature. Roche says it is the first new endocrine therapy in this setting in more than twenty years. It's also the first oral SERD approved in early disease, and early disease is where about ninety percent of these patients are diagnosed. That's a far bigger market than last week's second-line E-S-R-one-mutant label. Separately, BridgeBio's Welgemda, BBP-four-eighteen, was approved Monday afternoon, four days before its Friday goal date, as the first drug for limb-girdle muscular dystrophy type two-I. The disease is caused by F-K-R-P mutations that impair glycosylation of alpha-dystroglycan, which holds muscle membranes together. The oral pill feeds that glycosylation pathway. The F-D-A cited improvements in walking speed and lung function.

Fifth, a Phase three failure. Arcus stopped PRISM-one, which tested quemliclustat, its oral CD seventy-three inhibitor, plus gemcitabine and nab-paclitaxel in first-line metastatic pancreatic cancer. CD seventy-three makes immunosuppressive adenosine in tumors. At a pre-specified interim analysis, the overall survival boundary wasn't reached, and the data monitoring committee found the two arms performed about the same. The trial enrolled about six hundred fifty patients. Arcus's medical chief says both arms had the longest overall survival seen in a global first-line study, which says more about modern care than about the drug. It's another setback for adenosine-pathway immuno-oncology. It also leaves Revolution Medicines' RAS inhibitor as the clear first-line pancreatic story, a race we covered on Saturday's spotlight. Gilead had already handed this trial back to Arcus, which now relies on casdatifan, its HIF-two-alpha inhibitor in kidney cancer.

One launch to note. Sharon Mates, who sold Intra-Cellular to Johnson and Johnson for fourteen point six billion dollars, is back with Ritzey Therapeutics and a one hundred twelve and a half million dollar Series A led by Vida Ventures, with Fidelity and Alexandria. Ritzey bought French biotech Perha for an oral inhibitor of DYRK-one-A, a kinase on chromosome twenty-one that brakes pancreatic beta cell growth. It's taking the drug into Phase two for beta cell function and tau pathology in Down syndrome, and in type one and type two diabetes.
