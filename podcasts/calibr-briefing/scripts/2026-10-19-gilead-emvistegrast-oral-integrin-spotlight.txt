We're going deep on one result: Gilead's emvistegrast in ulcerative colitis, presented this morning as a late-breaker at United European Gastroenterology Week in Barcelona, and what it does and doesn't tell you about the most contested real estate in immunology.

Start with the mechanism, because the whole read depends on it. Alpha-four-beta-seven is an integrin on the surface of circulating lymphocytes. It docks onto a molecule called MAdCAM-one that is expressed almost exclusively on the blood vessels of the gut. Block that interaction and you stop gut-homing lymphocytes from leaving the bloodstream and entering the intestinal wall. You are not suppressing the immune system globally. You are redirecting traffic away from one organ. That is why this target has a reputation for being the safest validated mechanism in inflammatory bowel disease, and it's why the contrast with the older drug in the family matters: natalizumab blocks alpha-four broadly, including the alpha-four-beta-one that lets lymphocytes into the brain, and it carries a progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy risk. Gut-selective blockade does not.

The target is validated commercially as well as biologically. Takeda's vedolizumab, Entyvio, is an antibody against exactly this integrin and is one of the largest brands in inflammatory bowel disease. In the GEMINI one registration study, week-six clinical response on vedolizumab was forty-seven point one percent against twenty-five point five on placebo. It works. But it is a biologic, given by infusion or injection, with the cost structure, the infusion-center logistics and the payer friction that implies.

So the prize everyone in this field has been chasing for a decade is obvious: take the safest validated target in I B D and make it a pill. That is hard. Integrins are protein-protein interfaces, the classic undruggable shape for a small molecule, and the companies that have tried have mostly produced pharmacology without convincing efficacy.

Here is what Gilead showed. SWIFT randomized two hundred and thirty-eight patients with moderately to severely active ulcerative colitis to one of three doses of emvistegrast, twenty-five, seventy-five or two hundred milligrams once daily, or placebo, for twelve weeks. At two hundred milligrams: clinical remission thirty-one percent versus eight percent on placebo, a delta of about twenty-three points. Clinical response sixty-six versus thirty-seven. Endoscopic improvement thirty-nine versus twelve. Mucosal healing, which is the hardest of the four and the one most predictive of long-term outcome, twenty-four versus nine. On safety: adverse events in thirty-six percent on drug and thirty-seven percent on placebo. Four patients on drug, two percent, had serious adverse events, all adjudicated unrelated. Three patients out of two hundred and thirty-eight discontinued for an adverse event. No P M L.

Two things about that dataset are worth pausing on, and they point in opposite directions.

The first is that the efficacy is not just a remission number, it's the full ladder. Symptom improvement without endoscopic change is what a weak immunology drug looks like. Here the endoscopic improvement delta, twenty-eight points, is actually larger than the remission delta, and mucosal healing moved too. That's the internally consistent pattern of a drug doing something real to the bowel wall, which is exactly what you'd predict from a trafficking mechanism and exactly what you cannot get from a placebo response.

The second is that the primary endpoint registered on ClinicalTrials.gov is clinical response at week twelve, not remission. The remission rate everyone is quoting this morning is a secondary endpoint. That is not a sleight of hand, the drug hit both, and in a Phase 2 the secondary is often the more informative number. But it matters for Phase 3 design, because the FDA's expectation in ulcerative colitis is a co-primary structure with remission in it, and the gap between a response endpoint in a dose-ranging study and a remission endpoint in a registration study is where a lot of I B D programs have quietly died.

Now, the comparison everyone will make this morning, and why I think it's the wrong one. The obvious move is to line emvistegrast's thirty-one percent up against Abivax's obefazimod, which is presenting its forty-four-week maintenance data in the same building a few hours later, and whose Phase 3 induction remission rates were in the twenty to twenty-two percent range at week eight against three to six percent on placebo. Emvistegrast looks better. But look at the placebo arms: eight percent here, three to six percent there, in trials with different durations, different definitions and crucially different prior-therapy mixes. The Abivax trials deliberately enrolled a population almost half of whom had already failed an advanced therapy, with no cap on how many. Cross-trial comparison in ulcerative colitis is the single most reliable way to be wrong in this field, and the number that will determine where emvistegrast actually sits is one we don't have yet: performance in biologic-experienced patients, and the week-fifty-two maintenance data, which are still blinded and running.

The more useful comparison is to the oral alpha-four-beta-seven that got there first. Morphic Therapeutic's MORF-zero-five-seven is the same mechanism in a pill, and Lilly paid about three point two billion dollars for Morphic in twenty twenty-four largely to own it. Its Phase 2b, EMERALD-two, two hundred and eighty-odd patients with clinical remission at week twelve as the primary endpoint, has not reported. So as of this morning, Gilead has the first randomized, placebo-controlled efficacy data for an oral alpha-four-beta-seven inhibitor in ulcerative colitis, in a mechanism that Lilly has already valued at over three billion dollars at a stage with less evidence behind it. That is the competitive fact of the day. It is also the reason I'd expect Lilly's next EMERALD update to be read through this result rather than on its own terms.

On sentiment: STAT's Adam Feuerstein reported the improvement exceeded Wall Street analyst expectations, which is the relevant framing because expectations were low. The sell-side positioning going into this readout, as recently as the spring, was that an emvistegrast readout would not move the stock. That tells you how little of Gilead's inflammation ambition was in the price, and it's the setup that makes an upside surprise worth more than the data alone warrant.

Which brings me to what this is actually about for Gilead. This is a company with an extraordinary H I V franchise, a cancer business it has spent heavily to build, and an inflammation and autoimmune effort that has so far been more capital than product. Gilead booked roughly eleven point two billion dollars of acquired in-process R and D in the first half of this year alone, tied to the Arcellx, Tubulis and Ouro Medicines transactions, and ran a reported loss per share as a result. The strategic question on every Gilead call for two years has been whether the diversification spend converts into assets that read out. This is the first clean, company-originated, randomized efficacy win in the inflammation portfolio. Gilead's own framing is that emvistegrast is both a potential best-in-class oral monotherapy and a foundation for combinations, which is the language of a company that intends to build an I B D franchise rather than sell a single drug into one. Third-quarter results land on October twenty-ninth, and that call is now a different conversation than it would have been on Friday.

The honest caveats. This is a two-hundred-and-thirty-eight-patient Phase 2 with a twelve-week induction readout. The maintenance phase is where oral I B D drugs lose their shine, because the question stops being whether you can induce remission and becomes whether exposure holds and patients stay on. The absence of P M L in a twelve-week study is reassuring about the mechanism's selectivity but tells you essentially nothing about multi-year safety, and gut-selectivity is a property you have to keep proving as exposure rises. Phase 3 is guided for twenty twenty-seven, which means a registration dataset is years out, into a market that by then will also contain oral I L-twenty-three receptor peptides, anti-T L one A antibodies now reading out of Phase 3, and combination regimens from companies like Spyre that are explicitly designed to raise the ceiling above any monotherapy.

Here's the read I'd take into the week. Ulcerative colitis is becoming a market where the differentiating axis is no longer potency but route and tolerability, because the efficacy ceiling for every monotherapy class is converging in the same band and nobody has broken it. In that world, a once-daily pill against the single mechanism with the cleanest safety reputation in the field is strategically valuable out of proportion to its delta over the next drug. Gilead just produced the first randomized evidence that the pill version of that mechanism works. The thing to watch is not this morning's remission rate. It is the week-fifty-two maintenance data and the biologic-experienced subgroup, because those are what determine whether this is a first-line oral franchise or a nice Phase 2.
