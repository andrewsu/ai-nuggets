Travis, today's spotlight is Replimune, because yesterday's approval is the payoff of the single most-watched regulatory drama of the summer — and it's a bigger story than one melanoma drug.

Here's what happened. On August sixth, the FDA granted accelerated approval to Tudriqev — the drug we've been calling R-P-one, generic name vusolimogene oderparepvec — in combination with Bristol Myers Squibb's nivolumab, for adults with unresectable advanced cutaneous melanoma whose disease has progressed on a P-D-one-based regimen. That's a hard-to-treat population: patients who have already failed checkpoint immunotherapy and are largely out of good options. The company put the list price at four hundred fifty thousand dollars per treatment course, and launched a patient-access program alongside it.

What makes this remarkable is how it got here. This was the third time Replimune brought R-P-one to the agency. It was rejected twice, and just weeks ago the FDA's own reviewers published briefing documents that called the pivotal trial data essentially uninterpretable and questioned whether the drug delivered any benefit at all. Then, on July thirtieth, the cell and gene therapy advisory committee voted ten to three that the data were, in fact, evaluable and clinically meaningful. Yesterday the agency followed the panel. So an approval landed on data that career reviewers had, in writing, tried to talk the agency out of.

Let me explain what the drug actually is, because the mechanism is the whole reason it's interesting. Tudriqev is an oncolytic virus — a genetically engineered herpes simplex virus type one. Two viral genes are deleted so it can't cause disease and can't shut down the immune system's alarm signals, and it's armed with two payloads: a fusogenic glycoprotein that makes infected tumor cells fuse and rupture, and human G-M-C-S-F, an immune-stimulating cytokine. You inject it directly into a tumor. It replicates inside cancer cells, bursts them open, and that burst dumps tumor antigens into a suddenly inflamed, immune-visible environment. Paired with nivolumab releasing the brakes on T cells, the idea is that you turn a tumor the immune system was ignoring into one it attacks — and that the attack spreads to tumors you never injected.

That last part — the uninjected tumors — is the crux of the entire scientific argument, and it's why the FDA's skepticism was legitimate rather than bureaucratic. The pivotal IGNYTE trial was single-arm: everyone got R-P-one plus nivolumab, with no control group getting nivolumab alone. So when patients responded, you genuinely could not statistically separate what the virus contributed from what the checkpoint antibody was already doing. This is the contribution-of-components problem, and it's the exact issue that sank the drug twice. The way the company and the panel answered it: look at responses in lesions that were never injected, in patients whose disease had already progressed on a P-D-one drug. The approved label rests on ninety-one such patients — a response rate of about twenty-four percent, with responses lasting a median of just over fourteen months. Durable shrinkage in tumors you didn't touch, in people the checkpoint drug had already failed, is the closest thing to evidence that the virus is doing systemic work.

Now, why this matters beyond melanoma — three reasons.

First, this is only the second oncolytic virus ever approved in the United States. The first was Amgen's Imlygic, back in twenty fifteen — also an engineered herpes virus armed with G-M-C-S-F, also for melanoma. And Imlygic is a cautionary tale: it was approved and then essentially never scaled, a commercial afterthought, because intratumoral injection is logistically clunky and, as a near-monotherapy, its benefit was modest. So the field has spent a decade wondering whether oncolytic viruses were a dead end or just waiting for the right combination. Tudriqev is the test case for the second interpretation — a virus paired with a checkpoint inhibitor, aimed at the large post-P-D-one population where the medical need is real. Whether it scales where Imlygic didn't is the commercial question that every company still working on intratumoral immunotherapy is now watching.

Second, this is a genuine read on the FDA's evidence bar in the current era. There was a real fear across biotech that after the departure of Vinay Prasad, the vocal skeptic of single-arm accelerated approvals, the agency's career staff would hold an even harder line — and the R-P-one briefing documents looked like exactly that line being drawn. Instead, the panel and then the agency accepted single-arm data over staff objection, on an argument built from a clever secondary analysis and a large unmet need. The signal for your portfolio: accelerated approval on single-arm data is still alive, but the price of admission is rising. You need a mechanistic story that answers the contribution question, not just a headline response rate — and you need a confirmatory trial already running. Here that's IGNYTE-3, a randomized phase three that is the safety valve on the whole approval. If it misses, this comes back off the market.

Third, the read-through to the checkpoint franchise itself. This approval extends the commercial life of nivolumab into a new combination setting at the moment Bristol Myers is managing the eventual loss of exclusivity on that franchise. Every approved combination that keeps a checkpoint antibody central to care is worth something to the incumbent — and it's a reminder that a lot of next-wave immuno-oncology value is being created by partners bolting novel mechanisms onto the existing P-D-one backbone rather than trying to replace it.

So the bottom line for you. On the narrow view, a small oncology company just turned a twice-rejected drug into a marketed product with a four-hundred-fifty-thousand-dollar price tag — a striking corporate turnaround. On the wider view, this is the moment the oncolytic-virus modality gets its second, and much fairer, shot at proving it belongs in the immuno-oncology toolkit, and it's a concrete data point that the accelerated-approval pathway still bends toward unmet need when the mechanistic story is coherent. The thing to watch now isn't the launch — it's IGNYTE-3, because a randomized confirmatory readout is what will finally settle the contribution question that two rejections and one approval never fully could.
