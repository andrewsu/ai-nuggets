Good morning Travis. Here are your biotech and pharma headlines for Tuesday, August twenty-fifth, twenty twenty-six.

The tape broke wide open yesterday after three days of nothing. A two-point-three-billion-dollar obesity deal on a mechanism almost nobody is working on, a first-ever approval in a disease that has never had one, a reprieve for Capricor, a clinical hold that wiped out a fifth of a gene therapy company, and a ruling that effectively ends the industry's constitutional campaign against Medicare price negotiation. Nine items, then the week ahead.

First, and today's spotlight. Roche, through Genentech, licensed Hanmi Pharmaceutical's H-M seventeen three twenty-one for a hundred ninety million dollars up front and up to two point three billion dollars in total, plus tiered royalties, taking worldwide rights outside South Korea. The asset is a urocortin-two analog — a peptide agonist of the C-R-F-two receptor, which is the second corticotropin-releasing-factor receptor and is expressed on skeletal muscle. It is not an incretin. It does not work through appetite. The claim, from mouse and rhesus monkey work, is that it strips fat mass while actually adding lean mass, and that it stacks on top of semaglutide. Hanmi finishes the phase one, which is enrolling about ninety subjects, and Genentech takes over from phase two forward. Genentech's Boris Zaitra framed it as selectively reducing fat while improving muscle. What you should notice is the price: a hundred ninety million dollars up front, roughly eight percent of the headline number, for an asset with no human efficacy data at all. We take that apart in the spotlight — why Roche is buying body composition rather than weight, why this is a fundamentally different bet than the myostatin blockers, and the one number in the phase one that decides the whole thing, which is heart rate.

Second, a first-ever approval. The F-D-A approved Johnson and Johnson's Imaavy, nipocalimab, for warm autoimmune hemolytic anemia in adults and adolescents twelve and up who are on or have been on corticosteroids. Nipocalimab is an F-c-R-n blocker — it binds the neonatal Fc receptor, which is the recycling receptor that keeps immunoglobulin G in circulation, so blocking it accelerates the clearance of pathogenic auto-antibodies while leaving B cells alone. In warm A-I-H-A those antibodies coat red blood cells and mark them for destruction, so this is about as clean a mechanistic fit as the class has. The registrational trial, ENERGY, randomized a hundred fifteen adults across two dose schedules and placebo, and at the approved dose roughly three times as many patients hit a durable hemoglobin response — hemoglobin at ten grams per deciliter or above, with a two-gram rise from baseline, held for at least twenty-eight days without rescue. Median time to first response was four point one weeks against twelve point one on placebo. This is the second indication for nipocalimab after generalized myasthenia gravis in April of last year, and it is worth connecting to our argenx episode last week: the F-c-R-n class is now doing exactly what the thesis said it would, which is colonizing one I-g-G-driven disease after another. The competitive wrinkle is that survey work from June had hematologists more excited about Sanofi's rilzabrutinib, an oral B-T-K inhibitor with breakthrough designation here, than about nipocalimab. First approval versus oral convenience is the fight, and J and J just won the first half of it.

Third, Capricor. The silence I flagged Sunday and again yesterday broke. The F-D-A granted a three-month extension, moving the action date on deramiocel to November twenty-second, and the reason is that Capricor amended the B-L-A after that nine-to-three adjudicated defeat at the July advisory committee. The amendment does two things: it refocuses the proposed indication on upper limb function — which was the trial's original primary endpoint — instead of the cardiac secondaries that the panel spent its day attacking, and it adds twenty-four-month open-label extension data from HOPE-three. Linda Marban is calling it one of the most extensive upper-limb datasets in Duchenne. The stock rose as much as sixteen percent. My read is unchanged from yesterday's spotlight: this is the company retreating to the claim it actually pre-specified, which is the correct move and also an admission of what went wrong. Three months is not a lot of time to change nine minds.

Fourth, and this is the day's real casualty. Regenxbio was hit with a clinical hold on R-G-X one twenty-one, its A-A-V gene therapy for Hunter syndrome, mucopolysaccharidosis type two. An expanded M-R-I monitoring program found asymptomatic nodules or cystic masses in the spines of five of forty-eight patients from the CAMPSIITE registrational program, three to six years after intracisternal or intraventricular dosing. All five are reportedly doing fine clinically and stable or improving on neurocognitive testing, investigators called the findings nonserious and likely benign, and there is no pathology. But the company will not resubmit the B-L-A in the near term, which kills a third-quarter filing that the agency itself had green-lit in June after reversing a February complete response letter. Shares fell twenty-two percent premarket. The context that matters: in January, Regenxbio's other lysosomal program, R-G-X one eleven for Hurler syndrome, went on hold after a tumor in which genetic analysis showed A-A-V vector integration into an oncogene. Curran Simpson says these findings are unique and limited to R-G-X one twenty-one. Two holds in seven months, both involving masses, both in C-N-S-delivered A-A-V, is a pattern the field will read whether or not the company likes it. And note the meta-point for anyone tracking F-D-A posture: this is the same agency that reversed a rejection three months ago. Flexibility on the efficacy bar has not translated into flexibility on integration risk.

Fifth, the policy item that closes a three-year chapter. Judge Colleen Kollar-Kotelly of the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia rejected Merck's challenge to the Inflation Reduction Act's Medicare drug price negotiation program. She threw out both theories. The First Amendment claim — that the program compels manufacturers to publicly endorse a price they did not agree to — failed. The Fifth Amendment takings claim failed on the ground that participation in Medicare and Medicaid is voluntary, so the government is not seizing anything. That is now the near-uniform answer across every district and appellate court that has taken these cases. Merck filed in June of twenty twenty-three, and this is the last of the big constitutional swings. Practically: the negotiation program is a fact of life, and the industry's leverage has moved entirely to the legislative and administrative side.

Sixth, two bispecific items that sit close to your bench. Antengene got clearance from Australia's regulator for the phase one ATTRACT study of A-T-G two-oh-one, a C-D nineteen by C-D three T-cell engager aimed at B-cell-driven autoimmune disease rather than cancer, following China's N-M-P-A in June. The engineering is the interesting part: steric-hindrance masking to blunt off-tumor activation, a two-plus-one bivalent format for low-density targets, and C-D three binders with deliberately fast on-and-off kinetics to hold down cytokine release. U-C-B holds worldwide commercial rights. Separately, Astellas dosed the first patient in a phase three of A-S-P twenty-one thirty-eight, a claudin-eighteen-point-two by C-D three bispecific, in claudin-positive, HER2-negative gastric and gastroesophageal cancer. Between them these are the two live questions in the engager field — can you tune an engager gently enough to use it in autoimmunity, and can a C-D three engager work in a solid tumor with a real epithelial target.

Seventh, a quiet safety signal. Boehringer Ingelheim put voluntary hold on enrollment in a Japanese phase one of B-I three-oh-three-one-one-eight-five, an oral candidate in psychiatry, after an undisclosed safety event. The suspension surfaced through a clinical trials database update on August nineteenth rather than an announcement. The compound had already run two other phase ones this year, including one in borderline personality disorder and A-D-H-D. No mechanism disclosed, and Boehringer has three undisclosed phase one psychiatry programs. Low information, but voluntary holds in healthy-volunteer studies are worth logging.

Eighth, a real dataset in narcolepsy. The Lancet Neurology published Alkermes' Vibrance-one, a six-week phase two of alixorexton, an oral selective orexin-two receptor agonist, in ninety-two adults with narcolepsy type one. All three doses pushed mean sleep latency on the maintenance of wakefulness test to twenty minutes or more, which is the normal range, and Epworth scores to ten or below, also normal. The six-milligram arm cut weekly cataplexy significantly. No serious adverse events. The reason this matters beyond narcolepsy: orexin-two agonism is the first genuinely new wake-promoting mechanism in decades, and it replaces a missing neuropeptide rather than stimulating downstream. The phase three Brilliance program is running in both type one and type two.

Ninth, a supply-chain argument worth filing. Curium, fresh off its eight-billion-dollar merger with Lantheus, is positioning its lutetium-one-seventy-seven P-S-M-A-I-and-T against Novartis's Pluvicto, which did one point three billion dollars in the first half, up fifty percent. Curium met its phase three primary endpoint back in twenty twenty-four and is now working out filing timing with the agency. The pitch is not efficacy, it is isotope. Lutetium-one-seventy-seven has a six point seven day half-life, so you cannot stockpile it, and the University of Missouri research reactor is the only domestic producer. Michael Patterson's line is that controlling your own isotope production is what lets you flex to the market. Pluvicto's twenty twenty-three shortage is the proof point. In radiopharmaceuticals, manufacturing is the moat.
