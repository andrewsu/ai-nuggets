Good morning Travis. This is your spotlight for Friday, October sixteenth. Today it's AstraZeneca's new deal with insitro. It's a portfolio story too: Foresite Capital has been an insitro investor since at least the Series B in twenty twenty and is still on the company's investor list this week. It's also a clean test of a question we keep asking in A-I drug discovery: what is a big pharma actually willing to pay for, and what has the platform proven so far?

Start with the terms, from both companies' releases. AstraZeneca and insitro signed a drug discovery collaboration and licensing agreement in two areas: metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis, which is MASH, and cardiomyopathies. insitro gets forty million dollars up front and is eligible for up to about one point four six billion dollars in milestones. insitro's release calls them discovery, development and commercial milestones. On top of that come single-digit royalties. The work splits cleanly. insitro uses its platform to find causal disease biology in both indications, then takes targets that AstraZeneca nominates from discovery to a development candidate. From there AstraZeneca runs all the clinical work, the regulatory work and the commercialization, with exclusive worldwide rights to the programs it picks. Neither company disclosed how many targets are involved, which cardiomyopathies are in scope, or any timelines.

The platform is what insitro calls the Virtual Human. In its own words, it's a "genetically anchored A-I engine" built on what it calls the world's largest integrated multi-modal collection of human and cellular data, gathered since twenty eighteen. Earlier releases describe the pieces. One is U-K Biobank imaging, clinical and genetic data, with machine-learned traits derived from the images. Another is induced pluripotent stem cell lines from patients and engineered donors. The third is high-content imaging and pooled optical CRISPR screens. The idea is that machine learning on human data lets you define a disease trait more precisely than a clinical label does. Then genetics tells you which genes cause that trait, rather than just travel with it. insitro's release spells out why these two diseases fit: "a single diagnostic label spans patients whose underlying biology differs." That describes MASH and the cardiomyopathies well. Patients get grouped by what the liver biopsy or the echocardiogram shows, and underneath sit several different disease drivers.

Here's why this matters. AstraZeneca isn't paying for molecule design here, at least not mainly. It's paying for target selection. Barr said in both releases that "one of the most critical decisions in R-and-D is selecting the right target." Daphne Koller made a related point to Bloomberg, which was quoted in translation. She said most A-I drug discovery companies focus on what happens after a target has been picked. That sets insitro apart from yesterday's spotlight company, Iambic, whose pitch is chemistry against known targets like H-E-R-two. It also sets it apart from the protein-design deals of the past week, like Generate with Zealand. insitro is selling the step before all of that.

Look at AstraZeneca's own pipeline in these two areas, and the fit makes sense. In MASH, its Ionis-partnered drug A-Z-D-twenty-six-ninety-three was discontinued after Phase two last November. What's left in the clinic is the Phase one antifibrotic A-Z-D-twenty-three-eighty-nine. In cardiomyopathy, it just took a hit. In July, the CARDIO-TTRansform Phase three of Wainua, the eplontersen gene silencer it shares with Ionis, missed its primary endpoint of cardiovascular death and recurrent cardiovascular events in transthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy. Most patients were already on a stabilizer like tafamidis. Those patients got no benefit from adding Wainua. Only the monotherapy subgroup showed a nominally significant effect. The other cardiomyopathy program is A-Z-D-forty-sixty-three, in Phase one for a genetic dilated cardiomyopathy caused by the R-fourteen deletion in phospholamban. So AstraZeneca has a thin MASH pipeline and a cardiomyopathy franchise that just lost its main growth bet. In both, it's paying for new biology rather than another shot at known targets.

Now the price. Forty million dollars up front is less than three percent of the headline value, as PharmExec pointed out. That's normal for discovery-stage work. Insitro's own twenty twenty Bristol Myers deal in A-L-S paid fifty million up front. This week Zealand paid Generate about thirty million up front on a deal of about one point two billion. So the market price for an A-I discovery partnership at a big pharma is in the tens of millions up front, with the real value in milestones that only pay out if the biology works. AstraZeneca has also been one of the busiest buyers in this category. Last year it signed C-S-P-C for A-I-designed oral small molecules, worth up to five point three billion dollars, and Algen for immunology, worth up to five hundred fifty-five million. insitro adds target discovery to a collection that already covers chemistry and immunology.

Here's the uncomfortable part of the analysis. Has any target from an insitro pharma partnership reached patients? As far as I can find, no. Its first pharma deal, in twenty nineteen, was with Gilead, and it was also in what was then called NASH, the same disease now renamed MASH. That deal paid fifteen million dollars up front, with Fierce putting the headline value at about one point oh five billion. insitro said in twenty twenty-one that it had received the first operational milestone. After that, I found no public record of a Gilead target moving forward, or of how the deal ended. The Bristol Myers deal in A-L-S and frontotemporal dementia has done better. Bristol Myers nominated a first target, A-L-S-one, in December twenty twenty-four, worth twenty-five million dollars in milestones. In March of this year it added A-L-S-two and A-L-S-three. All three are still preclinical. insitro is also running its own oligonucleotide program on A-L-S-one while doing small-molecule work on the same target for Bristol. A twenty twenty-four set of agreements with Lilly gave insitro an option on Lilly's GalNAc liver-delivery technology for its own siRNAs. ClinicalTrials-dot-gov lists no insitro studies.

insitro's own clinical timeline has slipped too. Its lead internal program is C-T-R-O-ten-thirteen, a liver-targeted siRNA against I-R-S-one, insulin receptor substrate one. insitro calls I-R-S-one the strongest of four hundred eighty genetic signals for liver fat. At the A-D-A meeting in June, it showed mouse data where silencing I-R-S-one lowered the fibrosis and liver-injury markers TIMP-one and C-K-eighteen. The company said that held up partly independently of the drop in liver fat, and that the program was heading "toward first-in-human trials this year." This week's release says its liver programs enter the clinic "in early twenty twenty-seven." That's a slip of a quarter or two, not a crisis. But it matters given the history. In May twenty twenty-five insitro cut twenty-two percent of its staff, about sixty-five people, to stretch its cash runway into twenty twenty-seven, and said the cut ensured it would be ready for the clinic in twenty twenty-six. In September it hired Hideo Makimura as chief medical officer to lead its move to a clinical-stage company. He previously led a GalNAc siRNA against P-N-P-L-A-three, another genetic liver-fat target, through Phase one at Johnson and Johnson.

That raises a question the releases don't answer: how does an AstraZeneca deal in MASH sit alongside insitro's own MASH program? The structure suggests the answer. AstraZeneca nominates targets and gets exclusive rights to the programs it selects. So I-R-S-one and insitro's other liver-fat regulators presumably stay outside the deal, which would mean insitro is licensing its discovery engine while keeping its best-known output. If that's right, it's the arrangement you'd want as an investor: non-dilutive cash and a validation signal from a top-five pharma, without giving up the lead asset. I couldn't confirm the carve-out from the public documents. It's the first thing I'd ask management.

Now the Foresite read. Foresite Capital was already an insitro investor when the twenty twenty Series B was announced, and it put money in again in the four-hundred-million-dollar Series C in twenty twenty-one. insitro's boilerplate this week still lists Foresite among the backers of its more than seven hundred fifty million dollars in capital. I found no Foresite Labs involvement. For a company that last raised equity five years ago, cut staff to reach twenty twenty-seven, and is about to start paying for clinical trials, forty million dollars of non-dilutive cash plus a name-brand partner improves its options. It could raise privately again, or try the I-P-O window that Iambic just tested. Yesterday's lesson applies here as well. Public investors priced Iambic as a clinical-stage H-E-R-two company, not as a platform. insitro's equity story will turn on C-T-R-O-ten-thirteen getting into people and showing it lowers liver fat and fibrosis markers, much more than on how many pharma deals it signs.

For the field, there are two takeaways. First, big pharma's appetite for A-I discovery is spreading upstream, from designing molecules to picking targets. That's the step where most drugs actually fail, and where human genetics has the best track record. Second, the deal terms show that pharma isn't yet paying up front for the target-discovery claim. Forty million dollars is an option payment. The value of this deal, and of the category, depends on whether a Virtual Human target reaches a development candidate and then the clinic. Seven years into insitro's partnership history, none has done that publicly yet.

What to watch: whether either company says how many targets are in scope, and whether the first AstraZeneca-nominated MASH target shows up in insitro's updates. Koller told Bloomberg the MASH work is already quite advanced, while cardiovascular will take longer. Watch the first-in-human start for C-T-R-O-ten-thirteen in early twenty twenty-seven, and whether insitro raises money or files to go public on the back of this deal. That's your spotlight.
