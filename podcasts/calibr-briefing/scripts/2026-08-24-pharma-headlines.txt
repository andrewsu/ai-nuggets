Good morning Travis. Here are your biotech and pharma headlines for Monday, August twenty-fourth, twenty twenty-six.

Same caveat as yesterday, and then it breaks. The trade press published essentially nothing over the weekend — one analysis piece this morning and a handful of Friday and Saturday wire releases. But one of those Friday releases is genuinely the most interesting financing structure I have seen this year, and it closes today. Six items, then the week ahead.

First, and today's spotlight. Gossamer Bio, of San Diego, trading as G-O-S-S, announced a private placement of up to two hundred fifty million dollars, structured in three pieces. Twenty-five million funds at an initial closing that is expected to happen today. A further hundred twenty-five million is committed but does not fund until the FDA accepts the seralutinib new drug application for filing in pulmonary arterial hypertension — and the investors' obligation lapses if that acceptance does not happen inside calendar twenty twenty-six. Then a third tranche, up to a hundred million more, comes only through warrants that become exercisable if and when the FDA actually approves the drug. The buyers are EcoR-one Capital, six-eighty-three Capital Partners, RA Capital Management, Coastlands Capital, Samsara BioCapital and Rock Springs Capital, with Leerink and Cantor placing. Seralutinib is an inhaled dry-powder kinase inhibitor hitting P-D-G-F-R, C-S-F-one-R and c-KIT — a reverse-remodeling drug rather than a vasodilator. And the reason this structure exists is that the phase three trial, PROSERA, missed its primary endpoint in February: a placebo-adjusted thirteen point three meters on six-minute walk distance with a p-value of zero point zero three two, against a pre-specified alpha of zero point zero two five. Gossamer is filing anyway, in September, and the FDA told them at a pre-N-D-A meeting in June that the significance level and the size of the effect are review issues, not filing issues. The company had fifty-seven million dollars at the end of June and a market capitalization around eighty-five million in early August, and the initial tranche is priced at a common-stock-equivalent of fourteen cents a share. So what you are looking at is a syndicate of specialists writing a call option on a regulatory decision instead of writing a check on a drug. That is the spotlight, and it makes an instructive pair with yesterday's Abcuro episode — two companies, two narrow misses, two completely different answers.

Second, and this one is a real regulatory event that got almost no coverage because it happened on a Sunday. Scholar Rock said it has removed Catalent Indiana as the commercial fill-finish site from its apitegromab application in the United States, on FDA guidance, and moved to an alternate domestic facility. Apitegromab is a fully human antibody that inhibits myostatin activation by binding the pro- and latent forms in skeletal muscle, and it is for spinal muscular atrophy — an add-on to the survival-motor-neuron drugs, aimed at the muscle rather than the motor neuron. Catalent Indiana, which is now part of Novo Nordisk, drew an Official Action Indicated classification from an April inspection, and the consequence is that Scholar Rock pulled its European marketing authorization application by written procedure on August twentieth so it can resubmit with the new site rather than sit in a deficiency loop. The FDA review is proceeding to a September thirtieth action date, Japan's regulator has confirmed no additional clinical studies are needed with a filing due by year-end, and chief executive David Hallal is still guiding to a U.S. launch this quarter. Note the shape of that: a European withdrawal that is arguably good news, because it is a self-inflicted delay chosen over a rejection. And note that this is the third contract-manufacturing story on this show in a week, after Xspray's fourth complete response letter on its Italian site. When your molecule is fine and your filling site is not, you lose quarters.

Third, the FDA reliability question, which is the one piece published this morning. BioSpace ran an analysis putting Capricor and Replimune side by side as the two tests of whether the agency has stabilized after Marty Makary's tenure. Replimune got accelerated approval in melanoma after two prior rejections, off an advisory committee that voted ten to three in favor even as the agency's own briefing document called the submission not interpretable and one panelist called the data messy. Capricor went the other way — nine to three against on July twenty-ninth, and its August twenty-second action date has now passed with no announcement from either the company or the agency, which is consistent with the extension management flagged on August thirteenth. Mizuho's Uy Ear reads it as decisions remaining highly data-driven, and his advice to management teams is to minimize the areas open to interpretation and align with the agency in advance. Donald Fink, formerly of the FDA, adds the caution that a good meeting is not an approval. I would put a sharper point on it, and it is the through-line of today's spotlight: the agency will let you make a small claim, but only the claim you wrote down before you saw the data. The Capricor file's central problem is that its final statistical analysis plan is dated one day before unblinding and, per the securities complaint now pending in the Southern District of California, was never agreed with the FDA before the resubmission. That is not a story about how much slowing counts as enough. It is a story about provenance.

Fourth, a new modality reaching the clinic. Orum Therapeutics, split between Daejeon and Lexington, cleared an investigational new drug application for O-R-M eleven fifty-three, which they call a degrader-antibody conjugate. Take an antibody against C-D one-twenty-three, which is on acute myeloid leukemia blasts and leukemic stem cells, and instead of a cytotoxic warhead, hang a degrader of G-S-P-T-one on it — the translation termination factor whose loss is the actual killing mechanism behind the CELMoD class. So it is an A-D-C architecture delivering targeted protein degradation, which is a real attempt to buy the potency of a degrader with the therapeutic index of an antibody. First-in-human starts by year end, roughly forty-two patients initially at U.S. sites, in relapsed or refractory A-M-L, and the preclinical package claims activity in primary patient samples including T-P-fifty-three-relevant models. Worth watching mostly because C-D one-twenty-three has a rough history — the toxin conjugate in that lane carries a capillary-leak boxed warning — and whether a degrader payload changes that is the entire question.

Fifth, on the biosimilar side, Alvotech signed a licensing and commercialization agreement with Lotus Pharmaceutical worth up to about a hundred fifty million dollars in upfront and milestones, plus supply revenue, covering proposed biosimilars to durvalumab — that is AstraZeneca's anti-P-D-L-one antibody Imfinzi, six point one billion dollars in global sales last year — and to emicizumab, Roche's factor nine-a-slash-factor-ten bispecific Hemlibra, at roughly five point eight billion. The structure is the news. In the United States the agreement is semi-exclusive, meaning both companies can commercialize, and chief executive Lisa Graver's line is that for the first time Alvotech gets to participate directly in U.S. commercialization rather than being purely a development-and-supply engine. Lotus takes exclusive rights across eight Asian markets. A developer trying to move down the value chain into its own end market is the same pattern as Gossamer taking its rights back from Chiesi, just at the other end of the risk spectrum.

Sixth, a quick one from China. Antengene reported interim results and, per its own release, reached profitability for the first time. Small company, but the milestone matters as a data point on whether the Chinese biotech cohort that licensed in Western assets — Antengene commercializes the X-P-O-one inhibitor selinexor across Asia-Pacific — can get to self-funding rather than perpetual capital raises. File it, do not act on it.

Now the week ahead, and it is dense. Tomorrow, Tuesday the twenty-fifth, is the action date for Jazz's Ziihera, zanidatamab, a bispecific antibody engaging two separate HER2 epitopes, in first-line HER2-positive gastric and gastroesophageal adenocarcinoma. That application rests on HERIZON-G-E-A-oh-one, where zanidatamab regimens took median progression-free survival to twelve point four months against eight point one for trastuzumab plus chemotherapy, with a hazard ratio around zero point six four, and median overall survival of twenty-six point four months in the arm that added the P-D-one inhibitor tislelizumab. Thursday the twenty-seventh is Gilead's once-daily single tablet pairing bictegravir, an integrase inhibitor, with lenacapavir, the first-in-class capsid inhibitor, in virologically suppressed adults. Friday the twenty-eighth the European Society of Cardiology opens in Munich with fifty-nine trials across twelve Hot Line sessions. And Capricor is still silent.
