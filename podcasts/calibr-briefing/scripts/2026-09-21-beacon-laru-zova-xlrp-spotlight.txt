This is your spotlight for Monday, September twenty-first. This morning Beacon Therapeutics reported positive topline data from VISTA, the pivotal trial of laru-zova in X-linked retinitis pigmentosa. It is the first pivotal study in that disease ever to hit its primary endpoint with statistical significance. Johnson and Johnson failed here last year. Biogen failed here in twenty twenty-one, after paying eight hundred seventy-seven million dollars for the company that owned the asset. The drug that won was the one nobody wanted — a program that came off a failing Nasdaq microcap in a tender offer. So the question worth your time is not whether gene therapy works in this disease. It is why three groups aiming at the same gene, with broadly comparable biology, got three different verdicts. The answer is almost entirely about how they chose to measure vision.

Start with the disease and the target. X-linked retinitis pigmentosa is caused by mutations in R-P-G-R, retinitis pigmentosa G-T-Pase regulator, a gene that lives in the connecting cilium of photoreceptors and is required for trafficking proteins out to the outer segment. Lose it and photoreceptors die progressively from the periphery inward. It affects roughly one in twenty-five thousand males in the United States, Europe and Australia, it is about ten percent of the hundred thousand Americans with retinitis pigmentosa, symptoms begin in childhood with night blindness, and it ends in blindness. There is no approved treatment.

The gene has a specific technical curse, and it matters for this story. The disease-relevant mutations cluster in an exon called O-R-F fifteen, which is a long purine-rich repeat. That sequence is chemically unstable in the plasmids used to make viral vectors — it recombines and truncates during cloning, so you cannot reliably manufacture the full-length native gene. The field's standard answer, published back in twenty seventeen, was to codon-optimize the sequence: rewrite the D-N-A to encode the same protein with different, more stable nucleotides. Both of the programs that failed took that route. Beacon's asset did not. Laru-zova carries a stable full-length native R-P-G-R O-R-F fifteen, driven by a rhodopsin-kinase promoter to restrict expression to photoreceptors, packaged in a tyrosine-mutant A-A-V two capsid and delivered by subretinal injection. Whether that construct choice is the reason it worked is genuinely unresolved, and I would not build a thesis on it. It does mean the three programs were not interchangeable.

Now the endpoints, which is where this gets instructive. Biogen's XIRIUS trial, with cotoretigene, chose a microperimetry primary: the proportion of treated eyes gaining at least seven decibels of retinal sensitivity at five or more of the sixteen central loci on a ten-two grid. Biologically defensible, and it missed. Johnson and Johnson's LUMEOS trial, with bota-vec, chose a vision-guided mobility assessment — patients navigating a maze under reduced lighting. It missed too. But look at what LUMEOS reported on its secondary endpoints: retinal sensitivity improved significantly across all measures, forty-five percent of treated patients gained more than ten letters in low-light conditions, and twenty percent gained more than fifteen. That is a drug doing something. It failed on an endpoint that could not see it.

Beacon's VISTA chose, as its F-D-A-endorsed primary, the proportion of patients gaining at least fifteen letters of low-luminance visual acuity at month twelve against an untreated control. Eighty-five men and boys, twelve to forty-eight, randomized across high dose, low dose, and no treatment. Thirty-one percent of high-dose patients responded, with a p value of zero point zero zero one nine. Twenty-four percent of low-dose patients responded, at zero point zero one zero six. And the control group produced zero responders. Not few — zero.

Hold those two numbers next to each other. Johnson and Johnson saw twenty percent of treated patients gain more than fifteen letters in low light and called the trial a failure. Beacon saw thirty-one percent gain at least fifteen letters and has an approvable dataset. The effect sizes are in the same neighborhood. What differed was which measurement the statistical analysis plan was pointed at.

The load-bearing element is that zero in the control arm. X-linked retinitis pigmentosa is relentlessly and monotonically progressive; nobody spontaneously gains three lines of low-light acuity. That gives a responder analysis enormous leverage in a small trial, because the null hypothesis is not "no difference," it is effectively "nothing ever improves on its own." This is the same structural insight that carried Ultragenyx's Sanfilippo approval across the line last Thursday, and it is worth generalizing: in a disease with a deterministic downhill natural history, a responder threshold on a patient-relevant function is a far more powerful design than a mean-change comparison, and dramatically more powerful than a composite task like maze navigation, where a patient can fail for reasons that have nothing to do with photoreceptors.

Three caveats before you treat this as clean. First, the control was untreated, not sham-surgery. Patients knew which arm they were in, and low-luminance visual acuity is a psychophysical test — the patient reads letters. The protection here is masking at the level of the assessors, not the participants, and that is a weaker shield than a sham-controlled design. Second, the dose response is not monotonic. High dose won on the fifteen-letter threshold, but low dose beat it on the ten-letter threshold, fifty-eight point six percent to forty-eight point three, and low dose was the arm that reached significance on microperimetry, one point three one decibels against one point two zero for high dose, which landed just outside significance. Third, and following from that: microperimetry, the endpoint Biogen built its trial on, was marginal here. This dataset does not overturn the earlier failures so much as sidestep them.

Safety looks manageable and mostly surgical. Ocular adverse events were predominantly mild to moderate and attributed to the subretinal injection procedure. Drug-related treatment-emergent events ran twenty-five percent in the high-dose arm and thirty-eight percent in the low-dose arm. There were two ocular serious adverse events, both in the low-dose group, both procedure-related. Beacon has now dosed a hundred ten participants across five years of clinical experience including the HORIZON and SKYLINE and DAWN studies, which matters for a rolling filing.

On the people involved, Lance Baldo, Beacon's chief executive, called the results a landmark moment for patients who currently have no treatment options. The line I would pay attention to came from Robert Sisk, the investigator at Cincinnati Eye Institute, who said Beacon selected endpoints that would best capture improvements that matter to patients, particularly their ability to see in low-light conditions. Read that as what it is: the company saying out loud that endpoint selection was the strategy. Jason Menzo at the Foundation Fighting Blindness said this is the news the community has been waiting years to hear.

Now the competitive and financial picture, because the money story here is as interesting as the science. This asset was A-G-T-C five-oh-one, owned by Applied Genetic Technologies, a public company that could not finance it. Syncona took A-G-T-C private by tender offer in December twenty twenty-two, putting up ninety-six million pounds, about a hundred twenty million dollars, alongside Oxford Science Enterprises, and relaunched the program inside a new company, Beacon, in twenty twenty-three. Forbion led a hundred seventy million dollar Series B in July twenty twenty-four, with T-C-G-X and Advent Life Sciences joining; a Series C of over seventy-five million followed, bringing in Goldman Sachs Alternatives. Syncona now holds thirty-eight point four percent of Beacon, carried at one hundred eighty-three point four million pounds as of the end of June. Chris Hollowood, Syncona's chief executive, called the result a powerful validation of the decision to acquire the program. That is not boilerplate — Syncona spent this year fighting off a shareholder push to wind the vehicle down. Matthew Read at QuotedData called it a welcome result with clear scope for a meaningful net asset value uplift, and flagged the strategic vindication explicitly. Beacon's own framing is a rolling biologics license application starting later this year, with full data at the American Academy of Ophthalmology retina subspecialty day on October tenth.

Two things complicate the runway. MeiraGTx bought bota-vec back from Johnson and Johnson and intends to file in the United States, Europe and Japan on that failed-primary dataset, targeting a launch as early as twenty twenty-seven — so the company that lost the trial may reach the F-D-A first, and how the agency treats a filing built on secondaries will shape what Beacon's win is actually worth commercially. And Nanoscope's optogenetic therapy, which is mutation-agnostic and addresses retinitis pigmentosa broadly rather than the R-P-G-R subset, already has a B-L-A accepted. So the first approved product in this space may not be an R-P-G-R gene replacement at all.

The take I would carry out of this. The therapeutic hypothesis in X-linked retinitis pigmentosa has probably been correct since at least twenty twenty-one, and two large companies extinguished programs that were working because they measured the wrong thing. That is an expensive, repeatable failure mode, and it is concentrated in exactly the rare-disease and functional-outcome settings where capital is flowing right now. The corollary for your own diligence is uncomfortable but useful: in indications without a validated endpoint, the statistical analysis plan is part of the asset, and it is worth as much scrutiny as the construct.
