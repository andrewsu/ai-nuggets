First, and this is today's spotlight. On Monday evening the F-D-A approved Bayer's Sunharbo, chemical name asundexian, a once-daily pill that blocks Factor eleven-a, an enzyme that helps clots grow but plays only a small role in stopping bleeding. It is the first drug in the class approved in the U-S. The label is narrow: adults who have just had a mild-to-moderate ischemic stroke whose clot did not come from the heart, or a high-risk mini-stroke, taken on top of antiplatelet drugs. In the twelve-thousand-patient OCEANIC-STROKE trial, recurrent ischemic stroke fell from eight point four to six point two percent, a twenty-six percent relative reduction, and overall major bleeding was similar to placebo. The F-D-A did flag more major bleeding in patients over seventy-five and in the first ninety days, and more gastrointestinal bleeding. List price is seven hundred forty-eight dollars and fifty cents a month. Bristol Myers and J&J's milvexian has a stroke trial that just reached its primary completion date. The spotlight looks at what this label really covers and what it means for the rest of the class.

Second, a platform deal on the A-I side of discovery. argenx, the Dutch company behind Vyvgart, is buying Unnatural Products, a California company that combines machine learning with screening to engineer macrocyclic peptides. These are ring-shaped peptides big enough to grab flat protein surfaces that small molecules miss, and small enough, in principle, to be swallowed. argenx pays five hundred fifty million dollars in cash up front, with up to five hundred ninety-five million more in milestones, and expects to close by year end. The two companies have worked together for more than two years. They signed a licensing deal in July twenty twenty-five with a billion and a half in milestones, and argenx already held a minority stake. Unnatural Products also has deals with Merck and Novartis. argenx says it will aim the platform at oral, long-acting drugs for immune diseases. Vyvgart, its FcRn antibody, brought in four and a half billion dollars in the first nine months of the year. Leerink's Thomas Smith called the acquisition a source of "longer-term strategic value" and noted that pharma wants oral drugs that can replace injected biologics in immunology. The pattern is worth noting: a platform company partners first, the partner takes equity, and then the partner buys the whole company.

Third, A-I and China. Endpoints reports that Third Rock Ventures has entered a partnership worth up to two point one six billion dollars with Funnel Biosciences, a little-known Chinese company that calls itself an A-I-driven biologics developer. The terms are behind a paywall and we couldn't find a company release, so treat the number as a headline for now. It fits the fall's pattern of Western capital licensing biologics out of China. Last week Boehringer offered up to seven billion dollars for Chinese cancer programs. A Boston venture firm making a commitment this size is a newer step. Also on China: the Defense Department removed WuXi AppTec from its list of Chinese military companies to comply with an August court order. WuXi had sued, arguing the listing was arbitrary. Being on that list would have made it easier to label WuXi a biotech company "of concern."

Fourth, a safety label for the whole G-L-P-one class. The F-D-A ordered all twelve approved G-L-P-one drugs, including Wegovy, Ozempic, Zepbound, Mounjaro and Lilly's oral Foundayo, to add a warning about non-arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy. That's a stroke of the optic nerve that can cause sudden vision loss in one eye. In the agency's pooled analysis of a hundred twenty-six trials, the rate was six point four events per hundred thousand patient-years on a G-L-P-one versus three point eight on comparators. In its own real-world data, the F-D-A saw no excess risk against SGLT-two inhibitors but did see one against DPP-four inhibitors. BMO sees limited commercial risk, given the absolute rates and that this is a warnings update rather than a restriction.

Fifth, two obesity readouts from the end of AHA. Lilly's oral G-L-P-one Foundayo, orforglipron, met its Phase 3 goal in obstructive sleep apnea in about seven hundred patients. Breathing interruptions fell by up to forty-four percent, and up to a third of patients reached remission or mild disease. Lilly will file before year end, which would give it a second sleep apnea drug after Zepbound. Discontinuations for side effects ran twelve percent versus about four on placebo in patients not using a CPAP machine. Separately, Boehringer and Zealand's survodutide, a dual glucagon and G-L-P-one agonist, passed SYNCHRONIZE-CVOT, a fifty-five-hundred-patient cardiovascular safety trial, published in The Lancet. The worry was that glucagon raises heart rate, and the trial didn't show more major cardiac events. Boehringer will file, but William Blair expects use to be "confined to patients living with elevated liver-related risk factors" and still calls tolerability "a significant liability."

Sixth, a launch miss. Ionis lost more than a billion dollars in market value on Monday, falling about twenty-five percent, after cutting its full-year forecast for Tryngolza to about fifty million dollars from a hundred to a hundred ten million. Tryngolza is olezarsen, an antisense drug against APOC3 that lowers triglycerides. Third-quarter sales were nine million dollars against a consensus of twenty-one. Analysts say doctors have been slow to prescribe it. This is the launch that matters: the label was expanded in June from the rare familial chylomicronemia syndrome to severe hypertriglyceridemia, based on an eighty-five percent cut in acute pancreatitis events. That's also the market Arrowhead's quarterly plozasiran is chasing, and Monday's number makes Wall Street's blockbuster models for the indication look less certain. A good outcome on a hard endpoint doesn't guarantee prescriptions for a condition many doctors don't treat aggressively.

Seventh, psychedelics. Compass Pathways completed its rolling F-D-A submission for COMP360, its psilocybin therapy for treatment-resistant depression, a week after the nonprofit Usona Institute filed its own psilocybin drug for major depression. Both have Commissioner's National Priority Vouchers, which promise a review in a couple of months, so both could be approved within weeks of each other. Compass expects to launch in the first half of twenty twenty-seven.

Eighth, briefly, in neurodegeneration: London's Oyster Therapeutics added sixty million dollars to its Series A for tau degraders. The drug binds only disease-causing forms of tau. The company says its linker splits in the body into two small molecules that cross into the brain more easily. Tau has a long record of failures, and antisense and R-N-A-i drugs from Biogen and Arrowhead that lower total tau are years ahead in the clinic.
