Good morning Travis. Here are your biotech and pharma headlines for Saturday, September twenty-sixth, twenty twenty-six, covering Friday's tape. Two approvals, a billion-dollar delivery deal, a jury verdict against Lilly, and a conference in Glasgow where the myeloma field did something it has never done in the hundred and eighty years since the disease was first described: it wrote down what counts as a cure. That last one is today's spotlight, and it is the most consequential item of the week for anyone building cell therapy. Eleven items, then the week ahead.

First, and today's spotlight. At the International Myeloma Society meeting in Glasgow, Nikhil Munshi presented a joint consensus definition of cure in multiple myeloma on behalf of the society and the International Myeloma Working Group. The bar is specific and it is demanding. Five years in complete remission with no anti-myeloma treatment of any kind, counted from the day therapy stops rather than from the day the patient first responded. At least four measurable residual disease tests over those five years, including one at the five-year mark, all negative, with no positive result in between, and at a sensitivity of one myeloma cell in a million by next-generation sequencing or next-generation flow. Plus negative advanced imaging — either PET-C-T or diffusion-weighted whole-body magnetic resonance — at both ends of the window. Then on Friday, Legend Biotech presented the first major dataset into that frame: five-year follow-up from Cohort A of CARTITUDE-two, in which half of twenty patients given a single infusion of Carvykti after one to three prior lines remain alive and progression-free with no further myeloma treatment. We will go through what the definition actually changes, where Legend's claim is strong, where the twenty-patient denominator bites, and why the competitive squeeze on autologous C-A-R T-cell therapy got tighter in the same four days.

Second, an approval in an ultra-rare disease, and a two-horse race that did not exist a month ago. The F-D-A approved Mirum's zilurgisertib, to be sold as Atebrioz, for patients twelve and older with fibrodysplasia ossificans progressiva — the disease in which muscle, tendon and ligament progressively turn into bone. It is a once-daily oral inhibitor of A-L-K two, the receptor kinase that is abnormally active in most patients and drives the heterotopic bone formation. The population is roughly nine hundred people worldwide and about three hundred in the United States. Two things make this interesting beyond the science. Mirum does not own the molecule's origin story — it licensed global commercial rights from Incyte in April for sixteen million dollars up front plus regulatory and sales milestones, which is a very small check for an approved asset in a disease with no competition as recently as August. And it no longer has no competition: Regeneron's Pasatru, a monthly infusion, cleared in late August, and Ipsen's oral Sohonos was already there. So an ultra-rare indication with three hundred U-S patients now supports three branded products, which tells you something about where rare-disease pricing has gotten to.

Third, the other approval, and this one is a real commercial event. The F-D-A cleared Merck's Welireg in combination with Eisai-partnered Lenvima for second-line clear cell renal cell carcinoma after a P-D-one or P-D-L-one inhibitor. Welireg is belzutifan, the first-in-class inhibitor of H-I-F two alpha — the transcription factor that accumulates when the von Hippel-Lindau tumor suppressor is lost, which is the founding lesion in most clear cell kidney cancer. It was originally approved in December twenty twenty-three for third line. In the Phase three Litespark-eleven trial it cut the risk of progression or death by twenty-six percent against Cabometyx, extending median progression-free survival by about four months to fourteen point six, with response rate fifty-three percent against forty and — the number the clinicians care about — median duration of response roughly doubled, twenty-three months against twelve. Final overall survival was not statistically significant but favored the combination, thirty-three point seven months against twenty-eight point six. Katy Beckermann at Tennessee Oncology told William Blair she considers the result practice-changing, and Leerink projected rapid uptake on what they called compelling survival and durability with little detriment in toxicity. The strategic read: Welireg adds a genuinely new mechanism to a second line that has been two tyrosine kinase inhibitors arguing with each other, and it puts pressure on Exelixis's Cabometyx. Note the asymmetry, though — Merck cannot follow this into first line, because the Litespark-twelve triplet failed earlier this year, and analysts expect a slow adjuvant rollout too. Merck's answer is Litespark-thirty-three, testing Welireg with Exelixis's own next-generation tyrosine kinase inhibitor zanzalintinib.

Fourth, the deal of the day, and it is a formulation deal rather than a molecule deal. Novo Nordisk signed a global license with the Swedish drug-delivery company Nanexa worth up to one point one six five billion euros, about one point three billion dollars, for exclusive access to Nanexa's PharmaShell technology across up to five programs in obesity, type two diabetes and cardiometabolic disease. PharmaShell coats individual drug particles with an ultra-thin inorganic layer, which lets you dial the release profile; the stated targets here are monthly and quarterly dosing. Six hundred fifteen million euros of the total is upfront plus development and regulatory milestones, the rest is sales-based, and there are low single-digit royalties. Nanexa's stock more than doubled in Stockholm. The signal worth extracting is that Novo's second front against Lilly is no longer only about molecule potency — it is about dosing interval, and it is buying that capability rather than building it. This is the second small external deal Novo has done in a week.

Fifth, a courtroom result that matters to anyone who has ever signed an option-and-license with a large pharma partner. A federal jury in the Northern District of California found that Eli Lilly breached the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing in its license agreement with Nektar Therapeutics over rezpegaldesleukin, the pegylated interleukin-two conjugate designed to preferentially expand regulatory T cells. The jury awarded Nektar ninety million dollars, with interest still to be set by the court. Nektar had asked for a billion. The verdict is subject to post-trial motions and appeal, so it is not money in the bank. But the legal theory is the notable part: not that Lilly broke an explicit term, but that it failed to act in good faith in how it ran the program — which is the claim every biotech partner worries about and almost never wins.

Sixth, staying in Glasgow, AbbVie presented full Phase three results for etentamig, its second-generation B-C-M-A by C-D-three bispecific T-cell engager, in triple-class-exposed relapsed refractory myeloma. Three hundred ninety-three patients, median three prior lines. Response rate seventy-four percent against forty-five point seven for investigator's choice of standard therapy, and a progression-free survival hazard ratio of zero point four zero. Twelve-month survival was eighty-seven point nine percent against seventy-two, though the formal survival boundary has not been crossed. The engineering is the point: a deliberately low-affinity C-D-three binder paired with a high-avidity bivalent B-C-M-A arm, plus retained neonatal F-c receptor binding so you can dose monthly. Among patients who got a single step-up dose, cytokine release syndrome ran twenty-eight percent, all grade one or two, and neurotoxicity was one patient. That is an off-the-shelf drug with monthly dosing and manageable step-up, and it is exactly the pressure the spotlight is about.

Seventh, the class list. Endpoints published its Endpoints eleven for twenty twenty-six, and the composition is a decent proxy for where venture money believes the frontier is. It is heavily artificial-intelligence and tooling: Medra, building self-driving laboratories; Vivodyne, whose human tissue factory is a bet that the preclinical bottleneck is the model organism; and Anew Labs, a ByteDance spinout positioned as a China-based rival to the Western artificial intelligence labs. On the therapeutic side, Azalea Therapeutics is melding gene editing with C-A-R T-cell therapy, NewLimit is looking well beyond Yamanaka factors for anti-aging biology, SonoThera is doing non-viral gene delivery, Stipple Bio is going after pancreatic cancer, and Corsera Health is the newest vehicle from John Maraganore and Clive Meanwell — pairing an artificial-intelligence risk-prediction platform with a once-yearly small interfering R-N-A shot meant to prevent cardiovascular disease in healthy people in their thirties, already in Phase one. Two of the eleven — Kali and K2 — are explicitly organized around the China question, one by turning away from it and one by leaning in.

Eighth, and this is the second R-N-A interference listing in a week. City Therapeutics filed its S-one for a Nasdaq initial public offering under the ticker C-T-Y, with Goldman Sachs, Jefferies, Stifel and Oppenheimer leading. Founded in twenty twenty-three by Alnylam alumni, it is running a next-generation small interfering R-N-A platform: the lead program is a GalNAc-conjugated small interfering R-N-A against Factor eleven, aiming for anticoagulation with a lower bleeding penalty; behind it are programs against retinol binding protein four for Stargardt disease, entering Phase one late this year, and transferrin receptor two to lower hepcidin in anemia of chronic disease. Coming two days after ADARx priced at four hundred forty-six million, this is a field that could not get a traditional U-S listing for a decade suddenly getting two in a week.

Ninth, an artificial-intelligence item from an unexpected source. The National Library of Medicine launched Linked Discoveries, an experimental tool that takes any PubMed citation and maps up to two hundred of its nearest neighbors in the literature, surfacing shared conditions, genes and chemicals, plus funding, citation relationships, retractions and reviews, in graph and timeline views. More than twenty-nine million publications are represented at launch, and it uses an artificial-intelligence-informed approach to define relatedness rather than citation count — which is the actual innovation, because it surfaces connected work that was never highly cited. N-I-H is positioning it as part of its replication and reproducibility initiative. Free, public, and worth ten minutes of your time if you do literature-driven target work.

Tenth, a lawsuit that is really a story about a dealmaking structure. China's Lepu Medical Technology has sued the business development consultant Su Zhang and her firm CoDevCo in New York, alleging that while serving as Lepu's exclusive dealmaking liaison for a portfolio of obesity and metabolic candidates, she was simultaneously acting as chief business officer for a direct competitor, and that her associates held concurrent roles at other Chinese rivals while holding Lepu's confidential information. Lepu wants its five hundred thousand dollar retainer back plus disgorgement and injunctive relief. The reason it is news is who Zhang is: during a short stint as global head of business development at Hengrui, she built the Hengrui-to-Hercules transaction that became Kailera Therapeutics — the deal that invented the China NewCo model, in which a Chinese company spins early assets into a Western-domiciled startup and keeps an equity stake. Kailera priced a six hundred twenty-five million dollar initial public offering in April, two years in. The structure is now everywhere; this is the first significant litigation about how it gets brokered.

Briefly, three more. The comment window on the F-D-A's revised draft guidance on substantial evidence of effectiveness closed on the twenty-second, and drugmakers are pushing the agency for far more specificity on how much confirmatory evidence is actually enough to support approval on a single adequate and well-controlled trial — which is the single highest-leverage open question in U-S regulatory policy right now. Indivior has handed back its long-running positive allosteric modulator collaboration with Addex ahead of its merger with Supernus. And Travere's Eric Dube gave his first interview about stepping down after nearly eight years; he leaves December first to focus on his health and family, and Bradley Campbell — who ran Amicus until BioMarin bought it in April — takes over. Separately, Lundbeck is moving its U-S headquarters from Deerfield, Illinois to greater Boston.
