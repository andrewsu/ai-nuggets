This is your spotlight for Friday, November twenty-seventh, 2026. On Thursday, HUTCHMED said China's drug regulator accepted its application to use Orpathys, or savolitinib, with AstraZeneca's Tagrisso, osimertinib, as first-line treatment for E-G-F-R-mutated lung cancer whose tumors overexpress MET. A China filing doesn't usually earn a spotlight. This one does because it's the first serious attempt to move MET from a resistance problem you deal with later to a biomarker you treat on day one. The data behind it are strong, but the benefit is concentrated in a narrower group of patients than the headline suggests, and the regimen is harder on patients than an all-oral combination sounds.

Start with the biology. Tagrisso is the standard first-line drug for E-G-F-R-mutant lung cancer, and it works for a long time. But tumors find a way around it, and one of the most common ways is to turn up MET, a second receptor tyrosine kinase that can drive the same growth signals E-G-F-R does. Susan Galbraith, AstraZeneca's head of oncology R-and-D, told Fierce Pharma that about a third of patients develop MET overexpression or amplification after a third-generation E-G-F-R drug. Savolitinib is a selective MET inhibitor. The original plan was to add it once MET shows up, after Tagrisso stops working. That's the global SAFFRON trial, which beat chemotherapy on both progression-free and overall survival and was published in the New England Journal last month. It's now under review in Europe and Japan and has been submitted in the U-S. SANOVO tests a more aggressive idea: some tumors overexpress MET at diagnosis, so hit both receptors from the start.

Here's what SANOVO showed. It was a blinded Phase three at seventy-four centers, all in China. Three hundred twenty-six untreated patients with E-G-F-R exon nineteen deletions or L-eight-five-eight-R mutations, plus centrally confirmed MET overexpression, got Tagrisso with either savolitinib or placebo. The data were presented at ESMO in October by Yi-Long Wu of Guangdong Provincial People's Hospital. In the full trial population, median progression-free survival was twenty-four point nine months on the combination versus thirteen point eight on Tagrisso alone, a hazard ratio of zero point five six. By blinded independent review the gap was similar, twenty-seven point nine months versus sixteen point four. Overall survival is the striking number: a hazard ratio of zero point four eight, with median survival of forty-four and a half months versus about thirty-five. At three years, sixty-seven percent of patients on the combination were alive, versus forty-seven percent on Tagrisso alone. Wu called it "a pivotal evolution in how we approach first-line E-G-F-R-mutated lung cancer."

Now the details that matter. First, the biomarker. MET overexpression was measured by immunohistochemistry, and the trial used two tiers. IHC three-plus means at least ninety percent of tumor cells stain strongly. IHC two-plus means meaningful staining below that bar. About sixty percent of patients were three-plus, and the statistical plan tested that group first. In the three-plus group, progression-free survival went from eleven point one months to twenty-four point eight, a hazard ratio of zero point four five, and the survival hazard ratio was zero point four. In the two-plus group, the progression-free survival hazard ratio was zero point eight five, with a confidence interval that crosses one, and the survival hazard ratio of zero point six four also crossed one. So the effect sits mostly in the highest-expressing tumors. The question for China's regulator, and for anyone who later looks at this for the West, is whether the label covers all overexpressers or only the three-plus group. That decides the size of the market and what diagnostic test every patient needs at diagnosis.

Second, how hard it is to find these patients. Nearly nineteen hundred patients were screened to randomize three hundred twenty-six, roughly one in six. Not all of that is the biomarker, since screening failures happen for many reasons, but it means a first-line MET strategy requires a tissue IHC test with a validated cutoff on top of the E-G-F-R test every patient already gets.

Third, tolerability. All-oral does not mean easy. Grade three or higher adverse events hit seventy-seven percent of patients on the combination versus thirty-eight percent on Tagrisso alone. Serious events were fifty-five percent versus twenty-nine. Peripheral edema, a known MET-inhibitor class effect, showed up in sixty-five percent versus seven, and low albumin in sixty-three percent versus twenty-two. Nearly three-quarters of patients on the combination needed a dose change, and fifteen percent stopped for side effects, versus four percent. In fairness, patients on the combination stayed on treatment about twenty-two months against thirteen, so they had more time to report events. Treatment-related deaths were similar, about four percent in each arm. But this is not a free add-on.

Fourth, and most important for anyone outside China, the control arm. SANOVO compared against Tagrisso alone. That's still a standard of care, but no longer the only one. AstraZeneca's own FLAURA-two showed that adding chemotherapy to Tagrisso improves overall survival, with a final hazard ratio of zero point seven seven. Johnson and Johnson's MARIPOSA showed that Rybrevant, amivantamab, plus lazertinib beats Tagrisso, with a survival hazard ratio of zero point seven five. Rybrevant is a bispecific antibody against E-G-F-R and MET. So J-and-J already hits MET in the first line, in every E-G-F-R patient, without selecting anyone. SANOVO's hazard ratio is much larger, but it comes from a biomarker-enriched population, against a weaker comparator, at about forty percent survival maturity, so it isn't an apples-to-apples comparison. What SANOVO does show is that in the subgroup with the highest MET expression, adding a selective MET inhibitor to Tagrisso roughly doubles the time to progression. It doesn't show that this beats Tagrisso plus chemotherapy, or the J-and-J regimen, in those same patients. Nobody has run those trials.

On whether this travels, Galbraith was careful. Asked whether AstraZeneca would run a global first-line version of SANOVO, she told Fierce, "We will talk to regulatory authorities about the opportunity there." She also said that with China-only studies, "there's a question about whether the effect size is similar if you took it into a Western patient population," and suggested that SAFFRON, which did enroll globally, helps settle that. That sounds like a company hoping it won't have to run a full global trial. I'd be skeptical that the F-D-A accepts a first-line label from a China-only trial against Tagrisso alone, given how it has treated single-country data recently. A U-S first-line indication probably needs a global trial against a modern comparator.

The competitive picture got busier this week. On Wednesday, Bayer paid Hansoh one hundred fifty million dollars up front for an E-G-F-R-by-c-MET bispecific antibody-drug conjugate that is going first into E-G-F-R-mutant lung cancer. AstraZeneca has its own E-G-F-R-by-c-MET conjugate in the clinic, and AbbVie is running a first-line trial of its next-generation c-MET conjugate with Tagrisso. So the field now has three ways to hit MET in E-G-F-R lung cancer. One is a pill on top of Tagrisso, which is savolitinib. One is a bispecific antibody for everyone, which is Rybrevant. And one is a conjugate that uses MET or E-G-F-R as an address to deliver a toxic payload. SANOVO is the first randomized first-line evidence for the pill.

For HUTCHMED, this matters more than it might look. AstraZeneca commercializes Orpathys, so HUTCHMED's upside is milestones and royalties, and a first-line indication in China, where E-G-F-R mutations are far more common than in the West, is a much bigger market than the post-Tagrisso setting. The company has been run by its finance chief, Johnny Cheng, as acting C-E-O since August 2025, when founding C-E-O Weiguo Su took leave for health reasons. It holds a corporate day in Shanghai on December eighth, with an English webcast that evening Hong Kong time, which is seven a-m Eastern. Expect questions about the MET cutoff the label will use and whether AstraZeneca commits to a global first-line trial.

The takeaways. SANOVO is a real result. In patients whose tumors strongly overexpress MET at diagnosis, adding savolitinib to Tagrisso more than doubled progression-free survival and shows an early survival advantage. But the benefit sits mostly in the three-plus group, the side effects are substantial, and the comparator is no longer the bar outside China. The thing to watch is whether a global first-line trial gets announced, and which comparator it uses. If it's Tagrisso plus chemotherapy or the J-and-J regimen, AstraZeneca is confident. If there isn't one, this stays a China indication.
