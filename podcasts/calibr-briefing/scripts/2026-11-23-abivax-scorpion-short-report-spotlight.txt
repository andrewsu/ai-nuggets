Welcome to the spotlight. Today it's Abivax, and the short report that tried to knock out one of the most valuable late-stage drugs in immunology about five weeks before its F-D-A filing. The question isn't whether Scorpion Capital wants the stock lower; it told you that up front. The question is whether anything in the two hundred ninety-seven pages changes the odds that obefazimod gets approved, or how its label will read. My answer is that most of it doesn't. One allegation does matter, and it's the one the F-D-A was always going to test.

Start with what's at stake. Obefazimod is a once-daily oral small molecule for moderate-to-severe ulcerative colitis. It's Abivax's only drug, and the company is worth roughly six billion dollars. As we covered in June, Abivax's model is that it binds the cap-binding complex on newly made R-N-A and increases splicing of one long non-coding R-N-A, which produces an anti-inflammatory microRNA called miR-one-twenty-four. That damps down several inflammatory cytokines at once instead of blocking one. The clinical data are why the market cared. In the ABTECT maintenance trial, about fifty-one percent of patients were in clinical remission at week forty-four, versus ten point four percent on placebo. That's a wider placebo-adjusted gap than the I-L-twenty-three antibodies showed in their maintenance trials, from a pill without a boxed warning, in a population where nearly half had already failed an advanced therapy. The weak spot has always been cancer. In June, malignancies on drug, including several non-melanoma skin cancers, set off a forty percent one-day sell-off before the stock recovered. Abivax says it will file by the end of the year and launch in 2027.

Now Friday. Scorpion published "A Biotech Fraud for the Ages," disclosed a short position, and said it would start covering a substantial part of it on publication. That matters for the incentives: the report is built to move the stock on the day it comes out. The American depositary shares opened just above seventy-nine dollars, traded as low as sixty-two seventy, and closed at seventy-three twenty, down about nine percent from Thursday. Volume was nearly fifteen million shares, against one to two million on a normal day. Paris was down seventeen percent. After the close, Abivax called the report full of "numerous factually inaccurate and misleading statements, assumptions, and conclusions," and pointed out that some of them rest on studies Scorpion commissioned and paid for. The shares were up about ten percent in Paris this morning. That's only a partial recovery: the U-S shares were around ninety dollars two weeks ago and had already been drifting down before the report.

The report makes five main claims, and they're worth taking one at a time, because they're not equally strong.

The first is chemistry. Obefazimod is very lipophilic. Scorpion puts its measured log P at about six point three and sets it next to the "dirty dozen" persistent pollutants, including D-D-T and dioxins, to argue it's a bioaccumulating toxin. The best public rebuttal I've seen came on Sunday from a healthcare investor writing on Substack. That author is openly long Abivax, so weigh it accordingly, but the arguments can be checked. D-D-T accumulates because the body can't metabolize it. Obefazimod is quickly glucuronidated: the parent drug's half-life is about one to two hours, and the main circulating species is a water-soluble glucuronide metabolite. Scorpion's own comparison table lists etrasimod, Pfizer's approved S-one-P drug for ulcerative colitis, with a slightly higher log P. Lipophilicity is a reason to look at distribution and accumulation data. It doesn't show toxicity.

The second claim is that the mechanism is wrong. Scorpion argues the drug works through nonspecific toxicity, notes that it kills nematodes, questions whether the miR-one-twenty-four readout measures what Abivax says it does, and cites mouse data showing the drug activates the aryl hydrocarbon receptor, the receptor dioxin acts through. Some of that may well be right. Abivax's mechanism story has never been fully convincing, even to people who like the drug. But the F-D-A approves drugs on adequate and well-controlled trials, not on whether the mechanism is right. A-h-R activation also isn't a cancer signal on its face. Tapinarof, a topical A-h-R agonist, is approved for psoriasis and atopic dermatitis and had negative two-year carcinogenicity studies. In the gut, A-h-R signaling is usually considered protective for the barrier. The mechanism dispute matters more for the franchise, for Crohn's and future indications, than for this filing.

The third claim is the one that matters: light. Scorpion's lab says obefazimod absorbs U-V-A light at eight point four times the screening threshold in the I-C-H S-ten photosafety guideline. It calls the drug a potential photocarcinogen and links that to the skin cancers in Phase three. Be precise about what this shows. That threshold is a trigger for more testing, not a safety limit. Plenty of marketed drugs exceed it, including several fluoroquinolone antibiotics. Under S-ten, the next step is an in vitro phototoxicity assay, usually the three-T-three neutral red uptake test, and then animal studies only if that's positive. Scorpion did the first step and jumped to the conclusion of the last one. But this is also the one allegation that lines up with something already seen in patients, the skin cancers, and the one the N-D-A has to answer with data. If Abivax's photosafety package is clean, this goes away. If it isn't, it's a label problem, possibly with sun-protection and skin-exam language, rather than a fraud. I'd expect management to get pushed to disclose that package soon, and how fast they do is the best near-term tell.

The fourth claim is the skin cancers themselves. Scorpion counts ten non-melanoma skin cancers in maintenance, six at fifty milligrams, three at twenty-five, one on placebo, calls that dose-ordered, and counts seventeen malignancies across the program with sixteen on drug. The rebuttal makes three fair points. Randomization wasn't even; the fifty milligram arm had about twice as many patients as placebo, and longer exposure. The company has said the skin cancer cases had a mean age of about sixty-two, against about forty-two for the trial overall. And ulcerative colitis patients, especially those with prior thiopurine exposure, start out at higher risk of these cancers. With numbers this small, the confidence intervals are wide. My read hasn't changed since June: this probably isn't a reason to reject the drug, and it probably ends up in the label and in post-marketing commitments.

The fifth claim is trial conduct: about fifty sites in China, a large Eastern European share, and a North American placebo group where zero of thirty-six patients reached remission, which Scorpion treats as suspicious. The rebuttal cites China's trial registry, which lists fifty-nine patients in China against more than eleven hundred internationally in maintenance, roughly five percent of the program. Etrasimod's pivotal trial had an even smaller U-S share and still got approved. The North American placebo number is the one I'd watch. Large regional differences do come up in F-D-A review. But a zero placebo response in very refractory patients is a reason to compare on a placebo-adjusted basis, not evidence of manipulation. Poor trial conduct usually inflates the placebo response; it doesn't manufacture efficacy.

So where does the Street stand? Before Friday, the average analyst price target was around one hundred sixty-four dollars, more than double Friday's close. But the sentiment had been slipping for a while. Consensus 2027 revenue fell from about one hundred eighty-nine million dollars to about one hundred forty-five million in the ninety days into September, as analysts trimmed their probability of success after the June safety scare. Short interest was under five percent before the report, so this wasn't a crowded short that blew up. It was a new short at a time when long holders were already less sure. Abivax has roughly four hundred million dollars in cash, which gets it to the filing but not through a launch. That's why this fight matters beyond the stock price: Abivax will need to raise money or find a partner, and the terms depend on where the stock trades.

What's the read for Foresite and Calibr? First, this attack is aimed at the gap between a big valuation and a public data package that's thin on preclinical detail. Abivax hasn't published its full A-D-M-E, distribution or photosafety work. That's normal. Almost nobody does before approval. But it gives a short seller room to call missing data hidden data. For any portfolio company approaching a filing with a novel small molecule and a safety question, publishing the reassuring preclinical package early is cheap protection. Second, for Calibr's small-molecule programs, it's a reminder to run the I-C-H S-ten photosafety screen early and keep the results ready. It costs little, and it settles exactly the kind of question Scorpion raised. Third, it's another example of preclinical hypotheses being used against human data. The other side of that argument is the G-L-P-one class: rodent thyroid tumors led to a boxed warning, and more than a decade of human use hasn't borne that risk out.

Here's what to watch. First, whether Abivax files by December thirty-first as promised. A slip of even a few weeks will be read as validating the short. Second, whether management discloses the phototoxicity results, and when. Third, the F-D-A's filing decision about sixty days after submission, and whether it calls an advisory committee. A committee would probably focus on skin cancer and regional heterogeneity, the two places where Scorpion's report touches something real. Everything else in the report is mostly about making the stock fall on Friday.
