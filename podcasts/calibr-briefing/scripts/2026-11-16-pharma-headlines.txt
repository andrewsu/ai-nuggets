Good morning, Travis. It's Monday, November sixteenth, 2026. The weekend's biggest story came on Sunday: the F-D-A turned down Summit and Akeso's ivonescimab. Johnson and Johnson is reportedly close to a three-point-four-billion-dollar eye deal. And Flagship is taking its company-building model to London. Here's what you need to know.

First, the decision we've been tracking all week. On Sunday Summit Therapeutics said the F-D-A had issued a complete response letter for ivonescimab, its P-D-one by V-E-G-F bispecific antibody licensed from Akeso. The application covered ivonescimab plus chemotherapy in E-G-F-R-mutant lung cancer after a targeted pill stops working. According to Summit, the agency's reason was overall survival. In the global HARMONi trial, progression-free survival improved clearly, but the survival result missed statistical significance at the primary analysis, with a p-value of zero point zero five seven. Akeso's Hong Kong shares fell as much as thirty-two percent on Monday. It's the first rejection for this drug class, and it's today's spotlight.

Second, a big-pharma deal could land today. The Financial Times reports that Johnson and Johnson is in advanced talks to buy Ollin Biosciences for up to three point four billion dollars: two point two billion in cash upfront and up to one point two billion in milestones. Ollin launched in September 2025 with a hundred million dollars led by ARCH Venture Partners. Its lead drug, O-L-N-three-twenty-four, is a V-E-G-F by angiopoietin-two bispecific antibody for wet macular degeneration and diabetic macular edema. That's the same two-target approach as Roche's Vabysmo, but Ollin is betting on higher potency and a larger dose. Ollin raised three hundred thirty million dollars in June for late-stage trials. The F-T says a deal could be announced as soon as Monday. It also cites Stifel's count of forty-six biotechs sold for more than a billion dollars this year, against a previous annual record of thirty-five.

Third, a leadership crisis at the company behind much of A-I drug discovery's computing. Jensen Huang resigned as Nvidia's C-E-O on Thursday. He stepped down amid a board investigation of reported personal relationships, including one with the founder of an Nvidia customer whom U-S intelligence officials suspect of passing information to China's state security ministry. Beijing denies that. C-F-O Colette Kress is interim C-E-O, and the stock fell about thirteen percent last week. A G-E-N analysis on Sunday asks what happens to Nvidia's life sciences commitments. Those include the one-billion-dollar co-innovation lab with Lilly and tools partnerships with Thermo Fisher and Danaher. Its venture arm has also done a hundred twenty-two startup deals so far this year. Bernstein's Stacy Rasgon wrote that he wonders what will happen to the company's many investments and guarantees. Insilico's Alex Zhavoronkov was blunter. He predicted that A-I drug discovery startups with no drugs and no productivity metrics won't be able to raise money from Nvidia for a while.

Fourth, staying with A-I. Flagship Pioneering launched the Flagship Origination Lab in London on Monday, with an initial hundred million pounds. It's Flagship's first company-creation unit outside the U-S, and it will build what Flagship calls A-I-native platform companies, starting with a team of about fifty people. Flagship hopes to bring in another five hundred million pounds from partners, but that money isn't secured yet. It comes days after Flagship merged Valo and Montai, our spotlight last Thursday. Clearly Flagship hasn't lost faith in the A-I platform model.

Fifth, a San Diego launch. Seawolf Therapeutics came out of stealth with a hundred-million-dollar Series A. Versant founded the company, and Eli Lilly, the Novartis Venture Fund and CRISPR Therapeutics joined the round. Seawolf is working on gene delivery without viruses. Its Trident platform packages DNA, rather than R-N-A, in lipid nanoparticles, aiming for the durability of gene therapy with the ability to dose again, which viral vectors don't allow. The lead program is in metabolic disease. Sanofi also signed a research deal worth up to one point two billion dollars, starting with bleeding disorders. The C-E-O is Elizabeth Alcamo, formerly of 4D Molecular Therapeutics.

Sixth, more money for late-stage biotech. Sofinnova Partners closed its second crossover fund at seven hundred twenty-five million euros, about eight hundred fifteen million dollars. That's the firm's largest fund ever, and it's aimed at late-stage European companies that could become global leaders.

Seventh, the full data behind a win we covered in August. At ObesityWeek, Amylyx presented the Phase three LUCIDITY results for avexitide in post-bariatric hypoglycemia. Avexitide is a G-L-P-one receptor antagonist. It blocks the receptor that Wegovy and Zepbound activate, because this disease is caused by too much G-L-P-one signaling after gastric bypass. The drug cut the rate of serious low-blood-sugar episodes by fifty-five percent against placebo, met every multiplicity-controlled secondary endpoint, and caused no treatment-related serious adverse events. The trial was small, seventy-eight patients, but nearly half of them had a history of losing consciousness from these episodes.

Eighth, a label win in heart disease. The F-D-A expanded the label for Cytokinetics' Myqorzo, aficamten, a cardiac myosin inhibitor for obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. The label now includes the MAPLE-HCM data, where Myqorzo beat the beta-blocker metoprolol head to head on exercise capacity. Cytokinetics says no other drug in the class has comparative data against a beta-blocker on its U-S label. That gives its sales force an edge against Bristol's Camzyos.

Ninth, an accountability ruling in China. A Shanghai government work group fired two researchers whose experimental gene-editing treatment, injected into the spinal fluid, was followed by the death of a six-year-old girl in March 2025. Investigators found that a monkey safety report wasn't shared with the clinical team before she was dosed. They also found that a Nature paper on the work left out both the animal findings and her death. Both researchers are barred from government science funding for five years, and Shanghai has started inspecting clinical research on new biomedical technologies. Coming in the same week as the ivonescimab rejection, it puts China's clinical-data standards back in the spotlight.

A few quick ones. Novo Nordisk presented post hoc analyses from the REDEFINE-one trial of CagriSema. Two-thirds of patients lost at least five percent of their weight by week eight, and those early responders averaged about a twenty-five percent loss at sixty-eight weeks. The F-D-A decision is still expected this quarter. Apple and OpenEvidence announced a study around an experimental consumer health chat app at the HLTH conference. And STAT and WireScreen published their ranking of China's most powerful drugmakers, with BeOne and Hengrui at the top.
