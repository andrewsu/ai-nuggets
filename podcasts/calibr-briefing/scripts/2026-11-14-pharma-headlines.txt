Good morning, Travis. It's Saturday, November fourteenth, 2026, and this briefing covers both Thursday and Friday. The F-D-A has reportedly shelved its fastest review track. Rezolute got the Phase three win it badly needed after last year's failure. AstraZeneca has a new bladder cancer approval, Regeneron has a first drug signal in POTS, and Europe's regulators handed down their November opinions. Here's what you need to know.

First, the national priority voucher program is effectively over. On Thursday, Reuters reported, citing six sources and internal documents, that the F-D-A has quietly shelved the Commissioner's National Priority Voucher pilot. That's the program Marty Makary started in June of last year, promising reviews in one to two months. No vouchers have been awarded since Makary left in May, and the council of political appointees that ran the reviews has gone dormant. Companies can still apply, but according to one source, the applications go nowhere. H-H-S says the pilot is ongoing. The same day, Fierce Pharma noticed that the F-D-A hasn't added a complete response letter to its public database since early August. This is today's spotlight, so I'll stop there.

Second, Rezolute has its comeback. On Friday, Rezolute reported preliminary results from upLIFT, a single-arm Phase three of ersodetug in tumor hyperinsulinism. That's dangerously low blood sugar caused by insulinomas and other tumors. Ersodetug is an antibody that binds the insulin receptor at a site away from where insulin binds, and it turns down the receptor's signaling however much insulin the tumor is pumping out. Thirteen of sixteen patients met the primary endpoint, cutting their intravenous glucose infusion rate by at least half, and twelve came off I-V glucose entirely. The bar was nine responders. One patient left the study for hospice, died of cancer progression and was counted as a non-responder. There were no drug-related safety findings. The full statistical analysis is due by mid-December, with F-D-A discussions in the first quarter. The context matters. Late last year, the same drug missed its placebo-controlled Phase three in congenital hyperinsulinism, and the stock collapsed. Rezolute raised about a hundred million dollars the same morning at three dollars eighty-seven, which was Thursday's closing price, so there was no premium even on a pivotal win. One caveat: the comparison is each patient against their own baseline, with no control arm. In a disease where the alternative is a permanent I-V line, the F-D-A may accept that.

Third, an approval in bladder cancer. On Friday, the F-D-A approved AstraZeneca's Imfinzi, the P-D-L-one antibody durvalumab, together with Pfizer's Padcev, enfortumab vedotin, the Nectin-four antibody-drug conjugate. The approval is for muscle-invasive bladder cancer in patients who can't take cisplatin or have refused it. The regimen is three cycles of the combination before cystectomy, then durvalumab alone afterward. In the Phase three VOLGA trial of six hundred ninety-five patients, the combination cut the risk of an event-free-survival event by forty-nine percent compared with surgery alone, and the overall survival hazard ratio was zero point five eight. Merck's Keytruda plus Padcev got there first, a year ago. AstraZeneca's argument is that Padcev here is given only before surgery, which means less exposure to its skin and nerve toxicity.

Fourth, Regeneron has the first real drug signal in POTS. That's postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome, where the heart races on standing. It mostly affects younger women, and there is no approved therapy. Baloncibart is an antibody that blocks N-P-R-one, the receptor for natriuretic peptides. The idea is that blocking it lets the body hold onto more circulating blood volume. In the Phase two PINNACLE trial of eighty-two patients, a single four-hundred-fifty-milligram dose lowered the rise in heart rate on standing by eleven point four beats per minute more than placebo at day eight. The effect lasted to day twenty-nine, and so did improvements in patient-reported functioning. The lower dose faded by then. A Phase three starts in the first half of 2027.

Fifth, Kyverna has finished its biologics license application for miv-cel, its C-D-nineteen CAR-T for stiff person syndrome, and asked for priority review. If it's approved, it would be the first cell therapy for an autoimmune disease. At the same time, Kyverna shelved KYV-one-oh-two. That's the same CAR made with a faster process that skips apheresis, and only last spring the C-E-O was calling it the key to wider access. The company is putting all its resources behind the first launch.

Sixth, Europe. At its November meeting the C-H-M-P recommended approval for AstraZeneca's baxdrostat, an aldosterone synthase inhibitor for hard-to-control hypertension. It backed Roche's Gazyvaro, the C-D-twenty antibody obinutuzumab, in lupus, and Merck's Welireg, the H-I-F-two-alpha inhibitor belzutifan, with lenvatinib in kidney cancer. AstraZeneca withdrew its European application for anselamimab, the amyloid-clearing antibody for light chain amyloidosis that missed its overall Phase three endpoint last year. And on re-examination, the committee again refused Zevra's arimoclomol for Niemann-Pick type C.

Seventh, an I-P-O. Windward Bio raised a hundred seventy-five million dollars at seventeen dollars a share, the middle of its range, plus fifty million from Sanofi in a private placement. Sanofi's venture arm was already in its hundred-sixty-five-million-dollar Series B in May. The lead drug is WIN-three-seventy-eight, a long-acting antibody against T-S-L-P licensed from China's Kelun-Biotech. It's in a Phase two-three in severe asthma testing twice-yearly dosing, against Tezspire's monthly dosing. BioSpace counts it as the thirty-fifth biotech I-P-O of 2026, compared with eight in all of last year. The window isn't wide open for everyone, though. Rezera, the old NodThera, postponed its planned two-hundred-million-dollar I-P-O for N-L-R-P-three inhibitors shortly before it was due to price, citing market conditions.

Eighth, Bristol Myers is merging research and development under its chief medical officer, Cristian Massacesi, starting in January. Chief research officer Robert Plenge, a Celgene veteran, leaves his role at year end and advises through March. Eliquis and Opdivo made roughly half of last year's forty-eight billion dollars in revenue, and both are heading toward patent expiry.

Ninth, for the A-I file: the F-D-A cleared Biolojic Design's investigational new drug application for BD-two-hundred, an antibody-drug conjugate against Trop-two and Nectin-four. Biolojic calls it a "multibody." Each arm can bind either target, so the molecule can grip two Trop-twos, two Nectin-fours, or one of each, depending on what a given tumor cell displays. It carries a topoisomerase inhibitor payload. Biolojic's platform also produced the I-L-two antibody now called imneskibart, which it says was the first A-I-designed antibody to reach the clinic. A global Phase one is expected to start within weeks.

A few quick ones. Cellectis will cut up to seventy percent of its staff and stop enrolling its allogeneic CAR-T trials. It's pivoting to in vivo editing: a base editor against A-P-O-C-three for severe hypertriglyceridemia, and an epigenetic silencer of P-C-S-K-nine, both entering investigator-initiated trials in China. And Takeda launched Orzeyful, oveporexton, the first orexin-two receptor agonist for narcolepsy type one, after the D-E-A gave it the light Schedule four classification.

Looking ahead: Heidi Overton's F-D-A commissioner nomination has a committee vote scheduled for Wednesday, and William Blair says it's unclear she has the votes. We hadn't seen an F-D-A decision on Summit and Akeso's ivonescimab by Friday night. Capricor's deramiocel decision is due on the twenty-second. We didn't find any confirmed Foresite portfolio news on Thursday's or Friday's wires.
