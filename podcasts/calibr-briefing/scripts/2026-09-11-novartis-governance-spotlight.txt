Travis, today's spotlight is the Novartis governance fight, and I want to be clear up front about why it belongs here rather than in a market-color slot. Two Novartis trials failed this week and I have already taken both of them apart on this show — pelacarsen on Sunday, del-desiran on Tuesday. What happened overnight is different in kind. A large institutional shareholder went to Reuters and argued, on the record, that the defect at Novartis is not the science and not the chief executive but the board's ability to evaluate clinical evidence before signing a check. That is a claim about how drug-development risk gets priced inside a buyer, and if it gains traction it changes what your assets are worth when they walk into a corporate development meeting. So I want to do two things: lay out the indictment precisely, and then tell you which parts of it are right, because roughly half of it is not.

Here is the sequence. On the first of the month Novartis reported that remibrutinib, its oral B-T-K inhibitor, hit the primary endpoint in both Phase three trials in relapsing multiple sclerosis against teriflunomide, with no liver safety signal — which in the B-T-K-in-multiple-sclerosis class is the whole ballgame, and the stock rose about six percent. Then Monday, pelacarsen: eighty percent lowering of lipoprotein little-a and no reduction in cardiovascular events, and the shares gave back three percent. Then Tuesday, del-desiran: the Phase three HARBOR study in myotonic dystrophy type one missed its primary endpoint, and the stock fell more than ten percent in a single session, the worst day since March twenty twenty, roughly thirty billion dollars of market capitalization. That is the setup for Wednesday and Thursday, when the shareholders started talking.

The loudest is David Samra at Artisan Partners, managing director and a founding partner of the firm's international value group. His framing: the party is over. He wants Chairman Giovanni Caforio to act, and the ask has three parts. One, better talent on the board and better oversight of acquisitions — in his words, the acquisition track record is not very good. Two, accountability with teeth: if you do a twelve-billion-dollar deal and it goes to zero, management needs to be penalised for that. Three, and this is the part I think is the most consequential, a compensation structure built on real economic outcomes rather than adjusted metrics that exclude write-downs. He is not calling for Narasimhan's head. He credits him with running the business well and with more than sixty percent share appreciation since twenty eighteen. He is saying the deals were the board's job and the board did not do it.

And he does not stop at del-desiran. He raises MorphoSys, which Novartis bought in twenty twenty-four for about two point nine billion dollars for pelabresib, a B-E-T bromodomain inhibitor in myelofibrosis, and on which Novartis recorded an eight-hundred-million-dollar impairment between announcing the acquisition and closing it. Read that again: a quarter of the purchase price written off before the transaction had even completed. Whatever you think of the del-desiran outcome — and Phase three trials fail — MorphoSys is the strongest single item in this indictment, because a write-down that lands inside the closing period is not a statement about biology. It is a statement about the price.

The other shareholders are not all in the same place, and the spread is informative. Michael Hannig at D-J-E Kapital wants deals in the five-to-ten-billion-dollar range for late-stage assets with rigorous due diligence on the clinical evidence. Gillian Hollenstein at the Point Capital Navigator Fund says Novartis should have done smaller acquisitions and that it is going to take a while for confidence to return. Daniel Bolanowski at Arctic Asset Management says the issue is a deeper trust problem about business development that is bigger than any one failure. And Markus Manns at Union Investment takes the opposite view — that these outcomes sit inside the normal probability distribution of drug development, and the real consequence is simply that Novartis now has to work harder to hit its post-twenty-thirty targets. Guggenheim's analysts had said before the readout that del-desiran's success in HARBOR was crucial for validating the Avidity deal, which is as close to a pre-registered prediction as sell-side research gets.

Now let me separate the claim into the part that holds and the part that does not, because this matters for how you should generalize from it.

The part that holds is del-desiran, and I argued this on Tuesday before anyone was calling for board changes. Avidity's functional evidence in myotonic dystrophy came from an open-label extension benchmarked against an external natural-history cohort, on a performance-based primary endpoint — video hand opening time — that patients repeat, on camera, at every visit, knowing they are on drug. There are at least three independent mechanisms by which that design flatters a result, and all of them push the same direction. This is not hindsight and it is not specialist knowledge. The question "what is the comparator in the study that generated the efficacy signal" is a question a competent board member asks in twenty seconds, and the answer was available in the published record. So on this one, Samra's structural point lands: the diligence failure was legible in advance, and the twelve billion dollars was paid on a package whose central assumption had never met a concurrent placebo arm.

The part that does not hold is almost everything else. Take pelacarsen first. That is not an acquisition at all — it is an antisense oligonucleotide licensed from Ionis, and lipoprotein little-a is one of the best genetically validated targets in all of cardiovascular medicine: Mendelian randomization, dose-response across the allele range, the works. Pelacarsen lowered the thing it was designed to lower by eighty percent and the events did not follow. That is a target-validity failure, the one category of failure no amount of board scrutiny can anticipate, because the only instrument that can detect it is the outcomes trial itself. Folding pelacarsen into an M-and-A governance complaint is category error, and it is the tell that some of this week's commentary is stock-price-driven rather than diligence-driven.

Then take the phrase "goes to zero," which is doing a lot of rhetorical work and is factually wrong. Novartis did not buy one asset from Avidity. It bought a delivery platform — antibody-oligonucleotide conjugation, a transferrin-receptor antibody chemically linked to an oligonucleotide, which is the first thing that has reliably gotten nucleic acids into skeletal muscle — plus three clinical programs. And on the same day HARBOR missed, the F-D-A accepted the biologics license application for del-zota, the exon-forty-four-skipping conjugate in Duchenne, for priority review under accelerated approval. The third program, del-brax, targets D-U-X-four in facioscapulohumeral dystrophy, hit its primary biomarker endpoint, and has an accelerated-approval path open with a confirmatory Phase three running. So the honest accounting is that the deal's most valuable asset failed and its regulatory nearest-term asset is under priority review. You can argue twelve billion was too much. You cannot argue it went to zero, and a shareholder who says it did is telling you he is marking the position to the share price rather than to the pipeline.

Which brings me to the demand I would actually take seriously if I were on that board, and it is the third one, the boring one. Samra wants compensation metrics that reflect real economics instead of adjusted figures that exclude write-downs. Every large pharma reports core or adjusted earnings that strip out acquisition-related impairments and amortization, and every large pharma pays management substantially on those adjusted figures. The mechanical consequence is that the accounting cost of overpaying for an asset is removed from the number that determines the bonus. If you wanted to design an incentive that produces exactly the behavior Samra is complaining about — a thirty-billion-dollar, three-year acquisition and partnership program with insufficient scrutiny per deal — that is the incentive you would design. Board composition is a slow and easily cosmetic fix. Moving impairments back into the compensation metric is a fast one, and it is the only demand on the list that would change a decision before it is made.

Now the part that bears on your work. The reason Novartis is buying at this pace is not appetite, it is arithmetic: United States patents on Cosentyx, Kisqali and Kesimpta expire between twenty twenty-nine and twenty thirty-one, and those three represent roughly thirty-eight percent of first-half sales. That gap does not get closed internally on that timeline. So the acquisitions are not optional. The only variables are size, stage and evidence standard — and this week's episode pushes all three in the same direction. Hannig said it out loud: five to ten billion, late-stage assets, rigorous diligence on clinical evidence. That is a preference for a de-risked single asset over a platform story with one flattering open-label extension attached.

If that preference propagates — and I think it propagates, because the failure was public, expensive and legible — then the pricing of private biotech shifts in a specific way. The premium on platform narratives compresses. The premium on a properly controlled randomized readout expands, and expands more than the cost of running one. That is an unusual moment, because it means the marginal dollar spent on a concurrent placebo arm now returns more in valuation than the same dollar spent on a second indication or a bigger open-label extension. For an academic or foundation-backed translational program, that is close to good news: rigor is the thing you can actually afford to buy, and it is the thing that just repriced upward.

So, three things to watch. First, whether Caforio adds directors with genuine drug-development and clinical-diligence credentials before the annual meeting, and whether they land on a committee with a mandate over transactions rather than in the general pool. Note the irony sitting in the middle of this story: the chairman being asked to tighten deal scrutiny is the former chief executive of Bristol Myers Squibb, a company whose defining act was a seventy-four-billion-dollar acquisition of Celgene. Second, the compensation metric, for the reason I just gave — that is the one with a transmission mechanism. Third, del-zota's priority review and del-brax's confirmatory Phase three, because those two readouts, not HARBOR, will determine whether the Avidity deal was mispriced or merely early. If del-zota is approved and del-brax confirms, the governance story fades into a bad-luck story and Manns at Union Investment turns out to have been right. If they stumble, then twelve billion dollars bought a delivery platform that delivers and a clinical thesis that does not, and this week will read as the moment the market started charging big pharma a discount for platform acquisitions — which is a cost every seller in this industry ends up paying, including the ones with good data.
