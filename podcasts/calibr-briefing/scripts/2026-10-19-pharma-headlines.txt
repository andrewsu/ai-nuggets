First, the spotlight. Gilead's emvistegrast, an oral once-daily inhibitor of the alpha-four-beta-seven integrin, hit in the Phase 2 SWIFT study in moderately to severely active ulcerative colitis. At the two-hundred-milligram dose, clinical remission at twelve weeks was thirty-one percent against eight percent on placebo. Clinical response was sixty-six percent against thirty-seven. Endoscopic improvement, thirty-nine against twelve. Mucosal healing, twenty-four against nine. Safety looked clean: adverse events were actually slightly lower on drug than on placebo, and there were no cases of progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy. STAT reported the result exceeded Wall Street expectations, and Gilead's clinical development lead for inflammation, Swati Tole, told STAT the drug has, in her words, the potential to be a best-in-class oral for ulcerative colitis. Phase 3 monotherapy is planned for twenty twenty-seven. I'll take the whole thing apart in the spotlight, including why the comparison everyone will make this morning is the wrong one.

Second, Nouscom. The Basel company closed an oversubscribed hundred-and-twenty-million-euro, hundred-and-thirty-five-million-dollar Series D, co-led by M Ventures and Revelation Partners, with new money from Angelini Industries, the European Investment Bank through its co-investment facility, and Neva, the Intesa Sanpaolo venture arm. Every existing investor came along, including Five A M, Versant, E Q T Life Sciences, Andera, Bpifrance and C D P. What makes this more than a financing is what the money buys: a single global registration-enabling trial of NOUS-two-oh-nine, an off-the-shelf neoantigen immunotherapy, for cancer interception in Lynch syndrome carriers. Not treatment. Interception, in people who do not yet have cancer. The construct is two viral vectors delivering two hundred and nine shared frameshift-peptide neoantigens, the ones generated by mismatch-repair deficiency, which is why it can be off the shelf rather than personalized. FDA has granted Fast Track. The Phase 1b/2 abstract going to ESMO on Sunday the twenty-fifth reports that every evaluable carrier dosed was free of advanced adenomas and colorectal cancer by colonoscopy at year one, forty-three patients, and year two, seventeen patients, with T-cell responses boosted by annual re-vaccination. Be candid about the design: single-arm, the comparator is expected incidence rather than randomization, and most carriers in a two-year window were never going to develop a cancer anyway. The real lesson is regulatory. They settled an event-free-survival endpoint that counts precancerous events with both FDA and EMA before they raised. In interception, the endpoint is the product.

Third, Roche's ESMO preview, out of Basel this morning. The headline is Krascendo one: a Phase 3 head-to-head of divarasib, Roche's next-generation KRAS G-twelve-C inhibitor, against approved first-generation inhibitors in previously treated G-twelve-C-mutant non-small cell lung cancer. Roche says divarasib was superior, and it goes to a Presidential Symposium as a late-breaker. This is the only global head-to-head in the class, which means that for the first time the question is not whether a G-twelve-C inhibitor beats chemotherapy but whether the second generation displaces sotorasib and adagrasib outright. Roche also brings patient-reported outcomes from lidERA, the Phase 3 of the oral SERD giredestrant in early breast cancer, arguing the efficacy gain does not cost tolerability or quality of life, and Phase 3 data in Chinese patients on Tam-Peli, a next-generation B-seven-H-three targeted antibody-drug conjugate, in metastatic nasopharyngeal carcinoma. Levi Garraway framed the set as depth across the pipeline. Watch what the divarasib result does to the rest of the G-twelve-C field.

Fourth, Zentalis published the first overall survival data for azenosertib, its WEE1 inhibitor, in the ESMO abstract for a rapid oral on the twenty-third. In the DENALI part 1b study, median overall survival in cyclin-E-one-positive platinum-resistant ovarian cancer was fifteen point eight months. The company's claim is that cyclin E one, scored retrospectively by immunohistochemistry on a proprietary cutoff, is a selective and predictive biomarker, and that this number compares favorably with historical single-agent chemotherapy benchmarks. Hold that lightly: overall survival was a secondary endpoint in a hundred-and-two-patient study, and the comparison is to history, not to a control arm. But it is the datapoint that carries the biomarker-selected Part 2 and the ASPENOVA Phase 3.

Fifth, and this is live as you listen. Disc Medicine hosts a corporate webcast at eight a.m. Eastern this morning to discuss topline results from APOLLO, the Phase 3 trial of bitopertin in erythropoietic protoporphyria. Bitopertin is an oral glycine transporter one inhibitor, in-licensed from Roche in twenty twenty-one, that works upstream in heme biosynthesis by limiting the glycine supply to developing red cells, which lowers the protoporphyrin that makes these patients burn in sunlight. Disc already has the asset in front of FDA on an accelerated pathway. A company that schedules a webcast rather than a press release at seven is a company that wants to talk through the number, which cuts both ways. I'll have the result tomorrow.

Sixth, briefly. Cyrano Therapeutics said its Phase 2 FLAVOR study of C-Y-R-zero-six-four met its primary safety endpoint in persistent post-viral loss of smell, a hundred and fifty-one patients across fourteen US sites, no treatment-related serious adverse events. The efficacy signal was in patients under sixty, two-thirds of the trial, where the high dose beat placebo on two prespecified patient-reported smell scales over twenty-four weeks. Small, subgroup-driven, patient-reported. But there is no approved therapy in this indication at all, and the data were presented at the American Rhinologic Society.
