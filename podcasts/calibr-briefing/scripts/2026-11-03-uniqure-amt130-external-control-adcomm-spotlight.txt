Today's spotlight is uniQure. We covered this program in June, when the F-D-A first agreed that three-year data could support a filing for accelerated approval in Huntington's disease. I won't go back through the mechanism. In short, it's an A-A-V five vector, surgically infused into the caudate and putamen, that delivers a microRNA to silence huntingtin. Yesterday the agency accepted the application under priority review, with a decision date of May first, twenty twenty-seven, and said it is tentatively planning an advisory committee. Since June, the evidence has changed shape and the argument has moved to a different endpoint. Those two shifts are what the panel will actually be judging, so that's the focus today.

Start with what changed in the data, because it changes the take from June. Back then we called the three-year result the cleanest disease-modifying signal in a major neurodegenerative disease. On the high dose that was a seventy-five percent slowing on the composite Unified Huntington's Disease Rating Scale, the c-U-H-D-R-S, against a matched external control drawn from Enroll-H-D, the big natural-history registry. Then in September uniQure released two new cuts on the same day. The updated three-year analysis, now with fifteen high-dose patients, looked even better: eighty percent slowing on the composite and sixty-seven percent on Total Functional Capacity, the clinician-rated scale for work, finances, chores and independent living. But the four-year analysis, with twelve high-dose patients, came in at forty-four percent slowing on the composite. That's not significant, with a p-value of point one four four. Total Functional Capacity held at sixty-one percent, nominal p of point oh oh eight. The stock fell close to forty percent that morning.

The company's explanation is the most important detail in the story. uniQure says the four-year number is understated because the updated Enroll-H-D database has more patients but also more missing data and survivor bias, which makes the control group look like it's declining more slowly than it really is. Using the prior version of the control data, the same four-year comparison gives fifty-four percent on the composite and sixty-eight percent on function. That may well be right. But think about what it means. The size of the treatment effect moves by ten points or more depending on which version of the registry you compare against. That's the core weakness of an external control in a disease that progresses slowly: the treated patients are real, and the comparator is a modeled group whose composition changes with every database refresh. The trial did have a concurrent comparator, ten sham-surgery patients in the U-S cohort. But four of them crossed over to treatment after about a year, so there's no long randomized comparison to fall back on. Everything past year one rests on the registry.

The second shift is the endpoint. The three-year composite score is still the regulatory anchor for the application. But uniQure's confirmatory study, aligned with the F-D-A, uses Total Functional Capacity at thirty-six months as its primary endpoint: about two hundred patients, randomized two to one against standard of care, with screening in the U-S planned by the end of this year. William Blair's Lachlan Hanbury-Brown made the case directly. He called concerns about the softening composite "overdone," partly because it "is not the primary endpoint of the confirmatory study or the basis for the current B-L-A," and said function "increased separation from the external control group from 3 to 4 years." Stifel's September read was similar: the four-year data "continues to clearly support a drug effect, though there is regression in efficacy on c-U-H-D-R-S... whereas T-F-C data hold up."

Now compare that to the best-resourced competitor. P-T-C five-eighteen, the oral huntingtin splicing modulator still in Phase two when we covered this in June, is now Novartis's votoplam. Its Phase three, INVEST-H-D, began in March. It's double-blind and placebo-controlled, with about seven hundred seventy patients, and its primary endpoint is the change in the composite rating scale at thirty-six months, the same measure that softened for uniQure. Estimated primary completion is twenty thirty. So the field's largest sponsor is running a fully blinded trial on the composite. uniQure is asking for approval on a small external-control dataset and confirming against standard of care on the functional scale. There's no sham in the confirmatory study, even though the F-D-A strongly recommended a sham-controlled trial in March. Patients will know whether they had brain surgery, and functional capacity is rated from an interview. That doesn't make the design wrong; repeating sham neurosurgery for years raises real ethical and practical problems. But it does mean the confirmatory evidence will be less blinded than a competitor's ordinary pill trial.

Then there's the regulatory history, which explains why the market treats this as a question about people as much as data. In November twenty twenty-five, five weeks after the three-year data, the F-D-A told uniQure it no longer agreed that the Phase one-two data were adequate primary evidence. Then-Commissioner Marty Makary and then-C-B-E-R director Vinay Prasad both made public remarks that appeared to refer to the program. Makary described an unnamed brain-delivered therapy the agency had been "pressured" to approve despite finding no clinical benefit. On March second the agency formally recommended a sham-controlled study. The stock fell forty-two percent over two days, then rebounded fifty-eight percent within the week after Prasad announced he would resign. In June, with Karim Mikhail as acting director, the agency reversed again. Mikhail is now the permanent C-B-E-R director. Same data, different leadership, opposite answers. uniQure's C-E-O, Matt Kapusta, said in September that it "really doesn't matter who's leading CBER." The stock chart disagrees.

That's why the advisory committee matters more than the acceptance itself. Accepting the application is an administrative step. Stifel called it "an incrementally positive sign on the prospects of accelerated approval," and William Blair said it "further removes regulatory risk." But the stock slipped about three percent yesterday to roughly twenty-four dollars, and it's still below where it was before the four-year data. The market already expected the filing to be accepted. What it can't price yet is how an outside panel handles three questions. First, whether a few dozen treated patients against a registry, with an effect size that depends on the database version, counts as reasonably likely to predict benefit. Second, whether the composite or the functional scale is the right measure. Third, safety: about seventeen percent of high-dose patients had treatment-related serious inflammation in the central nervous system, all of which resolved. For the agency, a panel also provides cover for whichever answer it gives, which makes sense after a year of reversals.

On commercial stakes, GEN notes that after the three-year data one analyst put peak sales at four billion dollars by twenty thirty-two, against a consensus closer to one point seven billion. The gap reflects a delivery question we raised in June: a one-time therapy given through M-R-I-guided stereotactic neurosurgery is limited by how many centers can do the procedure, not just by how many patients exist.

So, what to take from this. If A-M-T one thirty is approved in May, it sets a precedent well beyond Huntington's: accelerated approval of a neurodegenerative gene therapy on a registry comparator, with the confirmatory endpoint different from the one the effect was first measured on. Any sponsor in a slowly progressing genetic disease with a good natural-history cohort, A-L-S, the spinocerebellar ataxias, the leukodystrophies, will cite it. But the case for it is weaker than in June. Since then the effect on the composite has shrunk with more follow-up, and it turns out to depend on which version of the control you use. A rejection or a narrow panel vote would push the field back toward the expensive blinded designs that Novartis is already running.

Three things to watch. The advisory committee date and the questions the F-D-A sends the panel: if they're framed around Total Functional Capacity, that's a tell. Whether confirmatory screening actually starts by the end of the year, since uniQure has said it expects the trial to be well underway by the time of any approval. And the U-K regulator, which has its own application and could rule independently. Before any of that, uniQure has to get through a public hearing where the counterargument has been written for a year and was voiced by people who used to run the agency.
