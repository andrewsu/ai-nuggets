Today's spotlight is Chai Discovery's G-S-K deal, announced Friday morning, and the question underneath it, which is whether the market for A-I antibody design has moved from pilot projects to procurement.

Start with what was actually announced, because the structure of the deal is more informative than the headline. G-S-K gets access to Chai's platform — the structure prediction models and the generative design models — to support discovery across its pipeline. Terms were not disclosed. What makes it different from the usual A-I partnership press release is the sequence. Before signing, G-S-K ran an evaluation. Chai's models produced designs against G-S-K's targets with no target-specific training on those targets. G-S-K then synthesized and tested those designs in its own laboratories, on its own assays, with its own people. According to both companies, G-S-K found binders to every target it tested. Christopher Austin, G-S-K's senior vice president of R and D technologies, said they ran a panel of diverse targets and saw designs with strong binding affinities. Then they bought.

That is the thing worth sitting with. We have spent two years reading A-I design results that were generated, benchmarked and reported by the company selling the model. Here a large pharma with a mature biologics organization and no incentive to flatter a vendor ran the experiment itself and came back with a result good enough to write a check. It is still not a controlled head-to-head against that pharma's internal discovery engine — nobody has published one of those, and G-S-K has not said how many targets, what class they were, what affinity threshold counted as success, or what its own hybridoma and display campaigns would have delivered on the same panel in the same time. But it is a meaningful step up the evidence ladder from a company blog post.

Now the technical record, because you should know what G-S-K was probably looking at before it ran the panel. Chai's public results come in two installments. The first, Chai-2 in mid twenty twenty-five, is the one everyone quotes. They took fifty-two protein antigens that had no existing antibody binders in SAbDab — so no structural template to lean on — and designed at most twenty antibodies or nanobodies per target in a single round, no iteration, no affinity maturation. Sixteen percent of designs bound. At least one binder came out for fifty percent of the targets. For miniprotein binders the hit rate was sixty-eight percent with picomolar affinities routinely. Against prior computational design methods, which run around a tenth of a percent, that is roughly a hundredfold improvement, and it collapses hit discovery from a screening campaign into a twenty-well experiment over about two weeks.

The second installment, late twenty twenty-five, is the one that should matter more to you, because it is the one that addresses the standard objection. Binding is not a drug. Most of the de novo design literature stops at single domains, nanobodies and single-chain variable fragments — formats that are easy to design and are not what you develop. Chai tested eighty-eight designed full-length I-g-G molecules across twenty-eight antigens on four developability criteria, using the thresholds from the Jain analyses of marketed therapeutic antibodies. Eighty-six percent of the designs had zero or one flagged property, which is the bar people use for preclinical candidate selection. Twenty-four of twenty-eight antigens yielded at least one design with a clean profile. So the output is not a binder you then spend a year engineering into something manufacturable. It is closer to a lead.

They also did two things that are harder to fake than a hit rate. They solved cryo-E-M structures of five designed antibody-antigen complexes and found that each antibody bound precisely at the epitope the model had targeted, with sub-angstrom agreement in the complementarity-determining region loops — the part of the molecule that is hardest to predict and the part that determines the epitope. And they went after targets where screening traditionally fails. Six G-protein-coupled receptors, ten to seventy-three designs each, at least one binder to every one of them, and agonists to two of them straight out of the model. G-P-C-R-s are about a third of all approved drug targets and only three approved antibodies address them, precisely because you cannot easily present a membrane protein to a mouse or a phage library in a native conformation.

Then the result that connects directly to your first headline this morning. They designed against peptide-M-H-C complexes, which is the hardest specificity problem in the field because the M-H-C scaffold is identical across all of them and the only thing distinguishing a cancer target from a healthy cell is one or two peptide residues sitting in the groove. Testing twenty-seven to fifty designs against three therapeutically relevant complexes, they got two hits against the K-RAS G-twelve-V peptide-M-H-C — and those binders did not bind G-twelve-D and did not bind wild-type K-RAS. That is single-residue discrimination on a target class the T-cell-engager world has been trying to drug for a decade. And it lands on the same morning that Revolution Medicines is being marked down because its small molecule does not cleanly separate G-twelve-D from G-twelve-V. The contrast is instructive. In small molecule space, mutation selectivity is a brutal medicinal chemistry problem. In designed-binder space, it is becoming a specification you hand to a model.

Now the caveats, and there are real ones. First, every number I just gave you is Chai's own, from technical reports that have not been peer reviewed, on panels Chai selected, with assays Chai ran. The G-S-K evaluation is the first substantial independent counterweight, and it was reported in a press release, not a paper. Second, the model G-S-K is licensing is probably not the one those numbers describe. Chai-3, the current generation, was first licensed to Pfizer in June. Chai says it roughly doubles Chai-2's success rate and produces antibodies that meet therapeutic affinity standards in about half of cases. There is no public benchmark for Chai-3 at all. Press accounts of its hit rate conflict with each other. So the best-documented public result is a generation behind the commercial product, and the commercial product's claims are unaudited.

Third, and this is the one I would hold onto: not a single Chai-designed molecule is in a human being. Not one is in I-N-D-enabling studies that anyone has disclosed. The entire thesis — that computational design compresses discovery timelines and opens undruggable targets — is supported by biophysics, structures and developability panels, and by zero clinical data. The honest framing of the competitive question is the one a skeptic put well recently: the real competitor is a mouse. Immunization plus hybridoma selection is slow and expensive and fails on hard targets, but it has a sixty-year track record of producing molecules that work in patients, and it solves problems the designer never specified — polyspecificity, immunogenicity, in vivo half-life — through selection rather than prediction.

So what did G-S-K buy. I think it bought optionality at a price that is trivially small relative to one failed program, and it bought it on targets where its own engine has probably already failed. That is the pattern across all six deals. Pharma is not replacing discovery. It is buying a second shot at the targets in the drawer — membrane proteins, conformational epitopes, peptide-M-H-C, anything where you need a specific epitope rather than whatever the immune system happens to find immunogenic. Epitope specification is the actual product here, more than hit rate. A screening campaign gives you binders to the dominant epitope. A model gives you binders to the epitope you chose, which is what you need if you are building a biparatopic, an A-D-C that must internalize, or a T-cell engager where the geometry of the synapse determines whether it works.

Which brings this back to Calibr. You should read the Chai-G-S-K deal against the Xaira disclosure from last week, because the two companies are making different bets and only one of them is yours. Xaira is building programs — X-A-one and X-A-four, a therapeutics company that happens to design computationally, with the bar set at what they call a progressable binder. Chai is building a tool and selling it to everyone, six times in nine months, on top of a four hundred million dollar Series C in July that followed a one hundred thirty million dollar round at a one point three billion dollar valuation seven months earlier. If Chai is right, de novo design stops being a capability a discovery organization builds and becomes a utility it rents, the way nobody runs their own sequencer core anymore. That is a very different strategic question for an academic drug discovery institute than the Xaira question. The Xaira question is who out-designs whom. The Chai question is whether, eighteen months from now, model access is a commodity you procure and the differentiation moves entirely to target selection, epitope choice, translational biology and the assays that tell you which designed binder actually does something in a human system. Those are the things Calibr is good at and a model is not.

The signal to watch is not the next partnership. On the current cadence Chai will announce one in six weeks and it will tell you nothing new. The signal is the first partner that discloses a designed molecule entering I-N-D-enabling studies, with a target and an epitope attached. Until then, a hundred percent of targets bound in G-S-K's wet lab is an impressive engineering result and an unresolved drug discovery question.
