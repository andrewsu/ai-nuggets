Number one, and today's spotlight. A one-week countdown with a plot twist. Capricor Therapeutics shares jumped about sixty percent after chief executive Linda Marbán told investors on the second-quarter earnings call that the FDA appears open to reviewing an amended application for deramiocel — the company's cell therapy for Duchenne muscular dystrophy. This matters because just two weeks ago an FDA advisory panel voted nine to three that the evidence did not support the drug for the heart-muscle problem, the cardiomyopathy, that the original application was built around. But the same panel was noticeably warmer on a different signal: the drug's effect on skeletal muscle, specifically upper-limb function, where Capricor's pivotal trial showed a fifty-four percent slowing of decline. So the company is trying to thread the needle — narrow the approvable indication to the muscle data the panel liked, before the decision deadline on August 22nd. We go deep on this in the spotlight.

Number two, a courtroom fight in the heart-drug wars. Cytokinetics filed suit in Delaware against Bristol Myers Squibb and its subsidiary MyoKardia, asking a judge to declare a Bristol Myers patent invalid and not infringed. The patent covers using a cardiac myosin inhibitor after a patient reduces or stops beta-blockers in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy — and Cytokinetics says Bristol Myers landed it despite having, in their words, zero involvement in developing their drug. The two drugs are direct rivals: Cytokinetics markets aficamten, brand name Myqorzo, and Bristol Myers sells mavacamten, brand name Camzyos — both cardiac myosin inhibitors that ease the obstruction in a thickened heart. The irony is thick: Cytokinetics originally invented this whole class, spun it out into MyoKardia, and then watched Bristol Myers buy MyoKardia for over thirteen billion dollars. Now the inventor is suing to keep its own newer drug free of the acquirer's patents.

Number three, a milestone two decades in the making. Pfizer and Valneva announced that the European Medicines Agency has validated the marketing application for their Lyme disease vaccine candidate, which means the formal review has begun. The vaccine is a six-valent shot that targets the outer surface protein A of the Lyme bacterium — it trains the immune system to attack the bacteria while they're still inside the tick, before transmission. The filing rests on more than seventy percent efficacy in the Phase 3 VALOR trial. If it clears, this would be the first human Lyme vaccine in more than twenty years. Valneva shares rose about twenty percent on the news.

Number four, a structural shift worth keeping on your radar. A BioPharma Dive analysis put hard numbers on the China licensing boom: the average upfront payment Western drugmakers hand Chinese biotechs has climbed two hundred thirty percent since 2022, to about a hundred seventy-two million dollars so far this year, and 2026 is on pace to break last year's record. The driver is simple math — a patent cliff that could strip as much as two hundred billion dollars in annual revenue from big pharma between now and 2030, colliding with shrinking internal research budgets. Licensing a China-developed asset is a capital-efficient alternative to a full acquisition, and the bargain-basement era is clearly over.

Number five, and your new-company watch. Boulevard Bio launched out of stealth with sixty-five million dollars from Deerfield Management, co-founded by the autoimmune researcher Georg Schett. Its lead molecule is a bispecific antibody that hits both of the B-cell survival factors known as BAFF and APRIL at once, aimed first at immunoglobulin A nephropathy — a kidney disease driven by antibody deposits. Early healthy-volunteer data support a quarterly dosing schedule, which would be a real convenience edge in a suddenly crowded B-cell-depletion field. It's a clean example of precision immunology money still flowing into well-credentialed founding teams.
