Today's spotlight is sevasemten, the Becker muscular dystrophy drug Servier bought from Edgewise for one point five five billion dollars upfront. On Tuesday Servier said the pivotal GRAND CANYON study missed. No statistically significant benefit on the North Star Ambulatory Assessment at eighteen months, and every secondary endpoint missed as well. Safety stayed clean. Servier hasn't released any numbers. It said only that the results "are not aligned with certain findings observed in prior clinical studies, including the Phase 2 CANYON study," and that it is reanalyzing everything. So we're working from the earlier data, and the earlier data is where the lesson is.

Start with the mechanism, because it explains both why people believed it and why it may have failed. In Becker and Duchenne, the muscle lacks enough working dystrophin, the protein that anchors the fiber's internal skeleton to its membrane. Without it, every contraction stresses the membrane. Fibers tear, leak their contents into the blood, and over years they get replaced by fat and scar tissue. The fast-twitch fibers, which generate the most force, are hit hardest. Sevasemten partly inhibits fast skeletal myosin, the motor protein in those fibers, to take the edge off the contractions that do the damage. It doesn't fix dystrophin. The idea is to protect the muscle you have, which is very different from the exon-skipping and gene therapy approaches that dominate this field. It's also a pill, and it was meant to work in any patient regardless of mutation.

Now, what CANYON actually showed in December 2024. It enrolled forty adults with Becker, randomized three to one, for twelve months. The primary endpoint wasn't function. It was creatine kinase, the enzyme that leaks out of damaged muscle. That fell twenty-eight percent versus placebo, with a p-value of point oh two. Fast skeletal troponin I, a marker specific to damaged fast fibers, fell seventy-seven percent. On the North Star score, the functional measure, the difference was one point one points favoring the drug, with a p-value of point one six. Edgewise said plainly that the trial wasn't powered for function. It also noted that the treated group started with more advanced disease than placebo, which ought to make a positive trend more impressive, not less. The treated group's score held flat, while the placebo group, only twelve people, declined about as much as natural history predicts. On top of that came the open-label extension, MESA, where patients on drug for more than three years kept stable North Star scores, compared against natural-history expectations.

Edgewise then said the CANYON functional data supported powering GRAND CANYON at more than ninety-five percent to detect a significant North Star difference at eighteen months. The trial was originally planned for about a hundred twenty patients and enrolled a hundred seventy-five. A well-powered, larger, longer trial came back with nothing.

There are two readings. Neither can be settled until Servier publishes, but they lead to different conclusions.

The first is that the biomarkers measured engagement, not disease modification. If a drug reduces how hard fast fibers contract, it will reduce how much enzyme leaks out of them almost by definition. A seventy-seven percent drop in a fast-fiber-specific troponin shows the drug is hitting its target in the right fiber type. It doesn't show that the muscle is being replaced by fat more slowly. In a slowly progressive disease, the step from less leak this month to more walking ability in eighteen months is a long inference, and the Phase 2 was too small to test it. On this reading, the North Star trend was mostly noise sitting on twelve placebo patients, and the open-label stability came from comparing against a historical control rather than a concurrent arm.

The second reading is about the comparator. Becker progresses slowly and very unevenly. Some adults hold their walking ability for decades, and the North Star score has a ceiling. If the GRAND CANYON placebo arm declined less over eighteen months than natural history predicted, then a drug that genuinely slows damage could still fail to separate within the trial window. That's the same problem we went through yesterday with uniQure in Huntington's: an effect measured against an external control looks different when the comparator is real people in the same trial. Here, the Phase 2 effect leaned on a twelve-person placebo arm and the extension leaned on natural history. The first fully powered, concurrently controlled test is the one that missed.

The practical point is the same either way. A damage biomarker moving in the right direction was not enough to predict function in a slowly progressive muscle disease. That should change how much weight anyone puts on creatine kinase or troponin as an early read in neuromuscular programs.

Next, who held the risk, because this is where the deal structure matters. Edgewise signed with Servier on June first and closed on July thirteenth. GRAND CANYON's primary completion date was September seventh. So Servier bought the whole binary outcome, paying full price before the last patient finished. Edgewise kept one point five five billion in cash and turned itself into a cardiovascular company. The proceeds, it says, fund EDG-seventy-five-hundred in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy through potential approval, with a Phase 3 starting this quarter, plus a Phase 2 of EDG-fifteen-four-hundred in heart failure with preserved ejection fraction. Edgewise's stock closed Tuesday down about one percent, at thirty-seven sixty-eight. The market had put little value on the milestones, and the downside landed on someone else's balance sheet. Servier is private and governed by a foundation, so there's no stock move to read. The cost will show up as an impairment.

Strategically, Edgewise did what a lot of single-asset biotechs think about and few manage: it sold the asset to a strategic buyer before the binary readout and used the proceeds to finance the next platform. For portfolio companies with a late-stage asset in front of a readout, that's the template. It works when a buyer wants late-stage entry badly enough to pay before the data. Servier's stated goal is to become a major player in rare neurology by 2030, and that's exactly the kind of buyer who will.

There's a nice scientific contrast inside Edgewise's own story. EDG-seventy-five-hundred is also a sarcomere modulator, the same turn-down-the-motor idea applied to heart muscle. In hypertrophic cardiomyopathy the motor itself is the disease, because the heart is hypercontractile, and mavacamten, already approved, and Cytokinetics' aficamten have shown that dialing it down improves symptoms and function. In dystrophic skeletal muscle the motor is normal and the defect is the membrane. So reducing contraction treats a downstream consequence, not the cause. GRAND CANYON doesn't read across to the cardiac programs, and the mechanistic distinction is a reasonable explanation for why one works and the other may not.

What's left for sevasemten is Duchenne, in two Phase 2 studies. LYNX has seventy-six boys, with primary completion expected in January 2027. FOX has forty-three, expected in March. Duchenne progresses faster, so there's more damage to prevent within a trial window, and that argues it's a fairer test. But the main supporting evidence for the mechanism just failed, and Servier hasn't said whether its Duchenne plans change. Becker is back to having no approved therapy, for an estimated twelve thousand patients across the U-S, Europe and Japan. The Muscular Dystrophy Association's statement made the most constructive point: GRAND CANYON advanced the outcome measures and trial design for a disease where nobody had run a study this size before, and the next sponsor inherits that.

Three things to watch. First, the full dataset, especially how much the placebo arm declined, because that decides which of the two readings is right. Second, whether Servier reports the creatine kinase and troponin effects in GRAND CANYON. If they replicated while function didn't, that's the cleanest evidence yet that damage biomarkers don't stand in for function in muscular dystrophy. Third, the Duchenne data early next year.
