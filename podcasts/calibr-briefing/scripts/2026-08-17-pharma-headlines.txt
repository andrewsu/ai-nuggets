It was a quiet summer weekend, so today's roundup catches up on the deals and approvals that landed late last week, and the spotlight goes deep on a first-in-class cancer drug that also quietly rewrote a regulatory rulebook. Five items.

Number one, and today's spotlight: Bristol Myers Squibb has won accelerated approval for Zenbexus — that's iberdomide — the first drug in a brand-new class called CELMoDs, cereblon E3 ligase modulators, for relapsed or refractory multiple myeloma, in combination with Johnson and Johnson's Darzalex and dexamethasone. Two things make this a milestone. First, the mechanism: iberdomide doesn't just nudge myeloma cells the way the old Revlimid-style drugs do — it's a targeted protein degrader that hijacks the cell's own disposal machinery to actively destroy the proteins the cancer depends on. Second, and maybe bigger for the whole field, the FDA cleared it on the strength of MRD-negative complete responses — minimal residual disease, cancer driven below the threshold of detection — rather than waiting years for a survival readout. Forty-one percent of patients hit that mark, versus twenty-one on the comparator. I'll unpack why that endpoint matters so much in the spotlight.

Number two: the biotech I-P-O window is genuinely open again. Braveheart Bio priced an upsized offering and banked more than three hundred eighty million dollars, at a valuation around one point six billion. Braveheart's drug, B-H-B eighteen ninety-three, is a next-generation cardiac myosin inhibitor for hypertrophic cardiomyopathy — the same mechanism as Bristol's Camzyos and Cytokinetics' aficamten, but engineered for a deeper, faster drop in the heart's outflow obstruction with less hit to pumping function, which could mean less of the repeated echocardiogram monitoring that weighs on the first generation. Alongside Attovia and Kardigan's four-hundred-million-dollar raise, it's a clear sign public investors are back for clinical-stage stories with a clean mechanism.

Number three: Sentynl Therapeutics and Mereo BioPharma signed an option and license deal for alvelestat in alpha-one antitrypsin deficiency lung disease. Alvelestat is an oral neutrophil elastase inhibitor — it blocks the runaway enzyme that chews up lung tissue in this rare genetic disorder — and if approved, it would be the first oral therapy for a disease that affects up to eighty thousand Americans and has no targeted drug today. Mereo keeps rest-of-world rights and leads the global Phase 3; the deal is worth forty million up front and R&D, plus up to four hundred thirty-five million in milestones and tiered royalties.

Number four: a reverse merger creating a new fibrosis player. Skye Bioscience is combining with U.K.-based Redx Pharma to form Fibrx Therapeutics, a Nasdaq-listed, fibrosis-focused company, alongside about one hundred twenty-five million dollars in fresh financing. The lead program is Redx's R-X-C double-oh-eight, a gut-restricted pan-ROCK inhibitor — ROCK being a kinase central to tissue scarring — aimed at fibrostenotic Crohn's disease, with Phase 2 data expected in the second half of twenty twenty-eight. It's a clean example of capital consolidating around one mechanism and one organ.

Number five, the deal-making backdrop worth keeping in view: China is no longer the bargain basement. New data show the average upfront payment on a Western-Chinese licensing deal has climbed to around one hundred seventy million dollars — up thirty-six percent this year alone, and up two hundred thirty percent since twenty twenty-two. China out-licensing hit a record one hundred thirty-eight billion dollars last year and is on pace to beat it. But there's a twist for buyers: as upfronts rise, royalty floors are falling — from about seven percent down to five and a half. The discount era is over, even as the structure of these deals shifts.
