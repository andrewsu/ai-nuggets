This is your spotlight for Friday, September fourth, twenty twenty-six. Ionis, Zanvastro, and the cleanest natural experiment this field has handed us in years — because the same company, the same modality, the same route of administration and the same class of pediatric neurogenetic disease produced a total failure on Wednesday night and a first-ever approval on Thursday morning, and the difference between them is not what most people will assume it is.

Start with what got approved. The Food and Drug Administration cleared Zanvastro, zilganersen, as the first and only disease-modifying treatment for Alexander disease, in pediatric and adult patients. It is an antisense oligonucleotide given as an intrathecal bolus, fifty milligrams once every twelve weeks. The approval landed nineteen days before its September twenty-second action date and came with a rare pediatric disease priority review voucher.

Now the biology, because the biology is where this diverges from Angelman syndrome and almost everyone will file both stories under the same heading. Alexander disease is caused by mutations in G-F-A-P, glial fibrillary acidic protein — the intermediate filament that gives astrocytes their internal skeleton. Critically, these are not loss-of-function mutations. They are dominant, usually de novo, missense mutations that produce a protein which misfolds and aggregates. The mutant filament accumulates into inclusions called Rosenthal fibers, along with alpha-B-crystallin and heat shock protein twenty-seven, and the astrocyte's own stress response then drives more G-F-A-P expression, which produces more aggregate. It is a feed-forward toxic gain of function, and the white matter fails downstream of astrocytes that have stopped functioning as astrocytes. Alexander disease is a leukodystrophy, but the primary lesion is not in the oligodendrocyte at all.

So the therapeutic ask is: reduce the amount of G-F-A-P being made. And that is the single thing antisense chemistry does best. Knocking down the production of a toxic protein is the home turf of this modality — it is how tofersen works in S-O-D-one A-L-S, it is how eplontersen and the whole transthyretin field work. Compare that to what apazunersen was being asked to do in Angelman syndrome: degrade a long non-coding antisense transcript in order to de-repress an imprinted, silenced paternal allele, and then have the newly expressed ubiquitin ligase reshape a developing brain. Both are antisense oligonucleotides delivered into cerebrospinal fluid. Only one of them is a knockdown problem. The other is a gene reactivation problem wearing a knockdown problem's clothes.

There is also a permissive fact underneath Zanvastro that deserves to be said out loud, because it is the reason the safety case was ever tractable. G-F-A-P knockout animals — mice and rats — are essentially normal. They are more vulnerable to central nervous system injury, but they develop and behave. That means the therapeutic window for lowering G-F-A-P is unusually wide, and you can pursue substantial knockdown without needing exquisite allele selectivity. Very few neurodegenerative targets give you that. Most of the time the protein you want to lower is doing something you need.

Now the trial, which is where the real lesson sits. The pivotal study was a global Phase one-through-three, seamless design, fifty-four participants aged one and a half to fifty-three years, across thirteen sites in eight countries, in a disease with fewer than a thousand patients in the United States and perhaps one in a million worldwide. Participants were randomized two-to-one, double-blind, to zilganersen or to a matching placebo administered by intrathecal bolus injection once every twelve weeks through week forty-nine, with a sixty-week double-blind period. I want to be precise about that, because several outlets covering the approval described the comparator as untreated controls. It was not. The trial registry is explicit: a placebo-comparator arm, receiving a zilganersen-matching placebo by the same intrathecal route on the same schedule, with participants and investigators both masked. Ionis ran real, repeated, blinded lumbar punctures in children with an ultra-rare degenerative disease, and then rolled everyone into open-label extension afterward. That is a hard thing to ask families to consent to and a hard thing to enroll, and it is the reason we are talking about an approval instead of an argument.

The primary endpoint was percent change from baseline in the ten-meter walk test — gait speed — at week sixty-one. The fifty-milligram cohort showed stabilization while the control arm declined, a mean difference of thirty-three point three percent, with a p-value of zero point zero four one two.

Sit with that p-value, because being honest about it is more useful than celebrating it. Zero point zero four one, in a fifty-four-patient study split across two dose cohorts and a control arm, means the pivotal comparison is running on a couple dozen patients per side at most. In a well-populated therapeutic area you would call that fragile and want a confirmatory trial. Three things change how much weight it can bear here.

The first is direction of travel. Alexander disease is degenerative. The control arm gets worse — measurably, on the order of a third of its gait speed over sixty-one weeks. When the untreated trajectory is steeply downward, stabilization is a signal with a slope behind it. Now contrast that with Angelman syndrome, where the endpoint was the Bayley four cognitive raw score in children aged four to seventeen. That score counts items a child can pass, and it rises with age in any developing child, treated or not, over forty-eight weeks. Ultragenyx's control arm was going up. Ionis's control arm was going down. The very same magnitude of drug effect is far easier to detect against a declining baseline than against a rising one, and no amount of enrollment fixes that if the instrument's natural drift runs in the same direction as your hoped-for benefit. That is the variable. Not the modality, not the target, not the delivery route, not the company.

The second is the biomarker, and this is the single most important line in Thursday's release. Plasma G-F-A-P fell thirty-three point six percent versus control at week sixty-one, at a nominal p-value of zero point zero zero three. Look at what that does. It is a direct readout that the drug reached the compartment, engaged the target, and lowered the protein it was designed to lower — and the statistics on the target-engagement measure are an order of magnitude tighter than the statistics on the clinical measure. That is exactly the pattern you want. It converts a marginal clinical p-value from a coin flip into a coherent story with a mechanism attached. And it is precisely what Ultragenyx did not disclose on Wednesday. Their release reported six null clinical endpoints and no target-engagement data at all, which means nobody outside the company can distinguish between the drug reactivating U-B-E-three-A in a brain too developmentally advanced to use it, and the drug never reactivating U-B-E-three-A at those doses in the first place. One of those outcomes refutes the hypothesis. The other leaves it untested. Ionis has no such ambiguity, in either direction. That asymmetry is a design choice, and it is the cheapest insurance in clinical development.

The third supporting element is coverage across the age range. In the youngest children, the gross motor function measure did not merely stabilize — it improved. That is a small, largely open-label observation and it should not be over-read, but it is the piece the agency evidently leaned on in approving down to the youngest patients rather than restricting the label to the population in which the ten-meter walk test was measurable. And every patient-reported, caregiver-reported and clinician-reported secondary trended the same way, with serious adverse events numerically lower on drug than on placebo.

On the regulatory posture: the agency approved nineteen days early, all ages, on fifty-four patients and one marginal p-value. It is worth naming what kind of flexibility that is, because we spent yesterday's briefing on a different kind. In the Replimune case, flexibility came from a senior official at the Center for Biologics Evaluation and Research overriding her own review team over their documented objections. Here the flexibility is structural and it is the system working as designed: ultra-rare prevalence, no existing therapy, a fatal or severely disabling course, a mechanistically coherent target-engagement biomarker, and a sponsor that ran the hardest available control anyway. Those are not the same thing and they should not be graded on the same curve.

Commercially, this is modest and it is strategically outsized. William Blair modeled roughly two hundred ninety-five million dollars in peak annual sales. Ionis retains United States rights and is launching independently, with Recordati holding rights elsewhere and European and Japanese submissions planned for twenty twenty-seven. Brett Monia called it the first independent launch out of the neurology pipeline, and that framing is the point — Spinraza went through Biogen, and Qalsody went through Biogen. The question hanging over Ionis as it transitions from a royalty-and-partnership company to a commercial one is whether it can actually launch a neurology drug itself. A quarterly intrathecal product for under a thousand American patients is close to the easiest possible test case: the prescribers are a countable number of leukodystrophy centers, the diagnosis is genetic and unambiguous, and there is no competitor. Do not read a hundred percent of this launch's execution into what happens with olezarsen or donidalorsen at scale. Do read the rare pediatric disease voucher as real money, on the order of a hundred million dollars in the current secondary market, arriving in a year when Ionis is spending heavily. Shares actually fell five point two percent on the day to fifty-eight thirteen, which simply reflects that the approval was fully expected ahead of a September twenty-second action date, and recovered a couple of percent overnight.

Which brings us back to Angelman syndrome, and to what I said yesterday. I argued that the read-through from Ionis's open-label HALOS study of obudanersen should be marked toward zero, because it shares its architecture with the Ultragenyx Phase one-two that just failed to predict a controlled result. Thursday's approval does not change that, but it does something useful: it isolates the variable. Nobody can now argue that Ionis cannot make antisense oligonucleotides that reach the central nervous system, engage a target and change a clinical trajectory, because Zanvastro demonstrably does all three. Nor is intrathecal delivery the problem. The remaining question in Angelman is specifically whether the paternal allele can be reactivated to a therapeutically meaningful degree, and whether a developmental disorder assessed with an age-sensitive instrument can show it. REVEAL is at least placebo-controlled, it enrolls from age two rather than four, and its primary endpoint is expressive communication rather than cognition. But the thing to demand of Ionis between now and the second half of twenty twenty-seven is a U-B-E-three-A target-engagement readout. After this week the argument for it is unanswerable: their own Alexander disease program shows what a biomarker buys you when the clinical p-value is uncomfortable, and their competitor's Angelman program shows what its absence costs you when the clinical result is null.

The general lesson, and it applies well beyond neurology. When we assess whether a therapeutic hypothesis is tractable, we spend almost all of our attention on target validity — is this the right protein, is the genetics human, is the direction of effect known. Alexander disease and Angelman syndrome both score essentially perfectly on those criteria. G-F-A-P was identified as the cause of Alexander disease in two thousand one; the twenty-five years since were spent not on discovering the target but on delivery chemistry, on finding an endpoint with a measurable slope, on a biomarker, and on the willingness to run a placebo lumbar puncture in a child. Two more variables belong in the tractability assessment alongside target validity: whether the disease trajectory runs in the opposite direction from your endpoint's natural drift, and whether you have an independent measure that the drug did the molecular thing you designed it to do. Get those two wrong and a perfect target buys you a hundred twenty-nine children, four years and a billion dollars of enterprise value in exchange for a result nobody can interpret. Get them right and fifty-four patients and a p of zero point zero four is enough to change a disease.
