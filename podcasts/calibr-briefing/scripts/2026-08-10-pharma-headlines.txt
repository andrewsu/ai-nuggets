Good morning, Travis. Here are your biotech and pharma headlines for Monday, August tenth. The weekend was quiet on the wire, so today is built around the clinic — a marquee readout this morning, and a piece of ALS science that is quietly rewriting what we thought was possible. Five items.

First, and leading today, AbCellera. Before the open this morning the company put out top-line Phase 2 data for A-B-C-L six-three-five, its antibody for moderate-to-severe hot flashes due to menopause. Two reasons this one matters more than a small vasomotor trial usually would. One, the target and the format. The drug hits the neurokinin-three receptor — the same clinically validated node that Astellas' Veozah, fezolinetant, and Bayer's elinzanetant address with daily oral pills. AbCellera's bet is that a long-acting antibody against that receptor can deliver the same relief with infrequent dosing and without the liver-enzyme monitoring that has dogged the oral class. Two, and bigger picture, this is the first wholly owned Phase 2 readout for a company the market has always known as an engine — the AI-driven antibody-discovery platform behind Eli Lilly's COVID antibody — that is now trying to prove it can develop its own drugs, not just license discovery to others. The bar management set publicly was concrete: at least a twenty percent placebo-adjusted cut in hot-flash frequency, or roughly two fewer hot flashes a day. The stock ran up going into the print, so the read-through to the whole pivot thesis hangs on how the data land against that bar.

Second — and this is today's spotlight — a striking ALS story. Three years after Biogen and Ionis won accelerated approval for Qalsody, tofersen, in the S-O-D-1 genetic form of A-L-S, clinicians are now reporting something the field almost never sees in this disease: not just a slowing of decline, but actual reversal. Patients regaining breathing capacity, strength, function — years out. The spotlight goes deep on why an antisense drug that simply lowers one toxic protein can turn the clock back, and what that means for treating A-L-S before symptoms even start.

Third, psychedelics are moving from the fringe to the FDA's front door. Compass Pathways is completing a rolling New Drug Application for COMP three-sixty, its synthetic psilocybin for treatment-resistant depression, with final submission on track for the fourth quarter. Two positive Phase 3 trials now anchor the package, and the second showed the benefit holding out to six months, with a second dose adding to the response. If it clears, it would be the first psychedelic ever approved as a medicine — a milestone that carries real regulatory weight, because the last psychedelic to reach the agency, Lykos' MDMA-assisted therapy, got a rejection in 2024 over trial-design and functional-unblinding concerns. Watch how Compass threads that needle on the label and the required in-clinic supervision.

Fourth, the muscarinic race in schizophrenia is heating up around Bristol Myers Squibb's Cobenfy. Cobenfy — xanomeline plus trospium — was the first new mechanism in schizophrenia in decades, an M-one-M-four muscarinic approach rather than a dopamine blocker, but the launch has been slower than hoped. Now the competition is arriving. MapLight Therapeutics reported Phase 2 data on its own M-one-M-four muscarinic agonist, betovumeline paired with an anticholinergic to blunt side effects. The result was split — the twice-daily dose hit statistical significance on the standard schizophrenia symptom scale, with a moderate effect size, while the once-daily dose missed. It is a mixed card, but it confirms the muscarinic class is now a field, not a monopoly, and Bristol Myers will be defending Cobenfy on both efficacy and tolerability.

And fifth, radiopharmaceutical consolidation. Curium struck a definitive deal to merge with Lantheus in a transaction worth up to about eight billion dollars — one hundred two dollars and fifty cents a share in cash, plus contingent value rights of up to twelve dollars tied to commercial milestones through 2030. The logic is to fuse Curium's therapeutic radioligand and manufacturing platform with Lantheus' U.S. diagnostic-imaging business, building an integrated diagnostics-to-therapeutics player as the radiopharma theranostics wave keeps drawing big-pharma and now big-money attention. The companies expect to close in the first half of 2027.
