Good morning, Travis. It's Saturday, November twenty-first, 2026. The F-D-A rejected Capricor's Duchenne cell therapy a second time and went public with data-integrity findings. The K-RAS race got more crowded at the Triple Meeting in Barcelona. And Merck's TROP2 antibody-drug conjugate won again in Phase three. Here's what you need to know.

First, a rejection that came with an unusual public explanation. On Friday evening, two days before its deadline, the F-D-A sent Capricor a second complete response letter for deramiocel. Deramiocel is an allogeneic cell therapy made from donor heart cells, called cardiosphere-derived cells, which Capricor says act by releasing exosomes that turn macrophages toward an anti-fibrotic, healing state. Capricor's release said only that the data weren't sufficient to establish effectiveness and cited "certain other clinical deficiencies." The agency said more in its own statement, as reported by several outlets. Its bioresearch monitoring inspection of the Phase three HOPE-three trial found unauthorized post hoc changes to the statistical analysis plan, violations of blinding procedures, and other protocol violations, which it said "cannot be remediated post-hoc." It also issued a separate regulatory responsibility letter tied to the inspection. HOPE-three randomized one hundred six older, mostly non-ambulatory boys and young men, and Capricor reported a fifty-four percent slowing of upper-limb decline. In July, though, an advisory committee voted nine to three that the evidence didn't support effectiveness in Duchenne cardiomyopathy, and Capricor then switched the requested indication to upper-limb impairment. Chief executive Linda Marbán said the company stands behind "the integrity of our data." The stock fell about eighty percent after hours. Capricor has about one hundred ninety-nine million dollars in cash and is hosting a call Monday morning. The regulatory point is that this F-D-A will publicly say why a review failed when the problem is how a trial was run, not just whether it worked.

Second, K-RAS in pancreatic cancer, which is today's spotlight. Revolution Medicines presented updated first-line data for Rasonque, or daraxonrasib, its oral inhibitor of the active, G-T-P-bound form of RAS, combined with gemcitabine and nab-paclitaxel. In sixty-one patients, sixty-one percent responded and median progression-free survival was twelve point eight months. On Friday, Erasca reported a forty-four percent confirmed response rate for its pan-RAS molecular glue in heavily pretreated pancreatic cancer, and sixty-nine percent in the G-twelve-D and G-twelve-V subtypes. Alterome showed its first data for an on-and-off-state pan-K-RAS inhibitor. Analysts split on whether any of it threatens Rasonque. We go through the numbers in the spotlight.

Third, Merck and Kelun-Biotech's sacituzumab tirumotecan, sac-T-M-T, an antibody-drug conjugate that targets TROP2, a surface protein overexpressed on many epithelial tumors, and carries a topoisomerase-one inhibitor payload. In the Phase three TroFuse zero fifteen trial in four hundred seventy-three patients with gastroesophageal adenocarcinoma who had received at least two prior lines of therapy, it improved overall survival over the doctor's choice of treatment. The benefit was consistent regardless of TROP2 expression. It missed progression-free survival, response rate wasn't formally tested, and no numbers were released. RBC wrote that this pattern is "not unheard of" and that survival "remains the gold standard." It's the second Phase three win for the drug after TroFuse zero zero five in endometrial cancer in October, where it cut the risk of death by thirty-two percent. Merck licensed it from Chengdu-based Kelun in 2022 and is running eighteen Phase three trials. On Thursday we covered AstraZeneca's claudin eighteen point two conjugate in the same later-line gastric setting, which only works in claudin-positive tumors. Sac-T-M-T doesn't need patient selection, and that could matter as both drugs head to market.

Fourth, an approval. The F-D-A approved pimicotinib, to be sold as Pimjetmi by Merck K-G-a-A's E-M-D Serono, for symptomatic tenosynovial giant cell tumor in patients for whom surgery could worsen function. It's a once-daily oral inhibitor of C-S-F-one receptor, the growth signal that recruits the macrophages that make up most of these joint tumors. In the Phase three MANEUVER trial in ninety-four patients, fifty-six percent responded at week twenty-five versus three percent on placebo, and seventy-seven percent of responders were still responding at a year. It's the third drug in this class, after Daiichi's pexidartinib, which has a boxed liver warning and a restricted-access program, and Deciphera's vimseltinib. Pimicotinib came from Shanghai's Abbisko, another Chinese-discovered molecule reaching the U-S market.

Fifth, an F-D-A delay instead of a decision. Savara said the agency will miss Sunday's goal date for molgramostim, an inhaled form of G-M-C-S-F, the cytokine that alveolar macrophages need to clear surfactant, in autoimmune pulmonary alveolar proteinosis. The F-D-A gave no new date. This was already a three-month extension, and the filing was refused once before in 2025 over manufacturing questions. Jefferies called missed goal dates "rare," pointed to possible F-D-A bandwidth problems, and kept "high confidence" in approval on the strength of the IMPALA-two data. Guggenheim also stayed positive.

Sixth, a pipeline cut. Celldex is ending development of barzolvolimab in atopic dermatitis after a Phase two study failed to clear what the company called its "internal hurdle rate." Barzolvolimab is an antibody against KIT, the receptor mast cells need to survive, so it depletes mast cells. It has now fallen short in three conditions outside chronic hives. Celldex still plans to file in chronic spontaneous urticaria after two positive Phase three trials, and analysts called the eczema miss "immaterial" to the company.

Seventh, the last of the CTAD data. Eisai and Biogen showed five-year results from the Clarity AD extension of Leqembi, or lecanemab, the anti-amyloid protofibril antibody. Among three hundred fifty-one patients treated continuously, the gap on the clinical dementia rating sum of boxes kept growing against external natural-history cohorts, reaching about two points against the A-D-N-I cohort by year five. Brain swelling on M-R-I, called ARIA-E, fell from thirteen and a half percent in year one to under one percent in year five. Without a placebo arm after eighteen months, this is evidence that the benefit lasts, not proof that it keeps growing, but it's the longest dataset this class has.
