Good morning, Travis — this is your biotech and pharma briefing for Friday, August twenty-first, twenty twenty-six. Yesterday was the melanoma vaccine and a big move in Moderna. Today's news is quieter and more structural, and I'd argue more useful: the FDA approved a gene therapy on an endpoint measured in grams of cornstarch, the buy side finally wrote up what a cheaply priced gene-editing IPO taught it a month after the fact, and fifty million dollars went into the argument that the binding constraint in biological artificial intelligence is access to human tissue rather than model architecture. Seven items.

Number one, and today's spotlight: the FDA granted accelerated approval to Ultragenyx's Genglycos — generic name pariglasgene brecaparvovec, formerly DTX four-oh-one — for glycogen storage disease type one-A, in patients eight years and older. It is the first therapy ever approved that addresses the underlying cause of that disease, it is Ultragenyx's first gene therapy and its fifth FDA approval overall, and it came a few days ahead of an August twenty-third action date. Priced at two point seven million dollars. Two things make it worth a deep dive. The vector is an AAV eight carrying a codon-optimized human G six P C gene under that gene's own native promoter and enhancer rather than a strong synthetic one, so treated liver cells still take instructions from insulin and cortisol — regulated expression instead of maximum output. And the approved endpoint is the amount of raw cornstarch a patient has to eat every day: a forty-one point three percent mean reduction on drug versus ten point three percent on placebo at week forty-eight. Ultragenyx also collected a priority review voucher, and recent vouchers have sold for a hundred eighty to two hundred million dollars. The spotlight goes into the promoter design, the burden-of-care endpoint, the safety trade you are actually making, and the part of this approval that is worth the most money — what it says about a factory in Massachusetts and next month's Sanfilippo decision.

Number two, the buy side has decided what the Scribe Therapeutics IPO meant, and the lesson is about price discipline rather than science. Scribe went public in late July at fifteen dollars a share — an upsized offering, eight and a half million shares, a hundred twenty-nine million dollars gross, about a hundred fifty million with the overallotment, plus a concurrent private placement to Sanofi. It closed its first day at twenty-one fifty and closed Wednesday at thirty-one oh five, roughly double the offering price. Set that against the two big derisked names of the year: Kailera raised six hundred twenty-five million and is down about thirty-one percent from its first-day price, and Parabilis raised six hundred seventy and is up about twenty-five percent. The earliest-stage company priced itself the most conservatively and has outperformed both. Scribe is the Jennifer Doudna co-founded company built on X-Editor, an engineered CasX-derived platform, and the lead program is the interesting part: S T X eleven-fifty does not cut P C S K nine, it silences it epigenetically, so you get the LDL lowering without a permanent double-strand break. Phase one is running now, with thirty to thirty-five million of the proceeds earmarked for it. Sanofi is a partner on CRISPR-based cell therapies and Lilly is a partner on in vivo genetic medicines; Lilly indicated interest in owning as much as eleven percent after the offering. Jack Bannister, who runs equity capital markets at Leerink and banked the deal, framed the appeal as readout speed — the kind of dataset where investors know quickly whether the drug is working. Leerink has a thirty-seven dollar target. The read-through: the window has reopened for genuinely early-stage stories, if they price like it.

Number three, on the artificial intelligence side, Network Bio launched with fifty million dollars from Section thirty-two, Thiel Bio, Founders Fund, Breyer Capital, Blue Venture Fund and JSL Health. The thesis is deliberately unfashionable. Instead of a new architecture, they built a multi-institution biobank network — Mass General Brigham, Penn, and Colorado Anschutz — with common sample-selection criteria, shared quality standards and harmonized data, linking patient-derived tissue and paired blood to molecular profiles and longitudinal clinical outcomes across immunology, metabolic, cardiovascular and autoimmune disease. Chief executive Asad Ali Ahmad's line is that every patient leaves a barcode of their disease in their tissue and nobody has been able to read those barcodes at scale. Mike Pellini at Section thirty-two, who chairs the board, frames the differentiation as biobank access plus an architecture built for medicine rather than adapted to it. They claim transfer of learning across diseases and tissues, and they have announced a collaboration with NVIDIA — Parabricks and the open-source BioNeMo recipes — to build a population-scale foundation model on cell-free RNA. The commercial validation is a co-development and licensing deal worth more than thirty million dollars with an unnamed Fortune one hundred healthcare company. Worth holding this next to Xaira, which launched at over a billion dollars with Foresite Labs co-leading and is running the opposite experiment — enormous capital into generative models plus purpose-built data generation. Network Bio is arguing you get further by aggregating and harmonizing what academic medical centers already have in their freezers. Those two bets resolve differently, and it is early on both.

Number four, Roche is putting seven hundred fifty million dollars into doubling Genentech's plant in Hillsboro, Oregon. What they are adding is end-to-end device filling — prefilled syringes and autoinjectors — with commercial operations starting in twenty thirty-one, two hundred fifty manufacturing jobs and two hundred construction jobs on a seventy-five-acre campus. This follows Holly Springs, North Carolina, where Roche has scaled its commitment to roughly two billion dollars for a site coming online in twenty twenty-nine, and both are aimed at obesity, where Roche has said it intends to be a top-three player alongside Lilly and Novo. The lead asset is C T three-eighty-eight, a dual GLP-one and GIP receptor agonist, the same mechanism class as tirzepatide. Two things to take from it. Injectable obesity at scale is a device and filling problem as much as a molecule problem, and the companies building fill-finish capacity now are telling you what they believe about volumes in twenty thirty. And note the juxtaposition: Genentech cut a hundred three jobs in South San Francisco in July, largely out of research and early development, bringing its total for the year to nearly three hundred fifty, while committing three-quarters of a billion dollars to filling capacity in Oregon. That is a fairly clear statement about where the company currently thinks value sits.

Number five, the FDA rejected Osteal Therapeutics' Nexchange Kit — the intra-articular antibiotic combination formerly called V T X seven — for periprosthetic joint infection of the hip and knee. Per Endpoints, the complete response letter cited trial-design problems and a risk of fungal joint infection. Two lessons. Breakthrough Therapy designation, which this program had, buys you agency engagement, not agency agreement with your pivotal design — and this one had a headline net treatment effect of forty-one percent at six months, which did not save it. And periprosthetic joint infection stays on the list of genuinely unmet needs in orthopedics: the current standard is effectively two surgeries plus months of systemic antibiotics, so a local-delivery approach that shortens that is worth a great deal to whoever gets the trial right.

Number six, and this is the one with policy teeth. The three deaths in Chinese investigator-initiated trials that we covered on Wednesday have now produced a legislative response. Representatives John Moolenaar, who chairs the House select committee on China, and Ben Cline are asking the FDA to refuse clinical data generated at Chinese trial sites unless the agency has recently audited that site. That sits on top of a House appropriations bill advanced in April that would bar the FDA from accepting trial data from China, Russia, Iran or North Korea in support of investigational new drug applications. Investigator-initiated trials are the channel a lot of Western developers have used to get cheap, fast first-in-human data outside the FDA's process, and the appeal has always been speed, low cost and limited public reporting. If either of these becomes policy, the cost of an in-licensed China-origin asset goes up by the price of repeating the early clinical work. I would treat it as a diligence item today: if a licensing candidate's first-in-human data came out of an investigator-initiated trial, ask who held safety oversight, what the adverse-event reporting obligations were, and whether that site has ever been inspected.

Lilly signed a research collaboration and license with Amplitude Therapeutics on trans-amplifying RNA vaccines for infectious disease — the self-amplifying RNA idea split across two molecules so you can dose far less RNA — starting with undisclosed targets and an option on up to two more. And WuXi Biologics is offloading its chromatography unit, continuing the pattern of Chinese contract manufacturers restructuring around Western customer sensitivities. Also worth knowing, PhRMA's chief executive search is reportedly down to a slate of Republican policy hands and former lawmakers, which tells you how the trade group reads the next two years.
