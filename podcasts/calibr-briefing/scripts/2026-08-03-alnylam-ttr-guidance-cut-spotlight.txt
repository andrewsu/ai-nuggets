Good morning, Travis. Today's spotlight is Alnylam, and it's a great case study in how a company can print its best-ever quarter and still lose ten billion dollars of value in an afternoon. The lesson isn't about a drug failing. It's about a market maturing faster than the model that everyone was pricing in — and the tell is buried in a two-hundred-million-dollar guidance cut that most headlines skated right past. Let me walk you through what happened and why it matters well beyond Alnylam.

Start with the numbers, because on the surface they're terrific. Alnylam's transthyretin franchise — that's the amyloidosis drugs Amvuttra and the older Onpattro — did just over a billion dollars in the second quarter, up eighty-nine percent from a year ago. Amvuttra by itself crossed a billion dollars in a single quarter for the first time, a milestone it hit barely fifteen months after getting its label in the heart form of the disease. Total company revenue was one-point-two-nine billion, up sixty-seven percent, and earnings comfortably beat. This is a company executing at full speed.

And the stock fell about twenty-eight percent. Roughly ten-point-eight billion dollars of market capitalization, gone. The trigger was that Alnylam cut its full-year guidance for the transthyretin franchise by two hundred million dollars — from a range of four-point-four to four-point-seven billion down to four-point-two to four-point-five. There were two other pieces of bad framing underneath: Amvuttra's one-point-oh-one billion actually came in just shy of the roughly one-point-oh-five billion the Street wanted, so the beat was on the P and L, not on the top-selling product itself. And this was, as William Blair put it, the second consensus miss in three quarters. Their note said, and I'm quoting, "This is disappointing and there is no way to sugarcoat it."

Now here's the reason management gave for the cut, and it's the whole ballgame: normalizing second-line demand. In plain English — for a while, a wave of patients was switching onto Amvuttra faster than the underlying disease population was growing, a pull-forward of pent-up demand. That wave has now crested and the run rate has settled back to the real, slower pace of new diagnoses and switches. Nothing about the drug got worse. What changed is that the easy, one-time burst of switching is over. To understand why that burst existed and why it faded, you need the competitive map of this disease, because it's genuinely one of the more interesting battlegrounds in medicine right now.

Transthyretin amyloidosis — T-T-R for short — is a disease where a liver protein called transthyretin misfolds and deposits as amyloid, either in the nerves, causing polyneuropathy, or in the heart, causing a stiff, failing heart muscle. The cardiac form is the big commercial prize, because it turns out to be far more common than anyone appreciated a decade ago, hiding inside a lot of what got diagnosed as ordinary heart failure. And there are two fundamentally different ways to treat it. You can silence the protein — knock down its production in the liver so there's less of it to misfold. That's Alnylam's whole game: Amvuttra and Onpattro are R-N-A-interference drugs that do exactly that, and Onpattro, back in twenty eighteen, was the first R-N-A-interference medicine ever approved. Or you can stabilize the protein — bind it so it can't misfold in the first place. That's the other camp: Pfizer's tafamidis, sold as Vyndaqel, which was first to market in the heart disease, and BridgeBio's acoramidis, sold as Attruby, which launched in late twenty twenty-four and has been taking share fast — it did over three hundred sixty million dollars in its first full year and has become a first-choice pick for many newly diagnosed patients.

So here's the strategic picture. The stabilizers, tafamidis and acoramidis, are oral pills and they got entrenched as first-line therapy. Alnylam's pitch in the heart disease was built on a trial called HELIOS-B, which showed Amvuttra cut cardiovascular events and deaths — a genuine, positive outcomes result. That data opened the door to using a silencer either instead of a stabilizer, or layered on top of one. And that add-on-or-switch opportunity is precisely the "second-line demand" that surged and has now normalized. The question sitting underneath the entire silencer growth story is: once a patient is already on an entrenched stabilizer, how much extra benefit does adding a silencer actually deliver?

And this is where a competitor's failure becomes the key to reading Alnylam's quarter. Ionis and AstraZeneca have their own silencer, an antisense drug called eplontersen, sold as Wainua. They ran a big heart-disease outcomes trial called CARDIO-TTRansform, and it missed its primary endpoint. Here's the crucial detail: in that trial, the majority of patients were already on a stabilizer as background therapy, and adding the silencer on top did not produce a statistically significant reduction in cardiovascular death and events. There was a hint of benefit in the small subgroup taking the silencer alone, without a stabilizer — nominally significant — but the headline add-on result failed. So the real-world experiment the whole field was running got an answer that's uncomfortable for the silencer camp: on top of a good stabilizer, the incremental value of piling on a silencer is hard to prove. That is the same force, seen from the clinical side, that shows up on Alnylam's income statement as second-line demand normalizing.

Now let me be fair to Alnylam, because the bears can overshoot here, and at least one analyst thinks they did. This is not eplontersen. Amvuttra won HELIOS-B — its own outcomes trial was positive, which is exactly what eplontersen's was not. The franchise is still growing north of fifty percent year over year and just crossed a billion dollars a quarter. Stifel, on the sell side, said the guidance cut "significantly surprised" them and could "raise questions on peak sales potential," but also called the selloff "probably overdone" given that growth rate. So the honest read is that this is an expectations reset and a demand-shape story, not a drug-quality story. The market had been extrapolating the pull-forward as if it were the steady state, and it wasn't.

There's one more wrinkle worth putting on your radar, because it complicates the "this is a buying opportunity" case. Alnylam's next act in this disease is a next-generation silencer called nucresiran, designed for once-a-year dosing — deeper, more convenient knockdown. But if the clinical lesson of CARDIO-TTRansform is that more silencing on top of a stabilizer doesn't clearly translate into more lives saved, then the value case for an even-better silencer gets harder to make, not easier. The differentiation may have to come from convenience and first-line positioning rather than from a bigger outcomes benefit. That's a real strategic question for the franchise's second decade.

So here's how I'd file this for your world. The RNA-interference modality is validated and Alnylam is unambiguously the leader — that part of the thesis is intact and this quarter doesn't dent it. What changed is the shape of the fight. The transthyretin market has gone from a land rush, where any silencer could ride a wave of switching, into something more like trench warfare, where the stabilizers own first-line, the easy switching demand has been consumed, and the silencers now have to win on outcomes differentiation, dosing convenience, and where they sit in the treatment sequence. The broader read-through, and it's the one I'd carry into any RNA or amyloidosis name you're underwriting: even a category-defining, fifty-percent-growth franchise can trigger a ten-billion-dollar selloff when the market matures faster than the model assumed. The drug was never the problem. The demand curve was. That's the spotlight. Talk soon.
