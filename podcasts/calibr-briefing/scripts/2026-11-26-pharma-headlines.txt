Bristol Myers got one hit and one miss in its two Phase three lupus trials of Sotyktu, and that's today's spotlight. Vertex's I-g-A nephropathy decision slipped three months. Bayer licensed another Chinese antibody-drug conjugate and pulled its kidney drug from Germany. Amgen has a settled date to launch a biosimilar of Opdivo. GSK's twice-yearly asthma antibody matched its monthly predecessor in a rare vasculitis. Pharming stopped making its main drug. And BioNTech's founders are reportedly raising a billion dollars for their next company. Here's what you need to know.

First, Bristol Myers and lupus. On Wednesday morning B-M-S reported the two Phase three POETYK trials of Sotyktu, or deucravacitinib, in systemic lupus. Sotyktu is an oral, allosteric inhibitor of TYK-two, the kinase that carries signals from interleukin twenty-three, interleukin twelve and type one interferon. The trials were nearly identical, about five hundred ten patients each, at the same dose, with the same primary endpoint: an S-R-I-four response at one year. POETYK SLE-one hit, and also won on BICLA, on steroid tapering and on fatigue. POETYK SLE-two missed. B-M-S says patients on the drug responded about as well as in the first trial, but more patients on placebo responded. The company released no response rates. B-M-S shares closed down less than one percent. This matters to Foresite because Alumis, which Foresite Labs incubated, has its own TYK-two inhibitor and missed in lupus in September. That's the spotlight.

Second, Vertex. Late Wednesday, Vertex said the F-D-A has extended the review of povetacicept in I-g-A nephropathy by up to three months. The decision had been due Monday and now comes no later than February twenty-eighth. Povetacicept blocks both BAFF and APRIL, two cytokines that keep the B cells making the damaging antibody alive. Near the end of the review the F-D-A asked for more information. Vertex answered quickly, and the agency classified the answer as a major amendment. That usually means the reviewers want more time to finish, not that they see a fatal problem. But Vertex now has more time behind Vera's Trutakna, the first dual BAFF and APRIL blocker, which was approved in July, and Otsuka's Voyxact, which blocks APRIL alone.

Third, Bayer went to Hansoh. Bayer is paying the Shanghai drugmaker one hundred fifty million dollars up front for HS-twenty-one-twenty-two, with up to three point one billion more in milestones and tiered royalties, for rights everywhere outside Greater China. HS-twenty-one-twenty-two is a bispecific antibody-drug conjugate. It binds both EGFR and c-MET on tumor cells and delivers a toxic payload. It's in Phase one, and early lung cancer data were shown at ESMO this fall. The bet is that hitting both receptors catches tumors that escape EGFR drugs by turning up MET. Hansoh has now licensed ADCs to Roche, GSK and others, and this is one more large Western deal for an early-stage Chinese asset.

Fourth, Bayer also picked a fight with Germany. It will pull Kerendia, its nonsteroidal mineralocorticoid receptor blocker finerenone, from the German market in mid-December. In October, Germany's joint committee said Kerendia adds no benefit over existing drugs in heart failure with an ejection fraction of forty percent or higher. Germany sets one price for all of a drug's uses, so that verdict dragged down what insurers would pay in kidney disease too. And because other European countries reference German prices, a deep discount there spreads. Companies have pulled dozens of drugs from Germany since its pricing law took effect in 2011. Bayer pulling a flagship drug from its home market is the loudest version yet.

Fifth, Amgen settled its patent fight with Bristol Myers and Ono over Opdivo. If the F-D-A approves Amgen's biosimilar, it can launch on October first, 2028. The F-D-A's decision on Amgen's application is expected by the end of this year. Opdivo brought in about seven billion dollars in the first nine months of the year. B-M-S is moving patients to Opdivo Qvantig, the under-the-skin version, which the biosimilar doesn't copy. Sandoz, Celltrion and others are behind Amgen. This is also why the lupus readout matters to B-M-S: it needs new growth drugs before 2028.

Sixth, GSK. In the head-to-head OCEAN trial, Exdensur, or depemokimab, an anti-interleukin-five antibody dosed twice a year, matched monthly Nucala in eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis, a rare vasculitis that comes with asthma and high eosinophil counts. GSK hasn't released the numbers. It plans to file in the first half of next year. Exdensur has sold about seventy-three million dollars so far this year, against about one point seven billion for Nucala. GSK forecasts Exdensur peak sales of three billion pounds, and moving Nucala patients to twice-yearly dosing is how it gets there.

Seventh, Pharming paused production of Ruconest, its C-one esterase inhibitor for acute hereditary angioedema attacks. A media fill failed at its contract fill-finish site. That's a routine sterility test in which growth medium is run through the line in place of the drug. Pharming has supply through mid-December, and what happens after that depends on the root-cause investigation. Ruconest is about eighty percent of Pharming's revenue, and the shares fell about fifteen percent in Amsterdam. The timing is bad, because patients now have an oral alternative for acute attacks, KalVista's Ekterly, which Chiesi now owns.

And finally, the Financial Times reports that BioNTech co-founders Uğur Şahin and Özlem Türeci are in talks to raise about one billion dollars for Arife, their new mRNA cancer company in Mainz. European firm Plural is leading. The round would be structured as seed financing at about a two point five billion dollar valuation, plus convertible debt. That would make it one of the biggest seed rounds ever for a European start-up. BioNTech will take a minority stake in exchange for mRNA technology rights, but it won't put in ongoing cash.
