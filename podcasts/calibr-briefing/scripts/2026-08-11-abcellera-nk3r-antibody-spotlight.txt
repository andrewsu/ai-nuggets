Today's spotlight is AbCellera, and I want to be clear up front about why a Phase 2 hot-flash trial is the most important story on your desk this morning. It is not really about hot flashes. It is about whether a company the entire market has always understood as an engine — an AI-driven antibody-discovery platform that finds molecules for other people to develop — can become a company that discovers and develops its own drugs and captures the value itself. Yesterday, for the first time, that question got a real answer, and the answer was yes.

Start with what was actually reported, because the data are genuinely strong. A-B-C-L six-three-five is an antibody against the neurokinin-three receptor, aimed at moderate-to-severe vasomotor symptoms — hot flashes and night sweats — due to menopause. In a randomized, placebo-controlled Phase 2 of ninety-two postmenopausal women, each averaging about ten moderate-or-severe hot flashes a day at baseline, a single subcutaneous dose cut the frequency by eighty-three percent at four weeks, against thirty-three percent for placebo. That is a placebo-adjusted difference of more than five hot flashes a day, and it was highly statistically significant. Severity dropped by fifty-eight percent versus twelve percent. Sleep improved, and patients' own global impression of their condition improved. And the safety read was clean for a first look — no serious adverse events, no severe events, and nobody dropped out because of side effects. The market reaction told you how that landed relative to expectations: the stock jumped roughly a third on the print.

Now let me explain the mechanism, because it is the key to why this is differentiated and not just another entrant. Menopausal hot flashes are driven by a small population of neurons in the hypothalamus — the KNDy neurons — that become hyperactive as estrogen falls, and they disrupt the brain's temperature-control set point. The neurokinin-three receptor sits on those neurons and is the switch that drives the misfiring. Block that receptor and you calm the neurons and the hot flashes recede. This target is already clinically validated — that is important context. Astellas' Veozah, generic name fezolinetant, and Bayer's elinzanetant are both neurokinin-receptor antagonists already on the market or at the finish line, and they proved the biology works. So AbCellera is not taking target risk here. What it is taking is a bet on format.

And the format is the whole point. Every other drug hitting this pathway is a small-molecule pill taken every single day. AbCellera built an antibody — a large, long-lived protein — against the same receptor, and delivered its four-week result from one subcutaneous injection. If that durability holds and the dosing interval turns out to be measured in weeks or a month rather than a day, you have a fundamentally different product profile: an occasional shot instead of a daily pill, which changes adherence and the whole treatment experience. There is also a potential tolerability angle. The oral neurokinin antagonists have carried liver-enzyme signals — Veozah, in particular, ships with liver-monitoring requirements — and a cleanly targeted antibody may sidestep some of the off-target liability that comes with a small molecule diffusing everywhere in the body. That is the thesis: same validated target, better delivery, potentially better safety.

Here is the part that should make you sit up as someone who watches AI in drug discovery. Antibodies against G-protein-coupled receptors are hard. Genuinely, notoriously hard. G-protein-coupled receptors are the single most productive drug-target class in medicine, but they are almost entirely the domain of small molecules, because they are embedded in the cell membrane and expose very little surface for an antibody to grab onto. That is precisely why the field is dominated by pills. A-B-C-L six-three-five is the first program out of AbCellera's dedicated platform for exactly these targets — G-protein-coupled receptors and ion channels — and it just delivered best-in-class-looking efficacy against one of them. That is not a one-drug result. It is a validation that the platform can do the thing that is supposed to be very hard, which is the entire investment case for everything else in the pipeline behind it.

Which brings me to why the equity story is what actually changed yesterday. For years, AbCellera was a picks-and-shovels business. It is the discovery engine behind Eli Lilly's COVID antibody, and its model was to license discovery to partners and collect milestones and royalties — lower risk, but also a permanent ceiling on how much value it captures from any given medicine. The strategic pivot has been to take its own molecules into the clinic and own them outright, which means taking on clinical and regulatory risk in exchange for the full upside if a drug works. A-B-C-L six-three-five is the first wholly owned asset to give a Phase 2 answer. So the read-through is not really about the size of the menopause market, large as that is. It is that the pivot is now de-risked at the point that matters most — the company showed it can pick a target, engineer a hard-to-drug antibody against it, and generate convincing human efficacy on its own dime.

Now the sober side, because the take has to be honest. This is ninety-two patients and four weeks. It is a clean, convincing signal, but it is not a Phase 3, and the two things that most define the commercial profile — how long the effect lasts from a single dose, and therefore the real-world dosing interval — are exactly what four-week data cannot fully settle. Longer follow-up has to confirm both the durability and the safety, particularly because the class it is joining has a liver-signal history that only shows up with time and numbers. There was no head-to-head against the oral drugs, so best-in-class is an inference from cross-trial comparison, not a proven claim. And AbCellera has not yet laid out Phase 3 design or timing, so there is execution and capital risk between here and a filing. It is also entering a market where two branded competitors are already establishing themselves and generics of the pill format will eventually arrive.

So how should you weigh it. On the strong side: a validated target, a genuinely differentiated long-acting format, best-in-class-looking efficacy, a clean early safety read, and — most importantly for the thesis — proof that AbCellera's platform can drug a hard receptor class and that its pivot to owning its own medicines is real. On the cautious side: small and short data, unresolved dosing interval, a class with a known long-term safety question, an undefined Phase 3, and established competition. The honest read is that yesterday was an inflection point for how to value this company. The question was never whether AbCellera could find good antibodies — it clearly can. The question was whether it could turn that engine into approved, owned products. This is the first hard evidence that it can. That is the spotlight. More tomorrow.
