Good morning Travis. This is today's spotlight, and it's on Bristol Myers Squibb's admilparant. Yesterday the drug won a Phase three in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, and it lost about four percent of Bristol's market value by the close. The question for the next several months is whether a trial where only the lower dose worked is a validation of a new mechanism or a warning about it. I'll walk through what Bristol disclosed and what it held back, why the dose pattern is less damning than it sounds but still unresolved, what it means for the one other company in this class, and how the day fits with Syndax's failure the same evening and Endeavor's win last week.

Start with what's actually on the table. ALOFT-IPF randomized about twelve hundred fifty adults with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis one to one to one to sixty milligrams of admilparant twice daily, a hundred twenty milligrams twice daily, or placebo, with or without background antifibrotics. The primary endpoint was absolute change in forced vital capacity, in milliliters, at fifty-two weeks. The sixty milligram dose was statistically significant against placebo. The hundred-twenty milligram dose slowed decline but was not significant. Discontinuations for adverse events were low in both drug arms and similar to placebo. That's the entire release. There are no milliliters, no p-values and no confidence intervals, and the release never uses the word liver. Detailed results will come at a future medical meeting. Bristol shares were up more than six percent before the open, and BioPharma Dive reports they reversed to a roughly four percent loss. Contineum Therapeutics, the only other company with an L-P-A-one antagonist in a randomized fibrosis trial, traded down about twenty-nine percent, to nine dollars forty-six.

Now the mechanism, because it explains why investors were so nervous going in. Lysophosphatidic acid is a lipid signal made largely by the enzyme autotaxin. Through receptor one, it does three things that matter in the fibrotic lung. It recruits and activates fibroblasts, it makes the vasculature leaky, and it pushes injured epithelial cells toward death instead of repair. Bristol pitches admilparant as acting upstream, at the epithelial-injury step, rather than slowing scar deposition after the fact. The pathway also has a long record of failed programs. Bristol's own first L-P-A-one antagonist was dropped for hepatobiliary toxicity. Amgen shut down the one it inherited from Horizon after a Phase two miss. And Galapagos's autotaxin inhibitor, which hits the same pathway one step earlier, was stopped in Phase three in twenty twenty-one. Then last month came the news that Bristol had amended this program's safety monitoring after a limited number of liver events, including one death from liver injury, in a patient whose treatment assignment was not clear. So the market came into Monday asking two things: does it work, and is the liver safe. It got a qualified yes on the first and silence on the second.

The dose story makes more sense once you go back to the Phase two. That trial tested thirty and sixty milligrams twice daily for twenty-six weeks. In the idiopathic cohort, percent-predicted F-V-C fell two point seven points on placebo, two point eight on thirty milligrams, and one point two on sixty. The thirty milligram dose did nothing. The sixty milligram effect was a one point four point difference, with a confidence interval that just crossed zero. The progressive pulmonary fibrosis cohort was stronger, a three point two point difference that was statistically clear. So going into Phase three there was exactly one dose with a signal, and that signal was modest in idiopathic disease. The hundred-twenty milligram arm was a hedge, meant to find out whether more drug meant more benefit. The answer, at least on this endpoint, is no.

There are three ways to read that, and the detailed data should tell them apart. The first is noise. Forced vital capacity is a variable endpoint, and the difference between significant and not significant is not itself a significant difference. If the hundred-twenty arm landed just short of the line with an effect similar in size to the sixty arm, the two doses could be statistically indistinguishable, and the right description would be a plateau, not a failure. This has happened before in this field. In Boehringer's Phase three of nerandomilast in progressive pulmonary fibrosis, the drug now sold as Jascayd, the nine milligram dose beat placebo by eighty-one milliliters and the eighteen milligram dose by sixty-seven. Nobody read that as evidence the drug doesn't work. The second reading is receptor saturation plus attrition. If sixty milligrams already blocks the receptor fully, a higher dose adds no pharmacology but can add dropouts, missing data and tolerability problems, and in an intention-to-treat analysis those dilute the observed effect. The Phase two showed bigger first-dose blood pressure drops on drug than placebo, so the higher dose has a plausible way to cost something. The third reading, and the one the stock was pricing, is that the effect is small and fragile, and that sixty milligrams got a lucky draw.

So when the full data come, look at four things. First, the absolute milliliter difference for each dose, and whether the two drug arms overlap. Second, discontinuation and missing data broken out by arm. Third, the effect with and without background nintedanib or pirfenidone, since most of these patients will get the drug as an add-on. And fourth, liver enzyme elevations by dose. If the hepatic events cluster at a hundred twenty milligrams, that's the cleanest explanation for the dose pattern, and it would also shape the label.

The sell side split along those lines. Leerink's David Risinger: "could be sufficient to support admilparant regulatory approval, but the lack of dose response raises questions about the profile. We await detailed results." William Blair's Matt Phipps was more constructive, writing that despite lingering questions on dose response and magnitude of benefit, the lower-dose success is "likely to open a blockbuster opportunity." His colleague Myles Minter also flagged the higher-dose miss, but William Blair read the clean tolerability as de-risking both hypotension and hepatotoxicity, and argued the data validate the Phase two observation at the same dose. Stifel's Paul Matteis called the dose pattern "an odd nuance" and said the readout leaves the L-P-A-one thesis more validated. JonesTrading's Debanjana Chatterjee pointed at what's missing: without numbers, the upside is capped.

My read is that the commercial bar here is tolerability more than magnitude. Ofev and Esbriet slow decline, but a large share of patients either never start them or stop because of diarrhea, nausea and weight loss. Jascayd, a P-D-E-four-B inhibitor, came in last year as the better-tolerated third option. Admilparant's pitch is a pill with placebo-like discontinuation that can sit on top of any of them. If the sixty milligram effect is in the range of the approved drugs and the liver story is quiet, Phipps is probably right. If the effect is half that, it becomes a hard sell to pulmonologists who already have three options.

Now the read-through, which is where the money moved. Contineum's PIPE-seven-ninety-one is a once-daily L-P-A-one antagonist from a scaffold built for slow, tight receptor binding. It's brain-penetrant, it's being tested at much lower doses, and its PROPEL-IPF Phase two has about three hundred twenty-four patients, with absolute F-V-C at twenty-six weeks as the primary endpoint. On paper Monday was good news for Contineum: a randomized Phase three of over a thousand patients says blocking this receptor slows lung function decline. The market sold it anyway, by close to a third, because Contineum's whole argument is that it occupies the receptor more completely at a lower dose. If more receptor coverage didn't help Bristol, that argument gets harder to make. Stifel's view is that the structures and doses are different enough to limit the read-through. That's fair. But a smaller company has to prove its exposure-response is real, and for Contineum the bar moved up yesterday.

Zoom out to the field. In one week, three different mechanisms in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis gave three different answers. Last Thursday, private Endeavor BioMedicines said its Hedgehog inhibitor taladegib improved lung function above baseline in a Phase two-b of two hundred thirteen patients, and claimed reversal rather than slowing. That claim still has no effect size attached, and Hedgehog inhibitors carry hair loss, muscle spasms and taste changes. Monday night Syndax's anti-C-S-F-one-receptor antibody failed outright, so depleting macrophages, on this evidence, doesn't move F-V-C. And admilparant sits in the middle: a real effect, of unknown size, that didn't grow with dose. The field's main open question is whether any mechanism can do more than slow decline. Endeavor says yes. Bristol is saying, at most, a better-tolerated way to slow it.

Two dates to watch. Bristol's detailed ALOFT-IPF presentation is the immediate catalyst, and the four items I listed will decide it. Then the progressive pulmonary fibrosis trial, about a thousand fifty-seven patients with the same two doses, reads out in early twenty twenty-seven. That's the indication where the Phase two signal was cleanest. If a hundred twenty milligrams fails there too, that settles the dose question. If both doses win, the idiopathic result starts to look like noise.

That's your spotlight. Admilparant validated L-P-A-one as a target, but whether it's a good drug depends on numbers Bristol hasn't released, and the rest of the class is waiting on them too.
