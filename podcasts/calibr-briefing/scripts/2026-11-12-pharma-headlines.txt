Good morning, Travis. It's Thursday, November twelfth, 2026. Wednesday was Veterans Day and a quieter wire, but it still delivered a private equity firm paying for Pfizer's obesity trials, Flagship folding two of its A-I drug discovery companies into one, Lilly's obesity pill clearing China, and a second Phase three win for Caplyta in bipolar mania. Here's what you need to know.

First, Pfizer is getting someone else to pay for part of its obesity bet. After the close on Wednesday, Pfizer and Blackstone Life Sciences announced that Blackstone-managed funds will cover up to one and a half billion dollars of development costs through 2028 for berobenatide, a once-monthly G-L-P-one receptor agonist peptide. That's the lead asset from Pfizer's roughly ten-billion-dollar Metsera acquisition last year, previously called MET-zero-nine-seven-i. Blackstone takes no rights and no ownership in the drug, and Pfizer keeps full control. In return, Blackstone earns milestone payments, paid out over several years, if the F-D-A approves monthly berobenatide on its own for chronic weight management and if certain sales targets are hit. The size of those milestones wasn't disclosed. Pfizer says it is running more than twenty obesity trials this year, ten of them ongoing or planned Phase threes of berobenatide. The new C-F-O, Cecile Guegan, framed it as keeping "the financial capacity and flexibility to invest in our broader pipeline across all therapeutic areas." Read that next to last week's pipeline update, where Pfizer dropped a Metsera combination study and other obesity programs. Pfizer paid up for an obesity portfolio, then narrowed it, and is now sharing the cost of the Phase three program it kept. It moves risk off Pfizer's books, and it tells you how expensive it has become to catch Lilly and Novo.

Second, and this is today's spotlight. Flagship Pioneering is merging Valo Health and Montai Therapeutics, two A-I drug discovery companies it founded in 2019. The combined company keeps the Valo name and Valo's C-E-O, Brian Alexander. Valo brings billions in headline partnership value from Novo Nordisk and Merck K-G-a-A but no wholly owned clinical drug. Montai brings a Phase one oral N-R-F-two agonist for ulcerative colitis and a preclinical oral STAT-six inhibitor. The spotlight looks at what each side was missing, and what the deal says about A-I platform companies that never got a clinical asset of their own.

Third, Lilly got its obesity pill approved in China ahead of Novo. Lilly's Chinese subsidiary says orforglipron, sold as Foundayo in the U-S and U-K, is the first oral G-L-P-one approved in China for weight management. It's a small-molecule G-L-P-one receptor agonist, not a peptide, so it doesn't need the fasting rules oral semaglutide comes with. More than half of Chinese adults are overweight or obese, but weight-loss drugs there are paid out of pocket, so the early market is wealthier urban patients. Lilly also has a type two diabetes application under review in China, an indication where Novo's oral semaglutide is already approved, and it is putting three billion dollars into Chinese manufacturing over ten years.

Fourth, J-and-J's Caplyta now has two positive Phase threes in acute bipolar mania. Lumateperone is an antipsychotic that combines serotonin two-A blockade with a lighter touch on dopamine D-two than older drugs. In the second pivotal study, forty-two milligrams a day cut the Young Mania Rating Scale score by three point seven points more than placebo at week three, an effect size of zero point four three, separating as early as day three. Forty-four percent of patients responded, against twenty-four percent on placebo. The most common side effects were dizziness, dry mouth and sleepiness. With the first study, reported in September, this supports a U-S filing. Caplyta is already approved in schizophrenia, bipolar depression and adjunctive major depression, and mania would give J-and-J a label that covers both poles of bipolar disorder. That was a big part of what it paid for when it bought Intra-Cellular.

Fifth, a big private round in schizophrenia. Neurosterix, spun out of Addex, closed a hundred-five-million-dollar Series B to take N-T-X-two-fifty-three into Phase two. It's a positive allosteric modulator of the M-four muscarinic receptor, meaning it boosts the receptor's response to the brain's own acetylcholine instead of switching it on directly. That's the same receptor family behind Bristol's Cobenfy, and Endpoints reports Neurosterix has hired Cobenfy veterans. The obvious caution: AbbVie's M-four modulator emraclidine, from its Cerevel acquisition, failed two Phase twos. Investors are betting that was a problem with that molecule, not with the M-four approach. Addex didn't put money in and now owns eight and a half percent, which values the company at roughly two hundred twenty million dollars after the round.

Sixth, Legend Biotech shares fell sixteen percent after its first quarter under a new C-E-O. Carvykti, the B-C-M-A-directed CAR-T it shares with J-and-J, did about seven hundred ten million dollars in global sales, up thirty-six percent, and Legend posted a profit. But U-S growth slowed to nineteen percent, and management said Carvykti is gaining share in earlier lines of myeloma treatment more slowly than expected. That's where the competition from off-the-shelf bispecific antibodies is heaviest.

Seventh, BioNTech is paying Scholar Rock two hundred five million dollars in cash for a rare pediatric disease priority review voucher, according to Scholar Rock's securities filing. The voucher cuts F-D-A review from ten months to six. BioNTech says it will use it on its late-stage oncology pipeline. At this price, a voucher is worth a lot to a cash-strapped seller, and the buyer has to have a filing where four months matters.

Eighth, enGene says it will begin its Biologics License Application by year-end for detalimogene, a non-viral gene therapy instilled into the bladder for B-C-G-unresponsive non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer with carcinoma in situ. In a hundred twenty patients in the pivotal cohort, fifty-six percent had a complete response at some point, but only twenty-four percent were in complete response at twelve months, and the median complete response lasted ten months. The C-E-O says that puts it "within range of approved products," and the pitch is ease of use in community urology practices. The twelve-month number is the one competitors will point to.
