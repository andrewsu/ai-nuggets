Good morning Travis. Today's spotlight is Iambic Therapeutics. It priced its initial public offering Wednesday night and starts trading on Nasdaq this morning under the ticker I-A-M. Iambic is the San Diego A-I drug discovery company that has come up on this show repeatedly, through its Takeda deal and its work with Revolution Medicines. This is the first real public-market test of the A-I-native cohort since Generate Biomedicines listed in February. The deal itself is simple. What matters is how it was priced, what the prospectus shows about the lead drug, and what that tells you about how investors are paying for A-I platforms right now.

Start with the terms. Iambic sold nine point three seven five million shares at sixteen dollars, right at the midpoint of its fifteen-to-seventeen range, for a hundred fifty million dollars gross and about a hundred thirty-five million net. With about forty-seven point four million shares outstanding afterward, sixteen dollars implies a market value of roughly seven hundred sixty million dollars.

Three details in the filing tell you more than the headline does. First, sixteen dollars is below the last private price. Adjusted for the reverse split Iambic did last week, its Series B-four round this spring was priced at about seventeen fourteen a share, and the round before that at about sixteen thirty. So the public market valued the company at a modest discount to its last private mark. That's a flat-to-down round in I-P-O form. Second, the demand was partly pre-arranged. ARK Investment Management, an existing holder, and Duquesne Family Office had indicated interest in buying up to sixty million dollars of the deal, which is about forty percent of it. Third, in August and September, just before filing, Iambic raised sixty-six and a half million dollars in convertible notes from KKR, Insight Partners, Perceptive, Millennium and others. Those notes convert at the lower of eighty-five percent of the I-P-O price or a nine-hundred-million-dollar valuation cap. At sixteen dollars, that means the crossover investors are getting in at about thirteen sixty. The deal was carefully engineered, and it needed to be.

The pre-I-P-O cap table makes the same point. Catalio was the largest holder at about eleven percent, then Nexus Venture Partners at eight and a half, with Ascenta and co-founder Fred Manby at around five. NVIDIA and Coatue were each above five percent at earlier rounds but are now below it. Foresite isn't on the cap table at all, so this is a read-through for the portfolio, not a portfolio event. C-E-O Tom Miller is a former Caltech chemistry professor, and the rest of the bench is drug-development-heavy, with the C-S-O from Mirati and the C-M-O from Zymeworks, where he developed zanidatamab.

Now the asset, because that's what investors were really pricing. I-A-M-thirteen-sixty-three is an oral inhibitor of H-E-R-two. The prospectus calls it the only known H-E-R-two tyrosine kinase inhibitor that binds the inactive, D-F-G-out conformation of the kinase, and also describes it as covalent. The design goal was a combination that existing drugs don't have. It needs to spare wild-type E-G-F-R, whose inhibition causes the rash and diarrhea that have limited this class. It needs to cover the many H-E-R-two mutants as well as amplified H-E-R-two. And it needs to get into the brain. Preclinically, Iambic claims about five-hundred-fold biochemical selectivity over E-G-F-R, activity across more than twenty H-E-R-two mutants, and tumor-to-plasma ratios up to about two hundred-fold in patient-derived models. In biopsies from patients, they measured roughly twenty- to thirty-fold enrichment in tumor.

The clinical data, as of an August thirty-first cut at doses of nine hundred sixty milligrams once daily and above, need careful reading. The best overall response rate was thirty-six percent, nineteen of fifty-three patients. But the confirmed response rate was twenty-three percent, twelve of fifty-three, with four partial responses still awaiting confirmation. In the brain, the best intracranial response rate was forty-one percent, nine of twenty-two. The confirmed intracranial rate was eighteen percent, four of twenty-two. Those twenty-two patients were mostly H-E-R-two-positive breast cancer patients who had almost all already received both Enhertu and tucatinib. Eight of the brain responses came in patients who had failed both of those drugs. That population has very few options, and that's why the prospectus names H-E-R-two-positive breast cancer with active brain metastases, after Enhertu and tucatinib, as the fastest path to approval, with a registrational trial as early as twenty twenty-seven.

The safety table is where the design thesis shows up. At nine hundred sixty milligrams, in sixty-eight patients, treatment-related grade three events were six percent, with no grade four or five. Diarrhea occurred in fifty-three percent, but only two percent was grade three. Rash doesn't appear among the side effects seen in at least ten percent of patients. For a drug in this class, that is the clinical signature of E-G-F-R sparing. If it holds up, it's the strongest argument that the platform designed something medicinal chemists had struggled to make.

The competition is real and recent. Boehringer's zongertinib and Bayer's sevabertinib are both approved for H-E-R-two-mutant lung cancer. Iambic argues that neither meaningfully crosses the blood-brain barrier, and it showed a head-to-head mouse study in which its drug beat zongertinib. Tucatinib gets into the brain but doesn't cover mutants and carries liver toxicity. Enhertu dominates H-E-R-two-positive breast cancer. One gap a listener should notice: the prospectus gives no separate response rate for H-E-R-two-mutant lung cancer, the indication where zongertinib and sevabertinib are winning. The combination cohorts, with trastuzumab, zanidatamab supplied by Jazz, Enhertu, and chemotherapy plus Keytruda, are where the bigger commercial story would have to come from. About seventy-five million dollars of the proceeds goes to this program.

Behind it are two programs that are more interesting as evidence for the platform than as near-term value. I-A-M-two-seventeen is a brain-penetrant inhibitor of K-I-F-eighteen-A, a mitotic motor protein that chromosomally unstable cancers depend on. Its I-N-D went in on September thirtieth. Iambic says it designed the compound without an inhibitor-bound crystal structure, using its own NeuralPLexer structure prediction model instead, and got from hundred-nanomolar hits to ten-nanomolar, brain-penetrant compounds in about six months. A C-D-K-two-and-four inhibitor follows, with its I-N-D planned this quarter.

The platform claims are the part to treat with skepticism. Enchant is Iambic's multimodal transformer for predicting clinical and preclinical properties. It has grown from one billion parameters to a forty-one-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts model this year. The filing claims Enchant beat a benchmark for predicting human half-life using as little as five percent of the clinical pharmacokinetic data, and outperformed "a leading L-L-M adapted for drug discovery" on several assay predictions. The automated chemistry lab runs plate-based synthesis weekly, and the models are retrained weekly. The headline speed claim is that I-A-M-thirteen-sixty-three went from program start to clinic in about two years, against an industry average of about five and a half years to I-N-D. These are all company-reported, without external benchmarks, and the risk factors say so frankly: "our approach to drug discovery is novel and unproven," and A-I "may have or produce errors or inadequacies that are not easily detectable."

The partnerships give some outside validation. Iambic lists eight collaborations since twenty twenty-four. Takeda signed in February for three targets with options on three more, plus paid access to NeuralPLexer. Revolution Medicines signed in May twenty twenty-five, and one milestone has already been hit. AbbVie signed for two targets just last month, and Lundbeck, Bayer and NVIDIA round out the list. No upfront amounts are disclosed. Collaboration revenue was nine point four million dollars last year and twelve point eight million in the first half of this year, while the net loss was fifty million dollars in that half. Pro forma cash after the offering is about four hundred nine million dollars, which the company says funds operations into or through the fourth quarter of twenty twenty-eight.

So how should you read the pricing? Kat Liu of I-P-O-X put it well to Reuters, calling Iambic "a clinical-stage biotech company with an A-I-enhanced drug discovery platform, rather than a pure A-I play." That's exactly how it got priced. The comparables are sobering. Generate Biomedicines also priced at sixteen dollars, in February, raised four hundred million dollars, closed its first day at twelve sixty-five, and sat at about twelve and a half on Wednesday, roughly twenty-two percent under water. Recursion trades below four dollars. Meanwhile ADARx, an R-N-A-interference company with no A-I framing, priced at the top of its range last month and was more than ten times oversubscribed. The market is open, with twenty-five biotech I-P-Os this year according to BioPharma Dive, far ahead of last year. But it pays for clinical assets with a near-term registrational path, not for platform narratives. Iambic got a fair price because it had a brain-penetrant H-E-R-two drug with confirmed responses after Enhertu and tucatinib, not because of Enchant.

For Calibr and Foresite, there are two takeaways. First, valuation. An A-I-native company with a clinical asset, eight pharma partnerships and a forty-one-billion-parameter model got about seven hundred sixty million dollars, below its last private round and with insider support. That's a useful marker for anyone pricing an A-I-enabled spinout or a platform partnership. Second, which evidence carries weight. The most persuasive thing in this prospectus isn't a model benchmark. It's a safety table without rash, and a K-I-F-eighteen-A program designed without a crystal structure. Those are claims that show up in the molecule. That's the standard the A-I discovery field is now being held to, whether by public investors or pharma partners.

What to watch: the opening trade this morning against sixteen dollars and against Generate's first-day drop. Then whether the four pending systemic responses confirm, and whether a H-E-R-two-mutant lung cancer dataset appears. After that, the registrational design in twenty twenty-seven, the first patient on I-A-M-two-seventeen, and whether Takeda or AbbVie programs reach the clinic with Iambic's fingerprints on them.
