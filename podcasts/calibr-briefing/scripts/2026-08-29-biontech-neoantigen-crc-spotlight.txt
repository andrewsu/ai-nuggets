Today's spotlight: nine days after the individualized neoantigen field won its first Phase three, its other lead program was stopped for futility. BioNTech and Genentech have terminated the Phase two trial of autogene cevumeran in resected colorectal cancer. The market treated this as a contradiction — BioNTech fell seven and a half percent, and Moderna, which had nothing to do with the trial, fell about six percent purely on association. I want to argue it is not a contradiction. Read together, the two trials are close to a controlled experiment on the three questions that actually determine whether this modality is a business, and the failure tells you more about which of the three matters than the success did.

Start with what was actually stopped, because the design is the whole story. The trial enrolled three hundred twenty-seven patients starting in March of twenty twenty-one. Eligibility was patients with high-risk stage two or stage three colon cancer, or stage two or three rectal cancer, who had undergone complete surgical resection — R-zero, pathology-confirmed — completed adjuvant chemotherapy, and who still had detectable circulating tumor D-N-A in their blood afterward. Those patients were randomized to autogene cevumeran, up to fifteen doses, or to watchful waiting. The primary endpoint was disease-free survival. Primary completion was scheduled for November of this year.

Now the three design decisions, each of which is the opposite of the choice Moderna and Merck made.

The first is monotherapy. There is no checkpoint inhibitor in this trial. The vaccine arm gets the vaccine and nothing else; the control arm gets observation. Compare that to INTerpath-oh-oh-one, where every patient in both arms received pembrolizumab and the question was whether adding intismeran on top improved on it. When we covered that trial on the twentieth, the point I emphasized was that the combination is not additive, it is enabling — the vaccine expands tumor-specific T cells, and the P-D-one blocker is what keeps those T cells from being switched off once they arrive. If that framing is right, then a monotherapy trial is testing the vaccine with the enabling half removed. This is the experiment that tests the claim directly, and it failed.

The second is the tumor. Melanoma is the single most immunogenic solid tumor in oncology — the highest mutational burden of any common cancer, the tumor where checkpoint blockade was invented and works best. Colorectal cancer is the other end. And this trial explicitly excluded microsatellite instability-high tumors, which is the roughly fifteen percent of colorectal cancer that is hypermutated and checkpoint-responsive. So the enrolled population was microsatellite-stable colorectal cancer: the canonical cold tumor, low neoantigen load, poor T cell infiltration, a stroma that actively excludes lymphocytes, and a decade of failed immunotherapy trials behind it. The trial deliberately selected the population in which the tumor microenvironment is most hostile.

The third is the comparator. Watchful waiting. That sounds like it should make life easy — no active control to beat. But it does the opposite of what people assume, because it means the trial has to demonstrate that the vaccine alone changes the natural history of microscopic residual disease. There is no partner drug to carry any of the load.

Put those three together and what was tested is: can neoantigen mRNA, by itself, with no checkpoint support, control minimal residual disease in the coldest common tumor there is. The answer is no. That is a real result, and it is a narrower result than the headline implies.

Here is the part that matters for how you weight it. The data monitoring board recommended stopping because of a numerical imbalance in overall survival between the arms, and because continuing was unlikely to change the efficacy outcome. BioNTech did not say which direction the imbalance ran. I want to be careful about the inference, but a monitoring board does not halt a trial for futility over an imbalance that favors the treatment arm. The company was, separately and explicitly, clear that the decision was not driven by safety and that no new safety signals were identified — which is the honest and important carve-out, because "numerical imbalance in overall survival" is a phrase that will make people assume harm, and that is not what was said. Also worth holding: this trial crossed its futility boundary once already, in October of last year, and the board let it continue on the grounds that the data were not mature. Leerink flagged that crossing at the time and moved to the sidelines on the asset. So among people paying close attention, this was not a surprise; it was a confirmation with ten months of extra follow-up attached.

Now the question that actually determines whether the platform survives: is the failure in the prediction pipeline, or in the tumor?

This is where the earlier data are unusually informative, and the answer is fairly clean. The Phase one program in advanced solid tumors ran two hundred thirteen patients — thirty on monotherapy, a hundred eighty-three in combination with atezolizumab, an anti-P-D-L-one antibody. Of the ninety patients with evaluable immune monitoring, sixty-four, or seventy-one percent, showed de novo induction or amplification of T cell responses against at least one of the neoantigens encoded in their own personalized construct. Seventy-one percent. The pipeline — sequence the tumor, sequence the germline, call the somatic mutations, predict which of the resulting peptides will be processed and presented on that specific patient's H-L-A molecules, encode up to twenty of them in a single mRNA, manufacture it, dose it — that pipeline demonstrably produces T cells against the intended targets in most patients.

And the objective responses in that same trial were in the low single digits out of two hundred thirteen. The authors were straightforward about the reason: heavily pretreated patients with prognostically unfavorable features, P-D-L-one-low or negative tumors, unlikely to respond to immunotherapy under any circumstances — and about a quarter of them progressed before the T cell response even peaked, which happens six to nine weeks after the first dose.

Then there is the pancreatic Phase one out of Memorial Sloan Kettering, Vinod Balachandran's group, first published in Nature in twenty twenty-three with three-year follow-up since. Sixteen patients with resected pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, given the vaccine with atezolizumab and FOLFIRINOX chemotherapy. Eight of sixteen mounted a response, those T cells were still detectable up to three years later, and the patients who responded immunologically had substantially longer recurrence-free survival than those who did not.

Line those up. The engineering works — you can reliably manufacture a patient-specific construct and reliably induce T cells against the predicted epitopes. What does not reliably follow is clinical benefit, and every setting where something did follow had a checkpoint inhibitor or chemotherapy in it. The binding constraint is not epitope prediction. It is what happens to those T cells after they exist.

That is worth sitting with, because it inverts where most of the field — and most of the venture capital — has placed its bets. The neoantigen prediction algorithm is the piece everyone models as the moat. It is the piece with the machine learning in it, the piece you can benchmark, the piece that makes a good slide. On the evidence here, it is largely solved, or at least solved well enough that it is no longer the rate-limiting step. The rate-limiting step is tumor immunology in the specific indication, which is not something you can improve by training a better H-L-A binding predictor.

Which brings me to what I think the practical takeaway is, and it is a question to ask rather than a conclusion. When someone brings you an individualized neoantigen platform, the two questions that carry all the information are: which tumor, and what backbone. Not how many epitopes you encode, not the architecture of the predictor, not turnaround time from resection to first dose — those matter operationally but they are not what separated these two trials. What separated them was a checkpoint-responsive tumor with a P-D-one antibody in both arms, versus a checkpoint-refractory tumor with nothing.

A couple of things to hold about what this does and does not kill.

It does not kill autogene cevumeran. The pancreatic Phase two, IMcode-oh-oh-three, is still running, and its design is the combination design — vaccine plus checkpoint inhibitor plus chemotherapy. That is the right experiment. It is also, on its face, a harder one, because pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma is if anything more immunologically hostile than microsatellite-stable colorectal cancer: dense desmoplastic stroma, low mutational burden, profound myeloid suppression. So there is a real tension. If the colorectal failure is fundamentally about the microenvironment, pancreatic should be worse. If it is fundamentally about the missing checkpoint inhibitor, the pancreatic trial could still read out. That trial is now the cleanest single test of the modality's breadth, and it is worth tracking closely.

On the money, this is a fifty-fifty problem for Roche. The Genentech collaboration dates to September twenty sixteen — three hundred ten million dollars in upfront and near-term milestones to BioNTech, with development costs and profits shared equally on the covered programs. Ten years and a terminated Phase two later, Roche has a decision to make about how much more of that shared cost it wants to fund, and colorectal was the largest commercial opportunity in the program. B-M-O's framing was that the termination reduces confidence in the asset and raises the question of whether vaccine-induced responses can prevent recurrence without concurrent checkpoint inhibition or chemotherapy. That is precisely the question, and it is now an empirical one with one data point against.

For BioNTech specifically, the value has already migrated. The company's late-stage story is pumitamig, its P-D-L-one-by-V-E-G-F bispecific, and its antibody-drug conjugates, not the vaccine platform. Leerink's position — sidelines until meaningful readouts begin in twenty twenty-seven — is about that migration as much as about this trial. A seven and a half percent move is roughly what you would expect for an asset the sell side had already largely written down.

And one last observation, about the six percent Moderna took on a trial it had nothing to do with. That move is the market pricing the modality rather than the company, and I think it is wrong in a specific way. What was validated on August nineteenth was a narrow claim: individualized neoantigen mRNA, plus a P-D-one antibody, in a checkpoint-responsive tumor, in the adjuvant setting after complete resection, where the ratio of T cells to residual tumor cells is as favorable as it ever gets. Every one of those qualifiers was doing work. Yesterday, the field learned that removing two of them — the checkpoint antibody and the responsive tumor — is sufficient to produce a null result. That does not weaken the Moderna claim at all. It sharpens the boundary around it. And a sharp boundary is more useful to anyone allocating capital than a vague enthusiasm was.

The honest summary is this. Nine days ago the field learned that the manufacturing and prediction machinery works well enough to win a Phase three. Yesterday it learned that the machinery is not sufficient on its own, and that the checkpoint inhibitor is not a nice-to-have — it is load-bearing. Those two facts are consistent, they arrived in the right order, and together they are a considerably better map of the modality than either one alone.
