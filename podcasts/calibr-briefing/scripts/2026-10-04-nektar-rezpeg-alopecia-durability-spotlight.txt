This is your Sunday spotlight, and it is on a question that keeps coming up across immunology right now: can a drug reset a disease instead of just suppressing it, and how would you know if it had? The test case is Nektar's rezpegaldesleukin, which everyone calls rezpeg, and its new alopecia areata data from the European dermatology congress in Vienna. Nektar presented on Thursday and called the data durable and deepening. The market sent the stock down about twenty-one percent intraday, to an eight-month low, on track for its worst day since February twenty twenty-three. Both reactions are defensible. The slide deck, which has more in it than the press release, tells you why.

Start with the mechanism, because the whole story rests on it. Rezpeg is a pegylated interleukin-two built to bind preferentially to the receptor complex on regulatory T cells rather than on conventional effector T cells. Regulatory T cells are the immune system's brakes. In alopecia areata, the hair follicle loses its immune privilege and T cells attack it. Every approved systemic drug for the disease is a JAK inhibitor: Lilly's baricitinib, Pfizer's ritlecitinib, Sun's deuruxolitinib. They work by suppressing the inflammatory signal, and when you stop them the signal comes back. Nektar's argument is that expanding regulatory T cells could restore the follicle's privilege, so the effect would outlast the drug. If that's right, it's a different kind of product: a course of treatment rather than a chronic prescription, with no boxed-warning class baggage and no lab monitoring.

Here is what they showed. The Phase two-b REZOLVE-AA trial randomized ninety-two patients with severe-to-very-severe alopecia areata, none previously on a JAK inhibitor or biologic, to two doses of rezpeg given twice a month, or placebo, for thirty-six weeks. Patients who had some regrowth but hadn't yet reached a SALT score of twenty or below could continue for sixteen more weeks. SALT twenty means at least eighty percent of the scalp is covered. Thirty-one patients went into that extension, twenty-seven of them on rezpeg, and eight of those twenty-seven reached SALT twenty or below by week fifty-two, against none on placebo. Those eight patients then stopped treatment. Six of the eight still held their response at four months off drug, and five of the eight at six months. Over the same six months, the share of the extension cohort with near-complete regrowth, SALT ten or below, went from seven percent to nineteen percent. That's two patients becoming five. One patient got to a SALT score of zero after stopping.

David Rosmarin of Indiana University presented, and his framing was that he has not seen this degree of sustained benefit and further regrowth after stopping any existing systemic therapy. He also made a practical point worth taking seriously: hair can take years to regrow, and patients come off drugs for insurance lapses, pregnancy, travel. A drug that tolerates interruptions is worth something even if it isn't curative.

Now the parts that explain the stock. The first is that the off-treatment claim rests on eight people. The second has been public since the primary readout: on the prespecified analysis of all randomized patients, the thirty-six-week primary endpoint missed significance. Mean SALT reductions were about twenty-eight and thirty percent on the two doses against eleven percent on placebo, with p-values of zero point one nine and zero point one two. They became significant only after a post-hoc exclusion of four patients for eligibility violations. The third is on slide twelve, in a footnote, and isn't in the press release. Patients who reached SALT twenty after only thirty-six weeks of treatment, rather than fifty-two, also stopped drug. Four of six held at four months. One of six held at six months.

That footnote changes how you read the whole result. One reading is that the Treg reset needs time to take: thirty-six weeks wasn't enough, a year was, and that's exactly why Nektar has written a fifty-two-week induction into Phase three. That's a coherent biological story, and it predicts a dose-duration relationship you could test. The other reading is that with six and eight patients per group, you're looking at noise, and the fifty-two-week cohort is also enriched for the slow responders who were still improving at week thirty-six. You can't separate those readings with this dataset. The company's own deck shows both groups, so this isn't hidden, but you only see it if you read past the headline.

The comparator problem is the bigger issue, and it's the one I'd push on. Nektar's slides claim superior off-treatment durability against historical JAK inhibitor data. The relevant benchmark is the randomized withdrawal substudy of Lilly's BRAVE-AA1 trial, published in JAMA Dermatology in twenty twenty-four. Baricitinib responders at week fifty-two were re-randomized to keep going or switch to placebo. At four weeks off drug, zero percent had lost benefit. At eight weeks, ten to eleven percent. By week one fifty-two, about a hundred weeks off drug, eighty percent had. Two things follow. The definitions don't match: Lilly counted loss of benefit as a twenty-point worsening in SALT score, while Nektar counts whether patients stay at or below twenty, so the percentages can't be compared directly. And even on a JAK inhibitor, which nobody thinks modifies the disease, relapse in alopecia areata is slow, because the hair cycle is slow. A six-month off-drug window can't tell a reset disease apart from one that's relapsing on a hair-cycle timescale. The data that would settle it is twelve to twenty-four months off drug, ideally randomized.

The Street split, and roughly along those lines. Jefferies' Roger Song called the selloff unwarranted, kept a Buy and a hundred-fifty-dollar target, and described alopecia areata as an underappreciated and untapped disease space for the drug. William Blair's Andy Hsieh kept Outperform, saying the update adds to evidence that rezpeg has a material durability advantage over current standard treatments. What the selling says is that holders had priced the durability story as more settled than eight patients can make it.

The atopic dermatitis data presented at the same congress is, I'd argue, the stronger commercial result, even though it got less attention. In the three-hundred-ninety-three-patient REZOLVE-AD trial, patients who had at least a fifty percent skin improvement at week sixteen were re-randomized to monthly or quarterly maintenance dosing through week fifty-two. Responses held and deepened. Among all re-randomized patients, complete clearance, EASI one hundred, rose from four percent to twenty-two percent on monthly dosing and from nine to eighteen percent on quarterly dosing. For context, the sixteen-week induction gave a sixty-one percent mean reduction in eczema severity on the top dose against thirty-one percent on placebo. That's solid but not better than Dupixent at induction. The differentiation is the maintenance schedule. A Treg agonist given four times a year, against an I-L-four receptor antibody given every two weeks, is a real convenience story, and it's the same kind of argument as the off-drug durability in alopecia, just made under randomized, blinded conditions with larger numbers. Nektar has now started a third Phase three in eczema, this one in patients who have already failed a biologic or a JAK inhibitor.

The competitive and historical context explains why this program matters beyond Nektar. The Treg-selective interleukin-two field is mostly a graveyard. Amgen discontinued efavaleukin alfa. Lilly licensed rezpeg itself, ran it in lupus, and returned it. And as we covered on September twenty-sixth, a federal jury just found that Lilly breached the implied covenant of good faith in how it handled that partnership and awarded Nektar ninety million dollars, though that's subject to appeal. So rezpeg is now the most advanced Treg agonist in dermatology, wholly owned, with Phase three running in eczema and the alopecia Phase three, called ZENITH-AA, starting early next year with about eight hundred fifty patients.

The detail I'd flag from that design is the primary endpoint: SALT twenty or below at week fifty-two, on treatment. That's an efficacy endpoint, not a durability one. As disclosed, the pivotal trial will tell you whether rezpeg works about as well as a JAK inhibitor on drug, not whether it does the one thing that would make it a different product. If Nektar wants a durability label claim, or the payer argument for a finite course of therapy, it needs a prespecified off-treatment follow-up with real numbers behind it. Whether ZENITH-AA includes one is the question to ask on the next call.

For your purposes, the general lesson is about what immune-reset claims need to show, and it carries well beyond this drug. CAR-T in autoimmunity, tolerizing vaccines, Treg cell therapies: they'll all claim drug-free remission, and they'll all face the same problems: small off-drug cohorts, diseases that relapse slowly, and comparisons against historical controls that used different definitions of relapse. The standard to hold them to is randomized withdrawal, run long enough to beat the natural relapse kinetics of the disease. Rezpeg's alopecia data is interesting and probably real at some level. It isn't yet proof that the disease was reset.
