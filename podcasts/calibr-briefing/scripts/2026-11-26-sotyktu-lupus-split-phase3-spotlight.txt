This is your spotlight for Thursday, November twenty-sixth, 2026. In September, when Alumis missed in lupus, we said the most important remaining data point for TYK-two in lupus was Bristol Myers Squibb's two Phase three POETYK trials of Sotyktu. If they hit, the class was alive. If they missed, it was finished. On Wednesday they reported, and the answer was neither. One trial hit and one missed. That split is worth working through, because it decides whether Bristol has a filing, what the F-D-A will make of it, and what's left for Alumis, which Foresite Labs incubated.

Start with what Bristol actually reported. POETYK SLE-one and SLE-two were global, double-blind, placebo-controlled trials, designed nearly the same way. Each enrolled about five hundred ten adults with moderate-to-severe systemic lupus. Each tested Sotyktu, or deucravacitinib, at three milligrams twice a day, on top of the patient's usual lupus treatment. The primary endpoint in both was an S-R-I-four response at week fifty-two. That's a composite: a patient counts as a responder if their disease activity score drops by at least four points, without new organ flares and without their doctor's overall assessment getting worse.

SLE-one met that endpoint. It also reached statistical significance on BICLA, which is the other main lupus composite, on S-R-I-four with a required steroid taper, and on patient-reported fatigue. The other key secondary endpoints favored the drug, but not significantly. Those were low disease activity state, a halving of swollen and tender joints, and a halving of skin disease. SLE-two missed its primary endpoint. Bristol says patients on the drug responded about as well as in SLE-one, but the placebo response was higher, and every key secondary favored the drug only numerically.

What Bristol didn't release matters as much. There are no response rates, no placebo rates, no p-values and no deltas, for either trial. "Consistent with SLE-one" and "higher placebo response" are Bristol's descriptions, and we can't check them yet. Full results go to a future medical meeting, and Bristol says it will discuss the whole program with health authorities, including its Phase two data.

The market took it calmly. Bristol closed at sixty-one dollars and fifty-six cents, down less than one percent. That tells you how little lupus was in the stock. Sotyktu is approved in psoriasis and psoriatic arthritis and has sold below expectations. Lupus was meant to be the big expansion. We found no named sell-side commentary we could read this morning, so the stock move is the best measure of sentiment we have.

Now, why would two nearly identical trials split? The mechanism is sound. TYK-two sits directly downstream of the type one interferon receptor, as well as the interleukin twenty-three and interleukin twelve receptors. Type one interferon is the best-validated pathway in lupus. Sotyktu binds TYK-two's regulatory pseudokinase domain rather than its active site, which is how it avoids hitting JAK-one, two and three, and how it avoids the JAK class's boxed warning. The Phase two PAISLEY trial was clearly positive. At week thirty-two, fifty-eight percent of patients on this dose were S-R-I-four responders, against thirty-four percent on placebo.

The problem is how lupus trials are run. Everyone stays on background therapy, which means antimalarials, immunosuppressants and often steroids. Composite scores like S-R-I-four depend on physician judgment and on which organ systems happen to be active. So placebo response rates in lupus Phase threes typically run from the thirties into the forties and vary a lot from trial to trial, especially across regions. When the placebo arm swings up by ten points, a real but modest drug effect can drop below significance. Bristol's own explanation fits that pattern. Its secondary results add a second point. Even in the winning trial, the endpoints that didn't separate, low disease activity, joints and skin, are the ones doctors care about most. Skin is where you'd expect an interferon-pathway drug to look best. So this looks like a modest drug effect, not a large effect that one trial happened to hide.

Can Bristol still file? There is a direct precedent, and Bristol's statement pointed to it when it said the Phase three results, "coupled with our Phase two SLE results," reinforce its confidence. The precedent is AstraZeneca's Saphnelo, or anifrolumab, the interferon receptor antibody. Its first Phase three, TULIP-one, missed its S-R-I-four primary endpoint. Its second, TULIP-two, used BICLA as the primary and hit. The F-D-A approved Saphnelo in 2021 on the package: two Phase threes, one of which missed, plus the positive Phase two, MUSE. So one hit and one miss is not automatically fatal in lupus, and the F-D-A has shown it understands the placebo problem in this disease.

The differences cut against Bristol, though. Anifrolumab's case was strengthened because a later look at BICLA in TULIP-one favored the drug, so both trials pointed the same way on the same measure. Here, the second trial showed only numerical trends on everything. And Saphnelo was the first new lupus drug in a decade, while Bristol would be filing an oral drug into a field that's far more crowded than in 2021. So the realistic range runs from a filing on SLE-one plus PAISLEY, with a modest label, to a third trial, which would push lupus out several years. The detailed data will settle it. The two numbers to watch are SLE-two's placebo rate, and whether the drug-arm response really matches SLE-one region by region.

Now the competition, because Sotyktu wouldn't launch into empty space. Approved, there's GSK's Benlysta, which blocks the B-cell survival factor BAFF, and Saphnelo. Roche's Gazyva, an anti-CD-twenty antibody, is approved in lupus nephritis. Further back, UCB and Biogen's dapirolizumab pegol, which blocks CD-forty ligand, has a positive Phase three and a second running. Biogen's litifilimab targets the plasmacytoid dendritic cells that make interferon. Novartis has ianalumab against the BAFF receptor. AbbVie is running Phase threes of Rinvoq, its JAK-one inhibitor, in lupus, and Rinvoq just got a vitiligo approval this week. Above all of these are the CD-nineteen CAR-T cells and T-cell engagers, which aim to reset the immune system rather than suppress it. A twice-daily pill with a clean safety record and a modest effect has a real role in that market. It's the drug you add before infusions, especially for patients trying to get off steroids. But it isn't the blockbuster the expansion plan assumed.

It also matters a lot to Bristol right now. Opdivo biosimilars have a settled U-S launch date of October 2028. Eliquis faces its own patent expiry. And Bristol has just reorganized research and development under Cristian Massacesi, who is quoted in this release. Sotyktu also has a Phase three in Sjögren's disease, reading out in 2027, a disease where Amgen's dazodalibep just reported positive Phase three data. That trial now matters more.

Now Alumis, which is the Foresite angle. Alumis's envudeucitinib is also an allosteric TYK-two inhibitor. In September its Phase two-b LUMUS trial missed on BICLA in the overall population. Alumis argued that patients with a high interferon gene signature responded strongly, and that too few of them had enrolled. Wednesday's result gives Alumis a modest lift. A TYK-two inhibitor beating placebo on S-R-I-four in a properly powered Phase three supports the idea that the class works in lupus. It also backs up the point we made in September: Alumis's problem may have been its endpoint and its all-comer population, not the target. But the result also shows the effect is modest even in Bristol's hands. So there's no strong case for Alumis spending its own money on a lupus Phase three, whether in the interferon-high subgroup or anyone else.

The market agreed. Alumis closed Wednesday at about four dollars fifty-three, essentially unchanged. Its lupus value was written off in September and Bristol's news didn't bring it back. What the company is now worth depends on psoriasis. In its November twelfth quarterly report, Alumis said it is on track to file for psoriasis approval this quarter, with a fifty-four percent rate of completely clear skin at week forty-eight. It had four hundred thirty-one million dollars in cash at the end of September, which it says lasts into the fourth quarter of 2027. In lupus, it plans end-of-Phase-two meetings with the F-D-A and says it is "evaluating development options." In practice, that probably means looking for a partner, not running a Phase three alone. The CNS-penetrant TYK-two inhibitor, A-zero-zero-five, starts a Phase two biomarker trial in Parkinson's in 2027.

There's a broader lesson for anyone running autoimmune trials, Calibr's programs included. The lesson isn't about TYK-two. It's that in lupus, the variance between trials can be as large as the drug effect. Bristol ran what was effectively the same trial twice, about a thousand patients, with a positive Phase two behind it, and still couldn't get two wins. Programs that do well here either produce effects big enough to clear that noise, which is the pitch for B-cell reset with CAR-T and T-cell engagers, or they reduce the noise by choosing patients by biology and using harder endpoints. A modest oral drug in an all-comer population is the hardest position to win from.

Three things to watch. First, the full POETYK data at a medical meeting, especially SLE-two's placebo rate, and whether the drug arm's response held up across regions. Second, Bristol's account of its talks with the F-D-A: a filing would mean the agency accepts one trial plus PAISLEY, the way it did for Saphnelo, while a new trial would mean it doesn't. Third, for Alumis, the psoriasis filing this quarter, then the F-D-A's acceptance and any partnership news. In psoriasis, Alumis is competing on how well its drug works. In lupus, this week suggests the class doesn't work well enough. That's the spotlight. Talk soon, and enjoy the holiday.
