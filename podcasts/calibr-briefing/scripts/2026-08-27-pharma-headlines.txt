Good morning Travis. Here are your biotech and pharma headlines for Thursday, August twenty-seventh, twenty twenty-six.

Yesterday was the biggest day a Foresite name has had in years. RAS, the target the field has called undruggable since the early nineteen eighties, now has an approved medicine in the cancer it drives hardest, and the approval landed thirty-five days after the application went in. Around it, a nearly eight-hundred-million-dollar ion channel deal, a China-only phase three that unseats Imfinzi in biliary tract cancer, a TL1A antibody that hit statistical significance and got shelved anyway, Cellares punching back at Bristol Myers, and a Massachusetts report that quantifies precisely which end of the funding barbell is starving. Eight items, then the week ahead.

First, and today's spotlight. The F-D-A approved Rasonque, daraxonrasib, from Revolution Medicines, for adults with metastatic pancreatic adenocarcinoma who have had at least one prior systemic therapy or who are not candidates for multi-agent chemotherapy. Rasonque is an oral, once-daily, non-covalent RAS-ON multi-selective tri-complex inhibitor. It is the first approved medicine from that entire class, and it is the first targeted therapy of any kind approved in the most common form of pancreatic cancer. In the phase three RASolute three-oh-two trial, five hundred patients, median overall survival was thirteen point two months against six point seven for investigator's choice chemotherapy, a hazard ratio of zero point four zero, a sixty percent reduction in the risk of death. Progression-free survival was seven point two months against three point six. And critically, there is no companion diagnostic on the label — the drug is approved for patients with or without an identified RAS mutation. Revolution Medicines is a Foresite Capital portfolio alumnus, so this one is close to home. The list price is thirty-nine thousand eight hundred dollars for a thirty-day supply, R-B-C models peak sales around eleven and a half billion dollars, and the company is now worth about forty-five billion. We take the whole thing apart in the spotlight, including why the wild-type patients are the most consequential number that has not been published, and what a thirty-five-day review says about the voucher program.

Second, the deal of the day. Biohaven licensed its K-v-seven ion channel platform and its lead epilepsy asset, opakalim, to S-K Biopharmaceutical of Seoul for up to seven hundred ninety-five million dollars in total value. The structure matters more than the headline. Four hundred million is near-term cash — three hundred fifty million at closing plus fifty million next year — with up to a hundred fifty million in development and regulatory milestones on top. The remainder is S-K assuming Biohaven's obligations from the twenty twenty-two Knopp Biosciences transaction that brought the platform in, up to a hundred eighty-five million, plus up to sixty million more in technology-related milestones. Opakalim is an oral once-daily selective potassium channel activator in focal epilepsy; the phase two-three RISE-three trial reads out in the second half of this year. Biohaven had two hundred seventy million dollars in cash at the end of June, so a three-hundred-fifty-million-dollar closing payment roughly triples the balance sheet ahead of a binary readout. Read that as what it is: Biohaven is selling the asset it is about to get data on, in order to fund the asset it would rather own, which is taldefgrobep alfa, the myostatin and activin receptor blocker it is repositioning as muscle-sparing obesity therapy after the spinal muscular atrophy failure. Mid-stage obesity data are due before year end. William Blair called this a catalyst-rich period that is not without risk but potentially transformative, and that is a fair description of a company that just de-risked its cash and kept all of its clinical risk.

Third, ivonescimab's first win outside lung cancer. Summit Therapeutics and Akeso reported that ivonescimab, the P-D-one by V-E-G-F bispecific antibody, beat AstraZeneca's Imfinzi in first-line biliary tract cancer in the phase three HARMONi-G-I-one trial in China. Six hundred eighty-two patients, prespecified interim analysis, primary endpoint of overall survival met, all key secondary endpoints — progression-free survival and objective response rate — also met. No numbers released. Summit's shares rose about fifteen percent premarket. Two things to hold onto. This is the first randomized phase three in advanced biliary tract cancer to show a statistically significant overall survival benefit, and it did it against an anti-P-D-one plus chemotherapy control, which is the internationally recognized preferred regimen. That is a real result. But it is a China-only trial with no disclosed data, and the last time Akeso delivered an N-M-P-A approval, the label revealed progression-free survival degradation in the supporting HARMONi-six study, which is why Leerink went on record worrying that the U-S accelerated approval trial, HARMONi-three, will not show substantial benefit when it finalizes this half. The real moment of truth is November fourteenth, the P-D-U-F-A date for ivonescimab in E-G-F-R-mutated non-small cell lung cancer.

Fourth, an instructive negative. Spyre Therapeutics reported the rheumatoid arthritis sub-study of its phase two SKYWAY basket trial of S-P-Y zero-seven-two, a long-acting TL1A antibody. Both doses beat placebo with statistical significance on at least one endpoint — the low dose hit the primary, change from baseline in DAS-twenty-eight-C-R-P, at minus one point nine against minus one point three on placebo — and both doses achieved complete and durable suppression of free TL1A through week twelve, so target engagement is not the question. Safety was clean, adverse events actually lower on drug than placebo. And Spyre is not advancing it in rheumatoid arthritis. Cameron Turtle's language was that the magnitude of effect did not meet the company's internal bar to prioritize monotherapy. Look at the numbers and you see why: A-C-R-twenty was sixty-three percent on high dose against forty-three on placebo, and A-C-R-fifty was thirty-one against nineteen. Those are respectable and they are not competitive in a market with J-A-K inhibitors and I-L-six blockade. This is a company with a stated combination strategy behaving consistently with it — proof of mechanism banked, monotherapy shelved, psoriatic arthritis and axial spondyloarthritis readouts next quarter, and a TL1A plus I-L-seventeen-A-F combination in hidradenitis suppurativa now dosing. The generalizable point for anyone running a basket trial: a prespecified internal bar that is higher than the statistical bar is the only thing that stops a significant-but-mediocre result from consuming five years of development spend.

Fifth, Cellares is not conceding. Yesterday I covered Bristol Myers terminating the Cell Shuttle relationship after concluding the system could not meet commercial requirements for Breyanzi, and roughly a hundred layoffs effective October twentieth. Cellares came back publicly to rebut the characterization. Fabian Gerlinghaus's position is that the platform has been proven in the clinic — that in the first quarter, cell therapies manufactured on the Cell Shuttle in an F-D-A-regulated environment were administered to patients for a paying customer, and that the response from other partners has been encouraging across the board. So you now have a direct factual disagreement between a large pharma and its vendor about whether a manufacturing platform is commercial-ready, conducted in public. That is unusual and it is worth watching, because "can produce clinical material under G-M-P" and "can produce commercial batches at scale for a launched product" are genuinely different claims, and both statements can be true at once.

Sixth, on the artificial intelligence side. Faro raised a thirty-seven point three million dollar Series B co-led by Merck Global Health Innovation Fund and S-thirty-two, with every existing investor participating — General Catalyst, Northpond, Polaris, P-T-X Capital and Zetta — plus Ankona Capital as a new name. Faro is not a molecule generator. It builds structured data and agentic A-I infrastructure for the clinical development layer: protocol design, clinical document generation, risk identification, workflow automation from first-in-human through approval. Six of the ten largest pharmas already use the platform, Bristol Myers is a named protocol-design partner, and the company's stated ambition is to cut trial timelines in half within five years. I flag this because it is the part of the A-I stack that has been undercapitalized relative to its leverage. Discovery-stage A-I compresses the cheapest and fastest phase of the value chain. Trial design, document generation and regulatory workflow are where the years and the dollars actually sit, and a strategic like Merck's fund co-leading is a signal about where large pharma thinks the automation payoff is.

Seventh, a number on the early-stage funding barbell. MassBio's report on twenty twenty-five and the first half of twenty twenty-six lands hard. Massachusetts biotechs raised nearly three and a half billion dollars in the first half, up twenty-five percent year over year, and eight Boston-area companies went public between January and July, with Parabilis Medicines setting a sector record for I-P-O proceeds. Underneath that recovery, the distribution has broken. Average seed round: four point six five million dollars. Average Series A: roughly eighty million. MassBio names the earliest startups with the riskiest science as its single biggest concern, because venture dollars have migrated to de-risked, later assets. Meanwhile the Massachusetts workforce shrank for the first time since the Life Sciences Initiative began, about three thousand six hundred jobs lost in twenty twenty-five, and China's pipeline grew thirty-six point two percent year over year and passed Europe for the first time while the U-S pipeline stayed flat. If you want a case study in what a four-point-six-five-million-dollar seed environment produces, look at Aurora Therapeutics — the bespoke C-R-I-S-P-R company Jennifer Doudna and Fyodor Urnov launched in January on sixteen million dollars from Menlo Ventures. Earlier this month it scrapped its lead phenylketonuria program, cut staff, and lost its C-E-O, Ed Kaye, seven months after launch. Endpoints ran a piece yesterday on how uncertain its future now is.

Eighth, and this one goes straight into today's spotlight. Immuneering issued a press release yesterday specifically to respond to the Rasonque approval and reposition itself. Ben Zeskind's argument is that second line is now addressed and the field's priority moves to first line, where Immuneering's atebimetinib — an oral once-daily deep cyclic inhibitor of MEK — is in the global randomized phase three MAPKeeper three-oh-one, already listing more than thirty-five sites. Their phase two-a in fifty-five first-line patients, atebimetinib plus modified gemcitabine and nab-paclitaxel, reported seventeen point three months median overall survival with eighty-four percent of evaluable patients maintaining or gaining weight at three months, and the only grade three or higher treatment-related events above ten percent were anemia at sixteen percent and neutropenia at eighteen, both attributable to the chemotherapy. That is a single-arm number against a randomized one and you should discount it accordingly. But notice what Immuneering chose to emphasize — weight maintenance, and the absence of rash, diarrhea, stomatitis and fatigue. That is not a coincidence. It is aimed precisely at Rasonque's label.
