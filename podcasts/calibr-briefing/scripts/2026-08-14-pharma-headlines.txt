Good morning, Travis. Here are your biotech and pharma headlines for Friday, August 14th, 2026. Five stories today.

Number one, and today's spotlight. A safety cloud is forming over Neurocrine's biggest bet. Three leading Prader-Willi syndrome patient organizations flagged that the FDA's adverse-event database has logged seven deaths and more than a hundred serious adverse events in patients taking Vykat X-R — the extended-release diazoxide choline drug for hyperphagia that Neurocrine paid 2.9 billion dollars for when it bought Soleno in the spring. The reports cluster around fluid retention and respiratory and cardiac problems, which happen to match both diazoxide's known mechanism and the exact vulnerabilities of Prader-Willi patients. The groups asked for slower titration and closer monitoring, not withdrawal, and stopped short of claiming the drug caused the deaths — baseline mortality in this population is already one to three percent a year. Neurocrine is standing behind the drug, the stock dipped only about two percent, and the deeper risk, as BMO framed it, is that physician caution quietly caps a launch analysts had pegged above two billion dollars. And it's not isolated — Aardvark's rival bitter-taste-receptor drug just got abandoned after its own cardiac signal. We go deep on this in the spotlight.

Number two. A clean Phase 3 win in lung cancer. Taiho and Cullinan announced that their drug zipalertinib, combined with chemotherapy, met its primary endpoint of progression-free survival at a planned interim analysis in first-line treatment of non-small-cell lung cancer driven by EGFR exon 20 insertion mutations. The independent monitoring committee recommended unblinding the trial early. Zipalertinib targets that hard-to-drug exon 20 population, and a first-line win positions it directly against Johnson and Johnson's amivantamab, Rybrevant, which has been staking out that same setting. The companies plan to take the first-line combination to the FDA; a rolling submission for the drug is already underway. For Cullinan, a company that has been repositioning around this asset, this is a meaningful de-risking.

Number three. A gene therapy changes hands out of bankruptcy. PTC Therapeutics won a competitive auction for Sangamo's Fabry disease gene therapy, ST-920, agreeing to about 111 million dollars upfront and up to another hundred million in regulatory milestones — outbidding Astellas and TerSera. ST-920 is a one-time AAV gene therapy that delivers a working copy of the GLA gene to liver cells so patients can make the enzyme they're missing; it's near the finish line, with a potential accelerated approval later this year and a launch in 2027. In the same bankruptcy sale, Eli Lilly picked up Sangamo's prion-disease program and some of its platform technology. It's a reminder that when a gene-therapy company runs out of money, the assets don't disappear — they get repriced and absorbed.

Number four. A royalty deal worth watching for the financing signal. Zealand Pharma sold most of its remaining economic interest in rusfertide — a hepcidin-mimetic drug for the rare blood cancer polycythemia vera — to Royalty Pharma for up to a hundred million dollars, fifty million upfront and fifty million on the first anniversary. Rusfertide was developed with Protagonist and is now carried by Takeda, with an FDA decision expected this quarter. Zealand keeps a small royalty on sales above one and a half billion dollars. The read-through: this is non-dilutive cash raised against a soon-to-be-approved asset, and it's the same polycythemia vera market where Silence's siRNA drug divesiran just posted strong Phase 2 data — a small rare-disease indication that's suddenly getting crowded.

Number five, and your AI-in-discovery watch. Two early rounds worth noting. Beyang Therapeutics, a Chinese drug-discovery company built around artificial intelligence, closed an oversubscribed Series A of nearly 30 million dollars. And Israeli developer Remepy raised a 36 million dollar Series A to fund global Phase 3 testing of its Parkinson's candidate and to expand a model that pairs a prescription drug with AI-driven therapeutic software — a "drug-plus-software" approach. Neither is a headline deal on its own, but together they mark the continued push of AI capital past pure discovery and into how trials and therapies are actually delivered.
