Today's spotlight is on something that sounds like semantics and is not. On Wednesday in Glasgow, the myeloma field wrote down what a cure is. On Friday, Legend Biotech presented the first headline dataset into that frame. Between those two events sits a real change in what a cell therapy has to prove, and it has consequences for how you design one.

Start with the definition, because the details are load-bearing. Nikhil Munshi presented it on behalf of the International Myeloma Society and the International Myeloma Working Group, jointly, on the first substantive day of the twenty-third annual meeting. Myeloma was described as uniformly fatal when it was first identified in eighteen forty-four, and for essentially all of the intervening hundred and eighty years the word cure was not used about it. Now there is a checklist.

A patient with newly diagnosed or relapsed myeloma may be considered cured after five years in complete remission with no anti-myeloma therapy of any kind. The clock starts the day treatment stops — not the day the patient first achieved remission, however long they were disease-free while still on drug. Over those five years there must be at least four negative measurable residual disease assessments, including one at the five-year mark, with no positive result in between, using next-generation sequencing or next-generation flow cytometry at a sensitivity of one myeloma cell in a million. And advanced imaging — PET-C-T or diffusion-weighted whole-body magnetic resonance — must be negative at the start and the end of the window, with no positive scan in between if any are done.

Three design choices in there are worth pulling apart.

The off-treatment requirement is the sharpest one. It exists to separate durable eradication of disease from pharmacologic suppression of disease. A patient in complete remission on continuous lenalidomide is not the same object as a patient in complete remission on nothing, and until now the field's endpoints — progression-free survival, response rate, even sustained measurable residual disease negativity — did not distinguish them. Making treatment-free time the spine of the definition is a statement that the interesting quantity is what the patient's own immune system and marrow are doing after you take the drug away.

The five-year window is empirical rather than arbitrary. Relapse risk drops substantially once a patient has been off treatment that long, and the investigators anchored the choice to Hodgkin lymphoma and testicular cancer, two diseases where the word cure is already standard. They were honest that late relapse still happens.

And the imaging requirement is the quiet one that will cause the most operational pain. Marrow-based measurable residual disease testing misses extramedullary and focal disease by construction — you are sampling one site in a spatially heterogeneous disease. Requiring a negative functional scan at both ends is an admission that a single-site molecular assay is not sufficient evidence of absence.

Now, why this happened now. Munshi told the room that the field's growing confidence came from three things: two decades of steady survival improvement, rising rates of deep measurable residual disease negativity, and follow-up data showing a real fraction of patients treatment-free and disease-free five years or more after therapy. The specific dataset he cited — and this is the part relevant to you — was CARTITUDE-one: thirty-two of ninety-seven heavily pretreated patients alive and progression-free at least five years after a single C-A-R T-cell infusion given with no maintenance. Munshi framed the requirement as convergence across three domains — in his words, statistical, clinical, and biological — and said that when all three align you can have a credible declaration. Historical estimates of how many patients older therapies may have cured range from five to thirty percent, with enormous variation by regimen, age and risk.

That is the frame. Now Friday.

Legend presented five-year follow-up from Cohort A of CARTITUDE-two — twenty patients treated with Carvykti, ciltacabtagene autoleucel, a B-C-M-A-directed autologous C-A-R T-cell therapy, after one to three prior lines rather than the four-plus of the original study. Median follow-up sixty point seven months. Ten of twenty patients, fifty percent, remain alive and progression-free with no further anti-myeloma treatment at five years or more. Median progression-free survival sixty point five months. Sixty-nine point two percent alive at five years, median overall survival not reached. Alan Bash, who runs the Carvykti franchise at Legend, called it evidence of unique curative potential, and Niels van de Donk at Amsterdam University Medical Center said half a cohort remaining progression- and treatment-free at five years is unique in myeloma.

Legend's argument is specific and reasonable: move the therapy earlier and the cure fraction goes up. Thirty-three percent in heavily pretreated patients in CARTITUDE-one, fifty percent in one-to-three prior lines here. Earlier line means less exhausted T-cell starting material at apheresis, less clonal evolution, less extramedullary disease. Mechanistically that is exactly the direction you would predict, and it is the whole strategic case for pushing autologous C-A-R T-cell therapy into second line and eventually first.

Here is where I would be careful, and it is not a criticism of the data so much as of how the number will get used.

Twenty patients is a cohort, not a trial. Ten of twenty is fifty percent with a confidence interval that comfortably contains the CARTITUDE-one number. Nothing in this dataset yet establishes that earlier line raises the cure fraction — it is consistent with that hypothesis and it is also consistent with noise around a single underlying rate. The honest statement is that both datasets support a durable treatment-free fraction on the order of a third to a half, and that the direction of the difference is encouraging.

Second, and more interesting: nothing presented Friday was actually scored against the new definition. The Legend readout reports patients alive and progression-free without further anti-myeloma treatment. It does not report four serial measurable residual disease assessments at one in a million with no intervening positive, and it does not report functional imaging at both ends of the window. The closest anyone has come is a twelve-patient single-center subset of CARTITUDE-one that had serial measurable residual disease and PET-C-T and was negative on both at year five or later — at ten to the minus five, not ten to the minus six, and n equals twelve. So on the day the field got a definition of cure, the best long-term cell therapy data in the field does not yet demonstrably meet it. That is not a scandal; it is what happens when you write a standard after the fact. But it is the assay burden every trial you design from here has to carry, and most existing protocols were not built to carry it. If you are writing a durability claim into a protocol now, the checklist is annual marrows, sequencing or flow at one in a million, imaging at both ends, and a treatment-free clock that starts at last dose.

Third, the denominator cuts both ways on safety too. Since the thirty-month analysis of this same cohort, one patient developed acute myeloid leukemia and two patients died, one of progressive disease and one of a new cancer. In a twenty-patient cohort, one secondary hematologic malignancy is five percent. Secondary malignancy after C-A-R T-cell therapy is a live regulatory question, and a cure claim and a five percent secondary leukemia rate belong in the same sentence, not in different press releases.

Now the part that matters most for how you think about the field, which is that the cure definition arrived in the same week as two pieces of evidence that the incumbent cell therapy is being squeezed from both sides.

From above, Arcellx and Kite's anito-cel. Surbhi Sidana at Stanford presented a matching-adjusted indirect comparison against CARTITUDE-one — individual patient data from iMMagine-one against published aggregate data from the cilta-cel trial. After adjustment, efficacy was statistically indistinguishable: response rate ninety-six percent against ninety-eight, complete response or better seventy-four against eighty. The safety differences were not indistinguishable. Any-grade cytokine release syndrome eighty-five percent against ninety-five. Any-grade neurotoxicity eight percent against twenty-three. Grade three or four infections nine percent against twenty-three. Non-relapse mortality three percent against eleven. And zero cases of non-neurotoxicity-syndrome neurological events in iMMagine-one against twelve in CARTITUDE-one, including five cases of parkinsonism. An unanchored indirect comparison against aggregate data with different follow-up is the weakest form of comparative evidence — it can only adjust for reported baseline factors, and the sponsor picked the variables — so treat the point estimates as directional. But the direction is the point: if efficacy is at parity, the competition moves entirely to toxicity and logistics, and that is a fight the newer construct is currently winning. The action date is December twenty-third.

From below, the T-cell engagers. AbbVie's etentamig, a second-generation B-C-M-A by C-D-three bispecific, beat investigator's choice standard therapy in a three-hundred-ninety-three-patient Phase three — response rate seventy-four percent against forty-six, progression hazard ratio zero point four zero — with monthly dosing after a single step-up dose and cytokine release syndrome at twenty-eight percent, all grade one or two in that group. And in a small first cohort, the cereblon modulator mezigdomide plus the engager elranatamab produced responses in all thirteen evaluable patients, ninety-two percent complete response or better, and no neurotoxicity at all. Those are off-the-shelf drugs — no apheresis, no manufacturing slot, no lymphodepletion, no bridging.

Put the three things together, because the combination is the message.

The field just defined its endpoint as treatment-free survival with a molecular floor. That definition structurally favors modalities that are given once and then stop — which is autologous C-A-R T-cell therapy's one irreplaceable property, and precisely what a chronically dosed engager does not have. Hans Lee at Sarah Cannon made exactly this point: because the definition hinges on sustained time off treatment, it strengthens the rationale for fixed-duration regimens over treat-to-progression. That is a clinical argument with a large commercial shadow, because treat-to-progression is how most myeloma revenue is booked. His summary: in myeloma the time is now to be thinking about cure, not just about control.

But the same week showed that C-A-R T-cell therapy's advantage on the endpoint is being attacked on both flanks — by a competitor with apparently equivalent efficacy and materially better tolerability, and by engager regimens that are trying to buy the one-and-done property through fixed duration rather than through a single infusion. The engager field is now openly optimizing for the ability to stop.

The design question that falls out of this is whether you can get the durability without the chain. The value of a single autologous infusion is the durability; the cost is apheresis, a manufacturing slot, lymphodepletion, a hospital, and an eleven percent non-relapse mortality in the oldest dataset. If a fixed-duration off-the-shelf regimen can produce four consecutive annual measurable-residual-disease-negative marrows at one in a million with clean imaging, it wins on every axis that is not durability, and it will win on durability often enough to matter. That is why in vivo C-A-R T-cell approaches and fixed-duration engager regimens shared a program in Glasgow with the cure consensus. They are answers to the same question.

One last commercial anchor, so the stakes are concrete. Carvykti did roughly six hundred fifty-seven million dollars in net trade sales in the June quarter, up fifty percent year over year, and it carried Legend to its first profitable quarter. The cure argument is not a scientific flourish. It is the specific asset that defends a growing franchise against a cheaper, safer, off-the-shelf alternative arriving in December. Which is exactly why having real criteria that a company either meets or does not is good for the field. It converts a marketing adjective into a falsifiable claim, and it puts the burden of proof where it belongs: on four annual marrows, two scans, and five years of nobody touching the patient.
