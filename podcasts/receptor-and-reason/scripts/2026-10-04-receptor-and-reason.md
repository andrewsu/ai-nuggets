## Script

Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Sunday, October fourth. Ten items today. A note on sourcing: bioRxiv's full-text channel was almost entirely shut this run, returning rate-limit stubs across several rounds of patient retries, so a few preprints landed late and others stayed unread. Today's lineup leans on two Nature papers, on medRxiv, which was wide open as usual, and on arXiv. A through-line emerged on its own, and it's worth naming up front, because four separate papers arrive at it from different directions: each discovers that the quantity its field has been maximizing is not the quantity that governs the outcome.

We start with the clearest instance, and it's a genuinely new way to antagonize a receptor. A group reporting in Nature has built what they call GTACs — GPCR–transferrin-receptor targeting chimeras. The idea is induced proximity, borrowed from the degrader world, but aimed at the cell surface rather than the proteasome. A GTAC is a bispecific antibody: one arm binds a GPCR, the other binds transferrin receptor one, and both are fused to an IgG1 Fc. Transferrin receptor one internalizes constitutively and fast. Tether a GPCR to it and the receptor gets dragged into the endolysosomal pathway and destroyed, whether or not it was ever activated.

Why that matters is that essentially every GPCR drug we have works by occupancy — you compete with the ligand, or you stabilize an inactive conformation. That model has delivered beautifully for the biogenic amine receptors and badly for nearly everything else. Peptide and protein receptors are the hard cases: endogenous ligands binding at picomolar affinity, produced locally in autocrine and paracrine loops so the local concentration is enormous, functional pockets buried, and some receptors signalling constitutively with no ligand to compete against at all. Occupancy is the wrong lever there.

The numbers make the argument. Across the angiotensin two type one receptor, the relaxin receptor RXFP1, the apelin receptor and adenosine A2A, GTACs depleted forty to ninety percent of surface receptor with picomolar to single-digit nanomolar potency, within an hour, sustained for at least twenty-four hours. The relaxin case is the one to sit with. Relaxin-2 binds its receptor at picomolar affinity inside a constitutively assembled signalosome, which is precisely why nobody has an antagonist in clinical development. The authors had a competitive nanobody. They affinity-matured it to a Ki of three hundred and forty picomolar, and it still barely inhibited signalling at micromolar concentrations. Build that same nanobody into a GTAC and you get near-complete antagonism with an IC-fifty around thirty picomolar — more than two hundred-fold better than the identical binder as a plain Fc fusion. The binder did not improve. The mechanism changed.

The rest of the panel extends the point. The CCR6 GTAC inhibited about ninety percent of primary Th17 chemotaxis where the parental antagonist antibody topped out near fifty-five. And the Epstein-Barr-encoded receptor BILF1, which signals constitutively, was depleted seventy to eighty percent with MHC class one restored — the conceptual proof, because that's exactly the case where a competitive antagonist has nothing to compete with. Mechanistically it's lysosomal and genuinely rewired rather than mimicking agonist traffic: proteasome inhibition does nothing, bafilomycin abolishes it, and receptors that don't internalize on agonist at all get internalized anyway.

The engineering result is the one I'd expect to travel furthest, and it's another instance of today's theme. They swapped the high-affinity transferrin binder for variants roughly a thousand-fold weaker — and the weaker effector arm made the degrader both better and safer, giving about eighty-five percent target depletion with no co-depletion of transferrin receptor itself. Detuning the effector makes co-engagement require the target to actually be there, converting a generic internalization shuttle into a cell-type-selective one. That logic is portable to any effector-target pair.

The caveats are real. Nearly all of this is in tagged, over-expressing lines co-transfected with transferrin receptor — standard practice, since endogenous GPCR expression is too low to assay, but it does mean the potencies come from a system engineered to make the mechanism work. And the transferrin receptor dependence is both asset and constraint. For a CNS audience there's a further question the paper doesn't touch: transferrin receptor is also the main transcytosis route people exploit to cross the blood–brain barrier. Whether that collides with a central GTAC or enables one is wide open.

Hold that thought — potency was not the limiting variable — because the next paper makes the same discovery in a field that's been stuck for thirty years. A group at SickKids in Toronto asked why immunotoxins keep failing in solid tumours. Immunotoxins couple a tumour-selective binder to a bacterial toxin payload; they work in haematologic malignancies and are defeated by dose-limiting toxicity nearly everywhere else. The standing assumption is that the fix lives on the targeting side: better affinity, better avidity, better discrimination between receptor-high and receptor-low cells.

They tested that assumption across CD123-, HER3- and HER2-targeted constructs, and it doesn't hold. Increasing binding affinity or avidity changed potency but gave remarkably little control over selectivity. What worked was the move nobody makes: deliberately crippling the payload. Attenuating the catalytic activity of the diphtheria toxin domain produced large selectivity gains, and in vivo the attenuated HER2 construct had a maximum tolerated dose roughly four hundred-fold higher than wild type, with robust tumour growth inhibition at one-sixteenth of that dose — while the wild-type construct showed no detectable efficacy at any tolerated dose.

The mechanism is a threshold argument, and it's clean. Killing a cell requires enough catalytically active toxin in the cytosol to cross a lethal threshold. If the payload is maximally potent, that threshold sits so low that even the trickle taken up by a receptor-low bystander clears it — so the differences in receptor engagement, which genuinely exist, never translate into differences in survival. Raise the threshold by weakening the enzyme and the delivery difference you already had suddenly becomes the difference between life and death for a cell. They show this directly: HER2 binding differences that produced no cytotoxicity difference with wild-type payload produced pronounced ones after attenuation.

The part to hold onto is that there's no universal answer. The optimal degree of attenuation varied by receptor and by toxin scaffold — the mutation ideal for a HER2-targeted diphtheria construct was suboptimal in the cholera toxin scaffold. So this isn't "weaker is better," it's "payload activity is a design parameter that has to be matched to the delivery differential, and the field has been pinning it at maximum by default." Put beside the GTAC paper, you get one lesson from two directions: when a modality stalls, check whether the axis everyone optimizes is the axis that governs the outcome.

The third paper takes that idea somewhere more unsettling, because the axis it dislodges is the dopamine receptor itself. Phasic reward prediction signals in VTA dopamine neurons and in basal forebrain bursting neurons look almost identical, and nobody has known whether that reflects independent computation, shared input, or one driving the other. This group recorded both simultaneously in freely moving rats and found tight trial-by-trial coupling in amplitude and spike timing, with dopamine activity consistently leading the basal forebrain by about ten milliseconds — during trained behaviour, during learning, and even outside task events. Optogenetic activation of VTA dopamine somata in DAT-Cre mice recruited basal forebrain bursting neurons at matching latencies, terminal activation was faster still, and inhibiting either suppressed both cue-evoked and ongoing activity.

Then the pharmacology. Ten milliseconds is far too fast for D1 or D2 signalling, which runs through G-protein second-messenger cascades. So they blocked both, with SCH23390 and raclopride, at doses already shown to alter striatal firing — and confirmed the drugs were working, since basal forebrain field potentials and locomotion both changed. The fast recruitment survived anyway: sixty-nine of seventy-seven responsive neurons kept responding across the full hour, essentially matching the sixty-five of seventy in vehicle controls. Anatomical tracing found glutamatergic dopamine-to-basal-forebrain synapses, pointing at glutamate co-release as the substrate.

I'd frame the significance carefully. This is not "dopamine receptors don't matter." It's that the fastest arm of dopaminergic reward signalling to this target is carried by co-released glutamate and is invisible to the antagonists we use to define dopamine's role. Every lesion-by-antagonist experiment in this literature has been silently blind to it, and a D2 drug prescribed for motivation or psychosis leaves this pathway running.

Staying with transmitter mechanics, a nice piece of slice pharmacology on the chloride channel ClC-2 — hyperpolarization-activated, long proposed to support chloride extrusion, but its acute contribution to the GABA reversal potential has stayed murky because the tools were poor. This group used AK-42, a genuinely selective ClC-2 inhibitor, in organotypic hippocampal slice cultures, with targeted optogenetic activation of single interneurons and gramicidin-perforated patch, which preserves the chloride gradient you're trying to measure. Blocking ClC-2 depolarized the reversal potential and raised intracellular chloride.

The detail that makes it more than confirmation is a timing dependence, and it's the part I'd actually carry away. Stimulate the interneuron two seconds after voltage-step onset and the drug shifts the reversal potential by twelve millivolts. Stimulate at one hundred milliseconds and you get five millivolts, which doesn't reach significance. The reason is that under control conditions the reversal potential is already about five millivolts more hyperpolarized at the two-second delay than at the short one — on the same neuron, in the same solution — because slow voltage- and time-dependent ClC-2 gating has had time to engage and move chloride during the step. The drug erases that difference.

Which means the GABA reversal potential is not a fixed property of a cell; it's partly a property of your voltage protocol. The authors say so outright, and it's a retrospective caveat on a large perforated-patch literature — any comparison where a manipulation could touch ClC-2, chloride, or membrane conductance needs stimulation timing matched across groups. The electrophysiology rests on five paired recordings, so treat the magnitudes as provisional; the independent chloride imaging in seventy-three neurons, showing a three-and-a-half millimolar rise under TTX, is the sturdier half.

To dopamine and motivation now, with a study that does something I wish more neurochemistry did: it leaves the lab. Twenty-one participants performed a cognitive effort discounting task during simultaneous PET-fMRI, with a selective non-displaceable D2 ligand giving receptor availability alongside task-evoked activity. They also did an N-back, and then reported the mental demand of their actual daily activities through a seven-day ecological momentary assessment protocol.

The headline is that caudate D2 availability predicted how much cognitively effortful activity people engaged in across an ordinary week — which is a real test of an assumption that effort-discounting research usually just asserts. But the internal structure of the result is more interesting than the headline, and it points somewhere other than where you'd expect. Dopamine availability had no relationship at all to the discount parameter itself — no association with how steeply people devalued an option as it got harder. What it tracked instead was capacity: higher D2 availability predicted a shallower decline in N-back accuracy as load increased, with no effect on reaction time. It also moderated how strongly choices tracked subjective value, reward, and load — low-availability individuals abandoned the hard option steeply as load rose, high-availability individuals chose it more often and stably.

So the parsimonious reading isn't that dopamine sets the price of effort — it's that dopamine sets how much capability you have, and the apparent preference follows. The authors can't test that mediation and say so: twenty-one people is underpowered for it. Two more honest notes. The choice effect loses significance once age is covaried, and the whole pattern is caudate-specific — ventral striatum was null on every measure including ventral striatal BOLD itself, which sits awkwardly with the standard ventral-striatum-as-motivation story.

The second Nature paper closes a hole sitting underneath the entire prion-like hypothesis of neurodegeneration. We've believed for years that tauopathies spread by templated seeding, and that distinct tau folds, resolved disease by disease with cryo-EM, are the physical basis of distinct strains. But the central claim of a strain — that it keeps its conformation when transmitted — had never actually been shown for tau.

The experiment is direct. Inject tau filaments extracted from the frontal cortex of people who died with Alzheimer's disease or with corticobasal degeneration into the striatum of wild-type mice, wait nine to twelve months, then solve the structure of what grew. Two things fall out. First, the pathology is cell-type specific in the disease-appropriate way: Alzheimer's seeds produced tau pathology confined to neurons, while corticobasal seeds produced inclusions in both neurons and glia, including the coiled bodies and astrocytic plaques that define the human disease. Second, and this is the result, the filaments that formed are made of endogenous mouse tau — human-specific antibody staining negative, mouse-specific positive — and their structures match the seeds. The corticobasal singlets came in at three-point-four angstroms and superimpose on human type one corticobasal filaments with a root-mean-square deviation of about half an angstrom; the Alzheimer's-seeded structure matches human paired helical filaments at around nine-tenths of an angstrom.

It works because of a sequence coincidence worth knowing: mouse and human tau are identical within the ordered filament core, so there's no species barrier for these folds. That also predicts where the method breaks. Adult mice express only four-repeat tau, so Pick's disease, a three-repeat tauopathy, can't be transmitted this way — but chronic traumatic encephalopathy and progressive supranuclear palsy should be. They also confirm the inoculum is gone: injected human filaments are undetectable after a week, so this is templating, not persistence of what was injected.

There's a verdict buried in here about model choice. It is the plain wild-type mouse, with its own unmanipulated tau, that reproduces the human fold faithfully — while the humanized transgenics the field has leaned on for twenty years produce structures corresponding to no human disease, and the analogous alpha-synuclein experiment gave inclusions that looked right while the structures only partly matched the seed. If you want to study a specific tauopathy's conformation, the transgenic is the wrong animal. The limitation the authors stress is the clinical one: these mice accumulate genuine disease-structured filaments and stay healthy. Whether propagation and toxicity are separable phases, as argued for prions, is unknown, and serial passage hasn't been done. So this nails the templating half of the prion hypothesis and leaves the neurodegeneration half exactly where it was.

Two neuromodulation papers now, and both are dose papers in the sense I flagged at the top. The first should change how transcranial ultrasound trials are dosed. A Stanford-led group ran twenty-four healthy adults through a double-blind, counterbalanced, within-subject design combining MR acoustic radiation force imaging — which measures the actual micron-scale tissue displacement the ultrasound produces in the brain, in vivo — with concurrent ultrasound-fMRI. Targeting the lateral geniculate nucleus increased visually evoked BOLD in the LGN and in ipsilateral primary visual cortex, and the cortical effect localized to the retinotopic projection zone of the stimulated site, which is about as specific a positive control as this field has produced.

The dosing finding is the one that matters. Measured displacement predicted effect magnitude both across participants and voxelwise within them. Simulated in-situ intensity — the acoustic model essentially every ultrasound study uses to set and report dose — predicted neither the response nor the displacement itself, explaining about two percent of each. It did get the focus location right to within about two millimetres. So the simulations are good for aiming and useless for dosing, which makes the increasingly common practice of titrating output on simulated in-situ exposure unsupported. Given how much heterogeneity sits in this literature, including outright sign flips between studies, that's a strong candidate explanation.

The companion result comes from a whole-brain modelling study of tDCS in drug-resistant epilepsy, in twelve patients undergoing presurgical intracranial recording. The pipeline builds a personalized head model, couples it to a neural-mass model on each patient's own diffusion connectome, and optimizes the electrode montage to suppress simulated seizure spread rather than to maximize field at a target. Be careful about what's shown, because the authors are: their montages suppress spread completely in all twelve by construction, so that number is tautological and they say so. The informative result is the divergence between pipelines.

And the divergence is striking. Against conventional field-targeting montages, the fields at the clinical epileptogenic zone are statistically indistinguishable — and at the propagation zone the conventional montages actually deliver stronger inhibition. Yet in the network model those montages fully suppress spread in only four of twelve patients, and in one case increase it. The cleanest contrast holds the target map fixed and varies only the network term: the montages differ by a few thousandths of a volt per metre, an order of magnitude below the median parcel field, and still produce markedly worse spread reduction in five patients and increased spread in two. And when they ask which parcels account for the discrepancy, five of seven needed five or fewer corrected — roughly as often because the montage incidentally excited a propagation-zone parcel that should have been left alone as because it under-inhibited one. The clinical arm is six retrospective patients and doesn't reach significance; the authors decline to call it predictive accuracy, and the pipeline needs intracranial recordings, so it's confined to presurgical patients. But as an argument that field dose is the wrong currency for network-level stimulation, it's well built.

Two computational items to close. The first reformulates mechanism-of-action prediction from Cell Painting profiles. The standard approach is representation matching: embed the morphological profile, find nearest reference compounds, inherit their labels. That fails in predictable ways, because the neighbours are contaminated by batch effects, non-specific cytotoxicity, phenotypic convergence between unrelated mechanisms, and source-to-source variability. This group treats retrieved neighbours instead as uncertain evidence that has to be weighed and sometimes rejected, with a multi-agent system that keeps a candidate-centric evidence memory, calibrates evidence weights offline against reference-set reliability, and arbitrates between a phenotype agent and a mechanism agent.

On JUMP Cell Painting benchmarks it reaches about sixty-three percent top-one accuracy against nine to fourteen for nearest-neighbour baselines on the same features. Ignore that comparison. The honest baseline is an LLM given only the compound's chemical structure and no image data whatsoever, and that scores forty-four — within twenty points of the full pipeline, and triple the retrieval methods the field actually benchmarks against. So the high-content assay is contributing real but incremental signal on top of what a model already infers from the molecule.

What rescues the claim is a control I wish more papers ran: shuffle the evidence streams. Shuffling structure costs about seventeen points; shuffling phenotype costs twenty-six and lands at the structure-only floor. The morphology is doing compound-specific work, not decorating memorized chemistry. And the setting where the architecture genuinely matters is the open-world one, where half the test compounds act through mechanisms absent from the reference set: seventy-nine percent there, and retrieval methods can't play at all, because they're obliged to assign every query an existing label. That's the real contribution — in first-in-class discovery the useful capability isn't ranking known mechanisms slightly better, it's being able to say none of them are supported, and retrieval is structurally incapable of saying that.

Finally, a human-subjects paper that psychiatrists should read, with a caveat about its own numbers. A large collaboration had one hundred and fifty-eight young adults aged eighteen to twenty-five export their complete ChatGPT histories — nineteen thousand nine hundred and thirty conversations — then surveyed them, selected five conversations reflecting acute distress, and had ten clinicians review and rewrite the model's responses.

Be clear about what that large number is doing: it's a denominator, not a sample. Filtering out homework, which is the dominant use, left seventy-seven conversations containing high distress — about four-tenths of one percent — and every qualitative claim in the paper rests on five of them. The survey half is where the population-level finding lives, and it's a dissociation worth sitting with. Participants above the distress threshold reported greater emotional engagement with the system and greater behavioural change from using it, both moderate effects. But they reported no more concern about dependency than anyone else; if anything slightly less. The group most likely to act on the model's advice is the group least likely to flag itself as over-reliant.

What the clinicians flagged is a process critique rather than a content critique, and that's the contribution. None of the ten thought the system should have refused to answer, and nine endorsed specific wording. What they identified were seven process failures — claiming to know things it couldn't know, jumping to solutions over exploration, burying the person in generic advice. There's a telling asymmetry in the rewrites: ChatGPT's responses got longer as distress rose, while the clinicians' got shorter. Their sequence was ask about safety first, bring the intensity down, then explore without agreeing. Treat the design guidance as hypothesis, not evidence. But the framing travels — the failure isn't that these systems say harmful things, it's that they say defensible things in the wrong order and at the wrong volume, and no content filter catches that.

That's the episode. The pattern worth carrying out of today: a potency you can't convert into selectivity, a simulated intensity that doesn't predict a response, a field dose that doesn't predict a network outcome, and a receptor blockade that doesn't block the signal. Four different fields, same structural mistake. Back tomorrow.
