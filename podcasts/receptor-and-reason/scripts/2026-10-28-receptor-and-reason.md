# Receptor & Reason — 2026-10-28

## Script

Good morning. Today's thread is the oldest mistake in applied statistics, and it keeps arriving dressed as a discovery: the difference between a difference and a difference in significance. Over and over in this week's crop, the sentence a paper is actually for is a comparison — this works here but not there, this measure is restored and that one isn't, this pathway reroutes — and what was run is two separate tests that landed on opposite sides of a threshold, on two non-identical sets of cells or animals, with no interaction term anywhere. Almost none of today's papers are bad. Several are excellent, and two are among the best things I've read this month. But the contrast on the slide is, in most cases, a contrast nobody tested.

We open with the best preclinical muscarinic paper I've seen since Cobenfy's approval, and its headline is a real dissociation — a stronger one than the abstract admits. A group at the University of South Carolina put dLight photometry into dorsal striatum of awake, behaving mice and asked what the M4-selective positive allosteric modulator VU0467154 — VU'154, the field's standard tool compound — is actually doing to dopamine while it produces its antipsychotic-like effect. The working story has been that M4 activation dampens striatal dopamine, and that this is why M4 PAMs blunt amphetamine-induced hyperlocomotion. Under basal conditions that story holds up partway: three milligrams per kilogram reduces the amplitude of spontaneous dopamine release events dose-dependently, with no change in event frequency, and no change in tonic levels when you raise the tonic signal with the D1 positive allosteric modulator DETQ.

Then they give amphetamine, and the story inverts. VU'154 attenuates the hyperlocomotion, as advertised — and dopamine after amphetamine is significantly higher, not lower, in the treated animals. Their own figure title says "potentiating." The abstract says "little to no effect." The data are more interesting than the summary of them. And the second half is better still: in mice with M4 deleted specifically from dorsal striatum, the behavioral effect is gone, while the reduction in spontaneous dopamine amplitude survives intact. Required for the behavior, not required for the dopamine. That is a clean argument for a postsynaptic mechanism on D1-expressing neurons rather than presynaptic control of release, and it matters, because dopamine normalization is the story that gets drawn on slides explaining why muscarinic agents work.

My one complaint is the thread of this episode. There is no genotype-by-treatment interaction tested anywhere in the paper — the only interaction term that appears is sex-by-treatment. The knockout conclusion is a significant t-test in controls and a non-significant one in knockouts, at five to seven animals per group. The finding is probably right; the effect sizes look convincing and it fits the circuit literature. But the test that would establish it is a two-way model with an interaction term, and running it would cost them nothing.

Staying with dopamine and with contrasts that rest on a threshold. A group at Arizona has built a bimanual string-pulling task for the unilateral 6-hydroxydopamine rat, and the title is "levodopa rescues reach height but not trajectory area." The task is a genuinely good instrument — rats pull a baited string hand over hand, markerless tracking gives you each paw's full trajectory, the unlesioned paw is a within-animal control, and a load cell gives you grip tension. The lesion effects are large and sensible: slower string speed, shorter bouts, slower paws with the reaching phase hit hardest, smaller trajectories — but no change in the variability around the trajectory and no change in tension. The movement keeps its shape and loses its size and speed, which is a concrete statement of what dopamine depletion does to a skilled limb movement.

Then levodopa, six milligrams per kilogram with benserazide, and the dissociation in the title is two p-values. Reach height is called fully rescued because, as a percentage of pre-lesion, it isn't significantly different from a hundred — p equals point zero-nine, at six rats. Trajectory area is called not rescued because the same test gives point zero-one. Reach height's median is eighty-nine percent of baseline. That's the restored one. No interaction, no equivalence test, no direct comparison of the two recoveries. And the six animals matter: twelve rats were lesioned and three were excluded for dyskinesia, which means the drug-response estimate is conditioned on the animals with the smallest levodopa response. Adopt the task. Read the headline as "speed recovered more than geometry did," not as two mechanisms.

Now the paper I'd actually put time into this week, and it's a pharmacology paper disguised as a cell-biology paper. A Nature Neuroscience group compared chronic progressive demyelination — a Plp1 transgenic model of Pelizaeus-Merzbacher disease — against the acute cuprizone and lysolecithin models, and found that microglia in chronic disease behave in a qualitatively different way. In cuprizone, microglia fill with lipid droplets. In chronic demyelination they have almost none, and instead accumulate electron-dense lysosomal material and cholesterol crystals. The decisive experiment is simple and beautiful: injected labelled cholesterol goes to lipid droplets in seventy-eight percent of wild-type microglia and to endolysosomes in ninety-eight percent of the chronic-model microglia. Microglia-specific knockouts of NPC1 and SOAT1 both phenocopy it, as do the corresponding inhibitors in culture. In human MS chronic active lesions, about three-quarters of CD68-positive phagocytes carry the same CCL4 marker, against thirteen percent in control white matter.

The therapeutic arms are where it earns attention, and they cut against the current clinical direction. DMHCA — a dual DHCR24 inhibitor and liver-X-receptor agonist — given from two to five months improved rotarod, reduced myelin loss and cut the inflammatory microglial signature, and reduced lesion burden in EAE. Meanwhile tolebrutinib and baricitinib significantly worsened rotarod performance, and interferon-beta increased abnormal myelin swellings. T-cell depletion and microglial depletion both made things worse. If that replicates, it is a serious caution about extrapolating relapsing-remitting pharmacology into progressive disease.

Two caveats, and the first is the episode's thread again. The argument that the mechanism is DHCR24 rather than LXR rests entirely on a pure LXR agonist, GW3965, showing no effect — and there is no brain target-engagement data for it at all. The only evidence shown for that compound is in cultured astrocytes, which is the wrong cell type for a microglial claim. A null arm without engagement is a dosing result, not a mechanism result. Same issue, worse, for tolebrutinib, where the stated dose is three orders of magnitude below the baricitinib dose in the same figure, with no occupancy measurement. And buried in the supplement: maraviroc, the CCR5 antagonist that blocks the chemokine axis the whole paper is named around, did nothing. CCL4 is a marker here, not a validated driver, and the paper's framing doesn't quite say so.

From cells to receptors, and to a missing comparison of a subtler kind. A nice piece of biophysics puts the unliganded beta-two adrenergic receptor under hydrostatic pressure, up to four kilobar, and reads out nine intramolecular distance pairs by double electron-electron resonance. Pressure drives the apo receptor along the same conformational coordinate that agonist does: the transmembrane four-to-six distance opens by ten angstroms, landing within an angstrom of the agonist-bound value, and four-to-seven and four-to-five converge on their agonist positions too. A two-state fit gives a free-energy gap of about two point one kilocalories per mole, predicting just under three percent active receptor at atmospheric pressure against three point seven percent measured. That agreement is satisfying, and the volume change of minus thirty-three millilitres per mole constrains mechanism.

The claim I'd push back on is the distinctive one: that the agonist-bound receptor samples a smaller conformational space than the pressure-revealed unliganded active state. No width statistic for either ensemble appears in the Results — and more to the point, it can't, because the analysis is a two-component matrix factorization in which the second component is defined as the state matching the agonist-bound distances. A two-basis decomposition forces the agonist condition to be a mixture of those same two shapes. The model used to measure the populations forbids the very heterogeneity difference the paper reports. Separately, three thermodynamic parameters are fit to five pressure points with the atmospheric point excluded; include it and the volume change moves from minus thirty-three to minus twenty-five.

Staying with GPCR signaling, and with a paper whose pharmacology is exemplary and whose inference is the episode's thread in pure form. A group in Vienna asked what PACAP does across cell types in temporal association cortex, using cell-type-targeted cyclic-AMP and calcium sensors in acute slices — including a new astrocyte-specific cyclic-AMP sensor. Every compound is dosed, there's a dose-response for the peptide itself, and the blocker panel is comprehensive. The findings are plausible: in receptor-expressing excitatory neurons PACAP raises cyclic AMP without raising calcium; in somatostatin interneurons it depolarizes through a MEK-dependent persistent sodium current; in astrocytes it raises calcium through Gq, phospholipase C and store release.

But "distinct signaling programs across cell types" needs those programs compared, and they never are. There's no interaction test anywhere, and the two arms of each dissociation aren't even tested the same way: the neuronal cyclic-AMP positive is drug against vehicle, while the calcium negative is an unpaired test of drug against its own baseline, six slices, no vehicle group. The interneuron depolarization is never reported in millivolts — it has a p-value and no effect size. And the astrocyte pathway map is an underpowered null map: in the cyclic-AMP panel every single blocker roughly doubled the median response and every one was called negative, while in the calcium panel a blocker that cut the response by fifty-eight percent is drawn as a negative arm on the schematic. Two things the framing leaves out: chemogenetic silencing of these neurons before retrieval did nothing to fear memory, and PACAP changed no synaptic measure at all — no evoked currents, no paired-pulse ratio, no spontaneous event rates — while forskolin did. The main positive finding is a cyclic-AMP signal that is, on their own data, synaptically silent.

Now to human single neurons, and the cleanest instance of today's problem in a paper I otherwise love. A multi-center group recorded nearly two thousand units in epilepsy patients during encoding of novel images, with a surprise recognition test later, and asked which neurons predict whether an image will be remembered. The answer is the amygdala, and specifically its category-selective cells: firing during encoding is higher for later-remembered items only when the image is from the cell's preferred category. The effect starts around three hundred and fifty milliseconds, essentially coincident with the sensory response. It grades with confidence, it tracks the recollection parameter rather than familiarity, and it replicates in a second cohort of ninety-five patients. Spike-field coherence between amygdala and hippocampus is higher on remembered trials. This is a lot of converging evidence.

But the title word is "stimulus-specific," and that word requires a selectivity-by-memory interaction. They report one — for the hippocampus. For the amygdala, specificity rests on a highly significant test in a hundred and ninety-six preferred-category neurons against a null result in two hundred and eight different neurons, with no interaction term in the Results at all. And nonpreferred trials sit near baseline by construction, so the asymmetry is confounded with dynamic range, with no firing-rate-matched control. Two other things worth knowing: the hippocampal effect does not replicate in the second cohort, and the emotion control uses normative ratings from twenty-four online raters rather than the patients' own.

The same structure, now in a study I think is genuinely important. A Technion and Mount Sinai collaboration had eighty-two inpatients with opioid use disorder, most on methadone, and thirty-four controls watch the first seventeen minutes of Trainspotting in the scanner and then freely recall it out loud. The behavioral result is large and clean: patients gave sixty-three percent of their recall to drug scenes against fifty percent for controls, Hedges' g of point six-eight, with controls exactly at parity. Correlating each person's speech bias against their brain response to those same scenes is an elegant way to ask whether encoding predicts what the mouth does afterward.

The abstract says the path from brain to words is rerouted — frontoparietal in controls, temporal in patients. Those are two separately thresholded within-group maps, three regions against fourteen. The direct between-group contrast returns exactly one cortical region, and none of the three patient temporal regions differs significantly from controls. And the effect they call the largest anywhere in the analysis, in nucleus accumbens, is a significant difference between two correlations that are each individually non-significant — the text says so plainly. Add a four-year education gap and a twelve-point reading-ability gap in a study whose dependent variable is how someone narrates a film, an imaging subsample that dropped fifty-seven percent of patients and twelve percent of controls, and craving analyses that are null throughout. The behavioral finding is real. The circuit story is a hypothesis consistent with the data, not one they establish.

Which brings us to a paper that does the comparison properly, and gets a quieter answer for it. A large consortium effort ran Mendelian randomization from ADHD liability to sixteen health and behavioral outcomes twice — once against population-based outcome GWAS, once against within-sibship GWAS from eighteen cohorts. Population-based, you reproduce the literature: more cigarettes, more alcohol, more depressive symptoms, lower educational attainment, lower cognitive performance, earlier first child. Within families, nearly all of it collapses toward zero. That is the right design, and the conclusion — that familial factors inflate these estimates — is probably correct.

Two honest caveats the title doesn't carry. The two between-design differences the abstract leads on are nominal p-values of point zero-four-eight and point zero-three-four across sixteen outcomes, uncorrected, while every other inference in the paper is false-discovery-rate corrected over those same sixteen — and the next three p-values are point zero-five-one, point zero-five-four and point zero-six-four, which is a continuum, not two hits. More importantly, the sibling confidence intervals are two and a half to four times wider, so for most outcomes "attenuated to the null" also means "could not have detected the population estimate." The paper concedes this in the text and then asserts inflation in the title. Also note that educational attainment survives within families, and that the effects being overturned were between point zero-two and point zero-nine standard deviations to begin with.

Briefly, a cautionary one for anyone doing molecular-imaging-to-cognition work. A study of forty-five adults correlates GABA and Glx from magnetic resonance spectroscopy in anterior temporal lobe against semantic association performance, and against receptor-subunit gene expression — and the correlations are respectable, with mediation models reporting up to ninety percent mediated. The problem is that there is no participant-level genomics in the study at all. The expression values come from sampling a fixed brain atlas through each person's spectroscopy voxel mask, so the entire between-subject variance in the molecular variable is where the voxel was placed. The tell is in the gene list: a retina-specific glutamate receptor and a cochlear hair-cell nicotinic subunit both carry significant associations with the metabolite and with semantic accuracy. Adrenergic and serotonergic genes track behavior as strongly as the excitation-inhibition genes the paper is about, which argues the whole pattern is one spatial gradient.

Two from the applied side, and both turn on baselines. A Bern group tested whether an open-weights twenty-billion-parameter model could pull comorbidities and their dates out of free-text sleep-clinic notes, with the structured entry date hidden so the model had to find dates in prose. The genuinely good result is that it beats two physicians on earliest-date agreement, eighty-seven percent against seventy-five at day resolution. But the task is thirty-two near-empty binary slots per record, so always answering "absent" scores ninety-two percent accuracy at patient level and ninety-eight at entry level — and the reported accuracies are ninety-eight and ninety-nine. The F1 of point eight-eight is a micro-average carried by sleep apnoea, with per-category values down to point one-one for hypertension, two true positives against thirty-two false. No macro-average anywhere. And one real bug: in both main tables, the reported Cohen's kappa is numerically identical to the reported accuracy, row for row, which is not what kappa does with sparse classes.

Then a benchmark from MIT's Lab for Computational Physiology — confusingly for this episode it's called M-four-Bench, no relation to the muscarinic receptor — asking whether giving a coding agent a clinician-written procedural specification helps it derive clinical variables from electronic health records. Twenty-eight derivation tasks, SOFA and sepsis-3 and KDIGO and the rest, scored against hidden references. Skills raise the mean reward from point seven-zero to point eight-four, improving twenty-six of twenty-eight tasks. The reason to read it is the control they ran on themselves: handing the agent the raw reference SQL scores the same mean reward as the skill, point eight-four against point eight-three — and yields forty-nine strict passes out of a hundred against seven. A sevenfold difference in actually getting the task right is invisible to the headline metric. In the main result, the reward gain buys about four percentage points of completed tasks, and row-level key F1 barely moves. It's an unusually self-critical paper; it also concedes the reference standard is community code rather than adjudicated clinical truth.

And to close, the counterexample — a paper that ran the null model and reported it. Genedex is a curated index of fifteen hundred Alzheimer's gene signatures from thirty-four studies, annotated by pathological process, cell type, brain region and direction of change. Its central claim is that apparent contradictions in the literature — ten and a half thousand genes reported up in one study and down in another — are mostly unexplained context rather than genuine conflict. Adding facet labels in sequence drops the unexplained fraction from seventy-three percent to thirty-four to thirteen. Any labelling scheme would do some of that, simply by partitioning lists more finely. So they permuted the labels, and the permuted sequence runs eighty-four, fifty-four, thirty. The real signal is the gap, not the drop — thirteen against thirty — and because they showed both numbers, you can see exactly how much is biology and how much is granularity.

That's the habit I'd take from today. When a paper's claim is a contrast, find the test of the contrast. Not the test on the left and the test on the right — the one that compares them. If it isn't there, the finding may still be true, but what you've been shown is two results, not a difference. Thanks for listening, and I'll see you tomorrow.
