# Receptor & Reason — 2026-08-08

## Script

Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Saturday, August eighth. Today's lineup runs from a psychedelic through the receptors and circuits that shape mood, motivation, and social pain, into a metabolic angle on Parkinson's, and then out through two computational drug-discovery tools before a light close on what psychedelics actually feel like. And one bit of follow-through from earlier in the week: the psilocybin synaptic-plasticity map I kept putting on the shelf has finally rendered, so it opens the show. Let's get into it.

We start with what is, to my knowledge, the largest single dataset on where in the brain psilocybin remodels excitatory synapses. A single one-milligram-per-kilogram dose in mice, then whole-cell patch-clamp recordings twenty-four hours later — well after the acute head-twitch has subsided — across a whole panel of cortical and limbic regions. The core readout is the miniature excitatory postsynaptic current, the tiny quantal events that report on excitatory synaptic input. Psilocybin raised the frequency of those events but left their amplitude alone, and — this is the important part — it did not do so uniformly. The effect was concentrated in the ventrolateral orbital area, prelimbic cortex, anterior insula, and basolateral amygdala, and it skipped neighboring subregions that a coarse label like "prefrontal cortex" would have lumped in. Frequency-without-amplitude points to more synapses or more release rather than stronger individual synapses, consistent with the new-spine story other groups have told structurally. Two things make this more than a catalog. First, they matched each recorded cell to a spatial-transcriptomics atlas and showed the size of the plasticity tracked local expression of the serotonin-2A, 2C, and 1A receptor genes. Second, they did the causal test: a CRISPR knockout of the 2A receptor confined to the insular-orbital patch abolished the frequency increase locally. So this is a spatially resolved map that ties a psychedelic's lasting synaptic effect to where its main receptor actually lives — and the regions that light up are exactly the value-updating and emotional-learning circuits you'd want engaged if the therapeutic story is real. It's a twenty-four-hour snapshot of a moving process, and they can't yet say whether it's new synapses or unsilenced ones, but as a foundation for mechanism-based psychedelic pharmacology it's a genuinely useful piece of work.

Staying with depression but moving to a fresh drug target, there's a nice paper nominating an orphan G-protein-coupled receptor, GPR151, for inflammation-associated depression — the treatment-resistant subtype where the immune system is driving the mood symptoms. The appeal is anatomical specificity: GPR151 is almost exclusively enriched in the habenula, the little hub for negative valence and aversion, and that topography is conserved between mouse and human. When they challenged mice with LPS — a bacterial endotoxin that provokes systemic inflammation — knocking out GPR151 blunted the passive-coping, giving-up behavior even though the animals lost just as much weight and mounted the same peripheral immune response. Then the elegant part: re-expressing the receptor only in the adult habenula restored that inflammatory behavioral vulnerability in males. The point is that most antidepressants act everywhere and pay for it in side effects, whereas a habenula-restricted, inflammation-linked receptor is the kind of regionally precise handle the field keeps asking for. Worth flagging that the re-expression rescue worked in males but not females, so there's a sex-specific wrinkle to sort out before anyone gets too excited — but as a target-validation story it's clean.

From the habenula's role in aversion to a different flavor of aversive state — the pain of social separation. A team imaged titi monkeys, one of the few monogamous primates that form real pair bonds, using PET to track the kappa opioid receptor before and during long-term separation from their partner. The kappa system, driven by dynorphin, is the brain's dysphoria and aversion signal, and it's a hot antidepressant target precisely because blocking it may lift anhedonia. During separation, kappa receptor availability in the nucleus accumbens dropped — which means either more endogenous dynorphin was occupying the receptor or the receptor was downregulating — and it did so in both sexes, alongside a clear rise in cortisol. The oxytocin story, by contrast, split by sex: plasma oxytocin fell in separated males but not females. The honest reading, which the authors give, is that the accumbens kappa change and the peripheral oxytocin change look like partially independent processes — a shared aversive-motivation signal through kappa, and a sex-specific oxytocin response running in parallel. Small sample, sixteen animals, and PET displacement can't cleanly separate "more ligand" from "fewer receptors," but it's rare mechanistic data on the neurochemistry of losing an attachment, in a species whose bonds actually resemble ours.

The opioid theme carries into a human imaging study, this time on the mu-opioid receptor and how we regulate our own emotions. The endogenous opioid system is a stress buffer, but its role in the cognitive control of emotion has been fuzzy. They combined PET with the mu-opioid tracer carfentanil to measure baseline receptor availability, then put the same people in an fMRI reappraisal task. Higher mu-opioid receptor availability predicted stronger blood-flow responses across frontal, temporal, and parietal regions — but the effect was driven mostly by heightened responses when people were simply viewing emotional images, with some of those same regions quieting down during active reappraisal. So the picture is that a higher endogenous opioid tone makes you more neurally reactive to emotional material in the first place, and that reactivity is still something deliberate reappraisal can rein in. It's a correlational, cross-sectional design, so no causal claims — but it's a concrete link between a druggable receptor system and the individual differences in emotion regulation that sit underneath a lot of psychopathology.

Now a dopamine paper that gives a computational spine to something clinically familiar — pessimism. Working in mice with a Pavlovian task built around genuinely ambiguous cues, and recording dopamine release into the nucleus accumbens, the authors separated two kinds of pessimism. State pessimism — a bad-feeling moment on a given trial — and trait-like pessimism, a stable individual skew toward expecting the worse. Both showed the same thing at the cue: reduced dopamine when the animal was reading an ambiguous signal pessimistically. But they diverged completely at the outcome. Transient state pessimism came with a compensatory, self-correcting shift — the animal became relatively more sensitive to reward and less rattled by omission, which is adaptive, it pulls you back toward baseline. Trait-like pessimism did the opposite: an outsized dopamine response to reward omission that would keep dragging expectations down. And they modeled it carefully, showing the trait pattern is better explained by asymmetrically weighted prediction-error signals — omission errors counting for more than reward errors — than by a difference in learning rates. That distinction matters for drug discovery, because a therapy that nudges a transient mood dip is aiming at a fundamentally different, self-limiting mechanism than one trying to correct a persistently miscalibrated outcome signal. It's a clean example of why "pessimism" isn't one thing at the level of the circuit.

From dopamine as a signal to the dopamine neurons themselves, and a metabolic take on Parkinson's from the Ryan lab, who've spent years on how synapses pay their energy bills. Their prior work pinned the rate-limiting step of synaptic glycolysis on the enzyme PGK1. Here they make the case that guanosine nucleotide metabolism — the GTP-and-GDP side of the ledger, not the more familiar ATP — has an outsized effect on synaptic transmission, and that a family of enzymes called nucleoside diphosphate kinases are the connectors tying these nucleotide pools to the machinery that powers neurotransmitter release. The Parkinson's hook is that this pathway runs through GCH1, already a known Parkinson's gene and the gateway to tetrahydrobiopterin synthesis, and they close the loop with human genetics from a large Parkinson's cohort. The translational tease is that guanosine supplementation rescued the synaptic dysfunction caused by Parkinson's-linked mutations in their cellular assays, and that inhibiting the pathway pharmacologically — with ribavirin, an antiviral that happens to block one of these nucleotide enzymes — perturbed transmission in a predictable way. It's mechanistic and preclinical, but it reframes part of Parkinson's as a synaptic-bioenergetics failure with a metabolite you could imagine supplementing, which is a refreshingly different lever from the usual dopamine-replacement or aggregation-clearance strategies.

Two quicker items on how the brain handles chemical insults. First, alcohol and the immune system. This group had already shown that knocking down an inflammatory transcription factor, interferon regulatory factor 7, in the anterior insula curbs escalating alcohol self-administration in rats. Here they close the loop on the cell type: chronic intermittent alcohol drove pro-inflammatory microglial activation, self-administration climbed in lockstep with that insular inflammatory factor, and minocycline — a tetracycline antibiotic that's a standard tool for damping microglia — blunted both the factor and the escalation during early abstinence. Neuroimmune signaling as a driver of the drinking itself, not just a consequence, and a repurposable drug already in hand to test the idea.

The second is a genuinely surprising bit of synaptic-vesicle biology. The textbook says amino-acid transmitters like GABA live in clear vesicles and monoamines like histamine live in dense-core vesicles. Using an electron-microscopy method that lets them actually see small amines inside vesicles, this group reports that in ordinary GABAergic vesicles histamine and GABA are co-packaged with a spatial structure — histamine condensed into a dense core, GABA arranged around the rim — and they find this same core-rim layout across central, autonomic, and endocrine systems. If it holds up, it's a structural basis for one vesicle releasing two transmitters on different timescales, and it quietly dissolves the clear-versus-dense-core distinction a lot of us learned as a rule. Early, and it'll need functional confirmation that the two pools release differently, but it's the kind of result that rearranges a textbook diagram.

Then two computational tools for drug discovery, which is where the extra weight for this audience lives. The first, from Recursion, is called Nesso-1 — a coarse-grained cofolding model for binding-affinity prediction that runs in about a second per prediction on a single GPU, more than an order of magnitude faster than the leading open-source baseline, Boltz-2, while matching or beating its accuracy, including on out-of-distribution assays far from the training data. Speed at that accuracy is what actually changes practice — it widens the slice of chemical space you can screen — and they've open-sourced the code and weights, which matters more than any single benchmark number. The second, called ClinOracle, goes after the harder translational gap: not "does this compound bind the target," but "will it actually work in patient-derived human cells." It's a hierarchical graph neural network that predicts functional activity conditional on target engagement, folds in developability from ADME and drug-likeness into a single priority score, and — this is the part that counts — they ran it prospectively across five targets spanning oncology, autoimmune, and neuroinflammatory disease, and the top-ranked compounds outperformed the lower-ranked ones through real experimental validation, out to in vivo efficacy. Prospective, forward-looking validation is rarer than it should be in this literature, and it's the right bar to hold these tools to.

To close, something lighter that loops us back to the opening. A group mined a couple thousand naturalistic first-person reports from a mobile app — experiences of psychedelics, cannabis, meditation, and dreams — and asked, with language and topic modeling, whether the old etymology of "psychedelic," meaning mind-manifesting, holds any water. Their read: psychedelic reports were semantically most like dream reports, fitting the hallucinatory character, but they uniquely also aligned with meditation on themes like personal growth. Cannabis showed the mirror image — it read like meditation on the surface but didn't carry the growth-and-transformation content. It's phenomenology from self-selected app users, so take the framing as suggestive rather than settled, but as a data-driven gesture at why psychedelics and contemplative practice keep getting mentioned in the same breath, and cannabis doesn't quite, it's a fitting place to end a day that started with what those same compounds are doing to synapses.
