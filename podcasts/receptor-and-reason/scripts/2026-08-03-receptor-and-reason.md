# Receptor & Reason — 2026-08-03

## Script

Welcome to Receptor & Reason for Monday, August third. Today opens with structural pharmacology — a paper that quietly rewrites how a couple of the field's most-used dopamine tool compounds actually behave — then moves through cortical remodeling in Parkinson's, the maturation of hippocampal plasticity, and an anti-inflammatory angle on panic, before a run of agentic-AI systems built for neuroscience and drug discovery, and a light closer on the resting brain. Nine items. Let's get into it.

We lead with a dopamine D1 receptor paper that should make a lot of us re-read our own methods sections. The D1 receptor signals across a continuum of activation states, and the question the group set out to answer is how a ligand encodes a particular signaling efficacy. Along the way they reassessed two compounds that essentially every D1 pharmacology experiment leans on — LE300 and SCH-23390, both catalogued for decades as neutral antagonists that just block the pocket without any intrinsic activity. In their cyclic-AMP assays, neither is neutral. LE300 actually suppresses the receptor's basal, constitutive activity — it's an inverse agonist. And SCH-23390 has modest but reproducible agonist activity of its own, about a quarter of dopamine's maximum — it's a weak partial agonist. Several antipsychotics long filed as D1 antagonists, including chlorpromazine and haloperidol, land as inverse agonists too. That reclassification alone matters, because if your "silent" blocker is quietly nudging the receptor one way or the other, a lot of published interpretations shift. But the payoff is structural: using those two ligands, they captured cryo-EM structures of previously missing states — an inactive receptor stabilized by LE300 and an intermediate active state induced by SCH-23390 — that bridge the fully active conformations we already knew. Together with molecular dynamics and mutagenesis, that lets them trace how ligand efficacy is encoded as progressive engagement of a conserved tryptophan toggle switch deep in the receptor core. And to prove they understood the mechanism, a single chemical modification to SCH-23390 converted it into a high-efficacy partial agonist. This is a genuine framework for efficacy-tuned design at D1, which is exactly what the clinic wants right now — think tavapadon, the D1-selective partial agonist in late-stage Parkinson's trials, where the whole bet is that submaximal, fine-tuned activation beats full agonism.

Staying with dopamine and Parkinson's, but moving downstream to the cortex. The reflex is to treat the primary motor cortex as a passive relay that just reports basal-ganglia dysfunction into motor symptoms. This group argues the motor cortex itself remodels maladaptively as parkinsonism progresses. They used the MitoPark mouse, a genetic model where midbrain dopamine neurons die off gradually over months rather than all at once, so you can watch the time course. As striatal dopamine falls, layer-five pyramidal-tract neurons in the motor cortex lose inhibition mediated by the alpha-5 subtype of the GABA-A receptor — normally supplied asymmetrically by somatostatin interneurons — and that disinhibition unleashes excessive NMDA-receptor activation at their dendrites, alongside a loss of dendritic spines from altered thalamic input. The therapeutically pointed part: L-DOPA given early prevented these cortical adaptations, and given late it rescued them. So the cortex is an active, dopamine-sensitive participant in the disease, and the alpha-5 GABA-A receptor — which already has subtype-selective ligands in development — becomes a candidate handle on the cortical side of the pathology, not just the striatal side.

That NMDA-and-AMPA theme carries into a developmental plasticity paper. The group tracked the basal phosphorylation state of glutamate-receptor subunits in rat hippocampus from weaning at three weeks to adulthood at twelve weeks, and paired it with theta-burst LTP in the CA1 region. The counterintuitive finding is about AMPA receptors: total GluA1 and GluA2 go up, but basal phosphorylation of GluA1 at serine 831 and serine 845 progressively falls over adolescence. Their read is that dephosphorylating those sites at baseline keeps them available for activity-dependent phosphorylation later — a primed-but-unloaded configuration — and right in step with that, theta-burst LTP gets progressively stronger into adulthood. NMDA-subunit and CaMKII phosphorylation move the other way, increasing. It's a clean statement that the adolescent maturation of LTP is set largely by the resting phosphorylation state of AMPA receptors, which is the kind of developmental window that matters for how susceptibility to epileptogenesis or drug abuse gets wired in.

Now a neuroimmune-pharmacology piece on panic. There's a running hypothesis that inflammation helps trigger panic attacks, with some patients showing altered circulating cytokines. Working in rats, this group used hypoxia — seven percent oxygen — as a panicogenic stimulus, which reliably drove panic-like jumping without changing general locomotion. That jumping came with time-dependent microglial remodeling in the periaqueductal grey, the classic panic-associated midbrain structure, shifting the cells toward an immunoresponsive morphology. Then the test: five days of minocycline — a brain-penetrant tetracycline antibiotic that also dampens microglia — reduced the panic jumping about as well as an acute dose of alprazolam, the benzodiazepine standard, and it prevented the hypoxia-induced microglial changes in the dorsomedial column. A non-benzodiazepine, already-approved drug with a panicolytic effect through microglial modulation is a genuinely appealing repurposing angle. Caveats are the usual preclinical ones — male rats only, an acute behavioral readout — but the microglia-to-panic link is worth taking seriously.

Pivoting to the computational side, and a system aimed squarely at the neuroscientist's daily grind. SeekBrain is an autonomous multi-agent framework for accelerating neuroscience discovery, and the design idea worth noting is how it's grounded. Rather than reasoning freely from scratch, it mines a repertoire of reusable analysis recipes from code-and-paper pairs — real published analyses with their code — and then couples that codified expertise to separate planning and execution engines, with a researcher-in-the-loop able to feed corrections back into the recipe library. On an expert-annotated benchmark called BrainArena, spanning tasks across species and data modalities, it beat general agent frameworks — Claude Code and Codex — by roughly twelve and eighteen percent. And in two live deployments it did real work: pulling low-dimensional neural manifolds of behavior out of a freely-moving zebrafish dataset, and, in more autonomous mode, formulating and testing a region-level decoding hypothesis in a mouse decision-making dataset. It's still a benchmark plus two case studies, and the mouse result is framed as a rediscovery rather than a novel finding, but grounding the agent in actual analysis code instead of open-ended reasoning is the right instinct for this messy, multimodal domain.

A close cousin, with a different emphasis, is CyberNeuro — an agentic workbench for cohort-scale neuroimaging and clinical data, built around privacy. The pitch is that getting large neuroimaging datasets AI-ready is a manual slog — metadata curation, pipeline execution, quality control — that shuts out labs without heavy infrastructure. CyberNeuro runs four dedicated agents — a planner, a validator, a dispatcher, and a reporter — communicating over a secure MCP bridge, and crucially it drives a tailored local model they call WandaMind so that clinical data never has to leave for a cloud provider. On the NeuroBench suite it lifted held-out domain accuracy from forty to sixty-nine percent over its baseline, and it kept a human-in-the-loop verification panel for biomedical quality control. The number I'd flag is the token economy: running locally used a small fraction of the tokens a cloud configuration burned on the same ten-batch cohort workflow. For anyone sitting on protected clinical imaging, a competent local-model agent that keeps data in-house is arguably more useful than a marginally smarter cloud one.

From analysis to design — a generative-chemistry paper that revives phenotypic drug discovery. Most molecule generators are target-based: they need a well-defined target or reference compounds, which strands you for diseases with no clean target. MGMG — morphology-guided molecule generation — instead conditions on cellular morphological profiles from compound treatments, the kind of high-content imaging readout where a drug's phenotypic fingerprint stands in for its bioactivity, combined with plain-text molecular descriptions. It generates candidate molecules with the desired phenotypic effect and no target information at all, and it holds up even when the text is thin or the morphology signal is weak. It also extends to genetic perturbations — designing activators from a gene-overexpression morphology. Their validation is in-silico docking, where the generated molecules matched reference binding affinities while introducing structural diversity, so this is a computational proof of concept rather than a wet-lab hit, but as a way to put phenotypic screening logic inside a generative model, it's a nice direction.

Our most technical footnote is a computational-pharmacology method on ADMET prediction, and I'd file it under interesting-but-modest. The authors propose a quantum-inspired preprocessing step: they encode the statistical dependencies among molecular descriptors into a parameterized many-body Hamiltonian, use mutual information to pick descriptors and set the coupling strengths, then simulate its time evolution and hand the resulting expectation values to a gradient-boosted CatBoost model as extra features. On an aqueous-solubility benchmark from the Therapeutics Data Commons, they reach a mean absolute error around three-quarters of a log unit — which is within the error bars of the best existing models. Two honest caveats: this is quantum-inspired and classically simulated, not run on quantum hardware, and the improvement is marginal. It's a feature-engineering idea worth watching, not a step change, but the notion of encoding descriptor correlations as an entanglement structure is a genuinely different way to build features.

And to close, something lighter and methods-forward. A resting-state fMRI study compared people scoring high versus low on a smartphone-addiction scale, and the point of interest is the analysis, not the diagnosis. Instead of static functional connectivity, they used two dynamic approaches — one that tracks transitions between discrete attractor states, and one that captures recurring quasi-periodic patterns across cortical networks. The high-scoring group showed reduced dorsal-attention-network participation in those recurring patterns, and context-dependent differences in how the default-mode and attention networks related, most pronounced during transitions between internally and externally focused states. It's correlational, the groups are defined by a questionnaire, and "addiction" is doing a lot of work in the framing — but it's a clean illustration that time-varying connectivity catches attentional dynamics that a static average washes out.
