# Receptor & Reason — 2026-08-09

## Script

Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Sunday, August ninth. Today's lineup opens with a dopamine paper that quietly reframes how the neurotransmitter actually shapes striatal circuits during behavior, moves through two glutamate-receptor stories in Alzheimer's and Rett syndrome, then into a run of molecular and computational pharmacology — GPCR structural dynamics, two structure-based design tools, and a sobering look at where variant-effect predictors fail in pharmacogenomics — before closing on two lighter behavioral items about alcohol and food reward. Let's get into it.

We start with a paper that goes after a question most of us think is settled: how does dopamine physically reshape the striatum during learning? The textbook answer, built largely from brain-slice work, is that dopamine does two things at once — it tunes the intrinsic excitability of spiny projection neurons, and it gates corticostriatal synaptic plasticity. This group asked whether that acute-excitability half actually happens in an intact, behaving brain, and to do it they pulled off something genuinely hard: whole-cell membrane-potential recordings from over three hundred identified striatal neurons in awake mice, while independently controlling cortical input with one opsin and dopamine release with another, and reading dopamine out with a genetically encoded sensor. Here's the surprise. When they drove phasic dopamine over seconds to minutes — the timescale of real reward transients — they saw only modest effects on synaptic transmission and, critically, no detectable change in membrane potential dynamics or intrinsic excitability. The acute-tuning story, the thing you can reliably evoke in a slice, essentially didn't show up in vivo. What did show up was learning. An associative task strengthened corticostriatal synapses onto both D1- and D2-type projection neurons — but only the D1 potentiation required dopamine. Block the dopamine receptors and D1 strengthening vanished, while D2 strengthening carried on regardless, presumably leaning on adenosine A2A signaling instead. Their reconciliation with the slice literature is elegant: in the intact brain, ongoing tonic dopamine may already hold D1 neurons at an excitability set-point, so an extra phasic bump does little, whereas a slice, cut off from dopamine for an hour, is primed to respond when you wash it back on. If that's right, it shifts dopamine's principal physiological job away from moment-to-moment excitability control and squarely onto gating which synapses get strengthened when something is learned — and that reframes how you'd think about every dopaminergic drug meant to act on motivation or movement in real time.

From dopamine's circuit actions to a different receptor system read out in human tissue — the NMDA receptor across the Alzheimer's continuum. We've long known NMDA receptor loss tracks with disease severity, which is part of why memantine exists, but this study asked a sharper question: does sex shape that trajectory, and how does regional receptor density map onto cognition? They ran quantitative autoradiography across hippocampus, entorhinal cortex, and parietal cortex in postmortem tissue spanning controls, mild cognitive impairment, and Alzheimer's, using both an NMDA-receptor ligand and a tau ligand on the same samples. In both sexes the steepest loss was in the CA1 field of the hippocampus, the usual suspect. But there was a real diagnosis-by-sex interaction, and it was driven by the transitional MCI stage: women at MCI already showed lower NMDA-receptor density than same-sex controls, while men at MCI did not. Across individuals, more receptor meant a better cognitive score, and in the entorhinal cortex receptor density fell as tau rose — but that receptor-versus-tau relationship reached significance only in men. The honest framing is that this is correlational autoradiography, not a mechanism, but it lands on something clinically live: women may lose glutamatergic signaling capacity earlier in the disease course, which is exactly the kind of sex difference that could be washing out the average effect in trials of glutamatergic drugs that don't stratify by sex.

Staying with glutamate receptors but moving to a developmental disorder and an actual drug, there's a nice Rett syndrome paper on positive allosteric modulators of the AMPA receptor. The tool here is CX1632, a clinically advanced AMPA-receptor potentiator — an ampakine, the class that boosts AMPA currents without directly opening the channel. AMPAkines have looked promising in Rett models before, but nobody had nailed down what determines whether they work, and the answer this paper gives is: timing. They dosed Mecp2-null males and Mecp2-heterozygous females at different developmental stages. A brief course of the drug given neonatally produced long-lasting gains — better survival, slower disease progression, improved motor function and cognition — whereas the same drug given later did much less for symptomatic null males, though it still helped the less severely affected females. And the benefit outlasted the drug: early treatment switched on sustained neuronal and synaptic gene programs and restored AMPA-receptor-mediated transmission weeks after the compound was withdrawn. The message is that responsiveness to AMPA potentiation is gated by disease stage, so the therapeutic window may be a genuine developmental window — which is a hard but important thing for a field that mostly enrolls already-symptomatic patients. It's preclinical, and the sex-and-severity interactions are a reminder that a single dosing schedule won't fit everyone, but as a piece of developmentally informed pharmacology it's clean.

Now from receptors counted in fixed tissue to a receptor watched in motion. A structural-pharmacology group used hydrogen-deuterium exchange mass spectrometry — a way of mapping which parts of a protein become more or less flexible when a ligand binds — on the GLP-1 receptor, but with a twist: they studied it already pre-coupled to its Gs protein, closer to the signaling-ready state than an isolated receptor. GLP-1R is of course the incretin target behind the whole obesity-drug wave, and the race now is for oral, non-peptide agonists. They compared two of those non-peptide agonists — danuglipron, Pfizer's oral small molecule, and an experimental compound — against the natural GLP-1 hormone. The non-peptide drugs produced overlapping flexibility fingerprints but with drug-specific effects inside the transmembrane bundle, while the natural hormone stabilized the receptor backbone more weakly, and its inactive breakdown metabolite actually destabilized a local region in the opposite direction. The detail that matters most: both peptides uniquely moved a specific flexible loop of the G protein called switch three, a known relay for downstream signaling. The takeaway for drug discovery is that agonist efficacy at this receptor is written partly in dynamics, not just in a static binding pose — so if you're designing the next oral GLP-1 drug, matching the flexibility profile of a good agonist may be as informative as matching the crystal structure.

That structure-versus-dynamics theme carries into two computational drug-discovery tools. The first is called CryoLigATE, and it targets an annoying, practical failure of structure-based design: in cryo-EM maps, the protein is often beautifully resolved while the bound ligand — the part you actually care about for medicinal chemistry — sits in mushy, poorly defined density. That happens partly because the deep-learning tools that clean up cryo-EM maps were trained mostly on protein, so they don't know what small molecules should look like. This group trained a hybrid convolutional-transformer network on over six thousand protein-ligand complexes spanning drug-like molecules, lipids, steroids, and sugars, specifically to enhance the density around binding pockets. On an independent test set of roughly six hundred and fifty complexes it substantially improved ligand resolvability, worst-resolved sites most of all, while leaving the good experimental density intact — and it does it in seconds on a desktop GPU with no manual map preparation. It won't rescue a bad map, but for the increasingly common case where cryo-EM is the workhorse for a drug program, sharpening the ligand you're trying to optimize is a genuinely useful, low-friction addition.

The second design tool is more ambitious and, to me, the more exciting of the pair — de novo design of proteins that change shape when a small molecule binds. Most designed binders are static clamps; this work builds a moving part. The strategy is to take a preorganized small-molecule binding protein and add a mobile lid domain that swings shut behind the ligand once it's in the pocket. As a demonstration they converted a protein that binds exatecan — the topoisomerase-one-inhibitor warhead used in several antibody-drug conjugates — into a drug-triggered conformational switch. Adding the lid didn't just add motion; it drove affinity into the sub-nanomolar range, gave a hundred-fold selectivity over a close chemical cousin, and produced ligand residence times measured in months, all with tunable kinetics. And because the shape change is real, they turned one design into a genetically encoded fluorescent biosensor for the drug — the kind of thing you'd want for monitoring drug levels in a clinical or research setting. This is the part of protein design that's moving fastest: not just recognizing a molecule, but building programmable mechanical responses to it, which opens a door to designed sensors, switches, and allosteric tools that we mostly used to borrow from nature.

From building predictors to watching them break — a pharmacogenomics reality check that this audience should sit with. Cytochrome P450 enzymes metabolize roughly three-quarters of clinically used drugs, and genetic variation in them is one of the biggest sources of person-to-person differences in drug response — think warfarin dosing through CYP2C9. The problem is that we know the functional effect of only a handful of "star" alleles; the vast majority of observed variants are uncharacterized, and everyone's hoping computational variant-effect predictors can fill the gap. This paper shows why the popular ones can't, at least not as-is. Tools like AlphaMissense and the protein language model ESM-2 rest on evolutionary conservation — and pharmacogenes systematically violate that assumption, because being a fast or slow metabolizer isn't the kind of thing strong purifying selection cares about. Concretely, AlphaMissense dumps CYP variants into its "ambiguous" bin at nearly twice its usual rate, and within that bin its scores are essentially uncorrelated with real deep-mutational-scanning activity for CYP2C9. The authors could patch the accuracy — a nearest-neighbor model over ESM-2 embeddings, ensembled in, improved that correlation roughly tenfold — but here's the sting: even with much better agreement to the biochemical activity data, the predictions still didn't line up with clinical annotations. Their diagnosis is conceptual, not just technical: for a multi-substrate enzyme, a variant's effect can depend on which drug you're asking about, so "function" may need to be redefined as substrate-conditioned. In other words, the field may be asking these models a question that doesn't have a single answer. That's a genuinely useful corrective for anyone tempted to bolt a general variant-effect predictor onto a pharmacogenomics pipeline.

Two lighter behavioral items to close, both about how the striatum handles what we consume. First, alcohol and the circadian clock. Circadian disruption is tightly linked to alcohol use disorder, and the clock gene Bmal1 in the striatum has been fingered before, but nobody had asked which striatal subregion matters. This group deleted Bmal1 selectively from the spiny projection neurons of either the dorsomedial or the dorsolateral striatum. The dorsolateral deletion did nothing to drinking. The dorsomedial deletion, though, cut alcohol consumption and preference — but only in females, with no effect in males, and no change in anxiety, depressive-like behavior, motor coordination, or sucrose preference, so it wasn't a general reward or malaise effect. The kicker: ovariectomy abolished the effect entirely, meaning the dorsomedial clock's grip on drinking runs through ovarian hormones. It's a tidy demonstration that "the circadian clock affects drinking" resolves, on closer inspection, into a specific subregion, a specific cell type, and a specific hormonal dependence — the kind of granularity you need before circadian biology becomes an actual therapeutic lever.

And to finish, a dopamine paper that loops us back to where we started, this time in the nucleus accumbens. Animals defend their protein intake fiercely, and this group asked whether protein hunger reshapes reward signaling. They put a genetically encoded dopamine sensor in the accumbens core and had mice work for either grain or sucrose pellets, across a normal diet, a protein-restricted phase, and a return to normal. Behaviorally, protein restriction made the mice work harder overall, especially for the grain pellets. But the dopamine signal was more selective than that: when a pellet was delivered, the accumbens dopamine response was larger for grain than for sucrose specifically during protein restriction — and that pellet-type difference simply wasn't there on the normal diet, or under a more effortful progressive-ratio schedule. So protein hunger doesn't just crank up reward signaling across the board; it selectively biases what the dopamine system treats as valuable toward the nutrient the body currently needs. It's a small, clean reminder that the same accumbens dopamine transient we spent the whole episode talking about is being tuned, moment to moment, by internal state — including something as basic as whether you've had enough protein this week.
