## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. I'm your host, and this is your daily pass through CNS pharmacology, psychiatric and neurology drug discovery, and the computational machinery closing in on both. Eight items today, and the thread running through them is the distance between the mechanism you can name and the mechanism that is actually doing the work. Today a drug engages its receptor and nothing happens. A drug engages its receptor and the opposite of the intended thing happens. A language model explains in careful detail why its designed protein will bind zinc, and then the zinc binds somewhere else entirely.

We open with the largest negative result in psychiatric drug development this week, and probably this year. Boehringer Ingelheim has published two Phase two trials of satoprodil, an oral negative allosteric modulator selective for GluN2B-containing NMDA receptors, in major depressive disorder. One trial was adjunctive, in patients already on an SSRI or SNRI with documented inadequate response, run across fifty-three centers in seven countries. The other was monotherapy, thirty-six centers in the United States and Japan. Both randomized two to one to one to two, placebo against five, ten and twenty milligrams once daily, both six weeks, both with change in the Montgomery-Asberg scale at week six as the primary endpoint. Four hundred sixty-eight patients randomized in total, three hundred thirteen of them on drug. That makes this, by a wide margin, the largest cohort ever exposed to a GluN2B negative allosteric modulator in depression.

The curves are flat. In the adjunctive trial, placebo fell twelve point zero points on the Montgomery-Asberg scale, and the three satoprodil doses fell seven point eight, eleven point nine and twelve point three. In monotherapy, placebo fell ten point three and the doses fell thirteen point six, ten point six and ten point zero. There is no monotonic structure there at all, and the formal dose-response analysis found no evidence of a non-flat shape in either trial. Response and remission rates track placebo. And critically, there was no separation at day eight either, so no acute, ketamine-like signal hiding under a null week-six result.

Now, the interesting part is not the failure. It is what makes the failure hard to interpret, and the authors are unusually candid about it. Satoprodil is extremely well tolerated. Adverse events ran forty-two percent against forty-four percent on placebo in the adjunctive trial, forty-six against forty-seven in monotherapy. There were no dissociative symptoms on the Bowdle scale, no abuse-potential signal, no withdrawal signal, none of the cardiovascular and neurological trouble that ended traxoprodil and onfasprodil — the earlier GluN2B compounds that did show antidepressant signals. And there is no receptor occupancy data. None. So the tolerability that reads as a triumph in the safety section is exactly what makes the efficacy section uninterpretable: you cannot distinguish "GluN2B blockade does not treat depression" from "we never occupied enough GluN2B to find out." The field has been here before with lanicemine, where a small trial with an eight-point placebo response was positive and a larger trial with a thirteen-point placebo response was negative, and this study's placebo arms sit right at that larger, unhelpful number. My read is that this is a genuine negative rather than a failed trial — the subgroup analyses are uniformly flat, and both trials agree — but the load-bearing missing experiment is a positron emission tomography occupancy study, and until somebody runs one, the NMDA hypothesis survives on a technicality. Neurocrine has a GluN2B compound in Phase two right now, so we will find out.

From a receptor that a drug could not move to an auxiliary subunit that turns out to be doing far more than anyone assumed. A preprint from Eric Schnell's lab at Oregon Health and Science University looks at alpha-two-delta-two, one of four isoforms of the alpha-two-delta auxiliary subunit of voltage-gated calcium channels, and the reason to care is that this subunit family is the binding site for gabapentin and pregabalin. Alpha-two-delta-two is peculiar in that it is selectively expressed in parvalbumin-positive interneurons — the group confirmed this by in situ hybridization in dentate gyrus, where alpha-two-delta-two message is in parvalbumin cells and nowhere else, and the other two brain isoforms are in every other cell type and not in parvalbumin cells. Knock it out and mice have spontaneous seizures. Nobody had asked what that does to hippocampal inhibition.

Quite a lot, as it turns out. Optogenetically evoked inhibitory currents from parvalbumin interneurons onto granule cells were seventy-two percent smaller in knockouts. Some of that is anatomical — parvalbumin-positive inhibitory terminals were down thirty-two percent by immunostaining — but a thirty percent loss of terminals does not produce a seventy percent loss of current, so most of it is functional. Paired-pulse ratio went up, meaning release probability went down, and the readily releasable vesicle pool shrank by forty-nine percent. Then the experiment that makes the paper: parvalbumin terminals in wild-type hippocampus are famously insensitive to EGTA, the slow calcium chelator, because their calcium channels sit in tight nanodomains right on top of the release machinery. In alpha-two-delta-two knockouts, EGTA now works. The coupling has loosened.

That is the part with pharmacological consequences. Alpha-two-delta is an entirely extracellular protein, tethered by a lipid anchor, and yet what it is doing here is positioning channels relative to intracellular release machinery — not counting them. Raising external calcium made the paired-pulse ratio converge between genotypes but never made the currents converge, which is the signature of a positioning defect rather than a channel-number defect. So when you give somebody a gabapentinoid, the thing you are binding is a spatial organizer of inhibitory release at parvalbumin synapses. It is a genuinely uncomfortable thought for a drug class whose main clinical use is suppressing excitability, and it is worth remembering that the null mouse here is epileptic. This is a germline knockout, not a pharmacological block, and gabapentinoids do not remove the protein — but it reframes what the target is.

Now to a drug that did exactly the opposite of what it was given for. David Holtzman's group at Washington University asked whether fingolimod, the sphingosine-one-phosphate receptor functional antagonist that was the first oral therapy for relapsing-remitting multiple sclerosis, could keep T cells out of the brain in tauopathy. The premise is solid: T cells accumulate in the brain in Alzheimer's disease and primary tauopathies, they track tau pathology in space and time, and depleting them is neuroprotective in mice. Fingolimod's whole mechanism is trapping lymphocytes in lymph nodes. So they gave one milligram per kilogram per day in drinking water to P301S tau mice carrying human APOE4, starting at six months, just before tangles arrive, and looked at nine and a half months.

The peripheral pharmacology worked perfectly. Profound lymphopenia, naive CD4 and CD8 cells stripped out, exactly as advertised. And the brains got worse. T cells in cortex and hippocampus went up, not down, in both CD4 and CD8 compartments. Microgliosis worse — Iba1, CD68 and MHC class two all up, homeostatic Tmem119 down. Astrogliosis worse. AT8-positive tau pathology worse in hippocampus and entorhinal cortex, and the degree of tau pathology correlated with the hippocampal T cell count.

The explanation is the useful part, and it is a lesson about which cells a drug can actually reach. Fingolimod preferentially sequesters naive and central memory T cells, because those are the ones that need sphingosine-one-phosphate receptor signalling to leave a lymph node. The T cells that accumulate in the tauopathy brain are CD8 effector memory cells, which have downregulated that receptor and are spared. So the drug does not just fail to remove them — by clearing everything else it enriches the circulating pool for exactly the population you did not want, and indeed the residual lymphocytes in blood were dominated by activated CD44-positive cells. This is the same reason fingolimod works in relapsing-remitting disease and does nothing for progressive multiple sclerosis, where the parenchymal T cells have gone tissue-resident. Two honest caveats: neurodegeneration and behaviour did not get worse, only the pathology and inflammation did, and the authors note their neurodegeneration measures were highly variable in this cohort. It is also possible fingolimod has direct neuronal protection offsetting the immune harm. But the headline stands, and it is a warning to anybody contemplating an immunomodulator trial in tauopathy on the strength of a T-cell-depletion result.

Staying with drugs behaving unexpectedly, a preprint from Prem Yadav's group at the Central Drug Research Institute in Lucknow and Charles Zorumski and Steven Mennerick's groups at Washington University makes a case for the histamine H3 receptor in the dorsal root ganglion. H3 is best known as a presynaptic autoreceptor in the central nervous system, constitutively active and Gi-coupled, and it is the target of pitolisant. What it is doing in sensory ganglia was essentially unstudied.

After chronic constriction injury of the sciatic nerve, membrane-associated H3 protein in the ganglion goes up. Re-analysis of published single-cell data puts H3 message almost entirely in neurons — not glia, not endothelium, not fibroblasts — and within neurons, in peptidergic nociceptors and C-fibre low-threshold mechanoreceptors, co-expressed with TrkA, the nerve growth factor receptor. Not in the non-peptidergic populations. That is a specific prediction: if H3 sits on the NGF-responsive nociceptors, blocking it should touch mechanical pain and leave heat alone.

It does. G-S-K three-three-four-four-two-nine, a selective H3 inverse agonist, at half a milligram per kilogram intraperitoneally, does nothing acutely — a single dose leaves mechanical thresholds where they were. Given twice daily from day five after injury, mechanical thresholds recover progressively from day six and peak at day twelve. Thermal pain on a fifty-degree hot plate is untouched, and spinal TRPV1, which goes up after injury, stays up. Then the mechanism, in dissociated ganglion neurons under current clamp: nerve growth factor raises evoked action potential frequency and drops rheobase, a single application of the H3 inverse agonist does essentially nothing, and two applications twenty-four hours apart normalize both. The dosing requirement in the dish mirrors the dosing requirement in the animal, which is a satisfying internal consistency and also a hint that this is a slow transcriptional or trafficking effect rather than acute channel modulation. The commercial reading is the interesting one. Anti-nerve-growth-factor antibodies were the great hope in this space and tanezumab died on rapidly progressive osteoarthritis. A small molecule that blunts NGF-driven sensitization in a specific nociceptor class, in a receptor family that already has an approved oral drug, is a different risk profile entirely. This is a rat model with one compound and no H3 knockout, so treat the receptor attribution as provisional.

A quick one on the endocannabinoid side, and I am going to be blunt about the evidence. A Korean group has run oral cocaine self-administration in freely moving common marmosets and tested whether two-arachidonoylglycerol, the more abundant of the two major endocannabinoids and a full agonist at CB1 and CB2, changes cocaine taking. Circulating two-arachidonoylglycerol is known to be low in abstinent people with cocaine use disorder, and it was low in these animals too. Systemic pretreatment at point zero one and point one milligrams per kilogram reduced active lever responding and restored plasma levels. So far so good, and there is no approved pharmacotherapy for cocaine use disorder, so any primate signal is worth noting.

Here is the tension. In synaptosomes from the same animals, two-arachidonoylglycerol enhanced the cocaine-induced dopamine elevation rather than suppressing it, and the behavioural pattern was a leftward shift in the timing of responses — animals front-loaded and then stopped. The authors read that as satiety: a stronger early dopamine response terminating the bout sooner. That is a coherent story, and it is also exactly the story you would tell if the compound were mildly reinforcing on its own. The GPR55 mechanism they propose rests on a fluorescent ligand signal and immunofluorescent colocalization with the dopamine transporter, with no receptor blockade and no genetic manipulation anywhere in the paper. And the behavioural cohorts are three and four animals. I would call this a well-executed model-building exercise with a hypothesis attached, not evidence that endocannabinoid supplementation treats stimulant use disorder.

Now a paper that actually delivers new targets rather than reinterpreting old ones. A large consortium has published whole-exome sequencing across three thousand nine hundred sixty-four individuals with obsessive-compulsive disorder, chronic tic disorders, or both — two thousand four hundred eighteen of them in parent-child trios — against seventeen hundred thirty-four control trios. Going in, the field had four high-confidence risk genes for these conditions in total. It now has thirty-six. That is a tenfold increase, and the effect sizes are the kind you almost never see in psychiatric genetics: an average odds ratio of about fifty-seven, carried by roughly three to eight percent of affected individuals.

Three things make this more than a gene list. First, the rare-variant and common-variant signals are converging — four of the high-confidence genes sit under loci from the obsessive-compulsive disorder genome-wide association study, which means the same biology is showing up from both ends of the allele frequency spectrum and a mechanism-driven treatment would not have to choose. Second, obsessive-compulsive disorder and tic disorders share substantial risk at the gene level but are not identical to autism or developmental delay: some of these genes have no association with either, despite hundreds of high-confidence genes already known for those conditions. Third, the expression analysis is where the treatment-relevant signal lives. Risk gene expression is elevated in cortex, striatum and thalamus both prenatally and postnatally, and distinctively in postnatal cerebellum, with enrichment in cortical excitatory projection neurons and striatal medium spiny neurons. That is the cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical loop that imaging and deep brain stimulation have been pointing at for twenty years, arriving this time from unbiased human genetics. The network analysis flags cell polarity, microtubule and cilia biology, which is convergent with prior tic-disorder work — though I would note that the convergence replicated in one protein interaction database and not in another, which the authors report rather than bury.

Two computational items to finish. The first is from Bill DeGrado's lab at UCSF, and it is the kind of experiment that only works because somebody was willing to look slightly foolish. They asked whether commodity language models — Claude, ChatGPT and Gemini, no structure prediction in the loop, no diffusion model, no energy function — could design ligand-binding proteins from natural language prompts alone. The metal-binding prompt was about eight hundred words, written in ten minutes: make a homodimeric four-helix bundle from helix-turn-helix monomers, roughly sixty residues each, bind two metal ions using histidine and explicitly not aspartate, glutamate or cysteine, give me ten sequences and explain your reasoning.

The models produced sequences with no BLAST or Protein Data Bank hits at all, along with genuinely correct chemistry in the rationale — zinc prefers tetrahedral, so build a four-histidine site; to push toward copper selectivity, position histidines for square planar and add second-shell residues that penalize tetrahedral geometry. Twelve designs went to the bench. Six expressed and purified, three were helical and dimeric by circular dichroism and size exclusion, and all three were selectively stabilized by zinc over copper, with one measured by calorimetry at a dissociation constant of thirty micromolar. The second campaign, for a binder to perfluorooctanoic acid, needed two rounds: the five-helix design had a cavity too tight and showed no fluorine-nineteen NMR broadening, and a six-helix redesign gave two binders.

Now the part everyone should sit with. The structure prediction confidence on these designs was terrible — average predicted TM score around point four nine and interface score around point two five, far below the point nine cutoffs any real design campaign would use. Only one of eighty designs was predicted to have the four-histidine site that was explicitly requested. The two successful zinc binders coordinate through one histidine and two aspartates, which is precisely what the prompt forbade. And the residues the model named as its key zinc-binding residues are too far apart in three dimensions to bind anything. So the design rationale is fluent, chemically literate, and not what happened. It is a real result — twenty-five percent hit rates from a ten-minute prompt are not nothing, and redesigning the same backbones with ProteinMPNN gave higher-confidence sequences in different sequence space, confirming the models are not sampling the most designable region. But the value here is accessibility, not accuracy, and the paper is refreshingly clear-eyed that a recent fully agentic design pipeline needed a fifteen-thousand-word prompt and a fifty-thousand-dollar compute budget.

And finally, a paper that does for single-cell foundation models what Kaplan and Hoffmann did for language models, and finds that the recipe does not transfer. A group trained transformer encoders on the CELLxGENE Census — ninety-six and a half million cells, sixty-one thousand genes — in two formulations: ranked gene identity, the Geneformer-style setup where you predict masked gene identifiers from expression rank, and binned gene expression, the scGPT-style setup where you predict expression bins. Pre-training loss does fall as a clean power law in model size and compute for the ranked formulation. It is much less regular for the binned one.

The hyperparameter results are where this earns its place. The optimal learning rate falls with parameter count as a power law, but with exponents between point two one and point five seven depending on formulation, against roughly point one four in the comparable language model work. And optimal learning rate increases with depth in the ranked formulation, which is the opposite direction from language models. Fitting learning rate jointly to size and depth gives a root-mean-square error of about point zero nine in log units for the ranked model, roughly plus or minus twenty percent, against point three zero for the binned model, which is a factor of two. That is a practical result: if you are about to spend real money training a virtual cell model, the transformer scaling folklore you absorbed from the language literature will point you at the wrong learning rate.

But the finding I would put in the abstract is buried in the downstream section. The two formulations reach comparable biological benchmark scores. Neither has an advantage. So the quantity that scales beautifully is pre-training loss, and the quantity you actually care about does not move between architectures that differ substantially in how well they scale. Every architecture here was trained once, with no seed replicates, so the uncertainty bands describe architecture sampling rather than run-to-run variance. Which is a fitting place to end, because it is the same shape as the satoprodil problem: the thing you can measure precisely is not always the thing that was supposed to be doing the work.
