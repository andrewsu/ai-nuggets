## Script

Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Monday, November second, twenty twenty-six.

Today's papers share a habit worth naming at the top. In almost every one, the comparison that would settle the headline is either missing, or it was run and quietly lost. Sometimes the authors tell you so, in a limitations paragraph or a sensitivity analysis, and those are the good ones. Sometimes the design makes the decisive contrast impossible before a single sample is collected. The point is not that these are bad papers — several are excellent. It's that the claim on the front and the measurement inside are answering slightly different questions, and the gap is where the interesting part lives.

Start with the best piece of drug discovery I've read this week, out of the Oxford Parkinson's Disease Centre with AstraZeneca's hit discovery group. They ran a genuine phenotypic screen in patient-derived dopamine neurons — not a reporter line, not a cancer cell proxy, the actual vulnerable cell type. The patient carries the G-B-A N-three-seventy-S mutation, the most common strong genetic risk factor for Parkinson's, and the phenotype they screened against is one this group established earlier: these neurons release less calcium when you hit them with ionomycin, which indexes reduced organellar calcium stores. They scaled their midbrain floor-plate differentiation up to flasks, miniaturised the calcium assay into three-hundred-eighty-four-well format, and screened four thousand one hundred and eight compounds from an AstraZeneca library, every one of them sub-hundred-nanomolar against its primary target, spanning more than eighteen hundred human proteins.

A hundred twenty-three compounds came out of the primary screen, a three percent hit rate; twenty-three survived concentration-response confirmation across multiple donor lines including healthy controls. Those mapped to seventeen annotated proteins, so they screened five hundred twenty-five analogues against that target list, and the most enriched target was fatty acid synthase. Then they did the thing that separates a real target deconvolution from a hit list: they screened a hundred ten FASN analogues and correlated each compound's effect on calcium against its biochemical potency on FASN, and got a Spearman correlation of zero point five two. They knocked FASN down with CRISPR interference in two separate neuronal systems and got the same increase. Untargeted lipidomics showed the lipidome remodelling the way FASN inhibition predicts. And FASN inhibition pulled several other Parkinson's-relevant phenotypes in the right direction: more active glucocerebrosidase, less extracellular alpha-synuclein, less ER stress.

Then the mechanism, and this is where it gets strange in a way I like. FASN inhibition works by activating SREBP1 — the lipid-synthesis master transcription factor — as a compensatory feedback response. Overexpressing either SREBP1 isoform on its own reproduces the calcium effect with no drug at all. So the therapeutic logic is: inhibit the enzyme, trip the feedback loop, and the feedback is the medicine.

Here is the comparison that decides how you should read this. They measured both genes in human midbrain from idiopathic Parkinson's patients and controls. S-R-E-B-F-one message was reduced in Parkinson's. FASN was unchanged. So the node that looks dysregulated in the human disease is SREBP1, and the node the screen landed on is FASN — which is unchanged. The screen found FASN because FASN is the one with a hundred ten potent analogues sitting in a pharma compound library and a decade of oncology and metabolic chemistry behind it. SREBP1 is a transcription factor with no chemical matter. That is not a criticism — it's an honest description of how phenotypic screening actually finds targets, and the authors are straightforward throughout. But the druggable node and the broken node are different nodes joined by a feedback loop, and the proposition is that you reach the second by perturbing the first. The in vivo data is genuinely preliminary: the tool compound gets into mouse brain above its potency there, lowers fatty acids in cortex, striatum and midbrain, and turns on the sterol-response program, but no disease endpoint was tested in the alpha-synuclein overexpressing mice and the authors say so plainly. And one thing to sit with: FASN protein goes up after FASN inhibition, as part of the same feedback. Chronic dosing would be fighting a target your own drug induces.

Staying in neurodegeneration and staying with a correlation doing more work than it can carry, a hippocampal culture study on activity-dependent tau phosphorylation. The setup is clean: drive neurons with bicuculline for twenty-four hours, or silence them with tetrodotoxin, and ask what endogenous tau does. Chronic hyperactivity increased dendritic tau phosphorylation at the S-two-oh-two T-two-oh-five site — the AT8 epitope, the one pathologists actually stain — and it did so only in excitatory CA1 and CA3 pyramidal neurons. Not in inhibitory neurons, not in dentate granule cells. It was site-specific too, hitting S-two-oh-two T-two-oh-five and not the nearby T-two-thirty-one. A targeted kinase screen pointed at protein kinase A, and two structurally distinct PKA inhibitors both blocked it, with PKA activity itself rising in dendrites under chronic drive. They then asked what the phosphorylation does, and mutating the three AT8-site residues to alanine prevented tau overexpression from pulling surface GluA2 down — a plausible link from tau phosphorylation to AMPA receptor homeostatic scaling. And they took it in vivo, with low-dose sub-seizure picrotoxin for two or fourteen days, and saw the same increase in CA3.

The cell-type and site specificity are the real contribution and I'd trust them. The causal chain is softer than the abstract implies. The claim that tau phosphorylation is downstream of activation rests on a within-neuron correlation between phospho-tau and c-Fos with an R-squared of zero point zero seven six — activity accounts for under eight percent of the variance. That's a real association in a large cell sample, but it is not a mechanism. The second PKA inhibitor experiment is weaker than the first: the effect being rescued sits at p equals zero point zero five two, and the inhibitor alone significantly lowers phospho-tau from baseline, so you are rescuing a non-significant increase with a compound that has its own main effect. The spine-specific GluA2 result only trended. And the in vivo work is three, five and seven mice per group. The arrow from activity to phosphorylation to receptor trafficking is drawn with a lighter pen than the abstract suggests.

Third, and here the decisive comparison is not weak but structurally absent. A group at Amsterdam UMC profiled the interactome of the whole TREM receptor family in human brain by expressing each receptor's biotinylated extracellular domain, pulling down from brain lysate and reading out by mass spectrometry. They recover the known ligands, which is the right positive control, and report new ones — TREM-L-one with a near-complete classical complement set plus the HDL apolipoproteins, and TREM-L-two with heparan-sulfate-modified proteins including glypicans, syndecans and neurexins.

Now the design. The abstract says human Alzheimer's and non-Alzheimer's brain lysate. There were four brains, two of each, and all four were pooled into a single lysate before any pulldown ran. So there is no Alzheimer's versus control comparison anywhere in the paper; it was made impossible at the bench, deliberately, to maximise the diversity of detectable interactors. Defensible for discovery — but every p-value is then computed with the between-subject variance term absent, and the disease framing in the title does work the data cannot support. Worse, apolipoproteins, complement and fibrinogens are precisely the high-abundance plasma proteins that stick to everything in affinity proteomics, and the only guard is a specificity score computed relative to the other TREM baits. No irrelevant-ectodomain control, no orthogonal binding measurement. For anyone tracking TREM-two agonist programs, this changes nothing — TREM-two was the weakest bait in the study.

From ligands to the clock. A group working on neuropathic pain asked whether you can treat pain by retuning the circadian system rather than the nociceptive one. They used PER-two luciferase reporter mice, so Period-two rhythmicity can be watched in real time in explanted tissue rather than reconstructed from animals killed at six timepoints — a genuinely better way to ask this question. Spared nerve injury, then two weeks of continuous osmotic-minipump delivery of nobiletin or tangeretin, citrus polymethoxyflavones with established clock-modulating activity, chosen deliberately with pumps so the dosing itself doesn't fragment sleep. Readouts were mechanical and dynamic hypersensitivity, wireless EEG and EMG for sleep architecture, and five days of bioluminescence from the dorsal root ganglia.

Nerve injury produced hypersensitivity in both sexes, disturbed the sleep-wake cycle in males, and dramatically raised the amplitude of Period-two oscillation in the dorsal root ganglia — from about six point seven counts per second in sham animals to forty-five. Both flavonoids brought the amplitude down and attenuated dynamic hypersensitivity; tangeretin also improved NREM sleep and wakefulness.

Two things complicate the word "normalizes." The restoration is measured on amplitude, and on amplitude the flavonoids don't restore, they overshoot — tangeretin lands at two point nine against a sham value of six point seven, less than half of normal rather than back to it. Meanwhile nobiletin significantly lengthened the circadian period, to twenty-five hours against sham's twenty-three point seven. One clock parameter is pushed past normal, a second is pushed away from it, and the claim rests on the one nominated as the endpoint. And the bioluminescence groups are four animals each, which is why the injured amplitude carries a standard error of twelve on a mean of forty-five, and why the period null includes a group with a standard error of nearly seven hours — on a twenty-four-hour measurement that is not a null, it is an absence of information. The sleep pharmacology was run only in males. The real-time reporter model is the lasting contribution; treat the flavonoid efficacy as a first pass.

Now to depression, and to a paper whose own control outperformed the thing it was controlling for. A connectome-wide dysconnectivity score for major depression, built the way polygenic scores are built: compute an effect size for every one of six thousand four hundred forty-one edges in a hundred-fourteen-region functional connectome in a discovery sample, then collapse them into a single weighted sum and apply it, frozen, to a completely independent sample. Discovery was a Japanese cohort, two hundred twenty-nine patients and eight hundred twenty-four controls across thirteen sites. The test set was German, six hundred ninety-four patients and over nine hundred controls. That it transfers at all across that gap is the real result — the score separates patients from controls with a z score of five point zero nine and an area under the curve of zero point six nine.

But the title says it tracks clinical status, and that is the part that doesn't hold up. Fully remitted patients did not differ from controls. The only within-patient gradient, acute versus fully remitted, survives correction at p equals zero point zero three seven and then stops surviving, at zero point zero six four, in the authors' own no-global-signal-regression sensitivity analysis. The specificity claim fails more sharply: they also built a schizophrenia-derived score as a comparison, and it separated depressed patients from controls slightly better than the depression score did, and tracked remission better — it was the only one that separated fully remitted patients from controls. The direct comparison between the two scores came back non-significant at p equals zero point zero five nine. So specificity rests entirely on which symptom correlations survived correction, and that set changes completely depending on whether global signal regression is applied: with it, loss of pleasure; without it, four different items and no anhedonia. The clinician-rated scale is null. To their credit the authors call this research-grade and not usable at the individual level. The honest summary is that a connectome signature of depression generalizes across continents, and we do not yet know what it is a signature of.

Turning to computational pharmacology, and to a model that is better than its own framing. PSICHIC-plus is a bioactivity foundation model from a Monash and Griffith team with NVIDIA, trained on about eighty-two million protein-ligand records across forty-nine hundred proteins. The idea worth stealing is the loss function. Instead of regressing a single affinity number, the model emits a beta distribution over normalized bioactivity, parameterized by a location and a concentration. That lets exact binding constants, I-C-fifty-derived calls, screening hits and decoys all train against one objective — continuous data gets a likelihood on the distribution, binary data a classification on its cumulative tail — with data-source quality entering as a penalty on how tight that distribution may be. No structure needed, just sequence and SMILES strings, at about one and a third milliseconds per pair.

On the screening benchmarks it posts a five-percent enrichment factor of thirteen point three, where the authors' own regression and classification variants of the same backbone get five point six and ten point eight. Be careful with that: the decoys in those benchmarks are property-matched library decoys, and property-matched library decoys are exactly what this model was trained to reject as its negative class. The sequence-identity exclusion removes the benchmark protein from training but does nothing about that shared decoy-construction prior, and no debiased screening set was run.

The hit-to-lead claim needs the same care. Mean Pearson correlation against free-energy perturbation ground truth is zero point five two for PSICHIC-plus, zero point six for Boltz-two, zero point six eight for the ensemble of the two, and zero point seven four for FEP itself. The "approaches FEP" line applies only to the ensemble — which calls Boltz-two on every pair and therefore inherits Boltz-two's twenty-second runtime, not the millisecond one. The real sequence-only number is zero point five two, an R-squared of about zero point two seven against FEP's zero point five five, and it beats Boltz-two on seven of sixteen targets. Take the millisecond screener for structureless targets and the distributional loss as a genuinely good idea; leave the FEP framing.

Which sets up two papers posted the same day from the same lab in Skopje — one senior author across both, so treat them as one coordinated argument rather than independent convergence. Both ask whether the sophisticated choice beats the dumb one in small-molecule ADMET prediction, and both say no.

The first asks whether active learning beats choosing molecules at random. Eight regression endpoints, seven acquisition strategies, two uncertainty backends, ten rounds, ten seeds, under a strict chemotype split. Against matched random, acquisition wins fourteen of forty-eight cells with Monte Carlo dropout and nineteen of forty-eight with deep ensembles — and loses the rest. After multiple-comparison correction, the number of significant wins is zero out of ninety-six. The mechanism is the valuable part: the median rank correlation between the model's own epistemic uncertainty and its actual test error is zero point zero seven eight, and the most-uncertain decile of compounds carries one point one four times the error of the rest. The uncertainty estimate is barely pointing at the errors, so prioritizing by it barely helps. Two caveats the authors disclose and don't ablate: the two-hundred-compound warm start was stratified on the target labels, which is informative in a way no prospective campaign can be, and the cluster split means the test chemotypes are excluded from the acquisition pool by construction, so no strategy can ever buy a label from the chemistry it's scored on.

The second paper asks the same question about model families. Six benchmark endpoints, scaffold split, matched fifty-trial tuning budgets for graph neural networks, frozen chemical language models and plain fingerprints, median over ten seeds. No family dominates, and the differences sit inside the seed-to-seed range. The sharpest number is their own ablation of the one apparent graph-network win: Caco-two permeability at an R-squared of zero point four five with fifteen R-D-Kit descriptors concatenated on, and minus zero point five one without them. The graph contributed essentially nothing; fifteen precomputed numbers did the work. No metaheuristic hyperparameter optimizer beat random search either. The honest limitation is that their graph network was deliberately chosen as a mid-ranked architecture with no bond features at all, while the ones that actually top these leaderboards are cited and never run — so the negative result is about one bond-blind model, not about message passing. The pair is still the right thing to hand anyone selling you an active-learning platform or a pretrained molecular encoder: show me the matched random control, and the ablation without the descriptors.

Moving to the clinic, and to what I think is the best-designed clinical AI evaluation I've seen this year, even though its headline doesn't survive its own statistics. A group at Mass General Brigham and Dana-Farber built a six-agent pipeline for detecting immune-related adverse events in oncology notes — splitting notes into events, detecting toxicities with three-run self-consistency plus a judge, grading against the standard oncology toxicity criteria, then assigning attribution and certainty. What makes it worth your time is the staging. First a retrospective evaluation on two hundred eighty-nine expert-annotated notes. Then a three-month silent prospective deployment on eight hundred eighty-four notes, where nothing touched care. Then an actual randomized crossover with seventeen clinical research coordinators doing timed annotation with and without the tool.

The retrospective numbers look strong — detection F-score of zero point nine three. The prospective numbers are the useful ones: detection fell to about zero point seven two, and note-level grade agreement was sixty-two percent. That drop is the finding, and almost nobody measures it. The randomized arm shows a real time saving — median annotation time fell from four hundred twenty-eight seconds to two hundred forty-two. But the accuracy claim in the title does not hold. The per-label test of grade accuracy is flatly null, a paired sign test at p equals zero point seven nine one. The only positive accuracy result is a composite requiring all six grades exactly right, nineteen to twenty-six percent at p equals zero point zero four five, unadjusted. And the dramatic jump in inter-rater agreement is close to guaranteed by the design, because annotators were pre-populated with the model's labels and converge on it — the authors concede they could not separate consistency from automation complacency. One more thing buried in the results: a seven-hundred-seventy-nine-term regular expression performed comparably to some of the language-model methods on binary detection. Use this paper as the template for staging a clinical AI validation. Don't use it as evidence that agents make curators more accurate.

I'll close with the cleanest example of today's theme, and it's a paper whose entire contribution is a negative result about its own earlier positive one. A group at Osaka built a multi-agent system for generating cross-domain biological hypotheses, and observed what looked like a clear quality improvement when they upgraded the synthesizer and critic agents to a more capable model. Then — and this is the admirable part — they went back and asked whether the model was responsible. It wasn't. The stronger model had been given a prompt asking for three hypotheses instead of two, had been the only one handed the quality rubric in its prompt, and had received a query with an extra sub-question. When they matched prompts, output schema, hypothesis count and query text and ran five repeats, the advantage vanished and reversed: the supposedly weaker model scored higher, significantly so on one of the two cases. Every apparent model-tier signature mapped onto a prompt variable.

Treat the absolute scores with suspicion — the grader is a model from the same family as the thing graded, the scale is saturated, and five literature citations turned out to be fabricated or unverifiable and were swapped by hand afterward, which the authors disclose. The phrase "beyond single-investigator reach" in the title has no human comparison arm behind it at all. But the demonstration at the centre is worth importing into any lab benchmarking these systems: a convincing, directional model-capability effect was manufactured entirely by uncontrolled prompt differences, and it took a deliberate matched re-run to find that out. That is today's lesson in its purest form. The comparison you didn't run is the one that decides what your result means.
