## Script

Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Thursday, October first. Eleven items, and the thread running through nearly all of them is how much weight a given piece of evidence can actually bear. A variant large enough to stratify a diagnosis. A rare-variant association whose real product is a power calculation. A drug that visibly stripped its target out of human brain tissue and saved nobody. A benchmark metric so poorly calibrated it hid several years of real progress. A language model that states a strategy and then reliably fails to execute it.

One housekeeping note. Both preprint metadata APIs were healthy this morning — about seventeen hundred records swept day by day — but the Cloudflare layer in front of bioRxiv full text refused every request, nine attempts across three documents, including through the reader proxy I normally fall back on. medRxiv was unaffected, which is why today leans clinical. Three casualties worth naming: a cryo-EM structure of the vesicular inhibitory amino acid transporter, the protein that loads GABA into synaptic vesicles and has essentially no pharmacology despite sitting upstream of every inhibitory synapse; a mapping of the ligand site in the TRPM3 voltage-sensor-like domain, which matters because primidone is a TRPM3 blocker and that channel keeps resurfacing as an analgesic target; and a report that spinal microglia are dispensable for opioid-induced hyperalgesia. All three are deferred, not rejected.

Let's start with the item I think matters most this week, from the Misión Origen biobank in the Paisa genetic isolate of Colombia, through a UCLA, Universidad de Antioquia and Broad collaboration. Psychiatric genetics has been living with a structural gap: common variants of tiny effect that collectively carry most population risk, and ultra-rare coding variants that confer large risk in so few people you can never study carriers as a group. Between those regimes, almost nothing — so nobody can point at a specific variant and ask what it does to a person's psychiatric phenotype.

A founder population changes that arithmetic. Exome sequencing of nearly twenty-two thousand cases — defined transdiagnostically, so schizophrenia, bipolar disorder and severe major depression together — against nearly twenty thousand controls yields seven gene-level associations, five of them new to serious mental illness, and for four of those the signal is carried by variants that drifted up to appreciable frequency through the founder effect. The headline is a frameshift in ADAP1, genome-wide significant for serious mental illness overall and separately for schizophrenia and for bipolar disorder, raising risk for both by more than threefold. ADAP1 is a brain-enriched Arf6 GTPase-activating and scaffolding protein tied to post-mitotic neuronal morphogenesis and dendritic differentiation.

The number to hold onto is the allele frequency, about zero point four percent in this cohort — high enough to do population-scale work on carriers, and they already have. Carriers differ from other cases on psychotic, manic and cognitive item-level phenotypes extracted from longitudinal health records by natural language processing, and show impaired executive function on direct testing. Among people carrying a depression diagnosis, where the variant itself is not significantly associated, carriers nonetheless shift toward bipolar-typical symptoms. That is the thing worth sitting with: a variant that does not respect the diagnostic boundary, at a frequency where you can actually recruit carriers, pulls the phenotype in a direction the clinical label did not predict. If stratifying serious mental illness by causation rather than presentation is ever going to be more than a slogan, it looks like this.

Second, the counterweight, from Edinburgh and Exeter — a study whose most valuable output is its own negative result. A targeted rare-variant analysis of the FADS cluster, the fatty-acid desaturase locus that keeps surfacing in common-variant work on depression and circulating lipids, in whole-genome data from about seven hundred forty thousand people across All of Us and UK Biobank. Rare variants there were strongly associated with all five blood metabolites they tested, and not associated with major depression at all. Mendelian randomisation threw up one surviving instrument, an intronic FADS2 variant giving a protective effect of docosahexaenoic acid on depression, but that is one hit out of roughly three hundred Wald-ratio tests with every multi-variant method null, so I would not spend it. The useful number is the power calculation: eighty percent power was reached in zero point zero six percent of their single-variant tests, and extrapolating from their median allele frequency and effect size they estimate you would need roughly four million people. That is the state of rare-variant psychiatric genetics outside a founder population, and it is exactly why the Paisa result matters.

Third, a drug-target Mendelian randomisation study from UCL asking whether any licensed antidiabetic is a plausible repurposing candidate for vascular dementia. The design discipline is most of the paper. UK-licensed drugs from the national formulary, their molecular targets from DrugBank, and eight targets that can actually be proxied genetically: the GLP-1 receptor, DPP-4, the SGLT2 transporter, PPAR-gamma, and the sulfonylurea and meglitinide targets including two potassium-channel subunits and histamine H1. Metformin and acarbose were excluded for the right reasons — metformin has no cleanly defined target to proxy, acarbose acts on a family of enzymes rather than one protein. Instruments came from glycated haemoglobin in about four hundred thirty-eight thousand people, with cortex-specific expression quantitative trait loci as a secondary approach, against clinically diagnosed vascular dementia in about seven thousand cases plus three neuroimaging markers of cerebrovascular disease.

The answer is essentially nothing, with type-two diabetes behaving correctly as a positive control. DPP-4 inhibition gave a suggestive odds ratio of zero point six eight with an interval from zero point four six to one point zero two, so crossing the null, and PPAR-gamma modulation moved one diffusion marker at a p of point zero four in the secondary analysis. Be careful what this does and does not say, because the pharmacoepidemiology on incretins and dementia is now enormous and relentlessly positive. This is a null for the specific proposition that lifelong genetically proxied target modulation, scaled through glycated haemoglobin, lowers vascular dementia risk. It cannot exclude a short-term pharmacological effect that genetics does not mimic, and seven thousand cases is a thin outcome. But it is the right test to run before a prevention trial, and it does not support one.

Fourth, the day's most sobering document: a postmortem biochemical evaluation of what anti-amyloid antibodies actually did inside the human brain, from the Dominantly Inherited Alzheimer Network at Washington University. Brain donors from the trials unit who had received either gantenerumab, which targets fibrillar amyloid and recruits microglial phagocytosis through Fc gamma receptors, or solanezumab, which targets soluble monomer — four people each — were compared by high-resolution mass spectrometry against forty untreated symptomatic mutation carriers and eighteen age-relevant controls with little or no plaque.

Gantenerumab worked biochemically, and not subtly. Insoluble, plaque-associated amyloid-beta forty-two and forty were down sixty to eighty percent against untreated carriers, consistent with the amyloid PET measured in these same people while alive, and the pyroglutamate-modified species at position three fell in both the insoluble and soluble pools. The downstream signal moved too: insoluble phosphorylated tau at threonine one eighty-one, two oh five and two oh eight was regionally lower, as were three microtubule-binding-region species, with no change in insoluble total tau — a shift in tau proteoform composition rather than bulk removal. Solanezumab did nothing measurable to either protein in either pool, which is the cleanest biochemical account yet of why that programme failed.

Now the part that should govern how you read it. Exposures ran two to six years, cumulative doses from about fifty-eight hundred milligrams up to forty-eight thousand. All eight treated participants progressed to moderate or severe dementia before they died, and amyloid never came down to control range. The mechanism moved, decisively and measurably, in the target tissue, and the outcome did not. For anyone building a disease-modification argument on target engagement, this is the reference case: the drug did what the mechanism said it would, in human brain, confirmed by mass spectrometry, and the people still died with severe dementia. Whether that means too late, too little, or the wrong causal model is the open question, and eight brains cannot settle it.

Fifth, the one piece of bench molecular pharmacology I could read in full this week, in Molecular Psychiatry. That group one metabotropic glutamate receptor-dependent long-term depression requires new protein synthesis is textbook. What this adds is a transcriptional gate upstream of that translation: ATF4, which the central nervous system literature has treated almost entirely as a stress-response and apoptosis factor. Activating group one mGluRs raises ATF4 and drives it into the nucleus, and ATF4 turns out to be required both for the mGluR-dependent translation and for the LTD itself — with a kinase upstream of ERK, multiple ribosomal proteins and two eukaryotic initiation factor three subunits among its hippocampal targets. Behaviourally, ATF4 is needed for the mGluR5-driven reduction in marble burying.

The result I would act on is the sex difference, and it is enormous. Hippocampal RNA sequencing in heterozygous ATF4 mice found thirteen differentially expressed genes in males and six hundred forty-three in females, with only six shared. Group one mGluR signalling has been known to be sex-dependent for years without a satisfying mechanism, and a transcription factor that reshapes the postsynaptic and translational landscape in one sex and barely touches it in the other is a candidate explanation. Given how comprehensively mGluR5 negative allosteric modulators failed in fragile X despite near-perfect preclinical rescue, that belonged in the stratification plan.

Sixth, turning to the computational half of the show, and the paper I would most like every agentic-discovery group to read. A collaboration out of MIT, Stanford and Lund benchmarked five frontier language models as Bayesian optimisation agents across seven combinatorial datasets spanning protein engineering, reaction optimisation, molecular design, peptide self-assembly and catalysis, against the published statistical baseline for each, scores normalised so random selection is zero and the best statistical model is one. The headline is deflationary: no configuration tested decisively beat a mean statistical baseline across domains. Prior chemical knowledge helps in expectation — Spearman rho of point two zero — but that prior is worth roughly one batch of experiments, with an interval from minus point two one to two point five eight. Equipping the model with a tool wrapping the very statistical model it is benchmarked against did not reliably help in any prompt condition.

The diagnostic machinery is the real contribution. They measure beliefs directly two ways — a judge extracting stated marginal probabilities from the reasoning trace, and a belief inferred from the actions — then apply martingale tests to ask whether belief updates behave rationally, correcting a measurement-noise bias that had been mislabelling rational agents as irrational. These models do not entrench on their priors, which was the usual worry; they overreact to incoming data. And the finding I keep thinking about: they sincerely intend to explore, say so in the reasoning, and then act exploitatively, consistently. That is not a gap between stated and revealed preference. It is a competence gap produced by context-stickiness, and the proof is that deleting the in-context history restores exploration. If you are handing an agent a real experimental budget, the lesson is that prior and accumulating data have to be architecturally decoupled, because inside one context window the data wins and the model cannot act on its own plan. The failure modes are wonderfully specific: two of the five systematically avoid the optimal ligand across seven hundred fifty selections, and the string "TrpB" makes one model over-select aromatic substitutions even though aromatics are worse on average here.

Seventh, a direct counterpoint on agent architecture, from Lingang Laboratory and Shanghai Jiao Tong. M3OS is a multi-agent system for small-molecule optimisation whose premise is that storing the optimisation history in the conversation is the bug. Instead a persistent directed graph holds every evaluated candidate keyed by canonical structure, with parent-child transformation edges, accumulated rewards and visit counts; Monte Carlo graph search over that graph proposes which node to expand, and the model picks from a shortlist. A critic scores every proposal against an executable task contract before anything enters the graph.

On the activity-constrained lead optimisation benchmark it reaches a final success rate of point five nine against point four eight and point four two for two strong general coding agents on the same backbone. But the number to highlight is one the authors make almost in passing. Their graph-any success rate — did the system ever find a qualifying molecule anywhere in the search — is point six one, so the gap between finding a solution and submitting it is about point zero two, against point zero nine and point zero five for the two baselines. The persistent graph's measurable contribution is not that it generates better molecules; it is that the system stops losing the good molecule it already found. That is more modest and more believable than most agent papers manage, and it connects straight to the previous item: what breaks when state lives in a context window is retention. The limits are stated — all properties are predicted, nothing synthesised or assayed, and the Monte Carlo selection policy is never isolated against alternative selection rules at matched budget.

Eighth, the cleanest experiment on multi-agent prompting I have seen, from Peking Union Medical College Hospital. The literature here is contradictory, and the authors identify why: two different things have been called "multiple perspectives." One is several personas voiced sequentially inside a single context, each conditioning on the ones before it. The other is genuinely independent opinions generated in isolation and only then combined. Nobody had varied independence alone while holding roles, model and prompt content constant.

So they did, across eighty-six clinicopathological conference cases, four hundred six cases from an external benchmark, and three hundred sixty-four real emergency department encounters, five seeds each. On the external benchmark the independent team beat the single call on both pre-specified recall endpoints — plus three points at top-three, plus three point nine at top-five — and the factorial attributes that gain precisely. Mechanical rank aggregation of the independent lists by Borda count never helped and sometimes landed below the single call, while a moderator synthesising the same independent samples recovered the full advantage; swapping those plain samples for five specialist role calls bought two additional tenths of a point. The personas are optional. What matters is independent generation plus moderated synthesis — and when a fresh moderator was fed five sequentially generated, mutually conditioning persona analyses, the result stayed down at persona level, because once the voices have heard each other the candidate pool has already collapsed.

Then the reversal, which is the part to remember. On real emergency presentations, inputs that end before any lab or imaging result, the single call beat the team on every metric: top-one forty point one percent against thirty-four point three, top-three fifty-four point six against forty-five point five, top-five sixty-one point six against fifty-one point nine, all at p below point zero zero one, in both reference-label strata, confirmed by a judge-free word-coverage check. Adding the visit's objective results lifted every arm without closing the gap. Neither temperature matching nor backbone scale explains it — a six-call team on the smaller model was indistinguishable from one call to the much larger one. And the moderated-independent arm spends about six times the tokens of a single call for its five-point top-three gain. So the deployment rule is neither "teams are better" nor "teams are hype." Multi-perspective generation pays when the input is information-rich and the reference is a confirmed diagnosis, and it actively hurts when the input is thin.

Ninth, a disciplined small study from Seoul National University Bundang Hospital on whether a language model can predict a trial's result from the information available at registration. Every retrospective version of that question is contaminated by recall, because a model evaluated on trials reported before its cutoff may simply have read the answer. Late-breaking congress presentations solve it: designs are publicly registered, results embargoed until the day of presentation.

So they screened all one hundred thirty-nine late-breaking trials at this year's European Society of Cardiology congress, selected fifty by pre-specified rules, built structured design summaries from the registrations with every results-related detail stripped, and locked the predictions on the Open Science Framework before the congress opened. Three models with no web access and no tools — Claude Fable five, GPT five point five, and Gemini three point five Flash — each gave a probability the primary endpoint would be met plus a point estimate and eighty percent interval for a pre-locked effect measure, adjudicated afterwards on the reported numbers rather than the presenter's wording, because spin in non-significant cardiovascular trials is well documented.

Of forty-eight scorable trials, twenty-eight were positive. Areas under the curve were point eight zero, point seven nine and point seven four, all significantly above chance, with accuracies of seventy-three, seventy-one and sixty-seven percent. Among the twenty-six trials whose effect was reported exactly as locked, direction was right in eighty-eight percent of predictions, and half the ratio estimates landed within about fifteen percent of the observed value.

Two caveats carry most of the interpretive weight. Agreement between models, kappa point six nine to point eight two, substantially exceeded any model's agreement with reality, kappa point three zero to point four four — so these are three correlated readers of the same conventional wisdom, not three independent forecasts, and ensembling buys much less than it looks like. And the same models predicted from titles alone, before seeing any design, at areas under the curve of point seven zero, point six seven and point six five. Most of the discrimination was in the title. Read as a tool for trial design, this says a model can tell you where the field's prior sits, calibrated — useful for powering a study, and not the same thing as independent insight.

Tenth, a Nature Biotechnology analysis that reverses a conclusion many people had already filed away: that deep learning models of genetic perturbation do not beat uninformative baselines, specifically that the mean baseline — the average perturbed profile in the training set — matches or exceeds them on mean squared error and control-referenced correlation. This group argues the metrics were the problem, and builds the argument the way an experimentalist would, with controls.

The negative control is the mean baseline. For a positive control they use a technical duplicate: split the cells from each perturbation in half and predict one half from the other, so both halves carry the same real perturbation signal. That should beat an uninformative predictor, and in one dataset it does — but in a genome-wide Perturb-seq dataset it loses to the mean baseline on mean squared error in about ninety-five percent of individual perturbations. The explanation is signal dilution: that dataset averages three point two five differentially expressed genes per perturbation against a hundred and two in the other, so a transcriptome-wide error metric is dominated by the unperturbed majority, where the mean is simply a better-powered estimate.

Their fix is a meta-metric, the dynamic range fraction, which asks per perturbation what fraction of the theoretically available improvement a metric actually realises between negative and positive control. Across eighteen metrics and fourteen datasets, mean squared error and control-referenced Pearson score near zero on this except in strongly perturbed data, while weighted mean squared error, a weighted delta R-squared, and a normalised inverse-rank metric stay well calibrated — all by upweighting the genes the perturbation actually moved. And once you evaluate on calibrated metrics, even the older models, scGPT and GEARS, mostly do beat the uninformative baselines — their perturbation-specific signal was there all along and the metric was hiding it. One more finding worth carrying: on combinatorial prediction the widely used Norman19 dataset is effectively saturated by a simple additive baseline, which captures about eighty-eight percent of the ideal performance gap because the training split covers under one percent of possible pairs, while on a dataset with ten percent coverage models do exceed additivity. The general lesson travels well past perturbation prediction: if your benchmark has a negative control and no positive control, you cannot distinguish a model that failed from a metric that cannot see.

And to close, something lighter but with teeth, from Idaho and Vassar. A thousand and three people with depression evaluated vignettes describing psychotherapy at four levels of AI involvement: a human therapist, assistive AI, collaborative AI, and fully autonomous AI. Humans won on every measure. The economics are where it gets concrete: against a human therapist, participants were willing to pay thirty-one point six percent less for a therapist using assistive or collaborative AI, and fifty-seven point two percent less for fully autonomous AI. Eighteen to nineteen percent said they would refuse treatment involving assistive or collaborative AI; thirty-one percent would refuse fully autonomous.

The finding I did not expect is that the preference for humans was strongest at the highest credential levels — doctoral and master's rather than peer or trainee. That inverts the usual deployment story. The intuition has been that AI augmentation extends scarce expert capacity; what these respondents say is that AI involvement discounts expertise most precisely where the expertise is greatest, and is comparatively tolerated where it substitutes for the least-trained provider. This is stated preference on vignettes, not revealed preference in a clinic, and that gap is usually large. But even assistive AI — a clinician using a tool — takes a thirty-one percent valuation hit and a near-one-in-five refusal rate. If you are building in this space, that is the number that belongs in your model, not the autonomous one.

That's eleven. Short version: a founder population delivers a large-effect psychiatric risk variant frequent enough to study its carriers as a group; gantenerumab demonstrably stripped insoluble amyloid out of human brain and every treated patient still progressed to severe dementia; genetics does not support repurposing any licensed antidiabetic for vascular dementia prevention; a transcription factor gates group one mGluR plasticity almost entirely in females; language-model agents say they will explore and then don't, because the context window won't let them; multi-agent diagnosis helps on rich inputs and hurts on thin ones; and a badly calibrated metric had been hiding real progress in perturbation modelling.
