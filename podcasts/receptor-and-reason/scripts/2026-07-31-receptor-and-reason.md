## Script

Welcome to Receptor and Reason for July thirty-first, twenty twenty-six. Nine items today, and there's a quiet throughline running through most of them: the gap between a system that runs and a system you can actually trust — whether that system is a language-model agent, a docking pipeline, or a mouse behavioral assay. Let's get into it.

We start with a paper that is really a state-of-the-field reckoning for agentic AI in computational biology, out of the University of Alabama at Birmingham. The premise is one a lot of us have felt but haven't formalized: large language model agents now plan, execute, and interpret biological analyses, and they do it fluently — but fluent output, a successful tool call, and a good benchmark score do not add up to scientific credibility. The authors survey a hundred and nine agentic or agent-adjacent systems and twenty-eight benchmark resources across genomics, single-cell and spatial omics, protein science, drug discovery, and computational pathology, and they propose that we stop judging these agents by their final answer and start judging them by their workflow trajectory — the inspectable trail of what data went in, what analytical choices were made, what tools and parameters were used, what failed and got repaired, and what evidence actually supports the conclusion. They wrap this in a framework they call Function, Evidence, and Validation: what did the agent demonstrably do, is there a traceable basis for each action and claim, and was the principal claim actually validated for the use case at hand. The finding that matters is a diagnosis of the field: planning and tool-mediated execution have raced ahead, while replayability, provenance, robustness, external validation, and prospective empirical testing have lagged badly behind. In other words, an agent can retrieve authoritative but irrelevant evidence, preserve a perfectly replayable computation that supports an unjustified interpretation, and test only a sliver of its own central claim — and still look great on a leaderboard. The take-home for anyone building or buying these tools: ask for the workflow trace, not just the answer. Final-answer correctness is the wrong unit of analysis, and this paper gives you a concrete vocabulary for demanding better.

From evaluating agents to a genuinely satisfying piece of circuit neuropharmacology, and one of my favorite items today. There's a longstanding paradox in the striatum: opioid reward depends on mu-opioid receptors on medium spiny neurons in the nucleus accumbens, yet mu-opioid receptor function has never mapped cleanly onto the textbook D1-direct, D2-indirect pathway split. A group at the University of Alabama at Birmingham, the Day lab, resolves it by identifying a rare medium spiny neuron population marked by a gene called Chst9. These cells express the mu-opioid receptor at exceptionally high levels, they're a specialized indirect-pathway subtype that's molecularly and spatially distinct from the canonical populations, and — importantly — the population is conserved across species. Opioids robustly silence these Chst9 neurons, and when the team selectively deletes the mu-opioid receptor gene from just this population, fentanyl conditioned place preference is abolished. That's a clean causal chain from a molecularly defined cell type to opioid reward, and it reframes the search for opioid use disorder therapies around a specific, conserved cellular substrate rather than the whole accumbens. It's the kind of result that turns a paradox into a target.

Next, a sobering but honest drug-discovery story from the Broad Institute — a good counterweight to easy optimism about human-genetics-to-drug pipelines. Rare loss-of-function mutations in GRIA3, the gene encoding the GluA3 subunit of the AMPA receptor, substantially raise schizophrenia risk, which makes a tidy hypothesis: build a positive allosteric modulator that preferentially boosts GluA3, the exact subunit human genetics points to, and you might get a safer, more effective AMPA-receptor PAM than the nonselective ones that have all failed to reach approval. The team did the medicinal chemistry and landed a GluA3-preferring modulator they call BRD3290, with reasonable potency, good tolerability, and solid brain exposure. And here's the honest part: when they dosed wild-type mice, BRD3290 engaged the established AMPA-receptor PAM target-engagement biomarker — so it was hitting the receptor in vivo — but it did not improve performance on novel object recognition, whereas an older nonselective AMPA-receptor PAM did improve it. The interpretation is that the very subtype-selectivity the genetics motivated may have been insufficient to drive the cognitive readout. It's a valuable negative: it shows that "the subunit the genetics implicated" and "the subunit that moves the behavior" are not guaranteed to be the same thing, and it lays out real molecular lessons about how hard subtype-selective AMPA modulation actually is. I'd rather see this published than buried.

Staying with glutamate but moving to computation, Xiao-Jing Wang's group at NYU has a modeling paper on metabotropic glutamate receptors in the higher-order thalamus. These trans-thalamic circuits route information between cortical regions through higher-order thalamic nuclei like the pulvinar, and they're loaded with group-one metabotropic glutamate receptors — whose signaling unfolds over seconds, which sits awkwardly with the thalamus's traditional millisecond relay role. The model gives those slow receptors a job. By suppressing potassium leak currents, thalamocortical metabotropic receptors on cortical pyramidal neurons strengthen the coupling between soma and dendrite and promote dendritic plateau potentials, boosting feedforward propagation of sensory signals and sharpening performance on a detection task. On the other side, corticothalamic metabotropic receptors on the thalamic neurons themselves integrate inputs over time and shift firing from burst mode to tonic mode — and this, they show with support from pulvinar recordings in behaving animals, acts like a wake-up call: it heightens detection of a novel stimulus while dampening responses to repeated ones. So a receptor system that looked too slow to matter for a relay turns out to give the higher-order thalamus a dynamic dial on cortical communication. It's a nice reminder that "too slow for the canonical role" often means "doing a different job."

Now three therapeutics-focused items in a row, starting with a novel modality for Parkinson's out of Inserm. LRRK2 kinase is a priority target in Parkinson's, and most of the field is chasing ATP-competitive kinase inhibitors. This group went after a protein-protein interaction instead: LRRK2 is dephosphorylated by the phosphatase PP1, and they built a bifunctional peptide, which they call PEP-3, designed both to cross the blood-brain barrier and to disrupt the LRRK2-PP1 interface. The peptide competes for that interaction in vitro, stays detectable in mouse brain for up to six hours after a retro-orbital injection, resists serum proteases across mouse, human, dog, and monkey, and shows no chronic toxicity in mice. And in alpha-synuclein-overexpressing mouse models, repeated intraperitoneal dosing over fifteen and thirty days was well tolerated and produced a significant reduction in dopaminergic cell death in the substantia nigra, alongside lower levels of pathological phosphorylated alpha-synuclein. It's early, it's a small in vivo package, and peptide therapeutics carry their own delivery headaches — but targeting the interaction rather than the catalytic site is a genuinely different way to modulate LRRK2, and the blood-brain-barrier-crossing design is the part worth watching.

The second therapeutics item is a nice example of an old natural product meeting a hot degeneration target — and it quietly uses AI structure prediction in the workflow. The target is SARM1, the enzyme that drives Wallerian degeneration by depleting axonal NAD-plus and triggering the axon to self-destruct after injury; SARM1 inhibition is one of the more exciting directions in traumatic nerve injury. A Penn group tested boldine — an aporphine alkaloid from the boldo tree that's already known to modulate connexin hemichannels and oxidative stress — and found it directly inhibits SARM1's NADase activity, with a half-maximal inhibitory concentration around seven and a half micromolar. They then used AlphaFold-3-style structure prediction plus machine-learning docking to propose that boldine binds at two sites — the catalytic pocket and a regulatory interface — suggesting a dual-site mechanism. And in a sciatic nerve explant model, boldine preserved the integrity of severed axon segments out to seven days. Micromolar potency and a computational binding model are a starting point, not a drug, and the explant model is a long way from a clinic — but a brain-and-nerve-penetrant natural product hitting SARM1 at two sites is a reasonable scaffold to build from.

The third computational-pharmacology item, and one where I read the full paper, is a network-pharmacology screen for tauopathies out of Universidad Maimónides in Buenos Aires. The logic: autophagic clearance of hyperphosphorylated tau is impaired in tauopathies, and fungal metabolites are an underexplored source of neuroactive molecules. So they took metabolites from Lion's Mane, Psilocybe mushrooms, and ergot fungi, ran structural, blood-brain-barrier, and toxicity predictions, and layered network pharmacology and protein-interaction analysis to find autophagy-linked targets. Three rose to the top — the nuclear receptor PPAR-gamma, the kinase GSK-3-beta, and casein kinase 2 — chosen for their complementary roles bridging autophagy and tau. Molecular dynamics and physics-based binding free-energy rescoring flagged specific fungal compounds — a couple of Lion's Mane erinacerins and hericioic acid, and the ergot alkaloid ergocryptine — as stable binders, benchmarked against emodin as a reference. Here's why I appreciate this paper despite it being entirely in silico: the authors are unusually disciplined about their own limits. Their machine-learning structure-activity models were only interpretable for the PPAR-gamma candidates, which fell inside the model's applicability domain; for the others, the predictions fell outside the domain and they explicitly decline to treat them as evidence either way. And their one experimental anchor — reanalysis of an independent Alzheimer's hippocampal proteome — showed casein kinase 2 altered specifically in the CA3 subfield, but PPAR-gamma and GSK-3-beta unchanged, which they report plainly rather than spinning. This is a nomination paper, not a discovery, but it's the kind of computational triage done with appropriate humility.

Back to AI applied directly to psychiatry, and a very practical one from Clario, part of Thermo Fisher. Depression trials live and die on the reliability of clinician-rated interviews — a noisy MADRS rating, the Montgomery-Åsberg Depression Rating Scale, can be the difference between a drug separating from placebo and a failed study. Most automated depression-detection work has been trained on social media or online text, not on the standardized structured interviews trials actually use. This team built a language-model pipeline aimed squarely at the trial setting: it takes the audio of an interview conducted under the structured MADRS interview guide, transcribes it, maps the content onto the ten MADRS symptom items, estimates severity for each, and flags ratings that look problematic. On real clinical interviews it reached an overall correlation of about zero-point-eight-seven with expert ratings. It's not diagnosing anyone and it's not replacing the clinician — it's a quality-control and consistency layer for exactly the measurement that decides whether a CNS trial reads out. Given how many promising antidepressants have died on placebo separation that may have been rater noise as much as biology, tightening this measurement is unglamorous and genuinely useful.

And we'll close on psychedelics, with a paper that speaks straight back to today's opening theme of reproducibility. A Dartmouth group led by Katherine Nautiyal went after an uncomfortable open secret in preclinical psychedelic research: the post-acute behavioral effects of psilocybin in rodents are inconsistent and hard to reproduce across labs. Rather than run one more small study, they assembled a large dataset — nearly seven hundred mice — and used data-driven analysis to ask which biological and experimental variables actually move the readouts. The acute response, the head-twitch and locomotor effects, was robust. The post-acute, therapeutic-like profile was far more variable, with the sturdiest signal in sucrose preference, an anhedonia readout. And three factors emerged as real modulators of the post-acute antidepressant-like and anxiolytic effects: sex, the stress protocol used, and serotonin 5-HT1B signaling. A random-forest classifier could only narrowly predict whether a mouse had gotten the drug from its post-acute behavior plus these variables — which is itself the point. The heterogeneity everyone's been fighting isn't noise to be wished away; it's structured by identifiable variables, and once you account for them you can specify the conditions under which the therapeutic-like effects actually show up. For a field trying to build a mechanistic bridge from a mouse to a patient, mapping your sources of variance is exactly the right unglamorous move — and a fitting note to end on, given where we started.

That's the show. Nine papers, one recurring question: not just does it work, but can you show your work. See you tomorrow.
