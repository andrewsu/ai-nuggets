## Script

Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Thursday, November the nineteenth. There's a thread running through today's papers, and it's the selection problem. Generating candidates — variants, hypotheses, differentials, flagged problems — has gotten cheap. Knowing which of them to act on has not, and several papers today measure exactly that gap, in wet-lab pharmacology and in machine-learning evaluation alike. Twelve items.

The lead is from the Gether lab in Copenhagen, with Vienna, the intramural NIDA medicinal chemistry group and Danish psychiatric genetics, and it is the kind of grinding, unglamorous paper the field badly needs. They took fifty-five missense variants in the dopamine transporter, identified in a large Danish case-control cohort nested in national psychiatric registries, and characterized every one of them molecularly: substrate uptake kinetics, binding of a fluorescent membrane-impermeable cocaine analogue, surface and total expression by immunoassay, confocal localization, amphetamine responsiveness, and constitutive dopamine efflux measured in sniffer cells expressing a genetically encoded dopamine sensor.

The output is seven phenotypic classes that collapse onto a smaller set of molecular mechanisms. The most severe group — eight variants with no detectable uptake — lost surface expression in parallel, which points at folding and trafficking failure rather than catalysis; molecular dynamics on one of them showed a bulky side chain inserted into a transmembrane helix wrecking its secondary structure. A second group retained more surface expression than uptake capacity, which is the signature of a catalytic defect layered on top of a partial trafficking defect. A third set of amino-terminal variants did something else entirely: they ran faster, with increased maximal velocity and otherwise wild-type affinity and binding.

Now the part that matters for interpretation. The two most severely impaired groups, thirteen carriers in total, were found exclusively in cases, zero in controls. The intermediate groups split twenty-four to four, and the functionally normal group a hundred and seventy-seven to sixty-nine. That's a dose-response between measured transporter dysfunction and case status, which is as close as this design gets to a mechanistic claim. And against that, the in-silico benchmark: PolyPhen-2 called thirty-one of the fifty-five damaging or possibly damaging and twenty-four benign — but several variants it scored benign showed major loss of function in the assays, and a structure-damage predictor is in principle blind to gain-of-function phenotypes like constitutive efflux, which is the phenotype you most want to catch in a psychiatric cohort. One more honest negative: a variant previously reported to mediate constitutive efflux did not show it here under two independent assays, including classical superfusion with a radiolabelled substrate. Variant-level efflux may depend on the host cell's D2 receptors and kinases, which heterologous systems don't share.

So: the catalogue of rare neuropsychiatric variants is growing far faster than anyone can functionally test it, prediction is not yet good enough to triage, and this paper is a demonstration of what the triage actually costs and what it buys.

Staying in the genetics of neurodevelopmental disease, but with the question pushed down to cell type. A preprint on CASK-related disorder — the X-linked syndrome caused by variants in the calcium-calmodulin-dependent serine protein kinase, where epilepsy sits alongside developmental delay, microcephaly and pontocerebellar hypoplasia. The group built sex-matched mouse models, female heterozygous knockouts and male hypomorphs, and confirmed that both have epileptic phenotypes, which closes a real gap — males are roughly a third of patients and had essentially never been modelled.

Then they deleted CASK from specific interneuron populations, and the result is the clean one. Deleting it from parvalbumin interneurons produced predominantly spike-wave discharges, the absence-like phenotype, and the electrophysiology showed reduced spontaneous inhibitory transmission. Deleting it from somatostatin interneurons produced generalized tonic-clonic seizures, with impaired action-potential-evoked release instead. Same gene, same tissue, two presynaptic failure modes in two interneuron classes, and two clinically distinct seizure types. For a gene whose protein does half a dozen different things at the synapse and in the nucleus, that's a much more useful handle than another list of interactors — it says the phenotype you see in a given patient may index which inhibitory population is most affected, and spike-wave versus convulsive seizures have genuinely different pharmacology.

Third, a mechanism for how a transient experience leaves a durable molecular trace. A preprint profiles hippocampal memory ensemble neurons after single-trial contextual fear conditioning, at single-cell resolution, across remote timepoints, with chromatin accessibility, transcription and function all measured together.

The canonical model is that immediate early gene induction kicks off a wave of late-response transcription that returns to baseline within hours. What they find instead, in dentate granule ensembles, is a persistent chromatin and transcriptional program in a subset of ensemble neurons, sustained expression of many synaptic genes — regulators of AMPA receptor trafficking among them — and shifts in plasticity that coincide with it, running out to ten days and probably longer. And that signature predicts which neurons get reactivated at recall. So the ensemble that is tagged at learning is not the ensemble that supports retrieval; a molecular subset of it is.

The mechanistic hook is the best part. The regulatory elements carrying this persistent state are heavily enriched for AP-1 motifs — the Fos-family binding sites — even though FOS protein itself decayed away weeks earlier. What is still bound there is FOSB, which has a long half-life and is enriched specifically in the neurons maintaining the enduring expression changes. That's a candidate answer to the question of how an hour-scale induction signal becomes a week-scale one: the immediate early gene wave hands off to a stable family member that holds the elements open. FOSB is of course already familiar from addiction, where its accumulation is the standing explanation for persistent drug-induced plasticity. Seeing the same protein do persistence work in a physiological memory ensemble makes that look less like a quirk of drug exposure and more like the mechanism being borrowed.

Next, a piece of straightforward and useful radiopharmacology. Tau PET has two workhorse tracers, flortaucipir and M-K-six-two-four-oh, and the field moves between them without a good head-to-head on human tissue. This preprint does the autoradiography: postmortem sections from Alzheimer's disease, Down syndrome, cognitively normal controls and Parkinson's disease, with an iodinated tau ligand as the common radioligand for competitive binding.

Affinities came out close — half-maximal inhibition of about two point nine nanomolar for flortaucipir and about six nanomolar for M-K-six-two-four-oh in anterior cingulate. But the binding in tissue was not equivalent. Flortaucipir gave high grey-matter signal in both Alzheimer's and Down-syndrome Alzheimer's sections, with grey-matter ratios over controls of roughly fifteen in frontal and temporal cortex in Down syndrome, and roughly twelve and nine and a half in Alzheimer's — higher than M-K-six-two-four-oh in the Down-syndrome tissue in particular. And on the standing worry about flortaucipir's off-target monoamine oxidase A binding, they saw none in anterior cingulate from control or Parkinson's cases. Down syndrome is now a major setting for anti-amyloid and anti-tau trials, with tau PET as a pharmacodynamic endpoint, and a two-fold difference in tissue signal between tracers is not a detail if you are pooling or comparing across studies that used different ones.

Two short alcohol-pharmacology items, both from Neuropharmacology this week, both abstract-level.

The first is a nice dose-inversion result. Rats got a single post-training injection of ethanol and were tested on inhibitory avoidance. An intermediate binge-like dose, one and a half grams per kilogram, impaired memory formation, as everyone expects. A higher dose, two and a half, produced a long-lasting enhancement of the aversive memory that persisted at least twenty-one days. Both effects ran through alpha-7 nicotinic receptors in the ventral tegmental area — blocking them with methyllycaconitine prevented the enhancement, while positively modulating them with PNU-120596 prevented the impairment at the lower dose. And activating prefrontal D1 receptors restored the enhancement after VTA alpha-7 blockade. One receptor population, opposite effects at two doses, which is a reminder that dose-response in alcohol memory studies is not monotonic and the standard single-dose design will give you whichever answer the dose happens to select.

The second: GLP-1 receptor agonists suppress alcohol intake in rodents and in patients, but the circuit is only partly mapped. This group went after the middle paraventricular nucleus of the thalamus, which expresses the receptor and modulates mesolimbic dopamine. Blocking the receptor locally there with exendin-3 attenuated the effect of systemic exendin-4 on alcohol-induced locomotor stimulation — so the thalamic site is carrying part of the systemic drug's action. Delivering exendin-4 directly into that nucleus reduced alcohol-induced locomotion and accumbal dopamine release, but did not alter conditioned place preference. That dissociation is the interesting bit: the site handles the stimulant and dopaminergic arm without touching the associative reward-learning arm, which argues the clinical effect of these drugs in alcohol use disorder is distributed across sites rather than localized to one.

A third pharmacology item, and this one I want to push back on. A paper in Translational Psychiatry is titled as identifying 2-arachidonoylglycerol as a biomarker for predicting response to short-term fluoxetine. Mice went through four weeks of chronic unpredictable mild stress, then seven days of fluoxetine at ten milligrams per kilogram, with hippocampal and prefrontal endocannabinoids and monoamines measured by mass spectrometry. Chronic stress itself did not significantly move hippocampal 2-AG or anandamide. Animals were then split into responders and non-responders on the basis of their behaviour, and responders showed elevated hippocampal 2-AG relative to controls, to stressed vehicle animals, and to non-responders, with a negative correlation between 2-AG and immobility in the tail-suspension test of minus zero point two eight.

The biology is plausible and the endocannabinoid-antidepressant link has independent support. But the design does not support the word predicting. Animals were classified as responders by their behaviour, and then the biomarker was measured and found to differ between the groups — that is a correlate of response measured after the fact, not a predictor measured before treatment. A predictive biomarker claim requires the measurement to precede the outcome and to beat baseline severity. And a correlation of minus zero point two eight at a p of zero point zero four, in a post-hoc split, is the regime where replication decides. I flag it because the terminology matters more in this area than most: a response correlate tells you about mechanism, a predictive biomarker tells a clinician who to treat, and the two get conflated in titles far more often than in designs.

Now to the selection problem in its explicit form. A preprint asks how often hypotheses generated by frontier language models match the actual withheld finding of a published study. Fifty tasks were built from peer-reviewed ageing-biology papers published after the models' stated training cutoffs; each task gave background and a research question and withheld the finding. Four models — GPT-6 Astra, Grok 4.5, Claude Opus 5.5 and Gemini 4 Argon — each produced five hypotheses per task by three methods, nearly three thousand hypotheses in all, scored by a panel of judge models from other developers.

The base rates are sobering and should be: single-request hypotheses matched the published finding thirteen to eighteen percent of the time. A multistep pipeline did no better. But the structure inside that number is the finding. The hypothesis the model listed first matched in thirty-seven and a half percent of cases, against ten percent for the hypotheses in positions two through five. The models are already ranking, and the ranking carries most of the signal.

Then they applied a selection rule, chosen from a hundred and twelve variants: keep only hypotheses that all four predictor models independently forecast would be experimentally confirmed, with the predictors never seeing the published result. Among unanimously selected hypotheses, match rates ran from forty-seven to seventy-four percent, against twelve to eighteen percent for a random control. Pooled, selection beat first-listed by twenty-three percentage points. The cost is recall: those short lists captured only about a third of all matching hypotheses. And the authors are careful about the ceiling — divergence from a published paper does not refute a hypothesis, since a study investigates one mechanism and the model may have proposed another that nobody has tested. There's also a manual audit, blinded to generator and judge score, which agreed with the judges in eighty-one of a hundred cases.

What I take from it: the useful product of a hypothesis-generating model is not the hypothesis, it's the ordering, and an independent ensemble forecasting experimental confirmation is a better ordering than the generator's own.

Which sets up the sharpest methods paper of the day. A group at Tianjin asked whether a language model can audit a black-box clinical prediction model through query access alone — no weights, no training data, just predictions. From the International Stroke Trial they trained thirty-two models across four families — logistic regression, explainable boosting machines, gradient-boosted trees and neural networks — and planted a known flaw in twenty-four of them: an under-predicted subgroup, a reversed risk factor, or an implausible step. GPT-6 Astra audited each one three times, blind, with the judging rules committed in advance and applied by program.

Three results. First, faithfulness: its surrogate models, with a median of a hundred and twenty-eight terms, reproduced the audited models' log-odds with a mean R-squared of nought point nine seven six — better than an analyst's additive surrogate. Second, detection: it found the planted flaw in sixty-nine of seventy-two runs, ninety-six percent, and in sixty-eight of those it listed the flaw first. The one miss was the smallest planted reversal. Third — and this is the selection problem again — precision. Of one thousand and eighty-nine checkable reports, sixty percent failed the pre-specified check against the model's average behaviour. Most of those were not inventions; they were small, real, local steps reported with the size the model had measured. But an auditor that returns a median of ten problems per run, and lists problems for almost every clean model too, has handed you a sorting job.

The comparator deserves the same scrutiny. A purpose-written audit script found all twenty-four planted flaws and reported nothing that failed the check — but it was written by people who knew which three kinds of flaw to look for and was handed the list of established risk factors the model had to derive itself. The authors say plainly that the prompt also named the three flaw types, so this study does not establish what a model finds when nobody tells it what to look for. And on the discovery promise — reading new knowledge out of a model — about one in nine of the relationships it proposed held up in later-randomized patients, roughly twice the rate of random pairs and below a simple regression scan's short list. Much of what a flexible model contains beyond the established factors is noise, and a faithful reader faithfully reports the noise.

The practical shape they propose is right: an auditor that proposes, with a program checking every proposal against the model by further queries, before a person reads any of it. The claims are checkable without any outcome data, which is the property that makes this deployable.

Related, from Hong Kong University of Science and Technology: can a multimodal model notice, unprompted, that a figure contradicts the text? They took thirty-one full manuscripts, each carrying a reviewer-documented figure-text inconsistency, and had six multimodal models review the whole PDF under a neutral prompt, with no hint that anything was wrong. Of fourteen hundred and forty-five open-ended reviews, sixty-one percent mentioned the flawed figure — but a grading rubric accepted only three point three percent as actually diagnosing the contradiction, and a blind audit judged that rubric lenient rather than strict.

The informative manipulation is what fixes it. Simply naming the figure to check — without saying it's flawed — raised accepted diagnoses from four point three percent to twenty percent. A generic instruction to check consistency did nothing. Coarse visual cues did nothing. And a baseline that let the model pick which figures to check, ranked by centrality, stayed at under five percent. So nearly all of the difficulty is in deciding what to verify, not in verifying it. If you have been reading the figure-comprehension benchmark numbers as evidence that models can review papers, this is the correction: those benchmarks hand the model the figure and the claim, which is the easy half.

Two shorter items to finish. A medRxiv study from a large urban academic children's hospital compared an LLM-based diagnostic agent against four attending pediatric emergency physicians on a hundred real cases, stratified by age, all admitted to the ward or to the operating room. At the initial physician-evaluation touchpoint, both the agent and the physicians produced a high-scoring differential in ninety-five percent of cases — difference zero, with a confidence interval of plus or minus six points — and the correct diagnosis was listed first about half the time for both. No difference at any of the four touchpoints. Two details are worth more than the headline. Adding a generated clinical abstract to the agent's input did not detectably improve accuracy. And in masked review, two physicians correctly identified which differentials came from the machine ninety-two percent of the time — they found them more verbose, more esoteric and more comprehensive. Parity on a Bond-score rubric, in other words, alongside an output that no clinician would mistake for a colleague's. Forty-one percent of these children had a complex chronic condition, so this is not a simple case mix, but it is one hospital, retrospective, and restricted to children sick enough to be admitted.

And to close, something cheerfully small. A group at Virginia built an instrument to record pupariation timing in Drosophila — a single-board Linux computer and a day-night camera, forty-five to seventy-five dollars in parts, sitting in an incubator and photographing fly vials every ten minutes. The twist is that a frontier coding agent runs its harness on the board itself: it helps with capture, writes code to fix errors, and checks the experiment daily, pinging the researcher's phone if recording stops.

The design lesson came from a failure. Capture had originally been a loop that pulled frames through the live-stream service; stopping that service during unrelated dashboard work silently killed acquisition, and fifty-one hours of data were lost before anyone noticed. So they made capture a separately supervised deterministic service that never depends on the agent, added a stall check, and put the heartbeat monitor outside the box. In a twenty-six-day run, the agent was logged out entirely for eleven of those days and capture carried on. The generalizable rule, and it applies well beyond fly incubators: do not let the agent be the thing that watches whether the system is alive, because the agent is one of the things that can die. Run a dumb parallel safeguard whose silence is itself the alarm.
