We open with the most mechanistically interesting pharmacology of the week, a drug-challenge imaging study in Neuropsychopharmacology on amisulpride and social reward. Amisulpride is a dopamine D2/D3 antagonist, but the twist that makes it a useful probe is that at low doses — here a single hundred-milligram dose — it's thought to preferentially block presynaptic autoreceptors and therefore, paradoxically, to increase dopamine release rather than suppress it. They ran it double-blind and placebo-controlled in a hundred and fifteen people, fifty-eight with major depression and fifty-seven healthy controls, while scanning them on a social incentive delay task — the reward on offer wasn't money, it was a smiling face at varying intensities, so this is anticipation of social rather than monetary reward, which is the undertested half of the reward literature. The result that survived correction was clean and specific: amisulpride raised activation in the left ventral tegmental area during reward anticipation, across both groups. What it did not do is move the ventral striatum, and it produced no behavioral effect on reaction times. So low-dose dopamine enhancement engaged the midbrain source but not the striatal target — a regional dissociation that contrasts with older monetary-reward and pramipexole work. The wrinkle worth sitting with is in the depressed group: the more anhedonic the patient, the higher their VTA, putamen, and insula activation under amisulpride — the opposite of the naive "anhedonia equals hypoactivation, drug restores it" story. The authors read that as compensatory or inefficient recruitment rather than improvement, which is really the wanting-versus-liking distinction wearing a BOLD signal. The sample was only mildly depressed, so effect sizes were likely attenuated, and the honest read is that this is a mechanistic probe, not a therapeutic signal — but it's a careful one, and it argues that where in the dopamine circuit you look, and whether the reward is social or financial, both matter.

Staying with depression but moving from circuits to causal genetics, there's a nice methods-forward paper in Molecular Psychiatry asking which depressive symptoms higher body weight actually causes. The problem they're solving is that BMI is influenced by hundreds of variants acting through very different pathways — some metabolic, some socioeconomic — so a standard Mendelian randomization estimate blends genuine biology with pleiotropic confounding. Their fix is to first cluster the BMI genetic instruments by how they associate across four hundred and seven traits in the UK Biobank, and then run the causal analysis cluster by cluster, symptom by symptom. And one symptom stands out: appetite change is the only depressive symptom with a robust causal effect from BMI that stays homogeneous across every cluster, biological and socioeconomic alike — effect sizes around zero-point-two to zero-point-three — with a large reverse effect too, appetite back onto BMI, so it's genuinely bidirectional. Depressed mood itself, psychomotor symptoms, suicidal ideation: minimal or absent. Fatigue, anhedonia, sleep: present but heterogeneous across clusters, which is the fingerprint of confounding rather than a clean causal path. The take-home is that "obesity causes depression" is too coarse — what higher weight causally drives is largely the appetite-metabolic arm of the syndrome, which is exactly the arm a GLP-1 agonist would be expected to touch, and a reminder that the socioeconomic genetics inflate the broader estimates. The obvious caveat is that the appetite item they used doesn't distinguish eating more from eating less, so it's a blunt readout of a specific pathway — but the modular, symptom-specific framing is the right one.

Metabolism and immunity carry us into a perspective piece, also in Neuropsychopharmacology, on inflammaging and late-life depression. This is an argument rather than a dataset, so take it as a framework. The claim is that depression in older adults should be modeled as a brain-body systems disorder in which age-related chronic low-grade inflammation and immunometabolic crosstalk don't just correlate with depression but moderate its trajectory. The proposed loop is self-reinforcing: inflammaging sits on top of insulin resistance and mitochondrial dysfunction, those amplify oxidative stress and central immune signaling, and that progressively degrades neurotransmission and brain bioenergetics to erode mood, cognition, and stress resilience. The reason it's worth flagging is the precision-psychiatry turn — the pitch is to use immunometabolic state, inflammation, mitochondrial function, insulin signaling, as the personalization anchors for who gets which intervention, rather than defaulting to monoamines. It's agenda-setting and light on specific targets, but it's a coherent reframing of late-life depression as an aging phenotype you could in principle intervene on upstream.

The metabolomics thread continues, and moves to the very start of life, with a Molecular Psychiatry study out of the NYU CHES birth cohort linking maternal pregnancy metabolites to child neurodevelopment. They measured a targeted panel of a hundred and eighty-eight metabolites — acylcarnitines, amino acids, phospholipids, sphingomyelins — in maternal urine across three points in pregnancy, in just over a thousand mother-child pairs, and assessed the children's emotional and behavioral problems and autism-related traits around age two. The most robust signal, one that survived stringent correction, was that a specific lysophosphatidylcholine measured in late pregnancy was associated with lower autism and behavior scores — protective — and only in boys. Secondary and softer: a higher kynurenine-to-tryptophan ratio early in pregnancy tracked with more autism traits, consistent with the inflammation-and-tryptophan story, and once diet was accounted for, higher taurine tracked with more behavior problems in girls. So the pathways implicated are lipid transport, mitochondrial energy metabolism, and immune-inflammatory signaling, with a strong and consistent sex specificity. It's associational and it's a single cohort with toddler-age outcomes, so this is a hypothesis-seeding map of prenatal metabolic windows, not a screening tool — but the sex-by-timing structure is the kind of detail that should discipline how these studies get designed next.

That pairs naturally with a neurodevelopmental biomarker paper in Biological Psychiatry, on whether you can read ADHD risk out of a newborn's blood. Working in the large Norwegian mother-and-child cohort, the authors report a nineteen-microRNA signature in cord blood that predicts later ADHD diagnosis, and the functional enrichment lands on biologically plausible ground — dopaminergic signaling and circadian rhythm — with the signature also tracking symptom severity, so it's not purely categorical. Now, they quote an area under the curve of about zero-point-nine-six, and I'd wave a caution flag there: an AUC that high in a first-of-its-kind neonatal predictor almost always means the discrimination is optimistic and hasn't yet been through the kind of external, independent validation that survives contact with a new cohort. So the honest framing is that this is the first demonstration that neonatal microRNAs carry ADHD-relevant signal at all, which is genuinely notable, but the headline number is a ceiling to be tested, not a clinical claim. Worth watching whether it replicates.

From newborns to the school-age window, a Biological Psychiatry paper uses the ABCD study to ask how early adversity gets into memory. In thirty-four hundred–odd youth followed from ages nine to ten, they combined latent change score modeling of episodic memory over two years with connectome-based prediction from resting-state fMRI. The central finding is a mediation: early-life stress predicted less improvement in episodic memory over that window, and stress-related psychopathology — the symptoms — statistically carried that effect, sitting on the path between adversity and blunted memory development. On the brain side, visual cortex connectivity predicted less memory improvement while cerebellar and subcortical connectivity predicted more, giving distinct network signatures for the two trajectories. It's observational, so the mediation is a model rather than proof, but it makes a useful point: the developmental cost of early stress on memory may run through the psychopathology it produces, which is at least a modifiable node.

Now the other end of the lifespan, and a moderator result in Neuropsychopharmacology that I liked for its clinical shape. This is a secondary analysis of the PACt-MD trial — two hundred sixty older adults, average age around seventy-two, with mild cognitive impairment or depression in remission, randomized to cognitive remediation combined with transcranial direct current stimulation, or to sham, and then followed for up to six years. The question was whether a baseline EEG measure predicts who benefits. That measure is prefrontal theta-gamma coupling — how much the amplitude of fast gamma oscillations is organized by the phase of slower theta, recorded during a working-memory task. Split the group at the median, and the result is stark: in the high-coupling group, active treatment meaningfully slowed long-term global cognitive decline versus sham, while the low-coupling group got no durable benefit, just a transient bump in executive function. The authors read theta-gamma coupling as a proxy for cognitive reserve — the neural flexibility to actually use the training and stimulation. Median splits and secondary analyses always warrant caution, but as a candidate stratifier for who should get combined neuromodulation, it's the right kind of biomarker question.

Several of today's items lean on individualized brain prediction, which makes a short commentary in Biological Psychiatry a fitting counterweight — this is the reason half of the show earning its name. It's a piece on selection bias in precision psychiatric neuroimaging. The argument is that the whole precision-imaging program — dense, multi-session scanning to build individualized functional maps — is quietly built on non-representative samples, because the very design selects for the people who can tolerate hours in a scanner, absorb the cost and inconvenience, and not drop out. Those people differ systematically from the patient population, on traits like anxiety among others, and conditioning your analysis on who stayed in the study is a textbook route to collider bias, Berkson's paradox in modern dress. The consequence is that individualized predictions calibrated on that subset don't transport to the clinic they're meant to serve. The remedy they gesture at is bias-aware, causal-inference-literate design rather than just bigger scanners. It's a healthy skepticism to carry into every "we can predict this from your connectome" claim — including a couple you heard earlier today.

And to close, the lightest item, but one squarely in this show's computational lane — a preprint on using agentic AI to translate bioinformatics code into Rust. The motivation is that Python and R dominate computational biology while being slow and memory-hungry, and rewriting scientific code by hand into a compiled, memory-safe language is exactly the kind of tedious, error-prone job nobody does. Their approach pairs static analysis with LLM coding agents — the static analysis scaffolds the structure and constrains the model, function by function, so the translation stays conservative and auditable rather than a free-form rewrite. The concrete result is on a real single-cell tool: after translation to Rust, its memory footprint dropped by roughly eighty-fold, build time by about ten-fold, and the key processing steps ran more than three times faster, with its old Unix dependencies gone. The candid parts are that this took eleven weeks of human-guided work across multiple frontier coding models, so it's not push-button, and it's explicitly not a benchmark of which model is best. But as a proof that agentic translation can retire the performance tax on legacy scientific Python without a from-scratch rewrite, it's a genuinely useful piece of infrastructure — and a reminder that a lot of the near-term value of these agents is unglamorous plumbing.
