## Script

Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Tuesday, July 28th. Nine papers today, and the shape of the day is glia and metabolism — three items on the cells around the neuron, then a turn through novel psychiatric targets, computational drug discovery, and neurodegeneration. Let's get into it.

We open with a microRNA that a group has turned from a disease marker into a candidate Alzheimer's therapy. The molecule is miR-223, a glia-enriched microRNA already known to run abnormal in the plasma and cerebrospinal fluid of Alzheimer's patients, and neuroprotective in stroke models. The question nobody had answered was whether restoring it does anything in amyloidosis. In the App knock-in amyloid mouse, both a one-time injection of a miR-223 mimic into the ventricles and long-term AAV overexpression rescued cognition, cut amyloid-beta plaque load, and restored synaptic markers. The mechanism is the interesting part. miR-223 pushes microglia to cluster around plaques and upregulate their phagocytic receptors — AXL, TREM2, CD11c — and when they depleted microglia pharmacologically, the plaque clearance vanished. So the benefit runs through microglial phagocytosis, not through neurons directly. In human iPSC-derived microglia, miR-223 directly targets a set of endo-lysosomal genes, including SPPL2A, which is itself an Alzheimer's risk gene. That's a satisfying convergence — a disease-associated microRNA acting on a bona fide risk gene to reprogram the exact cells that clear plaque. The caveats are the usual ones and one specific one. Amyloid clearance in mice has a long history of not translating to cognition in people. And a microRNA is a blunt instrument by design — it hits many targets at once, so "reprogram microglia toward phagocytosis" is a phenotype you'd want to characterize carefully before dialing it up chronically. But as a proof of concept for RNAi therapeutics in the Alzheimer's brain, with a clean microglial mechanism and a genetic anchor, this is a strong lead.

Staying with Alzheimer's and neuroinflammation, but going upstream — everybody invokes chronic neuroinflammation in Alzheimer's, and almost nobody can name what keeps it running. This next paper proposes a concrete culprit: persistent microbial material. The specific handle is poly-N-acetylglucosamine, PNAG, a surface polysaccharide made by a huge range of bacteria and fungi but absent from mammalian cells. Using a PNAG-specific antibody on human postmortem tissue, they found PNAG-bearing microbial material colocalized tightly with amyloid plaques across hippocampus, cortex, and substantia nigra in Alzheimer's brain — and almost none in controls. In fully human neuronal and three-dimensional brain models, purified PNAG and PNAG-bearing microbial vesicles activated TLR2-dependent inflammasome signaling and drove both amyloid-beta and phospho-tau accumulation. Then the therapeutic move: a vaccine against PNAG in APP/PS1 mice improved cognition, reduced glial activation and amyloid, shifted amyloid processing toward less aggregation-prone species, and did all that without disturbing the gut microbiome. Why this matters is that it turns the vague "microbes and Alzheimer's" literature into a specific, conserved, druggable antigen — and there's already an anti-PNAG antibody program in the infection space, so the translational path is unusually concrete. The honest caveat is that colocalization in human tissue is not causation, and vaccine wins in APP/PS1 mice are a crowded graveyard. But the arc — human-tissue colocalization, a mechanistic TLR2 route, and a vaccine that works in vivo — is coherent, and it dovetails with the microglial story we just heard.

One more on glia, this time in injury. The protein is transglutaminase 2, TG2, a multifunctional crosslinking enzyme, and the finding is that deleting it protects the brain from repetitive mild traumatic brain injury. In a weight-drop repeat-concussion model, TG2-knockout mice at 28 days showed markedly less white-matter and default-mode-network pathology on diffusion and resting-state MRI than wild-type. The mechanism came from multi-omics on cortical astrocytes: after injury, wild-type astrocytes clamp down metabolically — a kind of metabolic restriction — and losing TG2 lifts that brake, de-repressing gene networks for glutamate recycling and lipid metabolism, restoring the astrocytes' ability to fuel neurons. So the framing is that chronic concussion pathology is partly an astrocyte-metabolism problem, and TG2 is the transcriptional gatekeeper holding astrocytes in an unhelpful state. That's a useful mechanistic handle, and TG2 is genuinely druggable — CNS-penetrant inhibitors exist. The limitation is that this is a whole-body knockout, so it's neither astrocyte-specific nor a timed post-injury intervention, which is the therapeutically relevant question. But "restore metabolic versatility to reactive astrocytes" is a clean hypothesis to test with an inhibitor.

Now to psychiatry and a genuinely new target. This one nominates the volume-regulated anion channel, VRAC, as an antidepressant target, in zebrafish. Disrupting VRAC — either pharmacologically or by knocking down lrrc8aa, the obligatory pore-forming subunit — produced anxiety- and depression-like behavior in larvae and shifted expression of affective-disorder genes. Going the other direction, activating VRAC with zinc pyrithione, a VRAC opener, reversed those behaviors; and in adult fish under chronic unpredictable stress it produced antidepressant and anxiolytic effects on par with imipramine, and normalized the elevated monoamine oxidase expression the stress had induced. The appeal here is that this is a non-monoaminergic target — an ion channel tied to cell-volume regulation and cellular stress — with a bidirectional loss-and-gain-of-function case made in a single paper. The reasons for restraint: it's zebrafish, zinc pyrithione is a promiscuous tool compound, so I wouldn't over-read "comparable to imipramine," and VRAC is expressed body-wide, which makes central selectivity the hard problem. But as a fresh target nomination, this is exactly the kind of mechanism the mood-disorder field is short on.

Staying in the prefrontal cortex and mood, with receptor pharmacology. Deleting cannabinoid receptor 1, CB1, from medial prefrontal neurons — and specifically from the GABAergic interneurons where it's concentrated — reshapes emotional behavior in a way that depends on sex and on which cells you hit. Pan-neuronal CB1 deletion was anxiolytic in females; GABAergic-specific deletion was anxiolytic in males, and that tracked with increased interneuron activity on fiber-photometry calcium imaging. Fear conditioning split the opposite way — pan-neuronal deletion mattered in males, while interneuron-specific deletion heightened freezing in females. CB1 and mood disorders both show strong sex differences, and what this dissects is the interneuron contribution to prefrontal excitation-inhibition balance directly. The practical takeaway, as endocannabinoid-targeting drugs keep circling psychiatry, is a reminder that a CB1 drug's emotional effect can invert by sex and by which cell population it actually reaches.

To computational drug discovery. A group extended their virtual-screening pipeline, ProDock, into what they call a rank-resolved, multi-engine workflow, and the premise is one worth stating plainly: trusting a single docking score or a single top pose is how you generate false positives. So they combine global blind docking with DiffDock, a diffusion-based docking model, and local docking with GNINA, which scores poses with a convolutional network — and then layer on interpretable pose descriptors: binding-site occupancy, ligand localization, interaction-fingerprint similarity, clash counts. Optuna, a hyperparameter optimizer, learns the re-ranking thresholds. On DUD-Z, a standard decoy benchmark across 43 targets, the CNN-based enrichment improved — precision-recall enrichment rose from about 0.20 to about 0.29 — and the re-ranked poses were more native-like. Now, the discussion is refreshingly honest, and it's where the real lesson is. The gains were not uniform across targets. The interaction-similarity metric is anchored to each target's own co-crystal ligand, which means it's partly circular and may not transfer to novel chemotypes or to targets without a solved structure. And the held-out split is over compounds within each target, not across proteins, so this speaks to unseen ligands on known targets, not to new targets. Read that way, this is a sensible engineering consolidation — fuse complementary engines, report per-target instead of chasing one universal threshold, be transparent about the negatives — more than a breakthrough. For anyone building screening pipelines, that discipline is the takeaway.

Still computational, and squarely at the intersection this show cares about. The framework is called scGENet, and it tackles a real pain point with single-cell foundation models: the embeddings are powerful but opaque. scGENet turns those embeddings into interpretable, context-specific gene interaction networks. They fine-tuned a single-cell foundation model — scGPT — on brain transcriptomes, and the networks from the fine-tuned brain model showed the best agreement with curated neuronal pathways, with Parkinson's genetic risk loci, and with independent patient signatures. Then they applied it to iPSC-derived Parkinson's midbrain organoids and pulled out modules for neuronal differentiation and synaptic signaling, and — combined with single-nucleus sequencing — a conserved neurogenic program that's disrupted across both genetic and idiopathic Parkinson's, including reduced dopaminergic neurons and a dopaminergic subtype co-expressing SNCA and VGLUT2. The contribution isn't a new disease gene; it's a route from a foundation model's latent space to a network a biologist can actually read and cross-check against known disease biology. And benchmarking that interpretability against curated pathways and risk loci is the right way to earn trust in these models rather than asking for it.

Two neurodegeneration items to close, and the first is a caution aimed at a drug strategy. In Drosophila, knocking down the fly huntingtin gene specifically in the eight lateral-ventral neurons — the fly's core circadian pacemaker cluster — weakened free-running rhythms and substantially increased sleep. Restricting the knockdown to adults reproduced the sleep phenotype, so this isn't a developmental artifact. At the cellular level, losing huntingtin disrupted dense-core vesicle trafficking along the axons — fewer motile vesicles, altered velocity, and loss of the normal time-of-day rhythm in that trafficking — and lowered firing rate without touching resting membrane potential. The point is that this is about wild-type huntingtin's normal day job — neuropeptide trafficking, excitability, circadian output — not the mutant protein's toxic gain of function. And that's the direct relevance to therapy: the huntingtin-lowering approaches now in trials, the antisense and RNAi strategies, mostly knock down both the mutant and the normal allele. This work flags the cell-autonomous functions you'd be giving up, with circadian and sleep circuitry sitting right at the top of that list. A useful caution as the field pushes non-allele-selective lowering.

And to finish, something small and clean. Genipin is a bioactive iridoid from gardenia fruit, and it's being tested in a Drosophila Parkinson's model where pan-neuronal alpha-synuclein expression causes trouble. Earlier work had shown genipin rescues motility and survival in these flies. Here the group shows alpha-synuclein also causes sleep deficits, and that genipin treatment restored total sleep and reconsolidated the fragmented nighttime sleep. It's modest and incremental — a fly model, a natural product with a broad mechanistic profile — but it's a tidy reminder of two things: that sleep is a quantifiable, tractable readout of alpha-synuclein toxicity, and that a non-motor Parkinson's phenotype can respond to the same intervention that rescues the motor one. A good note to end on.
