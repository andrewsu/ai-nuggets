# Receptor & Reason — 2026-08-01

## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. It's Friday, August first, and today we've got a reward-circuit story that pushes the cerebellum somewhere the textbooks don't usually put it, a run of receptor pharmacology from biased agonism to a proteome-wide druggability map, a reality check on what large language models actually know about enzymes, and a couple of clever pharmacological levers for neurodegeneration. Let's get into it.

The lead is a preprint out of the NINDS intramural program on cerebellar encoding of reward, and it's the most interesting version of that argument I've seen. We've known for a few years that cerebellar granule cells and climbing fibers carry reward-related signals, not just motor commands. The perennial objection is that reward and movement are hopelessly entangled — a mouse that gets water also licks, chews, and swallows, so any "reward" signal could just be dressed-up motor activity. This group cut that knot cleanly. They trained head-fixed mice to push a handle for a reward that is delivered straight into the brain — optogenetic stimulation of dopamine neurons, either at the medial forebrain bundle or in the VTA — so there's no consummatory movement to confound the signal. Then they watched granule cells and climbing fibers with two-photon imaging while the animals worked for that dopamine.

Two things stood out. First, granule cells predictively encoded the coming dopamine reward with sustained ramping activity that switched off the moment the reward arrived — and crucially, that ramp stretched in time to match the delay. Train the animal on a one-second delay and the ramp fills one second; retrain on two seconds and the same population rescales to fill two. So the granule cells aren't just saying "reward is coming," they're timing it, linking the action to when the payoff is expected. Second, climbing fibers did something different — they fired just after dopamine was delivered, which looks much more like an instructive teaching signal than a prediction. And this wasn't a pale imitation of a natural reward: when they trained mice on both dopamine and water, the dopamine reward encoding matched or exceeded the water encoding, and a lot of individual neurons generalized across both.

Then they closed the causal loop in both directions. Chronically inhibiting granule cells during training disrupted the animals' ability to learn the self-stimulation task, and the deficit persisted into a laser-off washout, so it wasn't just acute motor impairment. And going the other way, using climbing-fiber stimulation itself as the "reward" drove moderate operant learning in naive mice — weaker than real dopamine or water, but real. The takeaway is that both cerebellar input streams carry genuine reward information, one predictive and one instructive, and that the cerebellum is more deeply wired into brain-wide reward prediction than the motor-control framing allows. For anyone thinking about the cerebellum's role in motivation, addiction, or the reward blunting you see in depression, that's a meaningful reframing of where the relevant computation lives.

Staying with dopamine and reward, there's a fiber-photometry study in rats looking at nucleus accumbens core dopamine during both positive and negative reinforcement. The group used the GRAB dopamine sensor and trained rats to lever-press for three different things — access to a same-sex peer, palatable food, and shock avoidance or escape. During the appetitive tasks they saw the now-familiar pattern: a phasic dopamine bump right after the cue that signals reward availability, and a slower ramp building up before the press itself. The interesting wrinkle is context sensitivity. Social reward dopamine was modestly larger when all three reinforcers were available in a session, but food dopamine was about twice as large when food was the only game in town. So the accumbens dopamine signal isn't reporting a fixed reward value — it's scaled by what else is on offer, which is the kind of relative, opportunity-cost-aware coding that matters a lot when you try to map these signals onto choice. It's more of a careful replication-and-extension than a bombshell, but it's a clean demonstration that you can't read accumbens dopamine as an absolute value signal.

From reward learning to the hard end of it: a preprint characterizing what happens behaviorally after chronic fentanyl in female mice. The motivation here is that the synthetic-opioid epidemic runs on fentanyl, women carry distinct vulnerabilities to opioid use disorder — including greater withdrawal severity and relapse risk — and yet the long-tail behavioral picture during abstinence is thinly characterized, especially in females. They used an oral fentanyl drinking model, a modified drinking-in-the-dark paradigm with four-hour daily access, then tracked the animals across five weeks of exposure and a full month of abstinence. Naloxone precipitated clear somatic withdrawal, confirming real dependence. The behavioral course was dynamic rather than static: thermal hyperalgesia showed up transiently during the middle weeks of drinking and then resolved, and affective and exploratory measures shifted across the abstinence month rather than settling immediately. The value here isn't a single mechanism — it's a longitudinal, female-specific behavioral map of protracted abstinence that gives you the time windows where relapse-relevant states actually emerge. If you're designing a study to intervene during abstinence, knowing that the phenotype moves week to week is the difference between measuring something and measuring nothing.

Now let's pivot from circuits to receptors, because there's a nice cluster of GPCR pharmacology this week. First, a medicinal-chemistry story on biased agonists for GPR183 — that's the oxysterol receptor, sometimes called EBI2, that senses 7,25-dihydroxycholesterol and helps position immune cells in lymphoid tissue. It's an appealing target for inflammation, and it signals both through Gi and through beta-arrestin. Starting from the scaffold of a known antagonist and a prior virtual screen, this group optimized their way to full agonists with low-nanomolar potency and complete bias for Gi signaling over arrestin — the lead compound, TUG-2604, matches the natural oxysterol for potency and efficacy. The result that makes it worth flagging: despite being a full Gi agonist, the compound did not itself drive human dendritic cell migration, and it actually blocked the migration the natural oxysterol induces. So you get a probe that pulls apart the G-protein arm from the functional output of chemotaxis — exactly the kind of tool you want to test whether biased signaling can keep a therapeutic effect while dropping an unwanted one.

Right alongside that, there's a chemical-biology preprint on how a tiny post-translational modification can steer receptor subtype selectivity without ever touching the receptor. The modification is pyroglutamylation — a cyclized glutamine at the N-terminus of a neuropeptide, very common in bioactive peptides. Working in a sea-slug PRXamide system with two related receptors, they found the same peptide had opposite effects depending on that one modification: pyroglutamylation suppressed activation of one receptor while enhancing activation of the other. And here's the mechanism — the modified end stays solvent-exposed and never directly contacts the receptor. Instead it reshapes the peptide's conformational ensemble, which redistributes the contacts the rest of the peptide makes. They then showed the same logic carries over to human neuromedin U receptors, where the pyroglutamylated form biases subtype preference. Whether a given receptor is turned up or down comes down to how tight its binding pocket is — a loose pocket tolerates the rigid modified peptide, a compact one prefers the unmodified version. It's a clean example of distal, allosteric control of selectivity encoded in the ligand rather than the target, and it's a design principle worth keeping in mind for peptide therapeutics.

The third receptor item is a deep-mutational-scanning study of rhodopsin — the visual GPCR — that's really about biophysical pleiotropy, the fact that one mutation can move several protein properties at once. They built a cell-based assay that reads out three phenotypes simultaneously: basal activity, ligand-dependent signaling, and cellular receptor abundance. Then they scanned roughly two thousand single-residue substitutions across the relevant domains, giving on the order of six thousand phenotype measurements. Mapping those onto structure, they found pleiotropic effects that act asymmetrically and impose joint constraints — the retinal binding pocket and the G-protein interface are where mutations are least tolerated precisely because a change there tends to wreck more than one property at a time. For a GPCR audience the appeal is twofold: it's a template for turning variant interpretation into a multi-phenotype measurement instead of a single readout, and it's a reminder that when you engineer or drug these receptors, you're rarely moving one dial in isolation.

That brings us to the computational stretch, and the first item is a genuinely proteome-scale drug-discovery tool. Most human proteins still have no chemical probe and no drug — the ligandable fraction of the proteome is small and stubbornly hard to expand. Activity-based protein profiling has been the experimental workhorse for finding covalently ligandable sites, especially reactive cysteines, but it's limited by probe chemistry, protein abundance, and the fact that different studies disagree about which sites are ligandable. This preprint introduces a model called AiPP — AI protein profiling — that's sequence-based and built on the ESMC protein language model, so it doesn't need a structure. Its main job is predicting covalently ligandable cysteines, but it carries seven additional task heads that annotate reversible binding residues, disordered recognition motifs, and cysteine reactivity and functional context. The clever engineering piece is a clustering step they call LatentLift, which uses similarities in the model's latent space to reconcile the conflicting experimental labels that have hobbled previous models — that label noise is a big reason earlier predictors underperformed. Applied across the human proteome, it produces a cysteine-directed ligandability atlas that reaches proteins that experimental profiling can't easily see, low-abundance ones included. For target identification and covalent-drug campaigns, a sequence-only druggability map is a genuinely useful thing to have on the bench.

The second computational item is a nice piece of humility about what large language models actually know, using enzyme classification as the test bed. There's a known anomaly: general-purpose LLMs get the coarse, first-level enzyme commission number right most of the time, but ask them for the complete four-level EC number and accuracy collapses toward zero, while specialized tools stay usable. This paper introduces EC-Reason-Bench, a training-free diagnostic that decomposes the failure into four separable levers — output structure, external knowledge, reasoning structure, and reasoning robustness — and tests each one at inference time against a zero-shot baseline that reproduces the near-zero result. The headline finding is that knowledge has to come before reasoning: closed-book, the models are uniformly bad, and the biggest recoverable gains come from feeding them the right external knowledge rather than from cleverer prompting or more reasoning steps. And a lot of that lost performance is recoverable without updating a single weight. The lesson generalizes well beyond enzymes — for any knowledge-intensive biological classification you hand to an LLM, the bottleneck is usually grounding, not reasoning horsepower, and the fix is retrieval and structure, not a bigger model.

Last two, and both are pharmacological angles on neurodegeneration. The first goes after cellular senescence in Alzheimer's, but with a specific culprit: LINE-1 retrotransposons. Using transdifferentiated induced neurons that keep the donor's age-related molecular signatures, the group shows that LINE-1 activity drives neuronal senescence and the senescence-associated secretory phenotype — and that you can push back on it pharmacologically. Nucleoside reverse-transcriptase inhibitors — the same drug class used against HIV — or antisense oligonucleotides against LINE-1 reduced the senescence marker p16, quieted the secretory and interferon programs, and dampened the reactive astrogliosis that senescent neurons induce in their neighbors. Long-read single-cell sequencing pinned the effect to a specific subpopulation of neurons with high LINE-1 activity that's enriched in Alzheimer's tissue, and RNA-velocity analysis suggested LINE-1 activation comes before the canonical senescence markers, which argues it's causal rather than a bystander. The reason to care is the translational shortcut: reverse-transcriptase inhibitors are approved, brain-penetrant drugs, so this is a repurposing hypothesis you could actually test as a senotherapeutic rather than a molecule that has to be invented from scratch.

And to close, a small but genuinely clever pharmacology idea about the water channel aquaporin-4. In the brain, AQP4 sits mostly on astrocytes and is enriched at the endfeet wrapping blood vessels — that perivascular pool is the one that matters for fluid balance and glymphatic clearance of metabolites like amyloid-beta, and it's the target of the autoantibodies in neuromyelitis optica. It turns out the perivascular version is a slightly extended isoform, AQP4X, produced when about a fifth of ribosomes read straight through the normal stop codon. Because that stop codon is a UGA, the group asked whether a purine analog known to promote read-through of UGA, 2,6-diaminopurine, could dial the isoform up. It could — the drug boosted read-through and raised AQP4X in cultured cells, and a single intracranial injection nudged perivascular AQP4X up by about ten percent in vivo. The control that makes it convincing: in a mouse engineered to read through that stop codon completely on its own, the drug did nothing, so the effect really is working through read-through and not some side pathway. Ten percent from one injection is modest, and this is early, but it's a proof of concept that you can pharmacologically tune a specific, disease-relevant protein isoform by nudging the ribosome — a nice reminder that the druggable space includes translational events, not just the proteins themselves.
