## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. I'm your host, and this is your daily pass through CNS pharmacology, psychiatric and neurology drug discovery, and the computational machinery closing in on both. Eleven items today, and the first two are an argument. One says the future of AI for science is domain-specific infrastructure that enforces methodological rules. The other, published two days earlier, says the specialists are already obsolete and the generalist coding agents will eat them. Both are worth taking seriously, and I think the tension between them is the most useful thing you can carry out of this week.

The lead comes out of the Poldrack lab at Stanford, with collaborators across Georgia State, Texas, Singapore and Queensland, and it's called Brain Researcher. The framing is the part I want you to hold onto. An analysis is not a claim. An agent can run a pipeline, get a number, and declare success — and every failure mode we already know from human-executed science comes back, dressed in tool calls. Selective analysis. Premature declaration of completion. Optimizing a criterion that isn't the one your claim depends on. Neuroimaging is the ideal proving ground for this because the analytic flexibility is enormous and well documented — when seventy teams analyzed the same dataset, no two used the same workflow and their conclusions diverged.

So Brain Researcher is not an autonomous scientist. It's a harness that runs inside the researcher's own computational environment and forces the methodological commitments into the open. Before anything executes, the researcher writes a commitment card: the question, the admissible analyses, the required checks, the scope of any claim that could follow. It's sealed with a content hash, so a later change to the plan is detectable. Afterward a review layer writes a claim card, and every claim lands in one of six states — accepted, qualified, revised, blocked, rejected or deferred — under an explicit adjudication rule. Underneath sits a knowledge graph of about seven hundred forty-six thousand nodes, aligned to a shared vocabulary so provenance survives across sources.

The benchmark numbers are large. Across sixty tool-calling tasks and seven frontier models, first-action correct tool selection went from twenty-three percent without the harness to ninety-four percent with it, all seven models improving. Verified grounding of cited evidence went from under five percent to twenty-two percent — nearly fivefold, and still leaving most evidence rows failing, which the authors say plainly.

But the part I'd actually pay for is the three collaborator studies, and specifically the one that went wrong. A schizophrenia functional connectivity project brought three pre-specified hypotheses; the harness expanded them into a four-hundred-eighty-specification multiverse. The headline hypothesis — that between-domain connections show larger group differences than within-domain ones — looked supported in half the contrasts. That pooled number was hiding a complete split by estimator: one hundred percent favorable under Pearson and Spearman correlation, zero percent under partial correlation and mutual information. Not a robust effect; an estimator-regime-dependent one. And the way they found that is the governance lesson. A server fault dropped execution to a general-purpose coding agent, which scored any specification with a significant permutation p-value as favorable regardless of sign — inflating support for a directional hypothesis to near-universal. The automated review layer did not catch it. A human reading the code and the specification curve did. Two checks then went into the system permanently: a directionality test, and a warning whenever execution falls back to a generalist agent.

That is the honest version of this technology, and it's why I'd put this paper above the ten other agentic-science preprints that landed this week. The review layer missed the error. The record made the error findable, and turned a one-off correction into an enforced check. In the other two episodes the checks did fire — a thirty-six-specification multiverse rejected all five pre-specified connectivity-behavior associations and converted the null into a replication plan, and an underpowered cross-cultural meta-analysis with six to eight studies per subgroup got blocked from confirmatory status rather than written up.

Now the counterargument, from Pavlo Dral's group at Xiamen — a Perspective rather than a research paper, surveying every agentic system for computational chemistry through the eighth of August. The growth curve is startling: four systems in twenty twenty-four, twelve in twenty twenty-five, thirty-three in the first seven months of this year. Forty-nine total, and since they read papers rather than running systems, they call it a lower bound.

Their argument is that this explosion is self-terminating. The same general-purpose coding agents that collapsed the barrier to building a specialized system also collapsed the reason to use one. Once your system reduces to a set of skills and tool endpoints, a generalist agent can call them — and the generalists are improving faster than any specialist. What makes this more than speculation is the confession. They released their own systems publicly to democratize simulation, and uptake was slower than expected — including inside their own group. Some of their people didn't realize their own lab's agent could fix scripts, and instead copy-paste into a chat window and then back to the cluster by hand. Others just use a generalist agent. Their conclusion: if the people closest to a free, capable system reach for something else, the binding constraint is not capability. Their phrasing is that everyone building agentic systems is digging their own grave and competing over who digs faster.

I don't think these two papers actually contradict each other, and the reconciliation is the interesting bit. Brain Researcher's value isn't tool routing — a generalist will get there. It's the commitment card, the claim states, the multiverse, the audit bundle. That's not a capability a general-purpose agent supplies by getting smarter, because it's a constraint, not a capability. The specialists that survive will be the ones encoding what you're not allowed to do.

Which brings me to a short third item that measures exactly the failure the first paper is built to prevent. It's a benchmark called PerturbTrace, out of Peking Union Medical College with collaborators at New South Wales and Tsinghua, and I'll flag that I could only read the abstract — the body hasn't rendered on the preprint server yet, so treat this as a pointer rather than a full read. The question is whether closed-loop discovery agents actually use feedback. They decompose each round-to-round transition into three links: does the feedback show up in the agent's stated rationale, does the stated strategy guide the next batch of perturbations, and does that batch beat random sampling. Four agents, seventeen screen-derived tasks. Every agent beat the strongest non-agent baseline on at least fifteen of seventeen. And yet, in controlled comparisons, true feedback gave no consistent advantage over random or absent feedback — and of five hundred seventy-six transitions, only forty-three, about seven and a half percent, completed the full feedback-to-state-to-action-to-outcome chain. Twenty-five of those forty-three were under random feedback. High final recall, essentially no demonstrable closed loop. If that holds up when the full text lands, it's the strongest version of a suspicion a lot of us have had.

Fourth, and staying in computational pharmacology but shifting from agents to inference. A preprint on evidence-constrained mechanistic synthesis, aimed at a stage of drug development that quantitative systems pharmacology usually skips over: you know enough biology to draw the mechanism and simulate perturbations, but nowhere near enough to justify one calibrated model. The insight is a taxonomy of what a finding is mathematically entitled to constrain. A statement that X affects Y buys you an edge. A directional experiment buys the sign. An ordinal result buys an inequality. A confidence interval buys a bound. Only a calibrated concentration-response experiment buys a magnitude or a functional shape. Each step is more informative and each demands stronger evidence, and the rule is to encode the weakest statement the finding actually supports.

The bookkeeping is where it gets clever. Evidence doesn't get converted into invented coefficients, and it doesn't get converted into a probability that a mechanism is true. It shifts the frequency with which a relation appears in a reproducible hypothesis ensemble. If compiled evidence supports an event at seventy percent allocation, about seventy percent of sampled hypotheses contain it, and the information added is audited against a declared reference frequency. Findings from one paper stay in a single dependency cluster, so a study reporting ten readouts doesn't count as ten replications, and a replication ceiling stops one very significant study from consuming a relation's whole information budget. The demonstration is in chronic spontaneous urticaria, deliberately chosen as an awkward evidence mixture — a hundred fourteen atomic findings from fifty-three sources compiled into eighteen executable constraints and a frozen four-thousand-ninety-six-hypothesis ensemble, of which thirteen findings are flagged as internally contradictory rather than forced into the model. Forty-three biologically permissible relations, fourteen with enough evidence to be executable, twenty-nine left as unresolved extensions — not declared absent. And crucially, changing the objective profile changed the selected control vector without touching the evidence ensemble. Preferences downstream of evidence. It's not a neuroscience paper, but if you build CNS systems models this is the cleanest statement I've seen of how to stop manufacturing precision.

Fifth, briefly, a benchmark from Wuppertal that's a useful corrective if you're picking molecular representations. Four pretrained molecular language models across six virtual libraries spanning drug discovery, organic materials and catalysis. Native embeddings from the language models varied wildly in downstream discovery performance, and plain molecular fingerprints were consistently strong and robust. Fine-tuning the encoders on structures from the target library fixed it, and several adapted encoders then topped the benchmark. The read is that the foundation-model premium in molecular representation is domain-conditional and not free — you get it after adaptation, and if you skip adaptation the fingerprint baseline may quietly beat you.

Sixth, and now into real pharmacology, and this is my favorite paper of the day on the wet side. Out this week in Neuropsychopharmacology from Stephanie Gantz's group: the first electrophysiological characterization of A eleven dopamine neurons. If that nucleus doesn't come to mind immediately, that's the point. A eleven sits in the dorsal posterior hypothalamus, holds fewer than two hundred dopamine neurons — the locus coeruleus, famously small, has over a thousand — and it is the only known source of dopamine to the spinal cord. Spinal dopamine gates nociception and itch, it's implicated in restless legs syndrome and in migraine through a projection to the trigeminocervical complex, and until now nobody had recorded from these cells.

What they found: small, morphologically simple neurons with input resistance around six hundred seventy megaohms, about seventy percent projecting to lumbosacral cord. Seventy-nine percent fire spontaneously at around five hertz with genuine pacemaker regularity — but unlike midbrain dopamine neurons they have no detectable hyperpolarization-activated current. The subthreshold drive is a persistent sodium leak of about sixty picoamps, and quiescent neurons instead have a potassium leak that outweighs it.

Then the opioid part, and here's the tension. Prior work had shown that systemic morphine raises lumbar spinal dopamine an hour later, and the proposed mechanism was disinhibition — opioids suppressing GABAergic input to A eleven. That is confirmed here: DAMGO, a selective mu-opioid receptor agonist, cut both frequency and amplitude of spontaneous inhibitory currents, consistent with presynaptic mu receptors on GABA terminals. But DAMGO also acted directly on the dopamine neurons, activating potassium conductance, erasing the subthreshold inward drive, and in cell-attached recording slowing pacemaking outright. So the net direct effect of a mu agonist is inhibitory, not excitatory. The reconciliation the authors offer is kinetic: the direct potassium effect desensitized within a few minutes of continued agonist, while presynaptic mu receptors are known to resist acute desensitization. So in vivo, disinhibition may simply outlast direct inhibition. That's a satisfying resolution, and it's also a hypothesis — the desensitization asymmetry wasn't measured in the same preparation. One more detail worth filing: ninety-two percent of A eleven neurons from male mice were spontaneously active versus sixty-one percent from females, in a system where the clinical conditions are more prevalent and more severe in women.

Seventh, a circuit paper in Molecular Psychiatry on the zona incerta to lateral habenula projection in a PTSD model, and the reason it's here is a dissociation that behaves like a drug-development brief. The model is single prolonged stress plus shock, which produces the anxiety, depression and fear phenotypes together. They found the zona incerta's GABAergic input to lateral habenula is weakened in these animals — reduced inhibitory event frequency onto the recipient neurons — which fits the standard story of habenular hyperexcitability arising from lost inhibition. Chemogenetically re-activating those zona incerta neurons, during either acquisition or expression, rescued anxiety-like behavior and reduced conditioned fear and aversion. It did nothing at all for the depression-like measures. Same circuit, two symptom clusters, one response.

Downstream it splits further. The lateral habenula neurons receiving zona incerta input project to ventral tegmental area and to rostromedial tegmental nucleus, and those are largely separate populations — ten percent triple-labeled. Optogenetically driving the habenula-to-VTA terminals produced strong aversion and left anxiety untouched; driving the habenula-to-rostromedial-tegmentum terminals produced anxiety and only mild aversion. Now the control that makes me trust this more, not less: when they drove all excitatory habenula output to either target non-selectively, they got aversion but no anxiety at all. So the anxiety effect is specific to the zona-incerta-defined subpopulation, which is a strong claim about how finely you'd have to target. The authors are also appropriately suspicious of their own aversion result on the rostromedial branch — there's a known projection from that nucleus to VTA, so some of it may be spillover. All male animals; they say so.

Eighth, an antipsychotic trial-design finding out of Adrienne Lahti's group, also in Neuropsychopharmacology, in eighty-one medication-naive first-episode psychosis patients followed sixteen weeks with resting-state imaging. Group-based trajectory modeling on positive symptoms gave three groups. Half were fast responders — lowest baseline symptoms, most of the drop inside six weeks, flat thereafter. Thirty-one percent were delay responders, who got to the same endpoint but didn't stabilize until after twelve weeks. Nineteen percent were partial responders, highest at baseline, smallest reduction, most variable. The trial-design implication is direct: a standard six-to-eight-week acute antipsychotic trial would score a third of eventual responders as failures. On the imaging side, baseline executive control network connectivity did not predict response, but the change from baseline to week six did, at an r of about point three four — climbing in the delay responders, falling in the partial ones. Eighty-one patients is small, there's no placebo arm for ethical reasons, and pandemic attrition ate into it, but the trajectory result replicates prior work.

Ninth, a preclinical safety paper on microbubble-enhanced focused ultrasound for blood-brain barrier opening, which matters because the field is moving toward larger volumes, repeated sessions and bigger payloads — antibodies, genes, cells. The design combines MRI, histology, acoustic emission dosimetry and a motor task sensitive enough to read movement kinematics, in rats, longitudinally. Three findings. First, barrier opening happened at every dose tested, including the ones with no detectable adverse effect — so barrier opening is not a safety readout. Neither is astrocyte activation, which was comparable across three groups of which only one had microhemorrhage and behavioral deficits. Second, targeting is part of the dose. The same acoustic parameters aimed at cortex alone versus cortex plus striatum produced different microbubble activity and, behaviorally, deficits only when the task-relevant subcortical circuit was in the field. Third, and most useful, the recovery time courses come apart: gross performance deficits resolved in about a week, fine-grained changes in learned movement patterns persisted at least two weeks in a subset of animals, and microhemorrhage and edema on imaging persisted at least three weeks. A single acute imaging timepoint will therefore both miss transient functional harm and overstate persistent structural harm. If you're designing a delivery study, that dissociation is the finding.

Tenth, from the clinical pipeline. Psychiatric Times ran a substantive piece yesterday on mitragynine, the principal alkaloid of kratom, entering an NIH-led phase one trial for opioid use disorder under the HEAL initiative. The pharmacology is genuinely mixed: partial mu agonist, competitive antagonist at kappa and delta, plus adrenergic and serotonergic binding — which is either the reason it might work for withdrawal or the reason it will be hard to characterize. The trial is a double-blind, placebo-controlled single-ascending-dose study, roughly thirty-two healthy volunteers, four dose levels from twenty-five to a hundred milligrams of an oral formulation developed at NIH and the University of Florida, with four days of inpatient observation, ECG and corrected QT interval, pupillometry and sedation scales.

Two things make this worth the segment. The regulatory backdrop is odd: within weeks of the trial announcement, DEA moved to schedule the metabolite seven-hydroxymitragynine and three related compounds as Schedule One — in synthesized, concentrated form only, with thresholds set above what whole-leaf ingestion or this trial's dosing produces. And there's a factual correction worth knowing. NIH's release said the isolated compound had not been studied in humans. A Maastricht group had already published a placebo-controlled phase one, fifteen volunteers, doses from five up to forty milligrams. Mitragynine did not move most outcomes, but five milligrams increased subjective arousal, sustained-attention accuracy and motor inhibition, while forty milligrams produced amnesia ratings and mild psychopathology. That's the classic kratom "double action" claim — stimulant low, sedative high — with human data behind it, and a real prior for dose selection here.

Closing item, and this one is a marker rather than a result. Protagenic Therapeutics got written pre-IND feedback from FDA on PT double-zero-one-fourteen for generalized anxiety disorder: no clinical hold issues, no objection to the three-part phase one design or the dose-escalation and safety-governance plan, and manufacturing and nonclinical packages judged adequate to support a filing. IND before the end of the year, first patient in the first half of twenty twenty-seven. The reason I'm mentioning a pre-IND letter at all is the mechanism, which is unusual: it's a neuropeptide acting on the teneurin C-terminal associated peptide pathway — the peptide cleaved from the tail of the teneurin adhesion proteins, with a stress-axis modulatory role. The pitch is acting on the central stress response rather than on downstream symptom neurotransmission, and after two decades of GAD development that has mostly recycled serotonergic and GABAergic pharmacology, a genuinely new target class is worth a note. Caveats appropriate to the stage: the existing phase one single- and multiple-ascending-dose data were generated outside the United States, in a limited number of healthy volunteers, not under an IND. There is no efficacy signal here. It's an on-ramp.

That's eleven. The thread, if you want one: today's best papers were all about the difference between a result and a claim — a harness that refuses to promote an underpowered analysis, a benchmark showing agents that win without learning, an inference framework that declines to invent coefficients, a barrier-opening protocol where the obvious endpoint is not the safety endpoint, and a trial-trajectory paper saying a third of responders look like failures if you stop the clock too early.
