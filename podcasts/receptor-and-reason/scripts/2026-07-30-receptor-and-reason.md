# Receptor & Reason — 2026-07-30

## Script

This is Receptor and Reason for Thursday, July thirtieth — ten papers today. Quick note on what's not here. The two receptor-structure papers I most wanted to lead with, a graded-activation story on the dopamine D1 receptor and a subunit-selective AMPA modulator for schizophrenia, both posted in the last two days, and their full text hasn't rendered yet, so I'm deferring them until I can actually read the body rather than the abstract. What did render is a strong run on glia, neuroinflammation, and the computational edge of psychiatry. Let's start in the astrocyte.

We open at the Crick Institute, with what might be the cleanest astrocyte-as-first-responder story in Alzheimer's I've seen this year. The claim is that astrocyte dysfunction in Alzheimer's starts in the lysosome, before any plaque, and that a single lysosomal calcium channel — TRPML1 — is the initiating lesion. The technical move that earns the claim is imaging calcium inside the lysosome itself, in the living brain, by parking a calcium sensor on the lysosomal membrane in astrocytes only. Most calcium imaging reads the bulk cytosol; this reads the organelle, and that's what lets them place the lysosomal deficit upstream of the cytosolic one rather than inferring it. In three-month-old amyloid knock-in mice, before plaques, lysosomal calcium transients are essentially gone — amplitude down about three-fold, frequency down five-fold — tracking a fall in astrocytic TRPML1. And when they restore TRPML1 with an astrocyte-only virus starting at two months, eight-month astrocytes hold wild-type calcium instead of going hyperactive, and — the headline — plaque area in the treated region drops from around four percent of the tissue to two-tenths of one percent. The caveat is that this is overexpression, not restoration to normal levels; it pushes calcium above baseline even in healthy astrocytes, so some of the rescue may be a supraphysiological override rather than a pure correction, and the amyloid reduction is local to where the virus went. Still — a cell-autonomous, pre-plaque, druggable target is a big deal.

Staying with glia, and staying with the theme that the interesting biology is upstream, the second paper asks how brain-cell senescence spreads. Senescent cells secrete an inflammatory cocktail — the senescence-associated secretory phenotype — and the question here is directionality: who infects whom. Using conditioned-media transfer — take the media off senescent cells of one type, put it on healthy cells of another, and see whether they turn senescent — they build a full directional matrix across five human brain cell types. And it isn't symmetric. Astrocytes and microglia are the spreaders; they drive senescence in themselves, in each other, and in endothelial cells, while oligodendrocytes and neurons can send the signal but can't receive it. Then they triangulate the mediators with a cytokine panel, receptor inference, and, crucially, specific drugs — and find you can block astrocyte-to-microglia spread with a DPP-4 inhibitor, the same target as the diabetes gliptins like sitagliptin, or with a CXCR7 blocker. The catch worth flagging up front: this is all immortalized human cell lines, cancer-derived, not primary glia and not in vivo, and the senescence readout in the receiving cells is modest and rests on one contested marker. So treat the directional hierarchy as a hypothesis-generating map, not settled fact — but a druggable, cell-type-specific map of how senescence propagates in the brain is exactly the kind of thing worth chasing.

That inflammation thread runs straight into the third paper, which is the one most likely to make you look twice at a drug you already prescribe. It's a phenotypic screen of about a hundred and ninety approved drugs for blockers of the NLRP3 inflammasome, and two of the hits are antidepressants — fluoxetine and duloxetine. The assay is genuinely clever: a single reporter that separates the two steps of inflammasome biology — the priming step, the NF-kappa-B signal, and the assembly step, the speck — in one readout, and they require the assembly hits to leave the inflammasome proteins intact, so they're not fooling themselves by simply knocking a component down. Fluoxetine turns out to act at priming, physically reducing how much bacterial endotoxin binds the cell membrane. And then it gets strange in a way that should interest this audience: in a lethal endotoxin model the protection is sharply sex-dependent — fluoxetine rescues female mice almost completely but barely moves males, while other hits, like mefloquine, protect males and not females. No mechanism is offered for the sex split, and it's all prophylactic pretreatment against pure endotoxin, not treatment of an established infection. But the reframing stands: the SSRI in your clinic is also an innate-immune modulator, and that may be quietly relevant to the inflammation-in-depression story.

The fourth paper is the other screen of the day, and it goes after a clock parameter almost nobody drugs: amplitude. Period and phase get all the attention because they're easy to read out; amplitude — the robustness of the oscillation — is what damps in aging, in neurodegeneration, and in metabolic disease, and there's essentially no pharmacology for it. So they ran fifty-six hundred repurposing compounds through a clock-gene luciferase reporter and pulled out thirteen confirmed amplitude enhancers, eight of them hitting targets never linked to the clock before — including a JAK3 inhibitor, a PDE-1 inhibitor, and dantrolene, the ryanodine-receptor blocker. The methodological discipline I liked: they confirmed each hit with a structurally unrelated inhibitor of the same target, so it's the target and not a compound quirk, and they showed in the suprachiasmatic nucleus — the master clock — that the amplitude gain is cell-autonomous, not just neurons synchronizing better with each other. The honest limitation is that a few of the target attributions wobble — some gave dose-independent effects, which smells of off-target action — and it's all dishes and slices, no animal behavior yet. But amplitude as a druggable axis, with repurposable starting points, is a genuinely useful opening.

From the clock to the stress axis. This next one is a methods lesson wrapped around a psychiatry finding. Early-life stress in mice — maternal deprivation plus limited bedding — and the group-average comparison is basically null: stressed males look fine, stressed females show only anhedonia. Which is exactly how the field misses real effects, because averaging buries the roughly one-in-four animals that are resilient. So instead they phenotype each mouse across four orthogonal tasks and use unsupervised clustering to split them into resilient, anxious, and depressive, then map that onto locus coeruleus noradrenergic activation, resolved by subregion. And the payoff is genuinely uncomfortable: the exact same signature — reduced activation in the caudal-dorsal locus coeruleus — maps onto anxiety in males but resilience in females. The same noradrenergic marker means opposite things depending on sex. And the cluster proportions mirror the human pattern — males skewing anxious, females depressive, roughly the two-to-one female depression risk. It's a single activity snapshot, correlational, with no causal manipulation, and the proposed mechanism is speculative. But the message — that resilience is an active, sex-specific state rather than just the absence of pathology — is one worth carrying around.

Sixth, addiction, and a paper about what actually converts heavy drinking into compulsive drinking. The variable they isolate is unpredictability. Rats get ethanol either as continuous within-session access or as unpredictable intermittent access — short availability windows chopped into cue-signaled epochs of random length and spacing — across seven weeks that include vapor-induced dependence. Same baseline intake either way, but only the unpredictable-access rats develop durable, aversion-resistant drinking: they keep drinking ethanol laced with bitter quinine, when the continuous-access rats — who are statistically indistinguishable from controls — quit. The conceptual point they lean on is that ethanol clears too slowly to give you the fluctuating brain levels that supposedly drive intermittent-access effects for cocaine or opioids, so whatever unpredictability is doing here is psychological and cue-driven, not pharmacokinetic. The nice technical piece is a within-session blood-alcohol estimate built lick by lick, which lets them model, trial by trial, how much you drink on this cue given what happened on the last one. The real weakness, which they state, is that the sexes were pooled because the study was underpowered to separate them — a genuine gap. But "unpredictable, not just intermittent" is a clean and testable lever on compulsivity.

Now two papers on plasticity and memory, and the first reframes a disease deficit as a control-theory problem. In a Rett syndrome mouse — MeCP2-deficient — they track the same CA1 place cells for seven straight days, and the finding is that Rett memory failure isn't a failure to form place cells, it's a failure to keep them. The culprit is too much plasticity, mis-targeted — specifically behavioral-timescale synaptic plasticity, the rule where a single large calcium plateau can build a whole place field in one shot. In normal mice, a faint residual trace at yesterday's field location biases today's plateau to rebuild the field in the right place — a location-specific consolidation signal. In Rett, that selectivity collapses: the residual is comparable, but they can't convert it into in-field plasticity, so new fields form in the wrong places. There's about forty percent more plasticity overall, all of it noisier. And the elegant part is a minimal model that reproduces the entire phenotype by turning a single knob — raising non-specific spontaneous activity, which drowns out the residual signal. The causal driver — weakened dendritic inhibition — is inferred, not manipulated. But "too much, mis-aimed plasticity" is a far more actionable frame than "memory is broken."

The companion memory paper is about what sleep is doing when it consolidates an inference. Mice learn that A leads to B and B leads to C, never A to C, and then have to infer that A relates to C. During sleep ripples, the hippocampus is known to replay — but here they show that primary visual cortex generates the never-experienced pairing too, co-activating the A and C cues without the intermediary B, and it does so specifically for the reward-associated set. The clean part is the directionality analysis: they predict each region's ripple firing from the other region's specific content while controlling for overall firing rate, so it's information that's being passed, not generic co-activation — and hippocampal content predicts visual-cortex content about eighty milliseconds before the ripple, not the reverse. Hippocampus teaches; cortex learns. And across training days, the cortex's ripple-locked sequencing progressively decouples from the hippocampus — which is exactly the systems-consolidation signature you'd want to see. It's small-n and purely correlational — no causal ripple disruption — but it upgrades consolidation from "strengthen what you already have" to "the hippocampus teaches cortex to build a generative model it can eventually run on its own."

Ninth, precision neurostimulation, and a useful corrective to a heuristic a lot of you have heard. The standard target for depression TMS is the patch of left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex most anti-correlated with the subgenual cingulate. Using resting-state connectivity in nearly four hundred healthy brains from the Human Connectome Project, they parcellate that prefrontal region into functional subclusters and show the heuristic is too coarse: only one specific anterior-central subcluster actually maximizes the subgenual anti-correlation, and it's the one carrying the cognitive-control and negative-affect profile you'd want to hit — while a neighboring posterior cluster is positively coupled to the subgenual cingulate, the opposite of what you want. They turn it into a probabilistic target map that's robust to the arbitrary choice of how many clusters to cut. The obvious limitation, which they own, is that this is young, healthy, pristine data with no actual TMS and no clinical outcomes — so it's an inference resting on the older anti-correlation-predicts-response literature, not new outcome data. But it's the kind of connectivity-informed targeting refinement that individualized TMS keeps promising and rarely operationalizes this cleanly.

And to close, the lightest item but the one with the sharpest warning label — a preprint stress-testing EEG foundation models for clinical decoding. These are the big pretrained EEG models that everyone hopes will do for brain signals what large language models did for text, and the question is whether they learn real neurophysiology or just dataset-specific quirks. The answer, via two nice controls: not great. Under targeted negative controls — shuffled labels, corrupted inputs — the models often keep suspiciously high accuracy, which means they're leaning on spurious statistical regularities; and they degrade badly across datasets, which means they've partly learned the identity of the recording rig rather than the brain state. It's a familiar story from the rest of foundation-model land, but it's worth hearing specifically if you're about to trust one of these for a psychiatric or clinical readout: scale and pretraining don't buy you generalization for free, and if you're not running dataset-identity and negative-control checks, you don't actually know what your model learned.
