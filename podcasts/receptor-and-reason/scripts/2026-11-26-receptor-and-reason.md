## Script

Good morning. This is Receptor and Reason for Thursday, November twenty-sixth. Eleven items today, and the lead is a negative trial that I think is more useful than most positive ones.

A multisite team — UCLA coordinating, with Mass General and the Medical University of South Carolina — has posted a double-blind, sham-controlled pilot randomized trial of transcranial focused ultrasound targeting the right amygdala in treatment-resistant generalized anxiety disorder. Thirty-one adults, randomized one to one, four sessions over a month, followed for a month after. The primary endpoint was change in the Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale from baseline to the final visit. Active ultrasound dropped it by just over ten points. Sham dropped it by just under nine. The between-group difference was one and a half points in the wrong direction, with a confidence interval running from minus eight to plus five, and an effect size of about minus zero point one six. Responder analysis at twenty-five, thirty and thirty-five percent thresholds: no difference at any threshold. The imaging arm found nothing either — no perfusion difference, no BOLD difference during sonication, no resting-state connectivity difference.

I want to be careful about what this does and doesn't say, because regular listeners will remember we opened an episode in September on a first-in-human amygdala ultrasound study in depression that had genuinely convergent target-engagement evidence — perfusion changes confined to the targeted amygdala, sparing the adjacent hippocampal head. The honest reading here is not "amygdala ultrasound doesn't work." It's that this trial probably didn't deliver enough energy to the right place. The authors did post hoc subject-specific acoustic modeling in four of the UCLA participants, and the numbers are the most valuable thing in the paper: on average about thirty-eight percent of the suprathreshold beam landed inside the right amygdala, but that beam covered only about seven percent of amygdala volume. Seven percent. So even in the participants where targeting was nominally good, most of the structure never saw suprathreshold pressure. They ran at an intensity pinned to the FDA's diagnostic-ultrasound guidance, which is the ceiling essentially the whole neuromodulation field has been working under, and the authors say plainly that they think insufficient neuromodulatory engagement, not absence of a biological effect, is the likeliest explanation.

What makes the paper worth your time is the combination of that modeling with a serious blinding analysis. They asked participants at two timepoints which arm they thought they were in. The sham group showed a blinding index that was slightly negative and then zero — a successful sham, people in it tended to believe they were getting active treatment. The active group ran around point four seven, which is partial unblinding. Overall correct-guess rates were not different from chance. So the sham held up well enough, and both arms still improved by nine or ten HAM-A points. That is an enormous expectancy effect in a treatment-resistant population, and it's the number any future trial in this space has to design against. The practical lesson for the field: if your target-engagement metric is "the beam overlapped the structure," you are measuring the wrong thing. The denominator matters — what fraction of the structure got above threshold — and this is the first trial I've seen that reports it and then interprets its own null in light of it.

From a null to something much stranger. A group largely out of Erlangen has posted a preprint testing mitragynine — the main alkaloid in kratom — as a pharmacotherapy for alcohol use disorder in mice, and as a treatment for tinnitus in gerbils, in the same paper. The origin story is that a patient reported using kratom to self-manage both conditions. That's an unusual way to generate a drug-discovery hypothesis, and the authors are explicit that it's the point: instead of designing a compound from a receptor rationale, start from what patients have already found by trial and error.

The behavioral pharmacology is more thorough than that framing might suggest. Experimenter-administered mitragynine reduced established ongoing alcohol drinking in a social setting with continuous access, and in isolated housing, though less so. It did not work prophylactically during abstinence — and in the isolated-drinking condition it actually made the subsequent relapse worse, which they report straight. It did attenuate relapse to binge-like drinking under time-restricted access, and it reduced alcohol seeking during acquisition, during retrieval, and during re-establishment after extinction. During withdrawal it had antidepressant-like effects in forced swim and novelty-suppressed feeding, with no anxiolytic effect in open field or elevated plus maze.

Two things lift this above a standard rodent screen. First, the compliance paradigm. Low adherence is arguably the binding constraint on every approved alcohol pharmacotherapy, and they built a test for it: after a short forced-exposure training, alcohol-drinking mice learned to orally self-administer mitragynine, and that voluntary intake was followed by reduced alcohol drinking. They flag honestly that they can't yet separate a therapeutic effect from simple drug substitution — mitragynine and morphine cross-reinstate in rats — but as far as I know this is the first demonstration of rodents self-medicating a psychiatric symptom. Second, the pharmacology surprise. They labeled mitragynine with a fluorophore expecting to confirm its partial agonism at mu-opioid receptors on neuronal membranes. Most of the binding instead showed up in white matter, at myelin-containing fibers, with only about eight percent of labeled structures overlapping nuclei. That's a result that could be an artifact of a bulky fluorescent tag, and it needs orthogonal confirmation — but if it holds, the mechanistic story for kratom's alkaloid is not the one anyone has been telling. The tinnitus arm, incidentally, showed behavioral improvement including in early chronic tinnitus, with no protection of hearing thresholds or inner-hair-cell synapse counts, implying a central rather than cochlear effect.

Staying with alcohol and opioids, there's a nice piece in Neuropharmacology from the Camerino group on the Marchigian Sardinian alcohol-preferring rat — the msP line, selectively bred for high alcohol intake. They asked whether that bred-in alcohol vulnerability extends to opioids. Male msP rats have higher Oprm1 messenger RNA in the ventral tegmental area than Wistars; females don't. Across two independent cohorts, male msP rats self-administered significantly more heroin. Then the causal test: knocking Oprm1 down in the VTA with a short hairpin reduced heroin self-administration over time, with a larger effect in msP than in Wistars. I like this because the field has no selectively bred line for opioid preference, and this suggests one already exists under a different name — with a specific, manipulable molecular correlate rather than just a phenotype.

On the genetics-of-vulnerability theme at much larger scale, a consortium preprint analyzed eight hundred and ten genetically diverse heterogeneous stock rats through oxycodone self-administration under both short access and extended access, scoring four domains: escalation of intake, motivation on progressive ratio, tolerance to oxycodone analgesia, and withdrawal-induced hyperalgesia. Those get combined into an addiction index that sorts animals into resilient, mild, moderate and severe. The headline result is a dissociation. After extended access, females took more oxycodone and worked harder for it than males. But the pain-related measures tracked addiction-severity group far more than they tracked sex — and critically, the magnitude of the sex difference did not vary across severity groups. Estrous phase had no significant effect on motivation. The useful inference is that "sex differences in opioid addiction" is too coarse a statement: it holds robustly for consumption and motivation, and does not hold for the pain dimensions, which are instead organized by individual vulnerability. They also note meaningful between-cohort heterogeneity in the pain measures, which is a quiet warning about single-cohort studies in this area.

A circuit-level companion to that, from a group working on chronic intermittent alcohol vapor exposure in rats. They put excitatory or inhibitory DREADDs — designer receptors activated by an otherwise inert ligand, here deschloroclozapine — into infralimbic cortex neurons projecting specifically to nucleus accumbens shell, then tested alcohol self-administration and elevated plus maze during acute withdrawal. In alcohol-dependent males, inhibiting that pathway increased drinking, suggesting it normally constrains intake. But exciting it did not reduce drinking in those same dependent males — it increased intake in the non-dependent control males instead. And in females, neither manipulation changed drinking at all. Meanwhile both manipulations altered elevated-plus-maze behavior in females, and excitation reduced open-arm time in males. So the anxiety-like readout and the drinking readout come apart within the same projection. That's two uncomfortable results for circuit-based therapeutics: the pathway is not a dial you can turn up to get less drinking, and the female phenotype is being driven by circuitry nobody has identified yet.

Shifting to affective neuropharmacology, Progress in Neuro-Psychopharmacology has a paper on galanin in the locus coeruleus that does the full bidirectional set. Rats selectively bred for high anxiety-related behavior showed elevated galanin messenger RNA in locus coeruleus, paraventricular hypothalamus and central amygdala, but not dorsal raphe or dorsomedial hypothalamus. Then three chronic antidepressants with three different mechanisms — desipramine, paroxetine, and the monoamine oxidase inhibitor tranylcypromine — all reduced galanin expression selectively in locus coeruleus. That's the mirror image of the high-anxiety phenotype, from three directions. Functionally, galanin infused into the locus coeruleus pushed low-anxiety rats toward passive coping in forced swim, up to the level of high-anxiety animals, and a non-selective galanin receptor antagonist reduced immobility in the high-anxiety line — while a GalR3-selective antagonist did not. That last detail is the one that matters commercially: GalR3 antagonists have been the galanin subtype of choice for depression programs, and this says the effect here runs through a different receptor.

Two cell-biology items now. First, a preprint that solves a specific disagreement in the literature about dopamine neuron vulnerability in Parkinson's. In mice, the Anxa1-positive subset of substantia nigra dopamine neurons is the most vulnerable population in toxin and genetic models, projects to dorsolateral striatum, and shows movement-correlated activity — but no one had a clean human counterpart. This group transferred the mouse taxonomy onto human single-cell data with a hierarchical mixture-of-experts model and recovered a human Anxa1-associated population, which turned out to be the most depleted subtype in Parkinson's brains of any human subtype yet described. They then inferred its projection target with a ligand-receptor method validated against mouse tracing: caudal putamen. And caudal putamen is exactly where dopamine-transporter SPECT shows the earliest and most severe axon loss in patients. Three independent lines — cross-species transcriptomics, inferred connectivity, and clinical imaging — converging on one striatal field. They finish by nominating CACNA1G, a T-type calcium channel, as a neuroprotective target for that population. I'd treat the target nomination as a hypothesis generated from a drug atlas rather than a finding, but the convergence underneath it is real.

Second, a tidy piece of synaptic physiology from organotypic hippocampal slice. The textbook claim is that structural and functional plasticity are coupled — spines grow with potentiation, shrink with depression. That's solid for NMDA-receptor-dependent LTD and contested for the mGluR form. This group induced mGluR-LTD with DHPG while imaging spine morphology and calcium, and found the resolution: mGluR activation causes persistent spine shrinkage only when it coincides with elevated neuronal activity. They call it MIND structural LTD. It needs mGluR1 or 5 and new protein synthesis, it does not need NMDA receptors, and the coincidence requirement holds at single synapses — two-photon glutamate uncaging synchronized with mGluR activation shrinks that one spine, unsynchronized doesn't. So the conflicting reports in the literature were probably reporting different background activity states. That's a coincidence detector built out of mGluR signaling rather than NMDA receptors, which is a mechanistically different gate than the one we usually invoke.

For structural pharmacology: a cryo-EM structure of the somatostatin-2 receptor bound to consomatin pG1, a somatostatin mimic from the venom of the fish-hunting cone snail Conus geographus, at one point nine angstroms. The interest here is selectivity. Endogenous somatostatin hits all five subtypes and is unstable; octreotide and vapreotide were engineered toward stability and SSTR2, and the cone snail arrived at a convergent solution independently — compact cyclic fold, a D-tryptophan residue — with better subtype selectivity than the clinical analogs. The structure plus receptor chimeras localizes that to the extracellular loops. Swapping zebrafish extracellular loop two into the human receptor abolished activation by somatostatin, octreotide and pG1 alike, so that loop is required for function generally. But the reciprocal experiment separates them: putting human loop two into the zebrafish receptor partially restored pG1 responsiveness and did nothing for octreotide. Different ligands, different loop dependencies, on the same orthosteric site. They used that to design pG1 analogs with better potency and retained selectivity. Venom peptides as a source of subtype-selective GPCR tool compounds is not a new idea, but this is a clean worked example of going from structure to improved analog.

Two computational items to close. The first is the one I'd most want drug-discovery groups to read. A team from Queen Mary and Recursion asks a question that sounds pedantic and isn't: when a language model ranks a novel target-disease pair as promising, is it forecasting, or is it reading the answer? They reframe each target-disease hypothesis as a dated event — the pair's first clinical trial — and rebuild a biomedical knowledge graph as it stood at the end of each year, so they can rewind to any decision point. Then they score six open-weight models under three conditions: no retrieval, retrieval from the graph as it stood before the decision, and retrieval from today's graph.

The results are a clean indictment of how these systems are usually evaluated. Without any retrieval the models already rank well above chance — but about as well as a trivial baseline, namely counting how many trials that target has already had in the same therapeutic area. That baseline gets AUROC around point seven five. And the models' performance barely changes across their own training cutoffs, which means the parametric floor reflects generic plausibility, not memorized trial records. Adding correctly dated, leak-free evidence from before the decision adds essentially nothing after the cutoff — significant for none of the six models. But retrieving from today's graph lifts five of six clearly above the baseline. Why? Because for most positives and almost no negatives, the retrieved evidence paths contain the pair's own trial edge, registered after the decision was supposed to be made. The retriever is showing the model the answer and the benchmark is scoring it as foresight. One more detail worth sitting with: dated genetic association was at chance as a ranking signal, because by the decision point fewer than one percent of the positives had a genetic association with the disease at all. Genetic support predicts later success in the pipeline; it doesn't predict first trial entry. The prescription is simple and nobody is doing it — date your evidence as well as your labels.

And last, something lighter but genuinely novel in form. Randy Buckner's group, with collaborators in Oslo, Umeå and Boston, has released an open simulator of brain and cognitive aging that was built with an agentic AI in the loop. Each simulated world initializes a hundred and twenty thousand individual lives with baseline parameters — genetic risk, birth year — and then evolves them through hypothesized age-linked cascades: amyloid-dependent, cardiovascular, and so on. You can then run studies on that world: cross-sectional versus longitudinal designs, different participant selection strategies, clinical trial designs with power calculations, and see which data patterns each choice manufactures. The pitch is that the brain-cognition association literature is full of effects that are partly artifacts of design, and a simulator lets you see which ones.

The reason I'm flagging it in this show rather than filing it under methods is the process disclosure, which is unusually candid. The agent ran the simulator headlessly, drafted figures and text, and iterated — one figure went through more than thirty versions, with twelve substantial revisions directed by the human authors. By the late drafts, under twenty percent of the main text still matched any six-word string from the agent's original. And at each major revision, a separate agent instance that had not done the work recomputed every number cited in the changed passages from the saved data and checked each statement against it. That verification pattern — a fresh instance auditing numbers it didn't produce — is, I think, the part worth stealing, independent of what you make of the simulator.
