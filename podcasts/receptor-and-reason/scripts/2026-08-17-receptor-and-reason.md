Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. I'm your host, and this is your daily tour of what's new in CNS pharmacology, psychiatric drug discovery, and the computational tools closing in on both. That's a real recall hit — most days the freshest mechanism papers come from there — so today leans on the journals, and it's a more clinical, less bench-preprint episode than usual. Let's get into it.

We open with a piece in Molecular Psychiatry that I think is the most mechanistically satisfying thing on the table today, and it's about astrocytes and adolescent drinking. The group modeled adolescent intermittent ethanol in male rats — binge-level exposures during a defined developmental window, blood alcohol levels in the human binge range — and then asked what's still broken in the dorsal hippocampus long after the alcohol is gone. Using super-resolution imaging plus in-vivo fiber photometry of astrocyte calcium, they show that the fine astrocytic processes that normally wrap around synapses pull back, and they stay pulled back into adulthood. Functionally the astrocytes go quiet: less calcium response to neuronal activity, even though there's more glutamate sitting around the synapse. And behaviorally, the animals over-respond in fear conditioning. Now here's the part that lifts it from correlation to cause. When they used an excitatory DREADD to selectively switch the hippocampal astrocytes back on, they partially normalized the exaggerated fear and fully restored the normal relationship between adenosine signaling and freezing. So astrocyte activity is causally sufficient to move the behavior. It reframes a chunk of adolescent-alcohol cognitive damage as an astrocyte problem — a glial target you could, in principle, drug — rather than a purely neuronal one. Caveat worth stating: males only, so we don't know the female story, and that matters given how often these phenotypes turn out sex-specific.

Staying with alcohol but moving from glia to receptors, there's a nice longitudinal PET study in Basic and Clinical Pharmacology and Toxicology on the nociceptin system in monkeys. Quick anchor: the NOP receptor — nociceptin/orphanin FQ — is sometimes called the fourth opioid receptor, and NOP-targeting compounds reduce ethanol drinking in animal models, which is why it's a live alcohol-use-disorder target. Human PET work has shown people with alcohol use disorder have lower NOP levels. This group did something you can only really do in primates: they scanned eleven monkeys with a NOP-selective tracer while ethanol-naive, then again after four months of induction and six months of heavy daily drinking. The clean finding is that baseline NOP binding predicted future drinking — in the orbitofrontal cortex for males, the caudate for females — and that chronic drinking then remodeled NOP availability across reward regions, going up in the putamen in both sexes but moving in opposite directions in the insula and amygdala depending on sex. So it's a bidirectional, sex-dependent relationship: NOP tone shapes how much you drink, and how much you drink reshapes NOP tone. For anyone developing NOP ligands for alcohol use disorder, that's both an encouraging target-engagement biomarker and a warning that the baseline and the sex will matter.

Our third item is the bigger clinical story of the day, and I want to be upfront that I'm working from the trial's published report rather than the full paper, so take the details as reported. It's in Lancet Neurology: a phase 1/2 trial of guanabenz in vanishing white matter disease. Vanishing white matter is a devastating childhood leukodystrophy — onset usually between one and six, progressive, fatal, no effective therapy — and it's caused by mutations in eukaryotic initiation factor 2B, which sits at the heart of the integrated stress response. Guanabenz is the interesting part: it's an old alpha-2 adrenergic antihypertensive that also happens to inhibit the integrated stress response, and it rescued a mouse model of this disease. So this is drug repurposing straight out of the mechanism. They treated thirty-three young children, still able to walk at baseline, titrated up over several years, and compared them to sixty-six matched untreated historical controls from a disease registry. The efficacy signal is striking for this disease: the treated kids had a hazard ratio of about 0.33 for losing the ability to walk with support — a two-thirds reduction in risk. On safety, the standout was transient hallucinations in just over half the children, mostly in the first four months and mostly resolving, plus some constipation and hypotension; no deaths. Two big caveats: the comparison is against historical controls, not a randomized arm, and the authors themselves call for a long-term extension to confirm disease modification. But as a proof of concept that you can drug the integrated stress response in a human neurodegenerative disease, this is a landmark — and the ISR is a target people care about well beyond this one rare disease, in ALS and the tauopathies.

That white-matter theme carries into a large meta-analysis in Biological Psychiatry: Cognitive Neuroscience and Neuroimaging, on white matter microstructure in autism. This is a multilevel random-effects synthesis — eight hundred and eighty-one effect sizes across sixty-six diffusion imaging studies — and the take-home is consolidation rather than novelty. They find robustly lower fractional anisotropy across nine major tracts, with moderate effect sizes, spanning the corpus callosum, the arcuate and superior longitudinal fasciculus, the uncinate, the cingulum, and more. The value here is that it settles an inconsistent literature: reduced white matter integrity in autism looks widespread and reproducible rather than confined to one pathway, and they map how age, sex, and IQ move the estimates. It's a reference point, not a mechanism, but it's the kind of quantitative anchor the field needed.

From tracts back down to single neurons, there's a clever human iPSC paper, also in Molecular Psychiatry, on Neurabin One haploinsufficiency. Neurabin One — I'll spare you the gene symbol — is a synaptic scaffolding protein, and loss-of-function variants keep turning up in neurodevelopmental disorder cohorts but have been stuck as variants of uncertain significance because nobody knew what they do in human neurons. So the group used CRISPR to make heterozygous knockout stem cells, differentiated them into cortical neurons, and found a genuinely surprising split. Morphologically the neurons look over-built — more dendritic spines, more neurite complexity. But electrically they're underpowered: reduced firing, blunted action potentials, and a transcriptomic and proteomic signature showing coordinated downregulation of sodium channels and glutamatergic machinery. Structure and function come apart — the cell builds more connections but can't drive them. That's a useful caution for anyone who reads spine counts as a proxy for function, and it gives clinical geneticists real mechanistic evidence to start reclassifying these variants.

Our sixth item is a tidy piece of circuit pharmacology in Neuropharmacology, on maternal behavior. The target is the tail of the ventral tegmental area — also called the rostromedial tegmental nucleus — which is essentially the brake pedal for dopamine neurons, a cluster of GABA cells that inhibit them. The group cannulated this region in postpartum rats and micro-injected a panel of tool compounds: glutamate to drive it, muscimol — a GABA-A agonist — to silence it, the mu-opioid agonist DAMGO, plus oxytocin and its antagonist atosiban. The logic is a clean push-pull: activating this brake suppresses maternal behavior, releasing it enhances maternal behavior, exactly what you'd predict if the region gates the dopamine that motivates pup-directed care. What makes it more than a curiosity is the opioid and oxytocin sensitivity — it places two neuropeptide systems we care a lot about in social bonding right onto this brake, at a specific anatomical node.

Seventh, a review in the International Journal of Neuropsychopharmacology on parvalbumin interneurons under stress that's worth a mention as a framework. These are the fast-spiking cells that keep excitation-inhibition balance and pace network oscillations, and the review's organizing claim is that they're a convergence point — that redox vulnerability, microglial inflammation, epigenetic hits, and the remodeling of the perineuronal nets that wrap and protect them all funnel through parvalbumin-cell dysfunction to produce stress-related psychiatric outcomes. The useful part is the therapeutic mapping: it lines up interventions that plausibly protect these cells — antioxidants, anti-inflammatories, and notably ketamine and its derivatives — against the specific vulnerabilities each one addresses, with attention to sex differences. If you work on stress circuitry, it's a clean scaffold for why so many unrelated-looking mechanisms keep landing on the same cell type.

Shifting to the clinic, there's new Phase 4 data on viloxazine extended-release — brand name Qelbree — in adults who have ADHD plus clinically significant depression or anxiety. Anchor on the pharmacology, because it's the point of interest: viloxazine is primarily a norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor, but it also has serotonergic activity — partial agonism at the serotonin 2C receptor, antagonism at 2B and 7 — and, fun history, its immediate-release form was actually marketed as an antidepressant in Europe decades ago. This was a pragmatic, real-world-flavored open-label trial: a hundred and four completers, deliberately loose eligibility that let in people using nicotine or cannabis. By fourteen weeks, ADHD symptoms dropped around forty-five to fifty percent, and depression and anxiety scores fell roughly fifty percent in parallel. The caveats are load-bearing, though: open-label, no comparator, fourteen weeks, mostly white and female, and the investigator herself was careful to say she can't tell whether the mood benefit is a genuine serotonergic antidepressant effect or just the downstream relief of getting the ADHD under control. And the boxed warning for suicidal ideation isn't academic here — three people discontinued for it. So: an encouraging real-world signal in a comorbid population that trials usually exclude, but explicitly hypothesis-generating.

Our last two are lighter, and both are about how we reason over drugs rather than about a specific compound. The first is a computational chemistry method on arXiv with a name I like: Localize, Then Reason. The observation is that current vision-language models reading a molecule either get handed a text description of its motifs or try to reason straight from the raw image, and neither actually looks at the chemically meaningful region first. Their framework learns to localize the relevant substructure in the molecular image, then reason about its property effects in a compact latent workspace before answering — mimicking how a chemist's eye goes to the functional group before the verdict. The headline benefit is efficiency: nearly ten times the throughput of a comparable text-reasoning baseline at the same accuracy. It's incremental and architecture-flavored rather than a breakthrough, but it's a clean illustration of a recurring theme — that giving these models a place to look before they think keeps paying off.

And we'll close on a conceptual one from the Journal of Psychopharmacology, out of the Carhart-Harris group, simply titled "Defining psychedelic." It's a phenomenology-first attempt to answer a question the field keeps fudging: what actually makes a drug a psychedelic? They had over two hundred people who'd experienced three different classes of drug — a classic serotonergic psychedelic, an entactogen in the MDMA family, and a dissociative in the ketamine family — rate their subjective experiences on a large battery of items. Factor analysis pulled out three or four independent dimensions of experience, and a machine-learning classifier could reliably tell the three drug classes apart from the subjective profile alone. Their conclusion is that "psychedelic" isn't just loose vibes — it's a definable category, anchored in a specific and distinguishable subjective state, not merely a pharmacological label. It's a small study and inevitably a bit circular, but I like it as an argument that the phenomenology deserves to be treated as data, especially as this whole class of drugs marches toward the clinic.
