## Script

Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Wednesday, November the eighteenth. Today's episode has a theme running through it, and it's one worth naming up front: the wrong compartment. In paper after paper this week, the effect turns out to live somewhere other than where the field has been looking for it — not in the membrane protein but in the soluble piece that fell off it, not in the neuron but in the astrocyte next to it, not in the simulator but in the text handed to the model. Eleven items. Let's go.

The lead is a preprint from Coimbra, with Mayo Clinic and Oxford, on autoantibodies against CASPR2 — the contactin-associated protein that shows up in patients with neuromyotonia, neuropathic pain, seizures and cognitive dysfunction. The standing assumption in autoimmune neurology is mechanical and obvious: the antibody binds an extracellular epitope on the cell surface, and does damage by cross-linking it, internalizing it, or blocking its partners. Most CASPR2 work has run on exactly that premise, looking at internalization and at crosstalk with contactin-2, potassium channels and AMPA receptors.

This group asked a different question, because CASPR2 is also detectable as a free protein in cerebrospinal fluid. It gets shed. And the same fluid that contains the shed ectodomain contains the antibody. So what if the pathogenic act isn't binding the cell at all, but mopping up the soluble piece?

They took purified immunoglobulin from three patients with high-titre CASPR2 antibodies and three controls, and put it on mature rat cortical cultures and human stem-cell-derived neurons on multielectrode arrays, at twenty micrograms per millilitre to approximate physiological brain IgG. The patient antibodies drove the networks hyperexcitable — firing rate, burst frequency, network burst frequency and cross-correlation all up, starting within hours and growing over five days, consistent across all three patients. Whole-cell recordings localized it: excitatory synaptic drive went up, inhibitory transmission was essentially untouched. A hyperconnected, excitation-shifted network, which is a decent cellular analogue of what these patients actually present with.

Then the key experiment. The antibodies depleted CASPR2 from the culture medium, and from patient cerebrospinal fluid — they were sequestering the shed ectodomain, not just decorating the cell surface. And when the group knocked down PMCA2, the plasma membrane calcium ATPase that the shed CASPR2 ectodomain is reported to regulate, the antibody effect disappeared. So the chain is: antibody neutralizes a soluble signalling protein, that protein is no longer available to restrain a calcium pump, calcium handling degrades, excitatory drive rises, the network goes hyperexcitable.

Why this matters beyond CASPR2: it opens a category. Neurons shed a great many ectodomains — there's a whole sheddome — and every one of them is a potential autoantibody target whose pathology would be invisible to the assays we use, because those assays test binding to cells. If you only ever ask whether the antibody binds the surface and what it does to the surface protein, you will systematically miss the soluble arm. The honest limitation is that this is all in culture; the passive-transfer animal work establishing CASPR2 pathogenicity exists, but nobody has yet shown in vivo that supplying shed ectodomain rescues the phenotype. That's the obvious next experiment and it's very doable.

Staying with the theme of looking in the wrong cell. A large preprint from the Broad, from the McCarroll and Nehme groups, asks what clozapine actually does to human neural cultures — and the answer is mostly about astrocytes. They grew genetically diverse stem-cell-derived neurons and astrocytes, profiled drug responses, mapped expression quantitative trait loci under drug exposure, and ran CRISPR interference on top. In astrocyte monocultures, clozapine produced a small global transcriptional response that was tightly concentrated in cholesterol biosynthesis and export, and it raised both intracellular and secreted cholesterol. That program overlaps with the cholesterol arm of what astrocytes do when they contact neurons — but not with the synaptic cell-adhesion arm. So clozapine engages part of the astrocyte-neuron coordination program, specifically the lipid-supply part, and leaves the adhesion part alone.

Two things give this traction. Haloperidol showed comparable genome-wide correspondence to neuronal contact but much weaker cholesterol induction, so this isn't a generic antipsychotic effect; and the magnitude of cholesterol induction varied across donor lines in a way that tracked baseline expression, with a clozapine-dependent association between HSD17B7 expression and nearby common variants. That's a pharmacogenomic handle on a drug we currently dose by trial and error and plasma level. Independently, repressing the LDL receptor reproduced the coordinated biosynthesis response, which tells you the program can be entered genetically, not only pharmacologically. The restraint I'd flag is that the authors are careful about causality: clozapine also shortened inter-spike intervals in co-culture, but they explicitly do not claim the cholesterol change caused the firing change, and no perturbation reproduced or reversed the schizophrenia-associated expression phenotype. It's an entry point, not a mechanism of therapeutic action.

Third, the target as a conformation rather than a protein. A preprint describes ACE-339, an orally bioavailable small molecule found with a computational chemistry platform called RESOLUTE, which disrupts insoluble TDP-43 aggregates. TDP-43 is the central pathology in more than ninety-seven percent of sporadic ALS: it leaves the nucleus and aggregates in the cytoplasm, which is both a gain of function in the cytoplasm and a loss of splicing regulation in the nucleus — the cryptic exons in STMN2 and UNC13A that have become the field's molecular signature.

The design hypothesis is the interesting part. ACE-339 binds misfolded, aggregated TDP-43 and not natively folded protein. They show it inhibits aggregation of the amyloid core peptide, the low-complexity domain and full-length protein, shifts pre-formed aggregates from insoluble to soluble by centrifugation, and — the elegant bit — that once it disaggregates its target, it loses its binding site and is released. When they separated bound and free fractions, most of the compound was free, and that free fraction still inhibited aggregation of freshly added peptide. A catalytic rather than stoichiometric mode of action, in principle. Consistent with that, potency in the biochemical assay is better expressed as a molar ratio than as an IC-fifty: greater than ninety percent inhibition at one-to-ten monomer-to-compound, greater than fifty percent at one-to-one. Downstream they report restored nuclear TDP-43, rescued mis-splicing and synaptic function, and in two mouse models of TDP-43 pathology, improved survival, lower neurofilament light and neuroinflammation, and better behaviour. Read this with the appropriate industrial-preprint caution — it's a company platform paper and the compound catalogue is the product — but conformation-selective disaggregation with target-release recycling is a genuinely good shape for a mechanism, and neurofilament light is the biomarker that would carry it into a trial.

Now a clean piece of systems pharmacology from Cambridge, from the Schultz lab — and if you've spent any time with reward prediction error, this is the paper that closes a gap you've probably noticed. Two macaques chose between options differing in reward amount and in effort, where effort was joystick resistance. The question: how do midbrain dopamine neurons handle effort cost? Is there a separate cost channel, or does effort enter the same currency as reward?

The answer is the same currency, with the opposite sign. At effort-predicting stimuli, dopamine neurons showed reduced reward-predicting excitation in proportion to the effort signalled. At experienced effort, more effort than predicted produced inhibition, less effort than predicted produced excitation — effort prediction errors behaving exactly like negative reward prediction errors. And because the signal appears at both the stimulus and the outcome, it satisfies the temporal-difference formalism rather than merely resembling it. Practically, this says that an effort manipulation and a reward manipulation are not separable at the level of this signal, which matters for anyone reading motivational anhedonia in depression or negative symptoms in schizophrenia off a dopaminergic measure. If the same neurons subtract effort from reward, then a patient who looks reward-insensitive and a patient who looks effort-averse may be producing the same dopamine phenotype by different routes.

Related in spirit, a preprint from awake mouse auditory cortex that takes on a debate psychiatry has a direct stake in: is deviance detection adaptation, or is it prediction error against an internal model? Mismatch negativity is one of the more durable biomarkers in schizophrenia research, and the interpretation of what it indexes has consequences. The two mechanisms are miserable to separate, because the context that establishes a prediction is the same context that drives adaptation.

This group used a modified oddball paradigm that pulls recent sound history apart from longer-term sequence statistics. Contextual effects ran the whole range from suppression to facilitation, and facilitation grew with deviance magnitude — the signature usually read as prediction error. But here's the result: neither facilitation, nor suppression, nor stimulus-specific adaptation depended on the longer-term statistics. If the cortex were running a predictive model of the sequence, the longer-term structure should matter, and it didn't. A normalization model in which a neuron's inputs adapt at different rates reproduced the whole range of effects, including facilitation, and reproduced their weakening in background noise. So adaptive normalization alone generates deviance signals that look like prediction error. This doesn't abolish predictive coding, but it does mean the facilitation component — the part people have specifically pointed to as the additive prediction-error term — is not evidence for a predictive model. If you use mismatch negativity as a readout of predictive processing, that inference needs a different argument.

Fourth compartment: inside the thalamus rather than between thalamus and cortex. A multi-dataset study pooled six fMRI and EEG datasets across natural sleep, propofol anaesthesia and unresponsive wakefulness syndrome, asking what preserves arousability in slow-wave sleep. Slow-wave sleep is the odd state out — unresponsive but rousable, where anaesthesia and unresponsive wakefulness syndrome are not. Most work has looked at thalamocortical pathways or at single nuclei. Here, during stage two and stage three sleep, the centromedian and ventral posterolateral nuclei converged in their edge-community profiles and showed increased closed-loop intra-thalamic motifs — changes absent under propofol and absent in unresponsive wakefulness syndrome. In stage three, directed connectivity along centromedian to ventral posterolateral to pulvinar increased relative to both wake and stage two, and stronger connectivity tracked higher delta power, which in turn was higher before awakenings than before descents into lighter sleep. And it had prognostic traction: the same measure was positively associated with three-month improvement in behavioural responsiveness in the unresponsive wakefulness patients. That last point is what lifts this above a state-classification exercise. It's correlational and the sample is pooled across heterogeneous datasets, but a thalamic loop signature that separates rousable from non-rousable unresponsiveness is clinically useful if it replicates.

Two clinical items. First, plasma proteomics from LATTICE, the randomized trial of low-dose lithium carbonate versus placebo over two years in eighty older adults with mild cognitive impairment, from Pittsburgh. They ran a hundred-and-twenty-protein CNS panel longitudinally. The headline finding is a negative one worth sitting with: baseline proteomic differences tracked amyloid PET status far more strongly than randomized treatment assignment, which is as it should be, and the treatment-by-time interactions they did see — calretinin, TREM2, amyloid-beta-forty — were nominal and none survived correction for multiple testing. What did survive, in the amnestic subgroup, was GDF15: consistently associated with Clinical Dementia Rating sum-of-boxes trajectories and with modification of the lithium treatment effect. Phospho-tau-two-seventeen behaved as expected, tracking amyloid status and hippocampal volume trajectory. So the protein that predicted who progressed and who responded was not the amyloid marker — it was a stress and inflammation cytokine. Eighty participants and twenty-one amyloid-positive is small, the authors call most of this exploratory, and they're right to. But if lithium's neuroprotective effect in mild cognitive impairment is real and heterogeneous, a cheap plasma marker of who benefits is exactly what the next trial needs to prespecify.

Second, a data-quality study that I think is quietly important for anyone doing pharmacology on electronic health records. The question: when a nurse charts a pain score at eight o'clock, when was the assessment actually made? Using intensive care data from one academic centre and seventy-eight hospitals, the authors found that sixty-eight percent of pain scores and seventy-six percent of sedation scores were charted exactly on the hour, and entered into the system a median of forty-one to forty-four minutes later. The charted time is a slot label — the row of the flowsheet — not a clock reading. And the consequence is not subtle. Analysed by charted time, oral opioids looked almost as fast as intravenous, with pain change at fifteen to thirty minutes of one point seven six versus two point one one. Restricted to records with exact timestamps, the same comparison was eight-tenths of a point versus two points — the pharmacology reappears. Across hospitals the on-the-hour share ranged from fifteen to ninety-two percent, so this is a hospital-specific practice, not a constant you can adjust for globally. Their simulation gives a usable rule: analysis by charted time is tolerable up to a slot-width-to-time-constant ratio of about two when rounding is to the nearest slot, but only up to about one-tenth when rounding has a direction. And the sting in the tail is that the data cannot identify which of those regimes you're in. If you are estimating onset, offset or any causal ordering from charted bedside assessments, the timestamp is a documentation artefact until proven otherwise.

Three items from the machine-learning side, and the first one is the sharpest. A group in Hong Kong built HINDAUDIT, an audit of where hindsight actually enters clinical diagnostic-agent benchmarks. The construction of these benchmarks is standard: the case the agent reads is rewritten by a language model from notes written after the diagnosis was known, usually the discharge summary, and tests the agent orders but that were never performed are answered by a simulator that typically sees the full record. The field's attention has gone almost entirely to the second step — instruct the simulator not to reveal the answer, filter out case texts that name the diagnosis.

They separated three channels and measured each against real data, across twenty-seven thousand four hundred and eighty-nine emergency admissions, with the simulator-side tests registered before running. Giving the diagnosis to five different simulators had a negligible effect on how strongly simulated lab results pointed at the true answer. The leakage everyone guards against was not there. The case text was where it lived: against a presentation-time reconstruction built from triage records, benchmark-style summaries lifted zero-shot accuracy for four models from twenty-five point four percent to thirty-nine point seven, and discharge histories to forty-five point three. Six interactive agents scored higher too — though, notably, their ranking barely changed. Masking the diagnosis name left the gap untouched. Two caveats the authors supply themselves: because this dataset has no contemporaneous bedside history, the gap is an upper bound on hindsight; and the case-text results were exploratory rather than registered, with a replication on held-out patients. Still, the practical lesson is clean. Guarding the simulator is guarding the wrong door, and a benchmark's rank ordering can be stable while its absolute numbers are inflated by twenty points.

Then DiscoveryAgent, from Stanford, from James Zou's group with several labs. It's a scientist-centric agentic research framework, and its contribution is less the autonomy than the audit. Scientists select, refine or replace hypotheses between rounds; the system checkpoints its work in a way that lets separate audit agents interrogate the record, trace claims and reproduce analyses. The number that justifies the engineering: audit agents found errors at higher rates than previous AI-scientist systems, and scientists working with audit agents identified fourteen times more valid errors than scientists doing manual review. That is an oversight-scaling result, and it's the piece most agentic-science papers skip. On capability, it produced the best variable-star classifier among the baselines tested, and with scientist steering, a protein variant-effect predictor beaten by only one of a hundred and six ProteinGym baselines. Three expert-led case studies followed, including a relay-gene analysis in CD4-positive T cells enriched for autoimmune associations. Worth pairing with yesterday's thread about groundedness being a property of the harness rather than the model: the same lesson shows up here as auditability being a property of the checkpointing, not the agent.

To close, something to calibrate all of this against. A group at NJIT analysed two and a half million full-text papers across five disciplines from 2015 to 2025, looking for structural breaks after late 2022 in lexical, structural, epistemological and social measures of scientific writing. The finding is mostly an absence. Apart from frequency shifts in some words, pre-2023 trends continued with limited detectable breaks. The exceptions are interesting: computer-science coauthor team growth accelerated significantly in Asia — and the apparent decline in biomedical topic diversity, which is the measure people have waved at when arguing for AI-driven scientific monoculture, largely reflected standardization of article formats, a trend that was already well underway before anyone had access to these models. I'd hold this loosely in both directions; three years is short, and writing is a lagging indicator of practice. But if your prior is that the literature has visibly degraded, the lexical and structural evidence at scale doesn't yet support it, and at least one of the headline signals turns out to be a measurement artefact rather than an effect. Which is, I suppose, the theme of the whole episode.
