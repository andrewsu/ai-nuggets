## Script

Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Thursday, October eighth. Eleven items.

A thread runs through nearly all of them, worth naming now because it should change how you read each one. In paper after paper this week, the measured effect is real — and credited to the wrong cause. Sometimes the wrong interface on the protein, sometimes the wrong contact on the electrode, sometimes the wrong questionnaire. The interesting work today is almost never the headline number; it is whether anyone ran the one control that could have identified the cause.

We open with the most consequential piece of chemistry on the list. A group spanning Columbia, Weill Cornell and Macao Polytechnic has produced the first small molecule that disrupts the interaction between NOS1AP and tau. NOS1AP is a genetically validated driver of tau pathology, and that interaction has sat there untargeted for years, because protein-protein interfaces are flat and unforgiving.

How they got there is unglamorous. Affinity-selection mass spectrometry on a ten-thousand-compound library gave one validated hit at thirty-seven micromolar, and then they did what they honestly call SAR-by-catalog — they bought eleven analogs off the shelf. Moving a pyridyl nitrogen from ortho to para took affinity to three hundred ninety nanomolar. That is ninety-four-fold from one atom moved, which is luck rather than medicinal chemistry; no chemistry was actually performed here.

What lifts this above the usual first-in-class preprint is the on-target control. They built a drug-resistant NOS1AP variant — two point mutations, Y16A and F42A — that still binds tau normally but loses seventy-eight-fold affinity for the compound. Then they re-expressed that variant in human neurons and watched the drug's effect disappear. That is the genetic experiment that distinguishes a real on-target mechanism from a compound that happens to do something useful, and most papers in this genre skip it.

The pharmacokinetics are real too: forty percent oral bioavailability, brain-to-plasma ratio climbing to one point two-eight by twenty-four hours, dose-dependent target engagement measured in brain by thermal shift. Chronic oral dosing rescued pathology, long-term potentiation and memory in both a tauopathy model and an amyloidosis model, sparing wild-type littermates.

And here is where the thread starts. A tau-interface disruptor lowered cortical amyloid-beta forty-two by thirty-one to forty-five percent. The paper does not explain this, and the parsimonious reading is that the tau interface is not the only thing being hit. The same molecule also disrupts the NOS1AP–nNOS complex — and at the lowest dose tested, it disrupts nNOS slightly more than it disrupts tau. Cortical nitrite falls. The entire rationale for this program is that NOS1AP drives tau nitration at tyrosine twenty-nine, and nitrated tau was never measured anywhere in this paper. To the authors' credit, the discussion says plainly that the selectivity is a difference of degree rather than of kind. The title does not. Treat this as a dual-interface NOS1AP disruptor with a genuinely promising profile, not as a tau-selective agent.

Staying with attribution, but moving to human electrophysiology. The Damisah lab at Yale has published the first human single-unit recordings from the claustrum tied to computationally defined learning variables — seven epilepsy patients, Behnke-Fried depth electrodes, microwires threaded past insular macro-contacts into a structure about a millimeter thick. One hundred ten claustral units during an aversive avoidance task.

The durable finding is a clean regional dissociation. Claustral neurons are biased toward the aversive outcome — fifty-one of seventy-eight responsive cells prefer the crash — while anterior cingulate cells are mixed-sign. That interaction survives leave-one-subject-out and log transformation, and it is a real anatomical claim about a structure we have almost no human data on.

The title, though, says these neurons encode uncertainty and prediction errors, and that is where I would push back. The only analysis controlling for the obvious confounds — spaceship position, which zone, which outcome — is a conditional mutual information test. In claustrum, the prediction-error version is null. In cingulate, every version is null. The one surviving result sits at an uncorrected p of point oh four nine, and the methods state no correction was applied. Uncertainty and prediction error are also statistically inseparable here — nearly every uncertainty-modulated cell is also prediction-error-modulated, which the authors concede. And the discussion refers to a single-neuron decoding analysis showing these variables were linearly accessible. There is no decoding analysis in the paper — not in the results, the methods, or any figure legend.

So: take the outcome dissociation, which is well powered and controlled. Do not take the title.

Two papers from Neuropsychopharmacology next, both about dopamine and kappa opioid pharmacology, both with a control that reframes the headline.

The first is kappa. A group at LSU shows that ten days of social defeat stress raises alcohol drinking weeks later in both sexes, and that the kappa antagonist norbinaltorphimine blocks that escalation without touching drinking in unstressed animals. They then localize it: norBNI into the bed nucleus of the stria terminalis works, and chemogenetically activating kappa-expressing basolateral amygdala terminals inside the BNST reproduces the effect, stress-selectively. That terminal-field experiment is the cleanest thing in the paper and it maps onto the clinical hypothesis that kappa antagonists should help the stress-driven drinker rather than drinking in general.

The section I would read differently is the knockdown. The header says deleting kappa receptors in basolateral amygdala prevents stress-induced drinking. What actually happened, in the authors' own words, is that unstressed mice with the receptor deleted drank more — up to roughly the stressed level — and stress then added nothing on top. That is a ceiling effect. The data are equally consistent with basolateral amygdala kappa acting as a tonic brake on drinking that was simply removed, which says nothing about the stress pathway and points the opposite direction from the therapeutic claim. Worth holding onto, because norBNI is also an inverse agonist with a weeks-long duration of action and the dosing here was not counterbalanced, so tonic dynorphin tone and constitutive receptor activity are not distinguishable in this design. The authors name aticaprant as the short-acting tool that would settle it.

The second is an extinction study from Williams College, and its pharmacology is genuinely surprising. Extinguishing a conditioned fear across three different contexts rather than one produces stronger between-session extinction — replicated in two strains across four cohorts. The best-designed experiment in the paper is the novelty control: pre-exposing animals to the upcoming contexts, so they are familiar rather than novel, does not abolish the benefit. So it is the context shift itself, not novelty, doing the work.

Then the receptor result, which runs against the field's default. The standard extinction story is D1-like signaling in infralimbic cortex. Here, D1 and D2 blockade in infralimbic cortex did nothing in either condition. Systemic D1 blockade impaired extinction only in the single-context group. The manipulation that mattered for the multi-context protocol was D2-like blockade in dorsal hippocampus. That is an unexpected place to look and a testable one.

Two cautions. The therapeutic framing is relapse prevention, and the relapse data do not support it: reinstatement is flatly null, and at thirty days the multi-context advantage in novel contexts is gone, because those animals spontaneously recovered. And the paper does not report a dose or concentration for either dopamine antagonist anywhere in the main text — which, for an argument that leans on raclopride's D2-versus-D3 selectivity, is the one number you would want.

A short anatomical item that is more useful than it looks. A Northeastern group crossed a D1 reporter mouse with an M4 reporter mouse and counted co-expression across striatum, accumbens and medial prefrontal cortex in eighteen animals. The number worth carrying is the cortical one: medial prefrontal cortex is about sixty-one percent M4-only, with co-expression down near nineteen percent, which inverts the striatal pattern almost exactly. If you are reasoning about xanomeline-trospium or any of the M4 positive allosteric modulators now moving through schizophrenia trials, the intuition that M4 counterbalances D1 signaling within the same cell is a striatal story. In cortex, a selective M4 agent is mostly engaging cells that are not D1-positive at all.

Two caveats before anyone quotes the percentages. These are proportions of reporter-positive objects, not of neurons. And the segmentation collapsed a twenty-micron stack into a single projection before calling overlap, which in densely packed striatum will merge somata stacked in depth and in sparse cortex will not — that alone could manufacture the gradient. The transcript validation cannot rescue it, because the two probes shared a fluorophore and had to be run on separate sections, so there is no transcript-level evidence that these genes are in the same cell, which is the entire point of the study.

The best preclinical paper on today's list comes from the Liston lab at Weill Cornell, and it has a result that should make you rethink a familiar readout. Head-fixed mice working a progressive-ratio licking task, with two-photon imaging of dorsal raphe through a prism lens. Nearly ninety percent of dorsal raphe neurons were modulated by the task — and here is the first surprise: more cells encoded effort expenditure than encoded reward, about fifty-three percent against thirty-five, with a quarter carrying both at the single-cell level. For a nucleus the field has framed around reward and patience for two decades, effort coding being the dominant signal is a genuine addition.

The second surprise is the stress result. Ten days of social defeat made mice work harder, not less — breakpoint went up. That is the opposite of the effort-deficit, anhedonia story, including one from this same lab. Their reading is that the extra responding is not motivation but habit, and the evidence is that stressed animals no longer reduce responding after the reward is devalued.

Then the part that matters pharmacologically. Optogenetically inhibiting GABAergic raphe neurons lowered breakpoint and restored devaluation sensitivity four days later. Activating them raised breakpoint. And inhibiting the serotonin neurons did neither. For a field that has spent twenty years on dorsal raphe serotonin, a bidirectional GABAergic controller of operant output is the most actionable thing here. Single-nucleus sequencing of those GABAergic populations nominated targets, with mu opioid receptor the strongest hit.

Three cautions, and they are real. The calcium imaging used a pan-neuronal promoter, so the reward hyperactivity was never actually measured in GABA neurons — that attribution comes from different cohorts. The sequencing pooled four animals per group into one library each, so there is one biological replicate per group. And the serotonin null came from five mice, where the statistical test could not have reached significance regardless of effect size. The bidirectional GABA result is the part that survives.

Two clinical neuromodulation items, and both are exercises in locating the effect.

First, an open-label pilot from the Medical University of South Carolina pushing transcranial direct current stimulation to six milliamps — three times the FDA-cleared home dose — twice daily for five days with custom high-capacity hydrogel electrodes. As an engineering result it succeeded: two hundred three of two hundred three sessions delivered, no skin breakdown, mean side-effect severity one-point-three on a five-point scale.

The efficacy numbers are where you should stop. The headline is a nine-point drop on the PHQ-9 with fifty percent response, from an unblinded self-report instrument — and the recall window of that instrument changed between baseline and endpoint, from two weeks to twenty-four hours, in eighteen of twenty participants. That inflates an apparent drop by construction. More tellingly, clinician ratings were added by amendment partway through, explicitly because the self-report changes were larger than anticipated, and in the seven patients who got them the Hamilton change did not reach significance. Roughly half the effect, on the scale a clinician controls.

Two safety signals deserve more prominence than they got. One participant had twitching around the right eye followed by rhythmic movements of the right arm during the ramp to six milliamps — judged probably related, resolved when the current was lowered, no EEG obtained. And both Black participants in the study developed hyperpigmentation persisting at one month, which the erythema-only grading scale used throughout cannot detect. Those are the findings from this study that should change the next protocol.

Second, deep brain stimulation for treatment-resistant schizophrenia, from Shanghai Mental Health Center — three patients, open-label, twenty-four weeks, targeting nucleus accumbens and the anterior limb of the internal capsule. Two met response criteria with reported PANSS reductions near sixty-nine percent.

Three things complicate that. The sixty-nine percent is floor-corrected rather than raw — raw is closer to forty-eight percent. The baseline was taken on the first postoperative day, weeks before the stimulator was switched on, and one patient had already improved thirty-five percent with the device off, which is a microlesion effect the discussion raises but never subtracts. And the stimulation that produced the result was not what the title describes: accumbens contacts caused dyskinesia and were discontinued in the best responder, whose therapeutic setting for the final twelve weeks was a single left capsular contact. The discussion itself starts calling it capsular stimulation partway through.

The part I would not carry forward is the local field potential signature — gamma up, theta down. Those are relative powers sharing a denominator, so they are one observation and not two, and the gamma band was defined as forty to ninety hertz while notch filters sat at forty and eighty. No statistics were run on it.

What is genuinely informative is an accident. One patient's non-rechargeable battery depleted after twenty-seven weeks. Two weeks later his PANSS was fifty-eight; thirty-six weeks later it had climbed to ninety, approaching his preoperative one hundred one. That unplanned off-phase is the strongest internal evidence in the paper that stimulation rather than surgery was carrying the benefit, and it is an argument for a proper on-off crossover rather than another open-label series. One more thing nobody measured: single-contact testing produced elevated mood, spontaneous smiling and pressured speech in two of three patients, described as resembling hypomania — and no mania scale was ever administered, while the improvements being counted include negative-symptom items like blunted affect and apathy.

Turning to the computational half, where the same thread holds with unusual force.

The sharpest result is a paper on medical question-answering systems that reach consensus. Multi-round agreement among sampled model answers is the standard signal these systems use to decide whether to trust themselves. The finding is that on MedQA, fifty-nine percent of questions reach unanimity and thirteen point four percent of those unanimous answers are wrong — and no agreement-based signal can flag them, because terminal agreement is constant by construction once a system stops at unanimity. On a harder expert benchmark, twenty-nine percent of questions reach unanimity and seventy-nine percent of those are wrong.

Their proposed fix reads the process rather than the outcome — trajectory features, whether the stated reasons cohere, and an active probe that retrieves counter-evidence and checks whether the consensus survives. The control arms here are the best methodological work I have read this week: a devil's-advocate instruction alone flips zero of seven hundred forty-six votes, irrelevant documents flip one percent, and counter-evidence under neutral phrasing retains nearly the full asymmetry. So the flip is driven by content, not framing.

And yet the paper's own ablations undercut its apparatus. Dropping the agreement features improves performance. Dropping the distribution features improves performance. The trajectory families correlate with simple difficulty proxies at rho above point nine three — they are measuring how hard the question was, not how the consensus formed. The only family that carries weight is rationale coherence, which you can compute from a single round of sampling with no deliberation at all. And inside the unanimous layer, plain sequence log-probability — free — scores essentially as well as a counter-evidence probe that doubles the cost of every question. The diagnosis is right and important. The agentic machinery is mostly not where the fix lives.

Next, a failure mode that will matter to anyone whose institution is deploying ambient documentation. A group at NYU Langone inserted patient small talk — a relative's elective surgery, a band called The Strokes, a coffee shop called Delirium — into clinical dialogues and watched what models wrote into the note. Frontier models incorporated the aside into thirty-five percent of notes; open-weight models, fifty percent. Note quality scores barely moved, which is the point: the standard quality metric is blind to this.

Two refinements from inside the paper. Most of that thirty-five percent is benign recording with correct attribution; the clinically consequential slice — misattributed to the patient, or acted on — is about three point seven percent in frontier models, but twenty-six percent in open-weight ones. Every open-weight model misattributed more than the worst frontier model. And the headline is inflated by benchmark composition: in short excerpts the inserted aside is nearly half the transcript, and on full-length visits contamination falls to about two point six percent. The authors say so.

There is also an audio arm: background speech from a separate encounter at minus ten decibels leaked into forty-eight percent of transcripts and reached five percent of downstream notes. The mechanistic claim — that the circuitry making models distractible is the circuitry supporting clinical reasoning — is the weakest part, since in one model their method selected nearly a quarter of all attention heads, and ablating a quarter of a model's attention degrades everything. Prompting the models to ignore irrelevant information did not reliably help.

Closing the computational set with the most useful number I saw this week, and it is buried in an appendix. A benchmark called BioStudyBench asks whether agents can reproduce the findings of biomedical studies published after their training cutoff, using public data they must locate themselves. The contamination controls are excellent — across seventeen thousand tool calls, not one returned record postdated the cutoff. With data and tools, the best models pass around ninety-five percent of tasks, against roughly fifty percent from prior knowledge alone.

The appendix is the real story. Given only the research question, with no tools and no data, a frontier model matched the direction and significance of eighty-three percent of findings across two thousand one hundred thirty-one post-cutoff papers — and eighty-one percent even on the papers it explicitly said it did not know. Ninety-three percent on one aging cohort, ninety-seven percent on an adverse-event database. It also reproduced publication bias faithfully, predicting a significant effect for sixty-one percent of papers whose true finding was null.

Sit with that. Most published observational biomedical research is guessable from the question alone. The benchmark then deliberately removes those papers, keeping about one percent as its task set — which is legitimate benchmark design, and it means the headline gain from data and tools describes an adversarially selected residue rather than the literature. The twenty-five surviving tasks are also entirely survey and registry epidemiology. No imaging, no omics, nothing neural.

And to end somewhere lighter and genuinely celebratory: this week's Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine went to Karl Deisseroth, Peter Hegemann and Georg Nagel for light-activated ion channels and optogenetics. Hegemann and Nagel on the channelrhodopsin biophysics, Deisseroth on turning it into a method for controlling defined neurons in behaving animals. The reaction in the field has been less surprise than relief at the timing. It is also a nice counterpoint to everything else on today's list — a case where a tool did exactly what it claimed, for twenty years, and the attribution was never in doubt.

That is Receptor and Reason for today. Eleven items, one recurring lesson: the number in the abstract is usually real, and usually about something other than what the title says. See you tomorrow.
