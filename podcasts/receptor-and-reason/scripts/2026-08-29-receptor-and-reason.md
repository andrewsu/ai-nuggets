## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. I'm your host, and this is your daily pass through CNS pharmacology, psychiatric and neurology drug discovery, and the computational machinery closing in on both. Eleven items today, and the first two share a spine: in both, a field's headline metric turns out to be measuring something other than what everyone assumed.

We open with a benchmark for single-cell drug perturbation models. The premise of that literature is that if you can predict the transcriptome of a drug-treated cell, you can screen compounds in silico — and those models are scored almost universally on expression similarity: mean squared error, energy distance, differential-expression overlap. This group asked a different question. From more than fifty thousand PubMed records they curated ninety-three studies into a hundred eighty-one datasets, four hundred twenty-three annotated response cases and two and a half million cells — recording, for each case, the direction specific genes should move under that drug in that cell context, taken from the original paper and verified against the measured matrix.

The motivating example tells you everything. Take a standard LXR agonist on human iPSC-derived microglia. The measured response upregulates the canonical LXR targets — the two ABC transporters and apolipoprotein E. One leading perturbation model predicted the opposite direction for all three, at both doses, while scoring high on expression similarity. So they built a mechanism fidelity score: is the key gene moving the right way, is the effect size calibrated, do functionally related genes move together, is signal enriched in mechanism-relevant genes rather than expression-matched controls, does pathway polarity hold.

Then twelve published models, three baselines, eight frozen single-cell foundation models plus PCA, across ten splits. Take cases in the top twenty percent for expression similarity and ask what fraction land in the bottom twenty percent for key-gene direction. In-distribution, thirty-four to fifty-nine percent. Under held-out-cell-line generalization — the regime you actually care about — mean squared error gives ninety-nine point one percent. Across a hundred seventy-five cross-metric comparisons the median correlation between the two families was point zero five.

Which means model selection is a coin flip depending on which family you score with. The two scores never picked the same top model across five splits, and in-distribution, mechanism fidelity ranked plain linear regression first. And it isn't only a reporting curiosity: feeding predicted profiles into a downstream drug-retrieval pipeline, selecting the upstream representation by mechanism fidelity improved hit-rate-at-five by twenty-seven percent.

Be clear about the ceiling. The annotations are directional summaries from the literature, this is transcriptional fidelity and not target engagement, and the foundation models were evaluated frozen. But the headline holds: absolute mechanism fidelity was poor almost everywhere, larger architectures did not reliably beat simple baselines, and hard-negative tests caught models producing plausible responses by reusing generic stress programs instead of the drug's actual signature.

The second item is the same disease in the agentic-AI-for-science literature. We mostly evaluate an autonomous research agent by whether the code ran and the numbers look reasonable. This group argues that criterion is empty, and they have receipts. Across thirty long-horizon reproduction runs spanning twelve machine learning domains, fifty-seven percent contained what they call methodological hallucinations. A retrieval-augmented generation reproduction where the generator wouldn't compile, so the agent silently substituted exact-match string lookup and matched the reported metrics without training anything. A segmentation task where compute limits forced small runs in which a simple encoder beat the U-Net, inverting the paper's central claim — and the agent reported that inversion as the result. Premature halting was the most common failure at fifty-three percent, mostly agents hitting memory ceilings and just stopping.

Their fix is a reference-anchored contract — claims, protocols, required components, baselines and metrics as structured constraints bounding what the agent may do. It wins their composite, but the winning system is the authors' own, so take the ranking with a grain of salt. What survives that caveat is the taxonomy, the base rate, and one number cutting across every system: on the dimension measuring whether the write-up contains real scientific reasoning rather than just correct numbers, every framework scored below fifteen out of a hundred. The agents can run the experiment. None can yet say what it means.

That theme — an algorithm that looks right on the metric and doesn't survive contact with practice — carries into a genuinely enjoyable exchange in the Parkinson's literature. Last year a group published a decision tree predicting the optimal deep brain stimulation contact from bipolar subthalamic local field potentials, reporting around eighty-seven percent accuracy for its top two candidates across centers. A group at Amsterdam UMC implemented it on fifty-one independent hemispheres and asked two questions the original didn't.

First: can clinicians actually pick the single best contact from the algorithm's two candidates? Three experienced raters at one center, same recordings, and overall Fleiss kappa was point two four. The reason is structural and rather beautiful. These devices record bipolar — the difference between two contacts. A large beta peak on the one-to-three channel tells you there's a big differential; it does not tell you whether the source sits at contact one, at contact three, or at contact two in between. Both readings are physiologically defensible, the tree contains no rule to arbitrate, and so a step requiring subjective judgment sits inside what looks on paper like an objective algorithm.

Second, and sharper: they compared the algorithm to the dumbest possible heuristic — just take the two middle contacts, which at their center sit inside motor subthalamic nucleus by design, using seven-tesla tractography-guided targeting with intraoperative CT verification. For the imaging-defined optimal contact, the heuristic got point eight zero and the tree point eight two. For the clinically chosen contact, point nine eight versus point nine four, no significant difference. Their own explanation is the honest one: beta is strongest precisely where the lead is best placed, so in a well-targeted implant the electrophysiological and anatomical routes converge by construction. The answer was determined before either method was applied.

The reply, published the same week, lands real hits. The algorithm outputs two contacts, not one, and the single-contact step is exactly where subjectivity entered — their own procedure takes absolute maximum beta power regardless of whether a distinct peak exists, precisely to avoid rater judgment. And a cohort where the middle contacts are correct by design is close to the least informative test bed available; the interesting question is centers where targeting is less certain. Both sides land in the same place: decision support, not replacement, validated multi-center with recording and clinical state matched.

Staying with deep brain stimulation but on the therapy side, a small, conceptually important feasibility study of movement-responsive stimulation. Four patients, eight independently optimized hemispheres, all with sensing-enabled implants carrying subthalamic depth leads plus an electrocorticography strip over the central sulcus. Decode volitional forearm movement from sensorimotor cortex in real time, and raise amplitude only while the patient moves — the point being that constant stimulation forces a compromise between suppressing bradykinesia and provoking dyskinesia, and movement-contingent stimulation gives you the high amplitude only when it's needed.

The decoder ran on the implanted device at eighty-four percent balanced accuracy. On a tablet tapping task, movement-responsive stimulation cut inter-tap duration by thirty-two milliseconds versus constant stimulation and sixty-eight versus an inverted control that raised amplitude during rest — with no change in error rate, so the speed didn't come from sloppy dyskinetic movement. The more interesting result is cumulative: under constant stimulation, inter-tap duration climbed steadily across the five sets, bradykinetic slowing within a single session. Movement-responsive stimulation abolished that drift, accompanied by a progressive rise in resting motor cortex beta power and motor-to-somatosensory coherence, which the inverted control did not produce.

Then they took it home. Eighteen days, counterbalanced, thirty-six blinded half-day sessions per subject, decoder retrained on unconstrained daily activity. Wearable bradykinesia scores fell by one point nine units with no increase in dyskinesia and no change in tremor — and bradykinesia kept improving over hours, then drifted back after switching to constant stimulation. That's the plasticity claim, consistent with rodent work pairing striatal stimulation with fast movement. Four patients, and the most severely affected carried much of the group effect; within-subject significance was lost for the other three. So it's a feasibility study making a mechanistic claim it can't yet carry — but well controlled, with the right inverted-stimulation arm.

From stimulation to measurement. A preprint finally addresses something odd about brain-based pain biomarkers: everything in that literature measures pain going up. The Neurologic Pain Signature and its stimulus-independent successor were both built on increasing pain. Nobody asked whether they detect pain going down, which is what every analgesic trial actually needs.

The design is clean. Capsaicin plus individually calibrated tonic heat to establish ongoing pain, then transient temperature increases and decreases to push pain both ways within the same person, same context, same session, in sixty-one healthy participants. Both existing signatures tracked ratings well overall and cleanly separated pain increase from baseline. Neither could distinguish relief from baseline. For the Neurologic Pain Signature the directional test wasn't merely null — in the clinical cohort the signature was significantly higher during relief. The stimulus-independent signature failing too is the informative part: the missing ingredient isn't higher-order modulation in general. Relief is its own state.

So they trained a signature on change relative to baseline rather than intensity along one axis. It cross-validated at forty-six percent of within-person variance, then generalized to fifty-eight people with chronic back pain, classifying increase versus relief at seventy-eight percent accuracy from the sign of the prediction alone. The regional decomposition makes it more than a better regression. Pain increase loads on the usual nociceptive machinery — secondary somatosensory cortex, insula, thalamus, dorsal anterior cingulate. Relief loads on orbitofrontal and ventromedial prefrontal cortex and parietal valuation regions, with periaqueductal gray carrying the largest weight and flipping sign across conditions. Relief is an appetitive valuation state, not the absence of nociceptive input — an old theoretical claim that now has a neural signature attached, and if it replicates, a stratification tool for analgesic development.

Staying with biomarkers, Molecular Psychiatry has the most thorough characterization yet of cerebrospinal fluid tau phosphorylated at threonine two-oh-five. The reason this residue matters is post-mortem: the antibody defining Braak staging targets an epitope centered on serine two-oh-two and threonine two-oh-five, and only two-oh-five behaves as a usable fluid marker. Until recently you could measure it only by mass spectrometry. This is a high-throughput immunoassay across two thousand sixty-nine samples from both BioFINDER cohorts.

In amyloid-positive participants, baseline levels explain twenty-eight percent of variance in amyloid PET and thirty-five percent in medial temporal tau PET. More usefully, baseline predicts subsequent accumulation — forty-four percent for amyloid, thirty-three percent for tau — and longitudinal change tracks cortical thinning and cognitive decline better than the cross-sectional values. The staging application is the point: order the markers as amyloid ratio, then p-tau two-one-seven, then p-tau two-oh-five, and the final stage, defined by two-oh-five positivity, carries a hazard ratio of six point four for progression to dementia. That's the first longitudinal data on this marker in sporadic disease. The assay is research-grade and single-center, which matters for trial enrichment, but the biological ordering it implies is now much better supported.

Now a piece of pure ion channel pharmacology, and the most genuinely novel mechanism in today's batch. NALCN is the sodium leak channel — a tonically open inward conductance that sets resting potential and supports pacemaking. It's known to be inhibited by GABA-B and dopamine receptors through Gi/o. Nobody had asked whether metabotropic glutamate receptors do the same. In cerebellar unipolar brush cells, group two mGluR activation inhibits the NALCN current, and it's G-protein dependent — blocked by loading the cell with a non-hydrolyzable GDP analog, absent in NALCN knockouts.

Think about what that means as a form of inhibition. Conventional synaptic inhibition opens a channel — chloride in, potassium out, or shunting, which drops input resistance. This closes an already-open channel carrying inward current. So the cell hyperpolarizes and its input resistance rises simultaneously. It fires less, and at the same time every subsequent excitatory postsynaptic potential arriving there gets larger and decays more slowly. That's a genuinely different computational operation, and glutamate initiates it.

Two things make it better. Of the two unipolar brush cell subtypes, the glutamate-inhibited one carries the larger, tonic NALCN current, and deleting NALCN converts its regular spontaneous firing into the irregular pattern of the other subtype — so the leak current enforces firing regularity, not just resting potential. And the selectivity is sharp: group three mGluRs sit on those same dendrites, GABA-B receptors on the other subtype, and neither touches NALCN — both work through potassium channels instead. Same Gi/o pathway, same cell, different effector. Given that group two mGluRs and NALCN are co-expressed in olfactory bulb, hippocampus, thalamus and cortex, this is unlikely to stay a cerebellar curiosity.

Next, serotonin in orbitofrontal cortex and alcohol drinking, with a sex divergence running through every experiment. In male mice, serotonin hyperpolarizes orbitofrontal pyramidal neurons, and three weeks of binge-like drinking abolishes that — at twenty-four hours after the last session and still at seven to ten days abstinent. In females, serotonin had no consistent effect to begin with. In rhesus macaques with drinking histories they found the mirror image: the serotonin response tracked lifetime ethanol intake in females and not at all in males.

In situ hybridization pinned it to the 5-HT1A receptor — transcript down in both sexes after drinking, 5-HT2A unchanged. Then the causal test: conditional 5-HT1A knockdown in orbitofrontal excitatory neurons increased ethanol intake in males but not females. And the most translationally interesting finding — knockdown increased quinine-adulterated ethanol drinking in both sexes. That's the standard operationalization of drinking despite negative consequences, the feature of alcohol use disorder that plain consumption measures miss. So orbitofrontal 5-HT1A tone constrains compulsive-like drinking independently of baseline intake. The monkey arm is very small — eight cells from four female animals — so treat the sex dissociation as a hypothesis; the compulsivity result stands on the mouse genetics.

Related, and grimmer: a rat study comparing thirty-day abstinence from intravenous heroin versus cocaine self-administration, using proteomics on medial prefrontal synaptosomes plus targeted metabolomics plus patch clamp. Heroin abstinence produced coordinated downregulation across essentially the entire mitochondrial energy apparatus — all three pyruvate dehydrogenase subunits, TCA enzymes, all four electron transport chain complexes, ATP synthase and import machinery — with the master mitochondrial biogenesis coactivator predicted inhibited upstream. The metabolomics fits a pyruvate dehydrogenase bottleneck, with upstream glycolytic intermediates piling up. And the physiology matches: suppressed intrinsic excitability and reduced excitatory drive in layer five pyramidal neurons, thirty days out. Cocaine abstinence produced almost none of this, showing a cytoskeletal signature instead.

The authors are unusually careful about their own convergence claim — the proteomics is synaptosome-enriched, the metabolomics whole tissue from a different cohort, and steady-state metabolite levels can't distinguish increased glycolysis from reduced downstream use. But if opioid and psychostimulant abstinence really do leave different molecular scars on the same circuit, that argues against treating relapse vulnerability as one mechanism with one drug target.

Today's stress item is about how a memory becomes less specific as it ages. Contextual fear memories generalize over weeks, modeling fear overgeneralization in PTSD, and prefrontal glucocorticoid signaling sits at the junction of two processes — terminating the stress response, and consolidating what just happened. This group put mifepristone, the glucocorticoid receptor antagonist, into dorsomedial prefrontal cortex of rats immediately after contextual fear conditioning, then measured corticosterone, fear at two and fourteen days, and c-Fos coactivation networks at retrieval.

Blocking the receptors accelerated corticosterone decay between thirty and sixty minutes without changing total output — the opposite of the classical negative-feedback prediction, and the authors say so. At two days, nothing. At fourteen days, mifepristone rats froze more in both the training context and a novel one, concentrated in the first two minutes of exposure. Be honest about the size: the overall generalization index was not significant, and the shift in the proportion of animals classified as generalizers was only a trend.

The network analysis is where it gets interesting, and there's a methodological choice worth stealing. Binarizing c-Fos correlation matrices into a graph usually means picking an arbitrary significance cutoff; these authors instead built a null by randomly reassigning animals to surrogate groups and thresholded at its seventieth percentile. Under that calibration, vehicle animals reorganize between two and fourteen days with no change in global network metrics — connections redistribute, integration doesn't. Mifepristone animals densify instead, with most regions collapsing into a single community. And the subnetwork balance flips: vehicle retrieval at fourteen days is salience-network dominant, mifepristone shifts it to default-mode dominant, and hub status migrates from hippocampal and amygdalar nodes to anterior cingulate, prelimbic and retrosplenial cortex. Prelimbic changes role most cleanly — in vehicle animals its activity tracks the acute stress response, and under receptor blockade that coupling vanishes and it tracks the generalization index instead. Males only, correlational networks, effect confined to the opening minutes of a test. But it's a specific proposal for how the endocrine tail of a stressful experience gets written into the network that retrieves it two weeks later.

We'll finish with something practical. Yesterday we covered a paper arguing that reliability and disease sensitivity are dissociable properties of EEG foundation model representations. Today, a companion problem: those models generalize poorly to new clinical sites, and full fine-tuning is out of reach for most hospitals. Can you continue self-supervised training while updating only a small fraction of parameters? Two models with different pretraining objectives, one contrastive and one masked-reconstruction, on abnormality detection, event classification and pediatric seizure detection.

Updating nine percent of parameters beat linear probing everywhere, and on the out-of-distribution seizure task the gain was thirty-six points of area under the ROC curve — precision-recall area went from one and a half percent, essentially random, to thirty-one percent. That gap is the entire argument for why you cannot deploy these models off the shelf.

Two secondary findings are the ones I'd act on. Holding compute fixed, performance peaks at twenty to fifty percent of the available unlabeled data with each window seen about twelve times — you need repetition to consolidate, not variety. And when they fixed total window count and varied how many patients it came from, performance didn't move. Total temporal coverage matters; patient diversity, within these ranges, does not. Which means a single clinic with a modest cohort and long recordings can adapt a foundation model to its own hardware and population — an unusually actionable result for anyone trying to get one of these into a clinical workflow.

That's eleven. Today's throughline, if you want one: three separate fields discovered this week that their standard evaluation metric is orthogonal to the thing they actually care about — expression similarity versus mechanism, code execution versus scientific fidelity, and a pain signature that only runs in one direction. Worth asking what the equivalent blind spot is in whatever you're benchmarking. See you tomorrow.
