## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. I'm your host, and this is your daily pass through CNS pharmacology, psychiatric and neurology drug discovery, and the computational machinery closing in on both. Twelve items today, and the thread running through nearly all of them is verification — not as a virtue, but as a measurement. Several papers today report a headline number and then, in the same paper, report how much of it survives being checked: a decoder that might be memorizing combinations, two estimators of the same quantity that disagree on its sign, a quarter of a training set that turns out to be censored, a drug-interaction signal resting on five case reports.

Start with the best result of the week, in Nature Neuroscience, from the UCSF BRAVO trial cohort. Brain-computer interfaces for communication have been built almost entirely around one channel at a time — decode speech, or decode a hand. But natural communication is concurrent and multi-effector. You talk and you gesture, and listeners use both. If someone has lost speech and limb function to stroke or to amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, restoring one channel restores a fraction of the bandwidth.

These authors asked whether a single high-density electrocorticography array over sensorimotor cortex can carry both. Working with three chronically implanted participants — two with severe vocal-tract and limb paralysis, one with severe vocal-tract paralysis and moderate limb involvement — they first showed that one array samples distinguishable representations of head and eye, hand and arm, and speech and orofacial movements, classifying well above a chance level of roughly two percent. Then they ran parallel speech and gesture decoders to drive a personalized virtual avatar, with participants attempting the two either simultaneously or in isolation.

Two findings matter. The first is practical and slightly annoying for anyone building these systems: models trained only on isolated movements degraded when the participant did both at once, and models trained only on simultaneous data degraded on isolated attempts. Training on both was better than either. Simultaneous behaviour is not the sum of its parts at the cortical level — the representations shift when the efforts overlap.

The second finding makes this more than an engineering demonstration. The obvious worry is that a decoder trained on simultaneous trials learns specific pairings — that "hello plus wave" becomes effectively one class. So they held pairings out, removing from the gesture decoder every simultaneous trial containing one particular phrase, and vice versa for speech. Each model still saw all individual classes in isolation, just never that combination. Accuracy on held-out pairings was statistically indistinguishable from accuracy on trained ones. The decoders are composing, not memorizing — the property you need for this to scale past a demo vocabulary, and the specific claim most multi-effector work has asserted rather than tested.

Staying with language and cortex but moving from decoding speech to diagnosing from it, a preprint asks whether psychosis involves a deficit of information compression in connected speech. Two findings have circulated separately in computational psychiatry — that language-model embeddings of psychotic speech have lower intrinsic dimensionality, and that the same speech carries higher surprisal. These authors argue they are two faces of one thing, a general failure of information compression, and that the compressing machinery is grammatical organization.

Their instrument is a measure called surprisal difference: the surprisal you would estimate from word frequency alone, minus the surprisal a contextual language model assigns. Frequency knows only lexical commonness; a contextual model knows structure. The gap between them indexes how much predictive work syntax is doing. In one hundred forty-four Turkish speakers — fifty-six with chronic schizophrenia, thirty-three with first-episode psychosis, seventeen with schizoaffective disorder, thirty-eight controls — surprisal difference was significantly attenuated in all three clinical groups, and it held at every window size when transcripts were chopped to matched lengths, so this is not a restatement of the well-known reduction in speech quantity. Intrinsic dimensionality was reduced in chronic schizophrenia and first-episode psychosis but not in schizoaffective disorder, the least symptomatic group, and syntactic complexity predicted surprisal difference, which is the link the hypothesis needs.

Here is the wrinkle, and credit to them for putting it in the results rather than a limitations paragraph. They estimated intrinsic dimensionality two ways, and the two estimators disagree on the sign of its relationship with surprisal difference — one gives about plus zero point two seven, the other about minus zero point four five. They chase it down by varying neighbourhood scale and show the effect is negative very locally and positive at broader scales. Defensible, but it means the compression deficit is a claim about a particular scale of the embedding manifold, and anyone replicating needs to say which estimator they used. The geometry is doing a lot of work here and it is not yet stable.

Third item stays with language models in psychiatry and moves from measurement to safety. A preprint introduces K-Bench, a clinician-calibrated benchmark for large language models in high-risk mental health conversations. People already use these systems as de facto crisis support, and existing evaluations are mostly single-turn and mostly not built by clinicians.

They evaluated one hundred twenty-five model configurations — thirty-three base models from fourteen providers — across two hundred multi-turn vignettes spanning suicide, self-harm, domestic violence, substance misuse and no-risk presentations. Two design decisions deserve attention. They validated their synthetic patients against a corpus of real voice-based mental health conversations and showed substantial overlap in embedding space, which most synthetic-benchmark papers skip. And they measured their human ceiling before trusting it: six clinicians agreed with each other on about eighty-nine percent of item comparisons overall, but only about seventy-five percent on risk severity, dropping to roughly sixty-six percent for suicide. Their frozen automated judge then matched clinician consensus on about ninety-four percent of comparisons, and on severity was more consistent with that consensus than the clinicians were with one another.

Be careful what that shows. Not that the judge is a better clinician — that the modal rating is a more reproducible target than any individual rater, which is a statement about rubric noise. But it is exactly the number you need before letting a judge stand in for clinicians at scale, and almost nobody publishes it. Two substantive results: therapeutic prompting helped, but the gains concentrated in the weaker configurations rather than lifting the good ones, and raising reasoning effort produced no average improvement at all. Safety here looks like a behavioural disposition, not a reasoning budget.

From evaluating models to a blunter question about them. A preprint tested reference fabrication across twenty-six models from eight developers, spanning releases from early twenty twenty-three to mid twenty twenty-six. They built sixty-nine passages from published biomedical papers across ten domains, removed one reference from each, and asked each model to supply it. Fifty of the removed references were ones this group had previously identified as fabrications already sitting in the published literature; nineteen were verified real, as controls.

Across all models, fifty-five percent of responses were fabricated, and only about fifteen percent were correct in every bibliographic field. The range ran from roughly ten percent fabrication — from a model that declined more than half the prompts — up to ninety-eight percent from a small open-weight model that produced no verifiable reference at all. Models released in twenty twenty-six were better but nowhere near solved, at about thirty-five percent fabrication.

The finding I would change my behaviour over is the decoupling of two failure modes. Two models in the same family produced nearly identical rates of verifiable references — around seventy-seven percent each — but named the authors correctly in seventy-nine percent versus twenty-nine percent of those verifiable cases. One finds real papers and describes them accurately; the other finds real papers and misstates who wrote them. A reference that resolves is not a reference that is right, so verify the metadata, not the existence.

That is the hinge into three papers on agentic target discovery, all of which arrive at the same conclusion about what their real deliverable is.

The most substantial is AutoScreen, from a Stanford and Princeton collaboration with a heavyweight author list. The bottleneck it targets is prioritization: screens and association studies now nominate hundreds of candidates, and the constraint has moved from generating data to deciding what to follow up. AutoScreen runs two modes — designing perturbation libraries de novo from a free-text research description, and re-ranking the hits of a screen you already ran — using specialist agents that search more than twenty-six biomedical databases and then synthesize the evidence.

Benchmarked on three hundred twenty genome-scale CRISPR screens, it recovered about nineteen percent more validated hits in its top hundred than the strongest agent baseline. But the wet lab is what earns trust. In a leukemia surfaceome screen, re-ranking pulled three natural-killer-resistance genes from raw ranks of one hundred eighteen, eighty-one and over thirteen hundred up to five, forty-four and six hundred fifty-nine — and all three then validated individually in tumour-killing assays with primary human natural killer cells. In two prospective cytotoxic T-cell screens, its designed libraries recovered about seventy-seven percent of the hits called by consensus of two gold-standard pipelines.

The authors' own framing is the interesting part. They argue the defining feature is auditability — the pipeline records which evidence anchor and which fusion step produced each ranking — and say explicitly that they regard this, not predictive accuracy alone, as the prerequisite for routine use in experimental design. They also note that gains on general reasoning leaderboards do not transfer straightforwardly to candidate selection in a specific biological context.

Its companion is a preprint on Emet, an AI research environment applied to lung squamous cell carcinoma. The disease choice is the argument: squamous lung is mutationally complex, has no dominant actionable driver, and has a long graveyard of failed single-agent targeted trials. Emet couples a knowledge graph of more than one and a half billion triples to over a hundred specialized databases, with an agentic research director running a graph-of-thoughts search and committing each round to persistent memory.

The result I like is that its top-ranked hypotheses were two-target combinations rather than single targets — the output structure itself recognized the redundancy. They advanced the four highest-ranked combinations to dose-matrix testing in two squamous cell lines, and two of four produced combination effects exceeding either single agent. Dual inhibition of C-D-C-seven and P-K-M-Y-T-one — using TAK-nine-three-one and R-P-six-three-zero-six, clinical-stage inhibitors of those replication-stress kinases — gave significant synergy in both lines. The second pair, a U-S-P-thirteen inhibitor with the P-I-three-kinase-alpha inhibitor alpelisib, was merely additive, but immunoblotting confirmed the predicted mechanism: time-dependent depletion of M-C-L-one, c-Myc and S-O-X-two driven by the deubiquitinase arm.

Two of four is the honest number, and it is what makes this useful. Four for four would invite the question of how much was already in the literature.

Third in the trio, a preprint called HypoEvolve, about collaboration structure rather than individual agent competence. We now have a pile of systems pairing language-model agents with evolutionary search and no clean way to say how much of the benefit comes from the agents' reasoning versus from how their outputs are combined. So they wrap specialist agents in an explicit generational genetic algorithm over a population of hypotheses, with defined crossover and mutation operators, making the collaboration itself the manipulated variable.

They evaluate on drug repurposing, scored against dependency data for target selectivity and a curated target-disease association resource. Against single-pass generation, selectivity went from about zero point zero four to zero point one seven and association from about zero point one six to zero point four three, winning on the large majority of cancer types, with tree-of-thoughts the strongest baseline and still well behind. It held up on twenty-seven held-out cancer types, and most final hypotheses came from crossover and mutation rather than being carried forward unchanged — so the search is doing work, not decorating a good initial guess. The caveat they state themselves is the standing problem with this genre: the advantage was largest on genetic association evidence, which is encouraging because it reaches beyond documented drug-disease pairs, but pretraining exposure to that evidence cannot be excluded.

Which brings me to the paper to read straight after those three, as a corrective. A preprint on drug-target affinity prediction asks a narrow, unglamorous question: models are trained to predict inhibition constants, but medicinal chemistry decisions are often made on half-maximal inhibitory concentrations, and those are not the same quantity. Can you align them empirically without pretending they are interchangeable?

Qualified yes — but the finding I will remember is incidental to the main claim. Inspecting their training set, twenty-three thousand of ninety-two thousand affinity labels, just over a quarter, were exactly the same value: a rounded censoring artifact from assays that never resolved a real number. Rebuilding from curated binding assays with censored records removed cut median fold error from roughly sixteen-fold to nine-fold on a kinase panel and from fifteen-fold to about eight-fold on a non-kinase panel, before any calibration at all. Similarity-guided calibration then brought the non-kinase panel to about three-fold.

And then they do what I wish more method papers did. Because cleaning removed a large share of what the calibration appeared to fix, they decline to call it a mechanistic conversion, describing it instead as a target- and chemistry-dependent empirical offset. They report that replicate variability in the underlying public data is itself between two-and-a-half and three-and-a-third-fold, which puts a floor under the remaining error. They flag that both coefficients of determination on their kinase panel were negative, and they name the targets where cleaning made things worse. The take-home for anyone building on public affinity data: a meaningful fraction of your labels may not be measurements, and the gain you attribute to your architecture may be available from a filter.

Two clinical pharmacology items, and they pair. The first asks whether anti-amyloid antibodies interact with antithrombotic drugs to produce amyloid-related imaging abnormalities or intracranial haemorrhage. This is live — patients receiving lecanemab or donanemab are frequently on aspirin or an anticoagulant, and the trials were not powered for it. They analyzed roughly eleven million adverse event reports, of which four hundred forty-three were classified as any imaging abnormality, testing interaction signals under four complementary statistical models.

The individual-drug results are a sanity check that the method works: all three antibodies threw enormous signals for every imaging-abnormality outcome, while cholinesterase inhibitors, memantine and every antithrombotic alone threw none. For interactions, aspirin combined with the antibodies as a class was positive in three of four models, including the oedema and haemosiderin subtypes, and lecanemab with aspirin held in two. For intracranial haemorrhage, tissue plasminogen activator with lecanemab was positive in all four.

Now the part that determines how to read it. That thrombolytic signal, positive in all four models and the most alarming number in the paper, rests on five reports. The authors say so plainly, and note a donanemab-anticoagulant signal came from a single report. Disproportionality analysis is hypothesis-generating; with five cases, four models agreeing tells you the models share an input, not that the finding is robust. The aspirin signal has enough behind it to be worth acting on, and the actionable version is narrow: review whether the aspirin indication is still justified, and be cautious about thrombolysis during anti-amyloid treatment.

Its companion is a careful characterization of a humanized amyloid knock-in mouse — the sort of paper that gets cited a thousand times and discussed never. Transgenic overexpression models compress pathology into the first few months and carry artifactual sex effects, leaving almost no therapeutic window for preclinical testing. These authors profiled homozygous knock-in animals from four to nineteen months with whole-brain three-dimensional plaque imaging, vascular amyloid quantification, transcriptomics and plasma biomarkers.

Plaques were sparse at four months, accumulated roughly linearly from six to twelve, then plateaued out to nineteen. Sex differences appeared only in midbrain, medulla and pons, with none in cortex, hippocampus or cerebellum — a direct contrast with the widely used overexpression lines. By twelve months the brain transcriptome aligned with human co-expression modules from post-mortem consortium data, and plasma p-tau two-seventeen, glial fibrillary acidic protein and S-N-A-P-twenty-five all rose with age. At twelve months the animals retained associative memory but showed memory interference and loss of C-A-one dendritic spines that was worse near plaques — a subtle phenotype, which is arguably the point.

And here is why it pairs with the previous item: vascular and leptomeningeal amyloid appeared in these mice by eight months. Cerebral amyloid angiopathy is the substrate that makes anti-amyloid immunotherapy bleed. A knock-in model that develops it on a defined schedule, with a six-month window before plaque load saturates, is exactly the platform you would want for the mechanistic questions the pharmacovigilance paper just raised. The mice are available without restriction from Jackson.

Two shorter items to close. In Neuropharmacology, a study on whether MDMA reduces alcohol drinking, and by what mechanism. MDMA-assisted psychotherapy has shown early promise for alcohol use disorder, with the obvious concerns about safety and misuse liability, and essentially no mechanistic account. These authors used Marchigian Sardinian alcohol-preferring rats, a line genetically selected for excessive drinking in which the dynorphin-kappa-opioid and nociceptin systems are constitutively dysregulated. Five days of systemic MDMA rapidly reduced ethanol consumption without affecting anxiety-like behaviour. Repeated ethanol had upregulated dynorphin and shifted nociceptin-system transcripts in amygdala and ventral tegmental area, and MDMA reduced dynorphin and kappa-receptor expression and modulated the nociceptin genes in those same regions — but not in nucleus accumbens.

Then they did the experiment that makes it mechanism rather than correlation, microinjecting MDMA directly into central amygdala, and into ventral tegmental area. Central amygdala reduced ethanol intake; ventral tegmental area did nothing. The effect localizes to the structure where the peptidergic dysregulation sits, and not to the canonical dopaminergic node most people would have guessed. If MDMA's anti-drinking effect runs through central amygdala kappa-opioid tone rather than mesolimbic dopamine or the subjective experience the psychotherapy model emphasizes, that opens a target that does not require giving anyone MDMA.

And to finish, something lighter that is quietly relevant to every trial any of us run. A preprint looked at how placebo-induced expectations reshape resting-state connectivity, in sixty healthy female participants given a placebo intervention targeting hunger. Resting state, note — no task, nothing to do. Participants who more strongly expected the placebo to reduce their hunger showed attenuated coupling between medial prefrontal cortex and angular gyrus within the default mode network; those with hunger-increasing expectations showed attenuated coupling between ventromedial and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, plus strengthened dorsolateral-prefrontal-to-thalamus connectivity.

Effect sizes are modest, the sample is single-sex, and hunger is a soft outcome. But the structural point is useful: a belief about a substance that contains nothing reorganizes intrinsic coupling among valuation, cognitive control and interoceptive relay regions, with no stimulus present at all. We routinely treat the placebo arm as the thing we subtract — and in CNS indications where the placebo response is the central obstacle to showing efficacy, the mechanism of what is being subtracted is not a side quest.

One cortical implant carrying speech and gesture at once, composing rather than memorizing; a compression deficit in psychotic speech whose sign depends on which dimensionality estimator you pick; a safety benchmark that measured its clinicians' disagreement before trusting its judge, and found extra reasoning effort buys nothing; fifty-five percent of biomedical references fabricated; three agentic discovery systems converging on auditability rather than accuracy as the deliverable; a quarter of a public affinity training set turning out to be censoring artifact; an aspirin signal worth acting on and a thrombolysis signal resting on five reports; MDMA cutting alcohol intake from the central amygdala and not the ventral tegmental area; and a placebo rewiring prefrontal coupling at rest.
