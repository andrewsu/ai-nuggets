## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. I'm your host, and this is your daily pass through CNS pharmacology, psychiatric and neurology drug discovery, and the computational machinery closing in on both. Eleven items today, and the thread running through them is regimen and route — the recurring discovery that the same target, the same model, the same intervention gives you a different answer depending on how and where you engage it. A drug that works centrally and fails peripherally. A drug that helps acutely and hurts chronically, or the reverse. A model that scores identically on two runs while doing two different things to a patient.

Start with the most consequential paper of the day, in Nature Medicine, and it is about that last case. A group built a fully on-premise clinical diagnostic agent and then, more interestingly, asked which observable signal tells you when to trust it. The framing they use is worth adopting: two separate kinds of trust. Operational trust is about governance — where the weights live, who can audit the logs, whether the model silently changes version under you. Decisional trust is about whether a specific output, on a specific case, at a specific moment, is reliable enough to act on.

The architecture is a physician agent talking to a patient agent, retrieving evidence through tool calls, returning a diagnosis plus a reasoning trace. On a seven-condition benchmark derived from intensive-care records, the best open-weight model they ran locally hit ninety percent accuracy against ninety-point-seven for a frontier cloud model — within a percentage point, on hardware an institution controls. On a second four-category abdominal benchmark, eighty-three-point-eight percent against a previous best open-weight result of seventy-point-five. The operational trust gap is now largely closed.

The decisional trust half is the part I'd want anyone designing evaluation to sit with. They computed three families of confidence signal: the model's own internal token likelihood, linguistic hedging in the output, and behavioural stability — simply, does the agent produce the same diagnosis when you run the identical case five times. Behavioural consistency was by far the best discriminator of correctness, with an area under the curve of point eight-six against point seven-five for internal likelihood, and that difference was statistically robust. Set a consistency threshold of point nine and you retain about half your cases at ninety-nine percent diagnostic accuracy, deferring the rest to a human. That is a usable triage rule.

Then they stress-tested it by un-grounding the patient agent, withholding the history so it could confabulate testimony. Accuracy collapsed. Consistency fell with it. Internal likelihood and linguistic certainty did not — in pneumonia, accuracy dropped while both of those went up and stayed above point nine. So the two signals everyone reaches for first, because they're free, are the two that lie to you exactly when the evidence is thin. One more finding because it runs against fashion: adding a differential-diagnosis critic agent did not help. Eighty-seven-point-one percent with the critic, eighty-eight-point-four without. More agents is not a method.

Which sets up the second item, a preprint that takes the consistency question and pushes it past diagnosis into action. The observation is sharp enough to state in one sentence: a clinical agent benchmark can report the identical verdict on identical inputs while the agent files a materially different order on each run. Diagnosis is a string you can compare. An order is a coded value, a dose, an analyte, and an endpoint — and benchmarks score one run and compare the string.

They built what they call same-input rerun. Replay the task with every input held fixed, then compare the orders instead of the score. A thousand runs across fifty tasks from the write-capable families of an existing agent benchmark, two small open-weight models quantized to four bits, two temperatures. Under the eight-billion-parameter model at temperature point seven: all forty-three ordering groups emitted a different set of orders across five identical runs. Twenty-six emitted the order on some runs and not others — the medication either got ordered or it didn't, same input. Twenty-eight recorded a different coded value, dose, or analyte. And in twenty-two of those forty-three, the benchmark reported the same failing verdict for materially different behaviour.

The detail that should worry anyone integrating one of these: orders reached different endpoints across runs, and in at least one case the record server rejected the write while the agent was told it had succeeded. That is not a reliability problem, that is a silent-failure problem, and no amount of accuracy reporting surfaces it. The authors are careful that their rates don't generalize — small quantized models, one benchmark — and they're right to be. But the existence claim is established, and the methodological demand follows: report action-level stability across repeated runs, and make your environment feedback execution-faithful.

Third item moves from trusting agent behaviour to trusting agent reasoning, and it's the cleanest experimental design I've seen on hypothesis generation. The question: when you hand a language model a knowledge-graph path connecting an enzyme to a disease, does it reason along that path, or does it read the two endpoints, ignore everything in between, and write something fluent from parametric memory?

They fused three databases — the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes, Rhea, and UniProt — into one biochemical graph of about seventeen thousand nodes, sampled five hundred fifty multi-hop paths from enzyme sources to rare disease endpoints, and confirmed that ninety-four-and-a-half percent of those source-disease pairs never co-occur in the published literature. Then six models generated thirteen thousand two hundred hypotheses under four conditions: source only, full path with compression, full path with interpretation, and — the crucial control — endpoints only, no path.

The result is uncomfortable and useful. Endpoint-only generation produced the highest-scoring hypotheses on the overall rubric. Give a model two names and it writes something persuasive. But those hypotheses were less grounded in the actual evidence, while full-path conditions produced hypotheses more consistent with known mechanistic relationships. They call it evidence-disciplined reasoning, and then they verify it properly by shuffling the intermediate steps while pinning the endpoints. Evidence grounding dropped by about point seven-nine on their scale, highly significant — so the models genuinely were using path structure, not decoration.

The lesson for anyone building a target-discovery pipeline: rubric score and evidence grounding are different quantities that can move in opposite directions, and if you optimize the first you will ship confident mechanisms nobody can trace.

One more on the foundation-model side, and it's a methodological argument rather than a result. There are now foundation models for human brain recordings, and they are evaluated almost entirely by signal reconstruction, short-horizon forecasting, and transfer to downstream classification. None of those tell you whether the model stays valid when you actively perturb the system — which is the only regime that matters if you want to use one for neuromodulation, closed-loop stimulation, or predicting a drug response.

So this group defines perturbational validity: does the model preserve the conditional distribution of future trajectories given the current state and a controlled input, after limited adaptation to a new system. They demonstrate it in a simulation of stochastic bistable systems — two otherwise identical networks, one pretrained on passive trajectories, one on input-driven trajectories, then freeze the shared weights and adapt only a three-dimensional embedding per new system. With as few as two to five system-specific evaluations, the perturbationally pretrained model recovered controlled flow, landscape geometry, and dose-transition curves better. And they decompose why: input excitation, not coverage of transition states, was doing the work, cutting controlled-flow error nearly twofold in five of five runs.

It's a toy simulation and they say so, and the advantage shrinks as calibration data grows, so it's a few-shot transfer claim. But the framework is the contribution, and the field has no standard way to ask this question at all.

Now to the wet side, and we'll stay on cannabinoids for two items. In Neuropsychopharmacology, the first study to measure prefrontal neurometabolites during cannabis cue reactivity rather than at rest. Twenty-two people with moderate-to-severe cannabis use disorder, nineteen matched controls, and spectral-edited magnetic resonance spectroscopy — the HERMES sequence, which resolves GABA and the combined glutamate-glutamine signal in the same acquisition — from a dorsolateral prefrontal voxel while participants viewed cannabis or neutral cues.

At rest, the groups were indistinguishable on GABA, consistent with a prior literature that has mostly found nothing. Under cue reactivity they diverged: the cannabis group showed a significant rise in GABA during cannabis cues relative to neutral cues, with a large partial eta-squared of point three-one. Controls showed nothing. The glutamate-glutamine signal was flat throughout. Craving moved as expected, sharply up in the cannabis group.

Two reasons this matters more than the effect size suggests. The first is the design point: a group difference that is invisible at rest and appears only under provocation. If your biomarker programme measures tonic neurochemistry, you may be measuring the wrong thing. The second is where it points therapeutically — if cue-evoked prefrontal GABA is the dynamic quantity, then facilitatory neuromodulation protocols, intermittent theta-burst rather than inhibitory ones, are the ones matched to recalibrating it. Caveats are real and stated: the spectroscopy GABA signal is a pooled tissue concentration, not a measure of synaptic inhibition, the cannabis group smoked substantially more nicotine, and the sample is small.

The companion is a preprint from Miriam Melis's group in Cagliari, and it is the most mechanistically satisfying thing I read this week. The setup: endocannabinoid signalling at glutamatergic inputs to ventral tegmental dopamine neurons normally produces depolarization-induced suppression of excitation — you depolarize the postsynaptic cell, it releases endocannabinoid retrogradely, presynaptic CB1 receptors clamp glutamate release for a few seconds. Prenatal THC exposure is a known risk factor for psychotic-like outcomes in offspring, and this group has previously shown a male-specific latent vulnerability in a rat model, with elevated dopamine neuron activity.

What they find is not a weaker version of normal plasticity. It's a substitution. Prenatally exposed males showed a larger apparent suppression, around fifty percent versus twenty in controls — but it persisted, not for seconds but for at least thirty minutes, and it was entirely insensitive to a CB1 antagonist. Meanwhile the CB1 receptors themselves had become functionally impaired: two different agonists produced markedly less inhibition of glutamate release, and super-resolution imaging showed normal CB1 abundance at glutamatergic terminals but a substantial drop in the nanoscale density of bassoon, the active-zone scaffolding protein. The receptor is present; the release machinery underneath it has been reorganized.

The replacement messenger is nitric oxide. Inhibiting nitric oxide synthase abolished the plasticity in exposed animals and did nothing to normal endocannabinoid suppression in controls; intracellular inhibition of the neuronal isoform did the same, localizing the source to the recorded dopamine cell; blocking soluble guanylate cyclase blocked it too. And they imaged it directly with a cell-permeable nitric oxide indicator — depolarization produced no detectable signal in control slices and a robust rise in exposed slices. Then the reciprocal half: the same protocol produced a nitric-oxide-dependent long-term potentiation of GABA-A currents, roughly thirty percent, in exposed animals only, with the depression of glutamate requiring presynaptic GABA-B receptors — so the potentiated inhibition drives the excitatory depression, by spillover.

Why this is more than an elegant circuit result: it reframes what developmental cannabinoid exposure does. Not a lesion in a signalling pathway, but a rewritten rule for plasticity, with a different retrograde messenger recruited when the canonical one is compromised, shifting the excitatory-inhibitory balance over dopamine neurons in the same direction as the behavioural vulnerability. And practically — if you were screening for something to normalize this, you would be looking at nitric oxide synthase and guanylate cyclase, not at the cannabinoid system.

Staying with receptor subtype surprises, a short one on psilocybin in Progress in Neuro-Psychopharmacology and Biological Psychiatry. This group had previously reported that psilocybin's effects in the forced swim test are 5-HT2B-dependent — dissociable from the 5-HT2A mechanism that carries the psychedelic effect itself. Here they extend that to novelty-induced grooming in rats, scored with a microstructure algorithm that separates normal cephalocaudal sequences from fragmented bouts and aberrant transitions. Psilocybin reduced grooming on the coarse measures, and the microstructure showed why: it selectively suppressed the abnormal, non-comfort component rather than flattening grooming generally. RS-127445, a selective 5-HT2B antagonist, reversed both. So a second innate behaviour where psilocybin's action runs through 5-HT2B — which would be the part you could in principle keep without the trip, and is also the receptor with the valvulopathy history, which cuts the other way.

Next, a human experimental psychopharmacology study in Neuropsychopharmacology on a target most psychiatrists never think about: the renin-angiotensin system, on accumulating evidence that angiotensin signalling shapes reward processing and cognitive markers of depression. Sixty-eight healthy adults, randomized to a single fifty-milligram dose of losartan or placebo, tested at drug peak on an implicit approach-avoidance task — you respond to colour-filtered faces with a joystick, pull the grey ones and push the brown ones, so the emotional content is irrelevant to the instruction and any bias is implicit.

Losartan produced a significant emotion-by-group interaction: a medium-sized increase in avoidance of sad faces, Cohen's d of point five-five, and a trend-level reduction in avoidance of angry faces at d minus point four-two. No effect on happy or neutral faces, no group difference in blood pressure, reaction time, accuracy, or on the checkerboard control stimuli. Blinding held — both experimenters and participants guessed at chance.

The interpretive claim is that this pattern is the inverse of what's reported in depression, where patients under-avoid sadness and over-avoid anger, and it resembles what conventional antidepressants do to emotional processing early in treatment — selectively reducing the salience of negative stimuli without blunting responses to positive ones. That is a reasonable proof-of-mechanism reading. The honest limits are also stated: healthy volunteers, single acute dose, no imaging, and the study was powered for the interaction rather than the emotion-specific contrasts, which is why the anger effect should be treated as a lead and not a result. Still, an off-patent antihypertensive with a mechanistic signature on affective bias is not a small thing to have on the table.

Two dopamine items to close, and they connect through the striatum. First, a conditional knockout in Neuropsychopharmacology that turned out to be a much bigger phenotype than the title suggests.

N-ethylmaleimide-sensitive factor is the ATPase that disassembles SNARE complexes after membrane fusion — housekeeping machinery for neurotransmitter release and for trafficking membrane proteins, and it happens to bind the dopamine D2 receptor directly. Global knockout is embryonic lethal, so they deleted it selectively in D2-receptor-expressing cells.

The consequences cascade. Fewer D2-expressing cells in both dorsal and ventral striatum, confirmed independently with preproenkephalin as a marker that isn't driven by the D2 promoter, alongside an increase in D1-expressing cell density. Elevated apoptosis at postnatal day eight, resolved by day twenty-one, with striatal shrinkage and lower brain weight. And then the number that stopped me: striatal dopamine content was roughly ninety percent lower than controls at weaning, with reduced tyrosine hydroxylase and dopamine transporter in striatum and reduced tyrosine hydroxylase-positive cell density in substantia nigra but not ventral tegmental area. Ninety percent depletion is beyond the classic parkinsonian threshold — and yet rotarod performance was normal. Instead the animals were hyperactive and impulsive, an attention-deficit phenotype rather than a motor one.

Which is where the pharmacology comes in. Methylphenidate plus the D2 agonist quinpirole together alleviated both the hyperactivity and the impulsivity. The framing the authors offer — that a subset of treatment-resistant attention-deficit disorder might involve D2 receptor loss rather than transporter-level dysfunction, and would therefore need a D2 agonist alongside a stimulant rather than more stimulant — is speculative, built on one mouse line, and worth a well-designed follow-up rather than a clinical inference.

And to finish, two studies on the same drug that disagree in the most instructive possible way, both about where and when you engage the target. The first is from BenevolentAI, on phosphodiesterase 10A — a striatal-enriched enzyme with a long, mostly disappointing history as an antipsychotic target, now being revisited for weight management because inhibiting it browns white adipose tissue and raises energy expenditure. They tested a peripherally restricted inhibitor against a brain-penetrant one in diet-induced obese mice.

On its own, the peripherally restricted compound worked — dose-dependent attenuation of weight gain across three doses, about five to nine percent versus vehicle, reduced fat mass, reduced insulin and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and critically, no change in food intake, which is the signature of a genuine energy-expenditure mechanism rather than appetite suppression. Then they ran the clinically relevant paradigm: induce weight loss with semaglutide for twenty days, withdraw it, and see what maintains. The brain-penetrant inhibitor, mardepodect, was essentially indistinguishable from continued semaglutide on every readout. The peripherally restricted compound managed a non-significant trend. The conclusion, and the data support it, is that central target engagement is required for a durable anti-obesity effect. Which is an awkward place to land, because the whole point of peripheral restriction was to escape the neurological adverse effects that killed this target class in schizophrenia. You cannot have both, at least not this way.

The second is from Shelly Flagel's lab at Michigan, and it undercuts a large chunk of the literature on GLP-1 agonists and motivation. Everyone is interested in whether these drugs reduce drug and alcohol seeking, and most of the preclinical work uses acute dosing, while clinically these are chronic drugs with dose escalation. So they compared acute and chronic semaglutide in a Pavlovian conditioned approach paradigm, with cues predicting large or small food rewards, in male and female rats.

Both regimens reduced body weight. Their effects on cue-directed behaviour went in opposite directions. Chronic semaglutide increased sign-tracking — more behaviour directed at the reward-predictive lever cue — while acute semaglutide decreased every sign-tracking measure. And then the control that makes the paper: the acutely treated animals also ate fewer reward pellets and showed less food-cup activity during the intervals between trials, when there was no cue at all. That is not reduced incentive salience. That is a general suppression of behaviour, consistent with malaise — and semaglutide is known to produce visceral malaise acutely in rodents and nausea in roughly forty percent of people during dose escalation.

So the acute-dosing studies reporting that GLP-1 agonists reduce cue reactivity may be reporting that nausea reduces cue reactivity. The chronic finding points the other way entirely, toward enhanced motivational value of the cue, which fits this group's earlier work showing chronic semaglutide increased conditioned reinforcement and progressive-ratio responding for the same reward. Malaise and locomotion weren't measured directly, which they acknowledge, so the confound is inferred rather than demonstrated. But if you are designing an addiction trial on the strength of the acute preclinical literature, this is the paper to read first.

That's the episode. Behavioural consistency beating internal likelihood as a trust signal in an on-premise clinical agent, with the free signals failing exactly when evidence is thin; identical inputs producing different medication orders under an identical verdict; knowledge-graph paths that lower a model's rubric score and raise its evidence grounding; perturbational validity as the missing benchmark for brain foundation models; prefrontal GABA that only differs under cue provocation; prenatal THC swapping endocannabinoid retrograde signalling for nitric oxide; psilocybin suppressing aberrant grooming through 5-HT2B rather than 5-HT2A; losartan shifting implicit approach-avoidance in the antidepressant direction; a D2-cell knockout with ninety percent striatal dopamine loss and an attention deficit rather than a motor one; peripheral phosphodiesterase-10A inhibition failing where the brain-penetrant compound matched semaglutide; and acute versus chronic semaglutide pushing cue-motivated behaviour in opposite directions, with the acute effect looking a lot like nausea.
