Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Monday, October twelfth. Eleven items today, and a sourcing note up front, because it shaped the mix. bioRxiv rate-limited this machine almost immediately and stayed that way, so exactly one preprint from that server made it through with readable full text — though it happens to be the best thing I read all week. medRxiv was open, arXiv was open, and the Nature family was behind a bot check today that even the usual workarounds couldn't get past, so several Neuropsychopharmacology and Molecular Psychiatry papers I wanted to cover are deferred rather than dropped. The tilt you'll hear toward clinical and computational work is a network artifact.

There's a thread through most of today's papers, and it's worth naming before we start. In nearly every one of them, the headline summary number stays intact while the thing you actually care about lives somewhere the summary can't see — in a tail, in a trajectory, in whether an answer got delivered at all, in which part of the brain the manipulation actually touched. Several of today's authors go looking in exactly those places. A couple don't, and it shows.

We open with the one that matters most, and it's a null result — a careful, well-powered, multi-method null that should change how a sizeable literature talks about itself. The question is where in the brain a psychedelic produces the head-twitch response. The head-twitch is the workhorse rodent proxy for human psychedelic potency: it scales with 5-HT2A agonism, it's abolished in 5-HT2A knockouts, and essentially every psychedelic drug discovery program uses it as the behavioral readout that says a molecule is psychedelic-like. The standard story places the critical receptors in layer five pyramidal neurons of the medial prefrontal cortex. That story is doing real work — it's the reason non-hallucinogenic psychoplastogen programs talk about cortical 5-HT2A signalling as the thing to engineer around.

This group tested the cortical localization directly, with DOI — a selective 5-HT2A and 5-HT2C agonist used as the standard tool psychedelic — and four independent manipulations. First, the positive controls work beautifully. Systemic DOI in rats drives head-twitches hard. In mice, the genotype-by-drug interaction is enormous and the knockouts are flat — the behavior is 5-HT2A-dependent, full stop. They also score ear-scratch responses, which behave the same way, and digging and grooming, which DOI suppresses in knockouts too, so those are not 5-HT2A-mediated. Female mice twitch more than males; rats show no sex difference at the dose tested.

Then the localization experiments, and all four say no. Infusing DOI directly into prelimbic cortex at two, four, and twelve micrograms per microliter per hemisphere — a six-fold dose range — produced no increase in head-twitches whatsoever, with an F statistic of about zero point one and a p-value of zero point nine five. Infusing MDL100907, the selective 5-HT2A antagonist, into the same region before systemic DOI did not blunt the response; there was no drug-by-DOI interaction. Restoring 5-HT2A expression selectively in CaMKII-positive prefrontal neurons of knockout mice — with Western blots confirming the protein came back — did not restore head-twitching or ear-scratching. And deleting the receptor from the same population in floxed mice did not reduce either behavior.

So: necessary at the level of the whole animal, and not localizable to prefrontal excitatory neurons by any of four approaches. The authors are appropriately careful. They did not test interneurons or projection-defined subpopulations, and a modulatory role for cortex is not excluded — only a driving one. They note the conflict with an earlier optogenetic study, and they address the obvious objection, that local dosing was too low, by pointing out their doses equal or exceed prior reports that did see an effect. What they propose instead is that the behavior is generated subcortically or by a distributed network — claustrum, brainstem, periaqueductal gray. The practical implication for anyone designing non-hallucinogenic 5-HT2A ligands is uncomfortable but useful: if your screening assay's anatomical basis isn't where you thought it was, then "cortically biased" signalling is not obviously the dial that separates therapeutic plasticity from the trip.

From a localization null to a trajectory result. A Penn, Yale and NIAAA collaboration asked what a ketogenic diet actually does to brain dynamics during alcohol withdrawal. The background is one of the more interesting metabolic stories in addiction medicine: chronic heavy drinking shifts cerebral metabolism from glucose toward acetate, and when drinking stops the acetate disappears, leaving an energy-depleted brain. A prior randomized trial in detox inpatients found that a ketogenic diet reduced withdrawal severity, cut benzodiazepine requirements in the first week, and raised dorsal anterior cingulate glutamate. This paper reanalyzes the cue-reactivity imaging from those thirty-three patients, scanned at one, two, and three weeks.

The method is the interesting part. Rather than correlating regional activation with craving, they estimate directed information flow between regions using transfer entropy, then treat that network as the dynamics matrix of a linear control system and compute the control energy needed to move the brain between states defined by activation of each functional network. You get, per participant per week, a landscape of how expensive each transition is. They then summarize the landscape by how much of its variance loads on the first singular component — a measure of how structured it is, whether transitions are governed by one common organizational pattern or many unrelated ones.

And here's the shape of the finding: there is no group difference in that structure. What differs is its trajectory. The ketogenic group's landscape organization held steady across three weeks; the standard-diet group's degraded significantly. That interaction is the whole result, and change in landscape structure correlated negatively with change in alcohol wanting, rho about minus zero point five four. Rising beta-hydroxybutyrate tracked rising structure, and an indirect-effects model is consistent with ketones acting on craving through network organization rather than directly. The authors are scrupulous that this is not causal mediation — all three slopes were computed over the same three weeks, so there's no temporal ordering — and thirty-three patients is small. But as a bridge between a peripheral metabolic intervention and large-scale neural dynamics in a randomized design, it's a genuinely novel piece of work, and it's a good example of a measure whose average says nothing and whose slope says everything.

Staying with substances, and moving to genetics. An Amsterdam group applied genomic structural equation modelling and Mendelian randomization to the largest European-ancestry GWAS of six substance use behaviors and twelve psychiatric and personality phenotypes. The latent structure that fit best has three factors: one loading PTSD, major depression, generalized anxiety, ADHD and low agreeableness; a second loading bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, OCD and openness; and a third loading OCD, anxiety, extraversion and neuroticism. Smoking correlates with all three but most strongly the first. Cannabis correlates most strongly with the second — the psychosis-spectrum factor — which is the kind of differentiation that bare pairwise correlations wash out.

The Mendelian randomization is where the claims get sharper and fewer. Strong evidence that smoking continuation raises bipolar risk, that cigarettes per day raises schizophrenia risk, and that smoking initiation shifts personality — up on extraversion, down on agreeableness. In the other direction, genetic liability to schizophrenia and to PTSD raises cannabis use, and schizophrenia liability raises alcohol use. Everything else was weak, mostly drowned in heterogeneity and pleiotropy. I'd flag two limitations the authors raise themselves, because they're structural rather than incidental. The instrument threshold had to be relaxed for cannabis and for OCD, which makes those arms preliminary. And as psychiatric GWAS sample sizes grow, a fixed genome-wide significance threshold admits more and more small, pleiotropic variants — so the standard MR machinery gets noisier precisely as the data get bigger. That's a methodological problem the whole field is going to have to confront, not a flaw in this paper.

Now a short, clean piece of pharmacology that is entirely about a tail. Metoclopramide — the dopamine D2 and serotonin receptor antagonist used for nausea in pregnancy — gets given intravenously before cesarean section, and the newborn inherits whatever is circulating. A Radboud group ran human ex vivo placental cotyledon perfusions to measure transfer directly, built those parameters into a pregnancy physiology-based pharmacokinetic model, and simulated fetal plasma and fetal brain concentrations at twenty-four, thirty-two and forty weeks gestation. Peak fetal plasma concentrations after a standard ten-milligram dose run around thirty-eight to forty-seven nanograms per milliliter, and the striking number is the maternal-plasma-to-fetal-brain ratio, which at twenty-four weeks is essentially one to one and is still about one to zero point six five at term. The drug distributes into the newborn central nervous system freely.

They then convert brain concentration into D2 receptor occupancy, using the eighty-percent threshold above which extrapyramidal symptoms become likely — the same threshold antipsychotic dosing has used for decades. Mean occupancy stays below eighty percent at every gestational age, which is the reassuring headline. The tail is the finding. Simulated population variability puts roughly five percent of newborns at twenty-four weeks, and about one percent at thirty-two weeks, above the eighty-percent line under standard dosing. Extrapyramidal reactions in neonates after maternal metoclopramide are a known but rare and poorly explained event, and this gives a quantitative mechanism for why they'd be rare and why they'd cluster in the most preterm deliveries. It's exactly the sort of question that is unethical to study directly and tractable to model, which is the strongest argument for this class of work.

Which brings us to modeling at the other end of therapeutic scale, in Clinical Pharmacology and Therapeutics. Certara published a worked case study of a quantitative systems pharmacology platform for immuno-oncology, using tarlatamab — the DLL3-by-CD3 bispecific T-cell engager approved for small cell lung cancer. The model was calibrated on the hundred-milligram regimen and on atezolizumab monotherapy, then asked to predict the ten-milligram regimen it had never seen. It reproduced the tumor-shrinkage waterfall and the cytokine time courses for interleukin two, six, ten, interferon gamma and TNF-alpha out of sample, which is a real test rather than a fit.

The part worth paying attention to is how they handle cytokine release syndrome, because they're unusually honest about it. They do not model CRS mechanistically — they say outright that the biology isn't understood well enough. Instead they infer an interleukin-six threshold from aggregate published incidence, then apply it to simulated patients. Calibrated that way, the model reproduces forty-three percent incidence after the priming dose versus forty-one observed, and thirty versus twenty-nine after the target dose, and predicts that adding atezolizumab doesn't raise CRS risk. That last number is an extrapolation to a regimen with no clinical CRS data at all, which is the entire point of building these platforms — and also the reason to read the method before quoting the number. A statistical layer bolted to a mechanistic model inherits the mechanistic model's credibility in people's heads without having earned it.

Which is a decent segue into probabilities that look trustworthy and aren't. A pre-registered evaluation out of Mount Sinai and Columbia tested the calibration claims attached to the new class of so-called decision models — systems that return a probability for every option in one pass and are marketed as calibrated, which is precisely what makes them attractive for clinical triage. They put Jev one point one three against Claude Opus five point five with reasoning and Opus five with reasoning disabled, across the forty-five Semigran vignettes, a thousand synthetic patients from DDXPlus, and eighty simulated ophthalmic complaints.

On the short, distinctive ophthalmic complaints the decision model is fine — accuracy around eighty-nine percent, statistically indistinguishable from both language models, and actually better calibrated than the registered comparator. On the multi-symptom synthetic patients it is not fine: seventy-two percent top-one accuracy versus seventy-nine point five for the non-reasoning model and eighty-seven point four for the reasoning one. It lost to a comparator with reasoning switched off. And the failure mode is the one that matters for triage — it assigned exactly zero probability to the true diagnosis in sixty-two of a thousand cases, about one in sixteen. Neither language model ever did that. Then the behavioral probe: given the option list and no patient information at all, the decision model put up to ninety-six percent on a single diagnosis, while the comparators spread probability nearly evenly. Offered an explicit "none of these" escape, it declined all thirty uninformative inputs. So it can represent ignorance, but only when the answer format hands it a way to say so. Expected calibration error — the headline metric — did not differ between systems. Calibration was fine; the thing you'd actually route a patient on was not.

The harness paper from Northwestern makes a structurally similar point about agents. Their observation is that every benchmark score for a medical AI agent is a property of a model-and-harness pair — the harness being the scaffolding that decides what the model sees, which tools it can call, how history is managed, and when the episode ends. They built a hundred-and-seven-task benchmark across four clinical domains, ran five open-weight models under five harnesses including Claude Code and Codex, changing nothing but the harness, and decomposed the variance. The model accounts for fifty-eight percent, the harness twelve, their interaction twelve, and seed-to-seed variation eighteen. So the harness and its interaction are about a quarter of the outcome — and no harness wins across the board. Of twenty model-by-environment pairs, only two had a leader whose margin excluded zero.

The mechanism is the good part. When they compared the spread in answer rate against the spread in score on the questions every harness actually answered, the answer-rate spread was larger in nine of ten pairs: scores differed by two to six hundredths, while answer rates differed by up to zero point five four. Fifteen percent of episodes on one environment simply end without producing an answer. Much of what reads as a capability gap between agent systems is a delivery gap — whether the thing ever got submitted. And they show it directly: turning on one harness's submission check moved a model from zero point four two to zero point five one, and turning off another's tool search moved it from zero point one seven to zero point five zero. If you are evaluating clinical agents, measure how often they fail to answer before you believe anything about how well they reason.

A Sofia University group pushes on the same seam from the verification side. They built what they call an evidence-bound reasoning framework for glioblastoma radiogenomics — converting quantitative imaging features into addressable evidence records and machine-checkable claims, so that every statement an AI system makes about a patient can be mechanically traced to a measurement. The verifier hits a hundred percent exact-set accuracy on a sixty-six-hundred-claim corruption benchmark, and in a small pilot a frontier model reproduced all seventy-two prespecified atomic claims. The number I'd hold onto is from their controlled degradation experiment: as they degraded the underlying predictive model, area under the curve fell from zero point nine to zero point five — chance — while verification accuracy stayed at one point zero. They present this as a feature, and it is: verifiability is a separate axis from accuracy, and you want to be able to measure it independently. But read it the other way and it's the sharpest warning in today's batch. A fully auditable, perfectly traceable chain of reasoning can sit on top of a model that knows nothing. Their own external genomic prediction task came in at an AUC of zero point five four, which is roughly that situation in the wild.

Two shorter items to close, both about interfaces between models and neural or clinical reality. First, from Indiana University, a framework for generating realistic patient dialogue in substance use disorder counseling. The clinical problem is genuine: training and evaluating counselors needs simulated patients whose responses reflect actual cognitive states — beliefs, coping strategies, readiness to change — and large models produce fluent text that is cognitively flat. Their approach detects the latent cognitive components first, then generates dialogue conditioned on them, using distillation from larger teachers, preference optimization on human annotations, and attention-guided reward shaping to get this into a small model that can run locally. The deployment argument is the one I find persuasive — privacy, latency and cost all push against sending counseling transcripts to a frontier API. The gains are largest on the open-ended cognitive components, which is where generic instruction-tuned models are weakest. Evaluation leans on overlap metrics and model-as-judge rather than clinician ratings, so treat the size of the improvement as provisional.

And last, two EEG papers that make a nice pair. The first, from Shanechi's lab at USC, asks how to interface neural signals with foundation models at all, and answers it in a way that sounds like a hack and isn't: render multichannel EEG as structured images and post-train a general-purpose vision-language model on them, skipping EEG-specific pretraining entirely. It works across sleep staging, emotion recognition, workload classification and abnormality detection in one model. Two findings keep it honest — the choice of EEG-to-image representation drives performance substantially, so the visual encoding is doing real work rather than being a neutral container; and under graded noise injection, performance degrades gradually rather than falling off a cliff, which is their evidence that the model is reading EEG-relevant structure rather than superficial visual texture.

The second is the quiet one I'd leave you with. A group at Illinois fit personalized latent dynamical models to EEG from ninety-nine epilepsy and ninety-nine non-epilepsy subjects, and compared them two ways. By predictive fit, the groups are indistinguishable: held-out log-likelihood of minus zero point nine nine two versus minus zero point nine nine one, next-state prediction AUC of zero point eight five five versus zero point eight six one. By the structure of what the models learned, they are not: epilepsy subjects' transition-dependency graphs carry nearly twice as many nonzero dependencies, with a p-value around one times ten to the minus seven. Same predictability, different internal dynamics. Which is the day's thread in its purest form — if you evaluate a personalized model only by how well it predicts, you will conclude that two clinically distinct populations are the same, and the thing that actually distinguishes them is sitting in a part of the model you never looked at.
