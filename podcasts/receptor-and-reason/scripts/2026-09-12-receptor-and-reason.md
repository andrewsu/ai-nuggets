## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. I'm your host, and this is your daily pass through CNS pharmacology, psychiatric and neurology drug discovery, and the computational machinery closing in on both. Eleven items today, and the thread running through nearly all of them is averaging — specifically, what disappears when you average. A gene that looks like one drug target until you sequence its transcripts. A behavioural phenotype that looks like susceptibility until you ask which animal the mouse is avoiding. And, at the end, a language model whose demographic bias is invisible precisely because it runs through two channels that cancel.

Start with the one that should change how you think about a drug target you already know. A long-read sequencing study out of Oxford has mapped the full transcript repertoire of CACNA1C — the gene encoding the pore subunit of the L-type calcium channel Cav1.2, and the most reliable transdiagnostic common-variant hit in psychiatry, associated with both schizophrenia and bipolar disorder. Everyone in the field talks about CACNA1C as a single target. This paper shows it is not remotely a single target.

They ran PCR-targeted nanopore sequencing across six brain regions from four individuals, plus pooled heart and aorta, with nearly five million reads from brain alone. The headline result is that the ten most abundant isoforms in each of the three tissues are all distinct. Not overlapping with different weights — distinct. Heart runs almost entirely off start exon one-a; brain and aorta use one-b. Brain and heart preferentially include exon eight, aorta uses exon eight-a. And a splicing choice between exons twenty-one and twenty-two, not previously reported as tissue-regulated in humans, comes out cleanly split: heart and aorta include exon twenty-one only, brain includes exon twenty-two only. They also found a previously unannotated start exon, exon one-d, present in brain and aorta and absent from full-length heart transcripts.

Why this matters for pharmacology rather than just annotation: the dihydropyridines — the amlodipine class — inhibit smooth-muscle Cav1.2 isoforms at substantially lower concentrations than cardiac isoforms, and that selectivity is a consequence of exactly this kind of alternative splicing. So there is direct precedent for splice-variant-selective calcium channel pharmacology. If brain preferentially uses exon one-b, exon eight, and exon twenty-two, that is a specification for a brain-selective L-type blocker, which is the thing standing between the psychiatric genetics and a usable drug. Cav1.2 is everywhere in the cardiovascular system, and that is why nobody is repurposing amlodipine for bipolar disorder.

Two honest caveats. There is no electrophysiology here — the isoforms are predicted to encode functional channels, not shown to. And the brain donors were ancestrally diverse while the heart and aorta donors were all of European ancestry, which matters because linkage disequilibrium among the psychiatric risk variants inside CACNA1C differs by ancestry. One more thing worth carrying: most of the abundant isoforms were novel, absent from reference annotation. The transcript databases we build on are incomplete at this locus.

Staying with targets that look different once you stratify. A Toronto group has published the first PET study of monoamine oxidase B in treatment-resistant depression, using the carbon-eleven tracer S-L twenty-five eleven eighty-eight, which unlike the older tracers is reversible enough to give a proper total distribution volume. Twenty-nine participants with non-treatment-resistant major depressive episodes, twenty-six with treatment-resistant depression, forty healthy controls, and a subset of thirteen scanned before and after treatment.

Two findings. First, MAO-B binding was ten percent higher in grey matter in females than males — as far as the authors can tell the first report of a sex difference in MAO-B in human brain, and it runs opposite to the prior hypothesis that oestrogen suppresses the enzyme. Their preferred explanation is the gene's location on the X chromosome and possible escape from X-inactivation. Second, and more actionable, prefrontal MAO-B was twenty-three percent higher in the treatment-resistant group than in controls, and six percent higher than in non-resistant depression. And critically, duloxetine had essentially no effect on MAO-B binding, whereas rasagiline and phenelzine showed roughly ninety-four percent occupancy at ordinary clinical doses.

Put those together and you get an argument the field has not really had: there is a measurable, elevated MAO-B signal in the prefrontal cortex of treatment-resistant patients that serotonin reuptake inhibition does not touch, and a well-tolerated selective MAO-B inhibitor already on the market for Parkinson's disease that saturates the enzyme at standard dosing. Rasagiline in treatment-resistant depression is a small, cheap trial that somebody should run. The caveat is the usual one for cross-sectional PET — elevated MAO-B is as likely a marker of chronic astrogliosis in a sicker population as a causal driver, and thirteen people is too small to arbitrate.

Staying with treatment resistance, but moving from molecules to electrodes. A single-patient report describes chronic local-field-potential sensing from subcallosal cingulate white matter during deep brain stimulation, in a woman simultaneously self-administering intranasal ketamine at home. The numbers are the point: just over five thousand nine hundred hours of bilateral sensing, four hundred and eighty-five patient-triggered recordings across a hundred and ninety-three days, and forty-seven ketamine dosing episodes. Her depression scores fell from twenty-one at baseline to eleven, sustained to week fifty-two.

Ketamine shifts subcallosal cingulate activity toward a fast band around twenty-three hertz, bilaterally, replicably across dozens of at-home doses. That alone would be a nice biomarker story. The complication is that patient-reported anxious and sad episodes drive the same narrowband power up, in the same direction, with effect sizes around one standard deviation, peaking about fifteen minutes after the self-report. Neutral and happy episodes push it slightly down. So the same frequency, in the same electrode, means "drug on board" in one context and "feeling terrible" in another, distinguishable only by magnitude, laterality, and time course.

This is a direct warning for closed-loop psychiatric DBS. If you build a controller that titrates stimulation off subcallosal cingulate fast-band power, and your patient is also on ketamine or esketamine — which many treatment-resistant patients are — your control signal is confounded by the drug in a way that looks exactly like symptom worsening. Single patient, uncontrolled, home-entered event markers rather than synchronised timestamps. But five thousand nine hundred hours from one person is a different kind of evidence than twenty people scanned once, and the confound it identifies is structural, not statistical.

Next, a Copenhagen study that does something the Parkinson's field has talked about for two decades without demonstrating in the same people: link dopamine transporter loss, striatal recruitment, and a quantitative measure of movement vigour, within individuals. Fifty-three patients — thirty with levodopa-induced dyskinesia, twenty-three without — plus twenty-five age-matched controls, performing a reward-modulated grip-force task during three-tesla functional MRI, with thirty-five of the patients also getting DAT PET.

The behavioural result is the sharp one. Reaction time did not differ between groups. Peak force did not differ between groups. What differed was initial vigour — the first peak in the rate of force development — which fell from about fifteen hundred newtons per second in controls to around twelve hundred in non-dyskinetic patients and just over a thousand in the dyskinetic group on the most affected side. So bradykinesia in this paradigm is not slow initiation and it is not weakness; it is a failure to scale the initial agonist command. That is the prediction of the dopamine-as-vigour models, measured directly.

Lower putaminal DAT binding tracked both weaker putaminal activation and lower initial vigour. But here is the part I like. With DAT binding and putaminal BOLD in the same model, dopaminergic integrity was the dominant predictor and the BOLD signal added little — and in the whole-brain analyses, putaminal activation showed no relationship at all to trial-by-trial or between-subject variation in vigour. The authors are direct about it: the movement-related BOLD response is a composite of planning, execution and monitoring, a systems-level signature of impaired circuit engagement rather than a readout of vigour. If you are designing a Parkinson's trial with a task-fMRI target engagement endpoint, that is worth internalising. There was also blunted reward-related nucleus accumbens activity that correlated with vigour across all participants. And a nice negative: the dyskinetic patients had more severe putaminal denervation but no worse vigour and no worse striatal task responses, which argues that dyskinesia is not simply further along the same axis.

From the midbrain to the striatum, and to a drug everyone is currently arguing about. A fibre photometry study used a genetically encoded GABA sensor to record extracellular GABA in awake mice drinking water, saccharin, or an Ensure-water mixture. Dorsomedial striatal GABA release scaled with palatability — the largest transient decrease for Ensure, then saccharin, then water — while dorsolateral striatum showed the same initial dip regardless of what the animal was drinking. So the palatability signal is subregion-specific, and everything downstream is dorsomedial.

Then semaglutide, at point-zero-two-six milligrams per kilogram daily for three days. Consumption fell, as expected. The interesting part is how the GABA signal changed: the normal decrease at lick onset recovered faster, inhibitory signalling during consumption went up, and the coupling between GABA suppression and individual licking events weakened. Food deprivation did the opposite, delaying recovery. And optogenetic activation of dorsomedial striatal GABAergic interneurons reproduced the drug's signature on consumption structure — shorter lick bouts, less total intake — without any GLP-1 agonism at all.

That last experiment is what lifts this above a correlational photometry paper. It says a specific local inhibitory mechanism is sufficient to produce the behavioural phenotype, which is a much stronger statement about where in the circuit semaglutide's consummatory effect lands. What it doesn't establish is that this is where the drug acts — the GLP-1 receptors doing the work are almost certainly in hindbrain and hypothalamus, and the dorsomedial striatum is reading out a change imposed from upstream. Also, semaglutide reduced total distance travelled, so some of the consumption effect may be general behavioural suppression rather than specific devaluation.

Staying in striatum, a Sorbonne and McGill collaboration takes a rare human variant and asks a question about stress. Cholinergic interneurons in the nucleus accumbens express the vesicular glutamate transporter VGLUT3, which enables glutamate co-release and, through vesicular synergy, increases how much acetylcholine gets loaded into each vesicle. There is a human missense variant, threonine-eight-to-isoleucine, associated with compulsive behaviour toward food and drugs of abuse, and it leaves glutamate release intact while selectively impairing vesicular synergy and reducing acetylcholine release. So it is a clean genetic scalpel for the cholinergic arm.

They put knock-in mice through ten days of chronic social defeat and recorded acetylcholine and dopamine in the accumbens medial shell. Mutant males showed more social avoidance and more anxiety-like behaviour after defeat, with a blunted acetylcholine rise during the attacks themselves but an enhanced acetylcholine response on later re-exposure to the aggressor strain, and that enhancement tracked how severe the avoidance was. Dopamine barely moved.

Here's the reframe, and it's the best thing in the paper. By the conventional social-interaction ratio, the mutants are more susceptible. But when they tested avoidance toward a familiar non-aggressive strain, the mutants were fine — their avoidance was specific to the strain that had actually attacked them. The wild-type males discriminated worse and generalised more. So on the standard readout the mutant looks broken, and on a readout that separates threat from safety it looks like it has better threat-specific learning. Whether you call that susceptibility or intact discrimination depends entirely on which conspecific you put in the chamber, and most of this literature only uses one. Females showed the mutant-like phenotype regardless of genotype, which the authors don't fully resolve.

One more cholinergic item, and it puts a familiar drug target in an unfamiliar cell. A Paris group reports that in male mice, exposure to an oestrus female rapidly induces STAT3 signalling in ventral hippocampal astrocytes — and the trigger is not cytokines, which is what JAK2-STAT3 is famous for, but septo-hippocampal cholinergic input acting on alpha-seven nicotinic receptors on the astrocytes themselves. Knocking down astrocytic alpha-seven reduced male contact time with receptive females; expressing constitutively active JAK2 in those astrocytes dampened ventral CA1 pyramidal responses during social interaction and selectively altered behaviour toward females, leaving juvenile interaction and other hippocampus-dependent tasks intact.

The reason a pharmacologist should care: alpha-seven agonists and positive allosteric modulators have been in and out of clinical development for cognition in schizophrenia and Alzheimer's for twenty years, and essentially all of the mechanistic reasoning has been about presynaptic calcium and interneuron excitability. This says there is a transcription-factor arm, in astrocytes, running on the timescale of transcription rather than milliseconds, downstream of the same receptor. If your alpha-seven compound does something slow that your electrophysiology doesn't explain, this is a place to look. The authors are careful that knocking down astrocytic alpha-seven also removes alpha-seven-mediated calcium signalling, so the behaviour is not cleanly attributable to the JAK2 arm alone.

Now a clean negative, and a useful one. Mismatch negativity is the most durable electrophysiological biomarker in schizophrenia research, it matures across adolescence, and the dominant explanation has been microglial synaptic pruning — which is also where the complement genetics points. This group recorded local field potentials from mouse visual cortex and anterior cingulate during oddball sequences, and depleted microglia by about ninety percent with P-L-X five-six-two-two, a CSF1R inhibitor, in eight-day windows during peri-adolescence, mid-adolescence, or adulthood.

Deviance detection did emerge between juvenile and adult animals, and top-down cingulate-to-visual connectivity strengthened with age — so the developmental phenomenon is there and measurable. Microglial depletion did not touch it. Not in any window, not in either direction: the context-by-group interaction came out at p equals point three-eight, and long-range coherence between the two regions was likewise unaffected. What depletion did change was local cingulate oscillatory power in adulthood — more delta, less beta — so the manipulation clearly did something. It just didn't do the thing everyone assumed.

Ten percent of microglia survived, and the adult recordings happened more than sixty days after depletion ended, so a recovery window could have rescued the circuit. Those are real limits. But this is a well-controlled null on a hypothesis that has been assumed rather than tested, and it deserves to slow down the inference from complement genetics straight to pruning to mismatch negativity.

Turning to the computational side, and to an audit that is genuinely clever in its design. Medical schools are beginning to deploy language models as digital standardised patients — the model writes the case and then role-plays the patient for a trainee. A group audited this using an HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis screening scenario, with a two-hundred-and-sixteen-cell factorial design over age, gender, marital status, sexual orientation, education, and race, and four thousand three hundred and twenty simulated conversations.

The written cases carry the stereotypes you would guess. Anal sex appeared in a hundred percent of gay men's cases, eighty-three percent of bisexual men's, and three percent of heterosexual men's. Education predicted assigned socioeconomic status with an adjusted R-squared of point five-nine — stronger than race did. And the naming is almost comically blunt: all thirty-six African American men were named Marcus. The model had been explicitly instructed not to rely on demographic stereotypes.

But the finding that makes this a methods paper rather than another bias-in-language-models paper is this. They separated the case-writing channel from the live role-play channel, and the two sometimes had opposite signs. The model wrote women with higher alcohol use — and then role-played them as drinking less. In the composite pipeline, the two effects cancel and the bias is invisible. An audit of either pathway alone would have missed both. That is a general lesson for anyone evaluating a multi-stage generative system in a clinical context: end-to-end evaluation can be actively misleading, because compensating biases average out to something that looks fair.

Second computational item, from Genentech, and it is the most substantial agentic-AI-for-biology paper of the week. The problem is adaptive experimental design in CRISPR screens: you cannot test every perturbation, so you pick a few hundred, observe, and pick again. They assembled a benchmark of one thousand three hundred and eighty-nine screens across five phenotype families with a temporal holdout — trained on pre-2021 screens, tested on post-2021 — and then trained a transformer-based acquisition policy across that historical corpus.

The framing is what I want to flag. Their claim is that completed experiments are training data for how to choose the next experiment — that acquisition itself is an amortisable, learnable function rather than something you re-derive per screen with a Gaussian process. They pair that learned policy with a language model providing biological priors, via what they call an adaptive handoff: the language model picks early, when there is no assay-specific evidence to condition on, and the learned policy takes over once feedback accumulates. On held-out screens the combination reaches a hit enrichment factor of five point six-seven over random and recovers twenty-eight percent of hits after assaying roughly five percent of the library, against four point eight-three for the learned policy alone.

The same idea works inside a language model: a twenty-seven-billion-parameter open-weight model, fine-tuned on acquisition trajectories from a stronger teacher and then optimised with reinforcement learning on screen-level hit discovery, improves its enrichment factor by thirty-nine percent and comes close to its teacher. So experimental-design competence is transferable rather than an emergent property of scale. The honest limitation: this is retrospective simulation on published screens, and the hit labels come from the original analyses, so what is being learned is partly which genes people already found interesting enough to publish as hits.

And to close, a meta-research paper that every one of us evaluating clinical AI should read, if only to be irritated by it. The question is whether the medical literature on language models is keeping up with the models. They mapped eleven thousand six hundred and twenty-eight PubMed records from January 2023 through June 2026, across fourteen clinical domains — forty-five-fold growth — and found that two and a half percent used a randomised, controlled, or prospective design.

Then they defined evaluation lag: the interval between the release of the newest model a study names and the study's own publication. It widened from one point three quarters to six point one — roughly four months to eighteen. The obvious objection is that models age mechanically as they are discontinued, so they benchmarked against a counterfactual holding model composition fixed; migration to newer systems offsets only fifty-six percent of the drift. Randomised trials evaluated models a median of four point six quarters older than other designs, and sixty-two percent of them evaluated a model family that no longer exists.

The finding I'd underline is worse than the lag. Randomised controlled trials specified the model version — or an access date, or decoding parameters, anything sufficient to identify which system was tested — in fifty percent of cases. That is the lowest of any empirical design, below preprints at seventy-seven percent and below ordinary comparative evaluations at eighty-three. CONSORT-AI lists algorithm version as a required item. Half the randomised evidence in this corpus cannot be attributed to a determinate model. So rigour and currency are in tension, and the tension is not about research timelines — it is about which model people choose to evaluate, and whether they write down which one it was.

That's eleven. What keeps coming back today is that the informative structure is one level finer than the level people report at: the isoform rather than the gene, the striatal subregion rather than the striatum, the threat-specific conspecific rather than the interaction ratio, the writing channel and the role-play channel rather than the pipeline, the model version rather than the model family. Averaging is how the signal gets lost. Thanks for listening, and I'll see you tomorrow.
