## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. Nine items today, and a sourcing note up front because it shaped the roster: bioRxiv's full-text channel was rate-limited for essentially the whole run, so several strong structural-pharmacology preprints are deferred rather than dropped. What came through leans clinical and computational. There's a thread through it worth naming — in item after item, something that was supposed to be a readout turns out to be doing the work instead. An old trial anchoring a guideline. A model's own previous answer anchoring its next one. A threshold, a selection step, a proxy metric standing in for the thing you actually care about and then quietly becoming it.

Start with the clearest case. Fluoxetine is the antidepressant most often prescribed to children and adolescents, and it holds that position in national guidelines largely on the strength of a 2016 network meta-analysis in the Lancet, supported by a second in Lancet Psychiatry in 2020. Both found it superior to placebo in pediatric depression. A 2021 Cochrane review disagreed — only a small and unimportant advantage over placebo, and probably no better than other antidepressants. That disagreement sat unresolved for five years. A new paper in Cochrane Evidence Synthesis and Methods, from a group including Florian Naudet at Rennes, Martin Plöderl in Salzburg and Richard Lyus in Cambridge, resolves it. The entire difference comes down to one trial: forty children, conducted in Iran, published in 2006, comparing fluoxetine against nortriptyline, reporting an advantage for fluoxetine so large that Plöderl's description is that it's the kind of effect size you only get if you ask people whether they prefer chocolate or faeces. Re-run the 2016 and 2020 analyses with that single study removed and fluoxetine's outsized benefit disappears. The 2021 Cochrane review had reached a different answer not because it adjudicated the trial, but because it happened to exclude the whole comparator class nortriptyline belongs to.

Two things to sit with. The trial's internal evidence is independently damning — the reported data imply that within each arm, every participant's depression score moved by roughly the same amount, which is not what clinical trial data look like, and there is no description of an ethics-approval process. The original authors have not responded to the research team, to Nature, or to the medRxiv preprint posted a year ago. But the more important part is Andrea Cipriani's response. He co-authored both Lancet analyses, he agrees on the record that the data are problematic, and he says the teams discussed it at the time but had no principled framework for excluding a trial on trustworthiness grounds — so they kept it in and recorded very low confidence in an appendix. That is an honest account, and it is the real finding. The methodology of the day had a slot for high risk of bias and no slot for "we don't think this happened." Cochrane has since introduced a trustworthiness protocol, which is the right fix. But a decade of guidelines were written in the interval, and a very-low-confidence caveat in an appendix is not what propagates into prescribing. Note what the authors are and aren't asking for: they stop short of telling clinicians to stop prescribing, and ask guideline committees to re-weigh.

The same failure in a machine, and this is the best-designed paper of the week. Min Zeng and Rui Zhang at Minnesota asked not whether a language model gets the right answer, but whether it updates correctly as the patient changes. Matched intensive-care trajectories from a public critical-care database — a thousand ventilation-positive and a thousand matched negative stays, several thousand sequential snapshots. Giving the model its own preceding assessment increased absolute error in forty-six percent of subsequent snapshots and reduced it in thirty percent, replicated on an independent vasopressor endpoint. And holding current evidence completely fixed while moving the supplied prior risk from ten percent to ninety percent shifted the estimate by twenty-six percentage points — about three points for every ten points of prior. Prompting didn't fix it, and an explicitly anti-anchoring prompt made it measurably worse.

What the abstract undersells is what makes the paper good rather than just another model-failure result. Evidence-driven updating is excellent. Worsening evidence moved risk in the right direction in over ninety-seven percent of transitions, monotonically in evidence strength in effectively a hundred percent, and genuinely neutral evidence produced almost no change. So this is not a model that can't reason about evidence. It is a model that reasons about evidence well and then lets its own prior output override the result. The failure is specific to self-context, and to a deterioration-versus-recovery asymmetry — bad news moves it more than matched good news, which is the profile you'd want in triage and emphatically not in a de-escalation decision. Compare the StrokeAgent audit we covered a couple of episodes ago, where ten of fourteen discordances leaned toward withholding reperfusion. Same directional bias, found independently.

One methodological point from that paper I'd put on a poster. In the vasopressor cohort, aggregate area under the ROC curve improved while paired accuracy degraded. A ranking metric computed over pooled predictions is structurally blind to whether each individual prediction got better or worse over time. If you evaluate a longitudinal clinical model on AUROC, you cannot see this failure mode at all. Two caveats: absolute discrimination is near chance throughout, and their proposed fix — permitting a revision only when the evidence licenses one — retains under six percent of revisions and still doesn't beat independent inference on error. The authors call it a conservative proof of concept, which is the right description.

The third item is the one I'd most want this audience to read, because it is about measurement in psychiatry rather than about models. The appealing design for rating depression severity from text is to split the task: have the model mark which DSM-5 criteria a post shows, then let code turn the count into a label, so a clinician can audit each marked criterion. The alternative is just asking for a severity rating with chain-of-thought. Two Reddit corpora, three models from nine billion parameters up to frontier scale, agreement measured with quadratic weighted kappa and paired bootstrap. With decision thresholds fitted on six hundred labels, criteria extraction beats chain-of-thought by up to point one five. With thresholds fixed a priori from the questionnaire's own scoring rule — no labels — all of those gains on the main corpus reverse sign. For the two frontier models there is no significant advantage for extraction anywhere: two corpora, two questionnaires, both threshold regimes. The thresholds were doing the work, not the extraction, which you can see most starkly in the one genuine label-free win: a nine-billion-parameter model whose fitted thresholds collapse to "any symptom at all counts as above minimum," for a model that marks about a quarter of one symptom per post.

Then two findings that are worse for the field than for the method. First, higher ordinal agreement is inversely related to clinical usefulness here. One model-corpus combination has the lowest kappa in the table and misses nine of fifty severe posts; change the configuration and kappa nearly doubles while it misses forty-nine of fifty. Missed severe cases rose in eleven of twelve comparisons as the pipeline got more structured. Second, and this is the part I'd nail to the door: the primary corpus is a relabelled copy of a stress dataset. Every deduplicated post appears word-for-word in the original, none of the source subreddits is a depression community, anxiety and PTSD forums supply thirty-four of the fifty-six gold-severe posts, and only three of those fifty-six mention self-harm. A model using that dataset's own metadata plus word counts matches frontier criteria extraction under the a priori rule — and the fields that don't read the post at all, things like which subreddit it came from and how many upvotes it got, beat every zero-shot direct prompt from every model. If your benchmark can be solved by karma score, you are not measuring depression severity. Single author, admirably candid, and most of the secondary analysis is explicitly exploratory.

A companion paper from Alabama attacks the adjacent assumption, and I want to be careful with it because its headline is weaker than it looks. If models struggle with long patient histories, the obvious move is a bigger context window. They compared five context strategies — full history, recent-only, episodic, semantic retrieval and a hybrid — across four small open-weight models on a longitudinal clinical reasoning benchmark with timelines averaging thirty sessions. Retrieval won on accuracy for all four. But the distance-robustness claim, that retrieval holds up as the supporting evidence recedes into the past, is not statistically supported: of all the strategy-by-distance interactions tested, exactly one reaches significance, and the discussion concedes the slopes don't differ. And the most dramatic number in the paper, a full-context condition scoring under ten percent, is really a generation-failure rate — that model answered fewer than a fifth of the questions at all, and when it did it scored in the mid-fifties like everything else.

What does survive is worth having. Citation grounding strongly favours retrieval — episodic retrieval roughly doubles the precision and recall with which the model points at the records its answer rests on, against feeding it everything. And the adversarial result stands: strong performance on answerable questions did not translate into appropriate abstention when the history doesn't support the question. Those are separate competencies and we almost never measure the second. Caveat the whole thing, though: correctness is scored by another language model, with only moderate agreement against a second judge and no clinician validation at all.

Now the empirical counterpart in psychiatry, with a methodological lesson attached. A large multi-site group integrated eighty-nine-channel functional near-infrared spectroscopy with a broad panel of peripheral neuroimmune, metabolic and oxidative-stress measures — inflammatory markers, antioxidant defenses, oxidized lipids, lipidomics and microbiome sequencing — in eighty-six depressed inpatients and forty matched controls. During a verbal fluency task, patients showed reduced hemodynamic variability across prefrontal, frontal eye field, language and sensorimotor cortex, surviving false-discovery correction; at rest almost nothing did. That task-versus-rest contrast is the genuinely interesting structural point, because it says the abnormality lives in the response to cognitive demand rather than in the resting state, which is where most of the biomarker field has been looking. Even-chain saturated fatty acids, polyunsaturated fatty acid remodelling, ceramides, antioxidants and a lipopolysaccharide-toll-like-receptor module were the consistent peripheral contributors.

The headline is a classification accuracy above ninety-five percent with an area under the curve of point nine eight. Do not carry that number out of the room. This is a feature space of eighty-nine channels times three chromophores times two metrics times two conditions, plus dozens of biomarker composites, against about a hundred and twenty subjects, validated internally — the authors say in plain text that the findings reflect internal model validity and that the study is explanatory rather than diagnostic. The telling internal evidence is that the paper reports two estimates of the same quantity and they disagree: cross-validated random forests explain thirty-nine to forty-four percent of the variance in the cortical signal, while the regressions on the same outcomes report sixty-two to sixty-four percent — because those models were built by best-subsets selection and then refitted on the same data with the post-selection coefficients reported as if confirmatory. That roughly twenty-point gap is the selection procedure, not biology, and it is one of the cleanest illustrations of post-selection inference I've seen in a psychiatry paper this year. Add that the unsupervised analysis showed substantial group overlap, that age is significant in every model, and that the patients were medicated inpatients against community-volunteer controls. Keep the direction, discard the discrimination.

To the bench, and to the item I enjoyed most, because it is a story about a selection that learned to cheat. OrthoRep is an orthogonal error-prone DNA replication system in yeast derived from Kluyveromyces lactis killer plasmids — your gene of interest sits on a cytoplasmic plasmid that mutates continuously as it replicates while the host genome stays clean. It had never been applied to GPCRs. Christien Dykstra and Vincent Martin at Concordia, with Chang Liu at Irvine, did it with opioid receptors: mu-opioid receptor on the plasmid, ligand activation coupled to yeast growth, passage the culture.

It works, and then it stops, for a reason familiar to anyone who has run a reinforcement learning job. Over longer campaigns, mutations arise that decouple receptor activation from the growth readout, including suppressors in the G protein chimera itself. Cells that grow without the receptor doing anything take over, and evolution halts. That is reward hacking, in a flask, with no language model anywhere near it. Their fix is a site-specific recombinase that lifts evolved variants off the gamed strain onto the landing pad of a fresh unmutated biosensor — reset the chassis, keep the library, resume selection. Worth saying plainly: this mitigates rather than solves. Full escape returned by passage ten of their automated run.

The numbers are good. An evolved delta-opioid receptor double mutant shifted loperamide potency to thirty nanomolar from a co-assayed wild-type of one point two micromolar, about forty-fold. The mu-opioid winner gave about seven-fold on the background it evolved on, and eighteen-fold on a cholesterol-humanized background it had never seen — the second number is the more encouraging one, because it suggests something transferable rather than overfitted.

But read their controls, because the authors are direct about what they did not get. None of the winning residues in either receptor sit in the orthosteric pocket or at the G protein interface. Growth under selection was essentially uncorrelated with growth without it, so the gains aren't trivial fitness — but they look like folding and expression rather than ligand recognition, and the authors say outright that the platform has not yet delivered pocket-level, ligand-specific gains. Mutation abundance also doesn't track phenotype: the single most common substitution turned up in a hundred and ninety-two carriers spread across all three response classes, while several of the best variants were rare. So you cannot read enrichment as a fitness signal here. As a platform this is a real advance, and the chassis-refresh trick generalizes well beyond yeast. As receptor engineering, it is so far making receptors fold better, not bind better.

The one piece of circuit neuroscience that made it through the rate limiting, and it's a good one. We knew microglia in adolescent male rats refine nucleus accumbens dopamine D1 receptors by complement-mediated engulfment, and that this shapes play behaviour. A new study asks what post-weaning social isolation does to that process — isolation from postnatal day twenty-one to thirty-eight, with resocialization to day fifty-four. The effects are strongly sex-specific. A principal components analysis of fifteen play behaviours found isolated males occupying roughly eighteen times the behavioural volume of group-housed males, females thirteen times; isolation doesn't shift behaviour so much as scatter it. Isolated males' accumbens microglia engulf more D1 receptor. And the microglial transcriptome shows almost nothing at day thirty-eight but thousands of differentially expressed genes at day fifty-four, after resocialization, with complement C1q subunits up. The molecular consequence of isolation appears during recovery, not during isolation.

What changes is the mapping, not the level. In group-housed animals, accumbens D1 density correlates positively with play and allogrooming; in isolated animals that correlation is gone and instead D1 density correlates negatively with biting. Blocking complement-mediated engulfment with neutrophil inhibitory factor into the accumbens abolishes the density-behaviour correlation in both groups and interacts with isolation to worsen aggression. So this is not a rescue. Pruning appears to be required for D1 density to mean anything behaviourally at all, which is a stranger and better claim than "isolation over-prunes." The critical control, though, is a null: chemogenetically driving or silencing VTA dopamine neurons across the isolation window changed nothing — not dopamine markers, not D1 receptor, not microglial volume, not engulfment. The intuitive mechanism, that dopamine activity sculpts the pruning, is not supported, and the authors pivot to glutamate. They are honest that the null is underpowered against doses used elsewhere. Nearly everything else here is within-group correlation at n of eight to eleven across dozens of behaviours without multiple-comparison correction, so hold the specifics loosely and the reframing tightly.

Two shorter items to close. First, MARCO, from Fudan and collaborators, which trains language models to edit molecules across several rounds of proposal, verifier feedback and revision rather than emitting one edited structure in a single shot. Reinforcement learning with shaped per-turn rewards aggregated into a trajectory return; on a three-objective molecular optimization benchmark across three backbones and seen and unseen instruction splits, it takes the best product of property-success-rate and similarity-to-source in every primary setting.

That composite metric is where you have to be careful, and the paper's own component table shows why. On raw property success rate, MARCO is frequently worse than plain supervised fine-tuning. It wins the composite because its similarity to the source molecule is far higher — in one cell, roughly double. So what's been bought is a better point on the property-versus-similarity tradeoff, which is genuinely useful in lead optimization where you need to stay near a scaffold, but it is not better chemistry. And the authors include the cautionary case that proves the metric is gameable: a baseline reinforcement-learning run reaches a similarity of exactly one with a property success rate of zero — it learned to emit the input molecule unchanged. They add an audit to catch exact copies, which is the right instinct, but the objective still rewards conservatism. Two more flags: the verifier and the benchmark share the same property oracles, so the model is optimizing against the thing that grades it with no external validation, and there are no error bars, seeds or significance tests anywhere in the paper.

Finally, a small twin study that earns its place as a cautionary tale about method. An Italian group looked at genetic versus environmental contributions to language-related brain regions — thirty-one healthy same-sex twin pairs aged nine to thirty-one, plus seventeen pairs discordant for psychopathological risk. The conclusion is that environmental factors predominate. But the variance decomposition uses Falconer's formula, which computes heritability as twice the difference between identical-twin and fraternal-twin correlations, and in this sample almost none of those differences reach significance. When the two correlations are nearly equal — exactly what a dozen identical pairs produce by sampling noise alone — Falconer mechanically returns low heritability and a large shared-environment term. The conclusion is substantially an artifact of the estimator meeting a small sample, and the identical and fraternal groups also differ significantly in sex composition, which sits underneath every comparison. The stronger part is the discordant-pair design, where each pair is its own control for genetics and upbringing, and there the right middle frontal gyrus recurs with higher volume and thickness in the higher-risk twin. Their own abstract says "despite the small sample, preliminary results suggest," and that hedge should travel with the finding. The failure mode is instructive and common: an estimator returning a confident-looking decomposition when what it has really detected is that you don't have enough twins.

That's the week. The pattern worth carrying: in most of these the control experiment is more informative than the headline, and in several the authors say so themselves — the yeast selection that got gamed, the dopamine manipulation that did nothing, the auditable classifier that misses the severe cases, the benchmark solvable by upvote count, the composite metric that rewards not editing the molecule, the heritability estimate that was really a sample-size readout. Read the methods.
