## Script

Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Friday, October sixteenth. Ten items today, drawn mostly from medRxiv and the Nature family, with four from arXiv at the end.

Here is the thread that runs through today's set. In paper after paper, the group difference does not live in the sensor — it lives in the decision rule applied to what the sensor reports. Depressed patients see emotional faces as well as anyone and decide about them differently. Depressed patients feel force, but the signal arriving is noisier, so they lean harder on what they already expected. Levodopa's effect on the subthalamic nucleus depends on which contact you recorded from. A drug class looks protective until you ask which member of the class. Keep that in mind and today holds together.

We open with the strongest clinical psychopharmacology item of the week, a prospective observational study from Sainte-Anne Hospital in Paris. Fifty-three inpatients with treatment-resistant depression — seventy-two percent unipolar, twenty-eight percent bipolar — went through a four-week induction course of eight twice-weekly intranasal esketamine sessions, most of them starting at fifty-six milligrams. Fifty-three healthy controls matched on age, sex and education were assessed once. Everyone did an emotional facial expression morphing task: faces that drift gradually from neutral toward happy or sad, with the participant pressing a key the moment they detect an emotion, and then saying what they think it was. Patients were tested at baseline, twenty-four hours after the first administration, and at the end of induction.

The cleverness is in the analysis. Most prior work on emotional bias in depression reports global accuracy or misclassification rates, which conflate three separate things: how well you can tell an emotional face from a neutral one, how much evidence you demand before you will say an emotion is present, and how you interpret an ambiguous face once you have decided something is there. The authors used signal detection theory to pull those apart — d-prime for discriminability, criterion for the evidence threshold — and kept neutral-face categorization as a third, separate measure.

The baseline picture is sharper than the usual story. Patients needed more intensity to detect a happy face, had lower odds of detecting sadness, and were more than twice as likely to call a detected neutral face negative — fifty-five percent versus thirty-five percent in controls. But global d-prime did not differ between patients and controls at all. What differed was the criterion: patients were more conservative, demanding more evidence before reporting any emotion, with a between-group difference around a fifth of a standard unit and a Cohen's d of about zero point four. So the negative bias in treatment-resistant depression is not a perceptual deficit. It is a decision policy.

Then the treatment data. Forty-one percent of patients met the fifty-percent MADRS reduction threshold for response, and baseline severity did not separate responders from non-responders. Two things did. Responders showed an early, sustained rise in discriminability within twenty-four hours of the first dose, and by week four they had markedly less negative categorization of neutral faces than non-responders, an odds ratio of about zero point two four. Non-responders instead drifted toward a more liberal criterion early on. And prognostically, a lower baseline criterion — a more liberal starting threshold — predicted subsequent response, with an odds ratio of zero point one five, while baseline MADRS predicted nothing in the adjusted model.

Two reasons to be careful. This is a single centre, observational, with no placebo arm, so twenty-four-hour changes cannot be separated from expectancy or from acute dissociative effects, and the authors say so. And the categorization analysis only includes trials where a participant both false-alarmed on a neutral face and gave a valence response, which shrinks the sample to about thirty patients per timepoint. Still, the conceptual payoff is real: the cognitive neuropsychological model of antidepressant action — early bias change precedes mood change — appears to extend from monoaminergic drugs to a glutamatergic one, but the bias component that moves is the decisional one.

Which sets up the second item beautifully, because it makes the same move one level deeper, in motivation rather than emotion. A group studying effort processing in acute major depression took fifty inpatients and ninety-four healthy controls through an effort psychophysics task: squeeze to a specified fraction of your maximum voluntary contraction, then rate how effortful it felt. The clinical intuition everyone has is that depressed patients experience everything as harder. That is not quite what the data say.

What they found is compression. Depressed participants rated low and intermediate effort levels higher, yes, but the slope relating objective force to subjective effort was flattened — objectively distinct force levels felt more alike. And when they asked which of those two features tracked clinical state, it was compression, not elevation. Within the depressed group, lower effort discriminability correlated with symptom burden at a rho of about minus zero point three four, while the overall rating intercept correlated at essentially zero. A trial-level model made the same point: PHQ-9 score did not predict overall rating elevation, but the interaction of symptom score with objective effort was negative, meaning flatter slopes at higher symptom burden.

Then the mechanism. They fit a dynamic Bayesian observer model in which each rating combines a noisy current effort signal with a running prior, which lets you ask whether compression comes from an overly rigid prior or from a less precise sensory signal. Sensory uncertainty was credibly higher in depression — roughly twenty-three versus nineteen on their scale, a difference with posterior probability of one — while prior uncertainty did not credibly differ. The upshot is that depressed participants weight prior expectation more heavily, but not because the prior got stronger; because the evidence got weaker. And the compression measure connected to behaviour: lower effort discriminability predicted reduced effort sensitivity on a separate effort-reward decision task, where overall rating did not.

I like this paper because it reframes a symptom we usually describe in value terms as an inference problem. If the internal signal reporting how hard your body is working becomes unreliable, then the rational thing to do is fall back on expectation — and a person who cannot tell a small effort from a large one will stop discriminating between actions on the basis of cost. One honest caveat the authors flag: their model recovers the correct explanation in only about seventy-six to seventy-nine percent of simulations at the group level, rising to the low eighties with mixed classifications allowed. That is adequate for a group claim and nowhere near enough for an individual one.

The third item is the week's big mechanistic paper, in Nature Neuroscience, and it is a genuine candidate mechanism for cognitive reserve. The authors ran an in vivo genetic screen for factors induced in dentate granule cells by synapse loss, and pulled out Pla2g2f, a secreted calcium-dependent phospholipase that hydrolyses membrane glycerophospholipids into lysophospholipids and free fatty acids. Its expression is essentially undetectable in the healthy adult mouse brain, and it climbs in dentate granule cells with age — in mice and, in single-nucleus data, in humans.

The compensation argument then gets tested in both directions, which is what makes it convincing. Conditional deletion of Pla2g2f in dentate granule cells of middle-aged and aged mice reproduced an aging phenotype: dendritic spine loss with increased microglial engulfment of spines, impaired maturation of adult-born granule cells, a shift toward CD68-high, less ramified microglia and reactive astrocytes, accumulation of lipid droplets, and hippocampal-dependent memory impairment. Overexpression in aged mice went the other way — lower lipid droplet burden, restored microglial morphology, preserved memory. And the non-cell-autonomous part was reconstructed in human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived neurons and microglia, where neuron-derived PLA2G2F reduced microglial lipid accumulation and promoted export of cholesterol esters.

So the proposal is that an aging neuron, having lost synapses, secretes a lipid-hydrolysing broadcast signal that keeps the surrounding glia metabolically functional. The human anchor is the part to hold loosely: granule cell nuclei from cognitively resilient donors who had Alzheimer's histopathology showed higher PLA2G2F than donors with severe disease, with a p value of zero point zero four across a handful of samples and a few dozen nuclei. That is a direction, not a result. But as a mechanistic instantiation of compensation — induced by the insult, required for resilience, sufficient when restored — this is the clearest example I have seen.

Staying with aging interventions but moving to pharmacoepidemiology: a target trial emulation on individual angiotensin receptor blockers and dementia risk, run in the TriNetX linked network across forty-four US health systems. The class-level finding that ARBs beat ACE inhibitors on dementia risk has been around for years. The question here is whether the class is homogeneous, and it is not. Starting from twenty-two million screened individuals, two million eligible, ARB-and-ACE-inhibitor-naive adults aged fifty-five to eighty, with separate propensity-score matching for every pairwise comparison against lisinopril, pooled ARBs showed a hazard ratio of zero point eight six for incident all-cause dementia — but individual agents ranged from zero point six six to zero point nine three. Within-class comparisons against losartan reproduced the same ordering, with a range of zero point six eight to zero point nine four. Losartan, the most-prescribed ARB in the United States, had the least favourable associations.

The within-class comparison is what makes this worth discussing, because it removes most of the usual objection. Comparing an ARB to an ACE inhibitor leaves you worrying about who gets prescribed what; comparing ARBs to each other leaves far less room for confounding by indication, and the negative control associations were near null within class. The mechanistic story on offer is that ARBs differ in half-life, in affinity and dissociation kinetics at the angiotensin two type one receptor, and in central nervous system penetration. That is plausible, and this design cannot test it. What it can do is justify a randomized comparison of two specific drugs, which is a much more tractable trial than "antihypertensives for dementia prevention."

Next, a small, careful physiology paper on where levodopa actually acts in the subthalamic nucleus. Twelve people with Parkinson's disease undergoing bilateral subthalamic deep brain stimulation contributed thirty contacts, fourteen dorsal and sixteen ventral, recorded off and on medication, with simultaneous scalp EEG in six of them. Levodopa suppressed beta power far more strongly at dorsal than ventral contacts — about minus two point five decibels versus minus zero point one two. Dorsal contacts also showed larger theta-to-beta and gamma-to-beta state-space shifts, both driven mainly by the beta suppression rather than by gains at the other frequencies. And the directional coupling analysis, using the phase slope index, showed dorsal low-beta and beta interactions shifting toward cortex leading, with less consistent ventral responses.

The clinical reason to care is adaptive stimulation. Closed-loop DBS devices now in the clinic sense beta power and modulate stimulation in response. If the magnitude of the dopaminergic beta response depends on whether your sensing contact sits dorsal or ventral in the nucleus, then contact selection is not just a therapeutic-window question — it determines whether your biomarker is responsive at all. Twelve patients and thirty contacts is a small sample, the dorsoventral assignment depends on imaging-based localization, and six of the recordings were acute post-operative with the stun effect in play. Treat it as a strong argument for reporting contact anatomy in biomarker studies rather than as a settled effect size.

Sixth, a developmental paper in Neuropsychopharmacology that fills a genuinely empty space: circulating endocannabinoids in adolescence. Using the ABCD endocannabinoid substudy, nearly twelve hundred youth with a mean age of fourteen and a half contributed serum measured by isotope-dilution mass spectrometry for anandamide, two-AG, and three related lipid mediators — palmitoylethanolamide, oleoylethanolamide and two-oleoylglycerol, which share metabolic pathways with the endocannabinoids but signal largely through non-cannabinoid receptors.

Females had lower concentrations than males across all five molecules, with the largest gap in two-AG. More interesting is the puberty interaction. In males, more advanced pubertal status went with higher anandamide, palmitoylethanolamide and oleoylethanolamide; in females those associations were flat, and the only pubertal associations were negative, for two-AG and two-oleoylglycerol. The exploratory symptom correlations split by sex too: in males, more painful body sites tracked higher two-AG, while in females lower two-AG tracked greater internalizing problems and lower two-oleoylglycerol tracked attention problems. These are cross-sectional associations with pubertal stage, not within-person trajectories, the effect sizes are small, and almost everything past the primary sex comparison is exploratory. But if you are building pharmacology around the endocannabinoid system for adolescent pain or mood, this is the first normative reference to anchor on, and it says the baseline you are perturbing is sex-specific.

Seventh, a systematic integration of postmortem brain transcriptomics across suicide, bipolar disorder and major depression, out of Columbia. The design detail that matters is the comparison group for suicide: studies were only eligible if they compared suicide decedents with psychiatric non-suicide individuals carrying the same underlying diagnosis, which is what lets you ask about suicide as something other than a proxy for severity of mood disorder. Two hundred seventy-six genes were shared across all three conditions, concentrated in cellular stress and hypoxia, tumour necrosis factor alpha and NF-kappa-B signalling, the IL-6-JAK-STAT3 axis, complement activation, extracellular matrix remodelling and the unfolded protein response.

The distinct signatures are the more interesting half. Suicide-associated genes were predominantly downregulated and clustered in metal-ion homeostasis, stress response, synaptic regulation and circadian pathways; bipolar disorder showed downregulation in synaptic and vesicle processes with region-specific metabolic changes; depression showed changes in RNA processing, ribosomal function and protein synthesis. Genetic integration found that one hundred thirty-three of the genes shared between bipolar disorder and depression carried GWAS variants for the corresponding condition, and transcription factor analysis found twenty regulators with altered activity across all three conditions despite largely different predicted downstream targets — which is a nice way of saying the regulatory machinery converges even where the output does not. The limits are the limits of every postmortem integration: bulk tissue, heterogeneous platforms and brain regions, no single-cell or peripheral data because those do not exist for suicide, and medication exposure uncontrolled throughout.

The last three items are computational, and the first of them is the most directly useful to anyone running a CNS clinic. A group at Baylor and Texas Children's built a dual large language model consensus pipeline to extract treatment-response phenotypes for trofinetide in Rett syndrome from clinical notes — twenty-six thousand seven hundred sixty-three notes from one hundred eight patients with confirmed sustained exposure. The pipeline filters notes by keyword, runs each through two models, and requires both to agree before a positive call.

The validation is narrow and honest about it. Only diarrhea, the drug's signature adverse effect, has a chart-review ground truth. There, a prompt requiring the diarrhea to be both active and severe hit eighty-four percent accuracy with an F-score of zero point six seven, which is forty-seven percentage points better than regular-expression matching and about forty points better than negation-aware conventional NLP. Expert reviewers spot-checking six calls per phenotype agreed with ninety percent of them. The authors are explicit that the other seventeen phenotypes rest on spot checks and inter-model agreement rather than full annotation, and that their rates are hypothesis-generating lower bounds.

What the extracted trajectories show is why this is worth doing. Severe diarrhea was documented in sixty-four percent of evaluable patients at six months and under twenty percent at every later visit — compared with roughly eighty percent for any diarrhea in the pivotal trial, a difference the authors attribute mostly to their narrower threshold. Medication refusal peaked at thirty-eight percent at eighteen months and fell to fifteen percent later. And weight-loss documentation was already at sixty-three percent before treatment started and stayed in that band throughout, which is exactly the kind of finding that ordinary pharmacovigilance would have misread as a treatment-emergent effect. A consensus architecture requiring two models to agree buys precision at the cost of recall, which for a signal-detection task on charts is the right trade — and it is a reminder that the main obstacle to real-world monitoring of a rare-disease drug is labour, not statistics.

Ninth, an evaluation framework for language models used in mental health support, from Sword Health with PhD-level psychologists. The two contributions are a rubric set, breaking response quality into explicit operational criteria with graded severity weights rather than an opaque Likert score, and a patient simulator built to resist the main failure of this genre — synthetic patients who are implausibly forthcoming and self-reflective. Their simulated patients carry a fixed profile with a clinical formulation and core belief, commit to a conversational move via a tool call before verbalizing it, and carry memory across sessions so the longitudinal arc is modelled.

Across eighteen proprietary and open-weight models, the headline is that frontier models have largely cleaned up surface-level quality and boundary violations, and the dominant remaining failure mode is guidance calibration — getting the balance of exploring versus directing wrong. The session-level dynamics make that concrete: mean severity rises from one point four six in the first four turns to three point seven two by turns eleven through twenty, for all eighteen models, and under-guiding is flagged in twelve percent of replies in the first two turns rising to about seventy percent from turn nine onward. Formulaic responding grows from two percent to twenty-six percent over a session. In other words, these systems do not fail by saying something harmful; they fail by continuing to explore when the patient has started expecting direction. The obvious caveat is that this is an LLM-as-judge pipeline evaluating LLM counsellors talking to an LLM patient, and the authors note the judge may show family self-preference — but the failure mode it isolates is the one clinicians would name.

And to close, a lighter but conceptually tidy item on scientific ideation. Most work on using language models to generate research ideas optimizes one idea at a time, which means any diversity among a set of candidates is incidental. This group trained against a set-level reinforcement learning objective instead: in a single generation the model writes several structured idea cards grounded in a set of source papers, and the set is scored jointly, with each idea's quality weighted by its distance from the ideas written before it. That gradient pushes each new idea away from its predecessors.

Their eight-billion-parameter model beat both frontier baselines on the combined quality-and-diversity measure on a contamination-free benchmark, four experts in a pilot judged its sets about as promising as the strongest baseline's while containing marginally more mutually distinct ideas, and when a coding agent ran proof-of-concept experiments, success rates were comparable. That last check is the one that matters, because the cheap way to win a diversity metric is to generate things nobody could execute. It is a small paper with a small human study, but the framing transfers: if you want a brainstorm rather than a sample, you have to score the set.

That is the set for today — a decision criterion that predicts esketamine response, an effort signal too noisy to discriminate, a phospholipase that may be what cognitive reserve is made of, a dopamine effect that depends on where you put the electrode, a drug class that is not one thing, the first normative endocannabinoid data in adolescents, a transcriptomic map of what suicide shares with and does not share from mood disorder, and three computational papers about reading charts, judging counsellors, and brainstorming properly.
