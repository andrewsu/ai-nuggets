## Script

Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Tuesday, October sixth. Eleven items. A sourcing note, because it shaped the lineup: bioRxiv and medRxiv were both wide open and full text came back on everything I asked for, but the Nature-family journals rendered as bare reference lists with no body at all — so three otherwise eligible journal papers, including a review of psychedelics in disorders of consciousness that would have paired beautifully with today's opener, are deferred rather than summarized. Today is preprints and arXiv.

A thread runs through most of what follows, worth naming now because it recurs across disciplines: the layer you can most easily measure is usually not the layer that's limiting.

We start with the clearest version. A collaboration spanning Fribourg, Stanford, Basel, Cambridge and Imperial took resting-state functional MRI from two crossover studies run on the same Basel scanner — one giving LSD, MDMA and D-amphetamine, the other LSD, psilocybin and mescaline — and asked what these drugs do to the architecture of the global workspace.

The framework is doing real work here. The information-theoretic version of global workspace theory, from Luppi and colleagues, splits workspace regions into two roles using integrated information decomposition. Gateways are regions whose interactions with the rest of the brain are synergistic — information you can only get by looking at several regions jointly. Broadcasters are regions whose interactions are redundant — the same information available from either region alone. Gateways gather, broadcasters distribute. That's a testable decomposition rather than a metaphor, and it has an independent track record: this synergistic capacity collapses under anesthesia and in disorders of consciousness, and returns on recovery.

The finding is that classic psychedelics increase the number of gateways in the frontoparietal control network — about four point four regions on average, significant separately under both LSD datasets, psilocybin and mescaline — and in the dorsal attention network under all four conditions. The default mode network moved much less, and only under mescaline and one of the two LSD datasets.

Now the part I'd emphasize. The frontoparietal network in the reference Human Connectome Project data is predominantly broadcasters. So psychedelics aren't adding integration on top of what was there; they are flipping a set of frontoparietal regions out of a redundant, distributing configuration into a synergistic, gathering one. The authors are careful about this and I think they're right to be: the headline isn't more integration, it's reorganized integration. That is a different claim from the familiar one, and it converges with recent work showing psilocybin shifts integration toward association cortex and away from sensory cortex rather than raising it uniformly.

Two more results carry the paper. The spatial topography of synergy and redundancy separates classic psychedelics from MDMA and amphetamine across two independent cohorts, under participant-level randomization tests that preserve the crossover structure — which matters, because the naive clustering was confounded by shared participants and shared placebo scans, and the authors say so. And the one with legs: the between-drug pattern of synergy effects correlates with the between-drug pattern of subjective effects on the five-dimensional altered states questionnaire at rho of point nine two. Drugs that reorganize the workspace similarly feel similar.

The caveats are load-bearing. The workspace itself was defined from an independent healthy sample and held fixed, so the analysis assumes membership doesn't change under drug — exactly the assumption a skeptic would attack. Resting-state data can't tell you which information reaches the workspace, so nothing here distinguishes ego dissolution from synesthesia. And there is only one mescaline dataset in existence. Still: this is the first application of the synergistic-workspace construct to psychedelic states, and the phenomenology correlation gives the theory something to lose.

One detail cuts against a common story. Both LSD conditions, MDMA and amphetamine all reduced gateways in the visual network — and amphetamine does not produce psychedelic visual phenomena. So the visual change is a general property of psychoactive drugs here, not the mechanism of hallucination. If hallucinations are workspace-related at all, they come from the visual change occurring alongside the higher-order ones, not from it alone.

Staying with psilocybin but moving to the clinic. A Johns Hopkins group has posted an open-label, waitlist-controlled randomized trial of repeated psilocybin in obsessive-compulsive disorder — thirty-seven enrolled, thirty completing follow-up, all of whom had already failed either medication or an evidence-based psychotherapy such as exposure and response prevention. Two sessions two weeks apart, twenty milligrams then thirty if tolerated. The immediate arm dosed within a month; the waitlist arm got identical support and dosing after eight weeks.

The efficacy signal on the Yale-Brown obsessive-compulsive scale is substantial — a large effect against the waitlist, persisting a month after the second dose, and persisting in the waitlist group once they crossed over. Fifty percent met responder criteria and about thirty-seven percent were in remission at one month.

Be careful about what that establishes, and so are the authors. This is open-label with a waitlist control, which means every participant knew they would get psilocybin eventually. A waitlist does not control for expectancy — it controls for the passage of time. The authors say plainly that efficacy here is preliminary and that the field's real problem is the absence of a convincing active comparator for a drug whose effects are unmistakable to the person receiving them.

More interesting than the effect size is the correlational structure, because it speaks to mechanism. Improvement tracked the mystical experience questionnaire total, and on the altered-states instrument it tracked Bliss, Unity and Disembodiment specifically — and ran the other direction for Paranoia on the challenging experience questionnaire. That is a cleaner dose-of-experience relationship than much of this literature produces, and it makes the set-and-setting conversation empirical. It also raises the obvious confound: in an unblinded trial, the people having the most profound experiences are also the people most convinced they got a real treatment.

A third clinical item, about trial design as much as schizophrenia. A pooled individual-participant analysis of five placebo-controlled antipsychotic-withdrawal relapse-prevention studies — five hundred and fifty-seven patients randomized to placebo, double-blind phases of at least six months — asked which baseline symptoms predict relapse.

The result is counterintuitive in a useful way. Positive and negative subscales predicted relapse in opposite directions at almost identical magnitude. Higher baseline positive symptoms raised risk — a four-point difference is about twenty-seven percent higher. Higher baseline negative symptoms lowered it — a four-point difference is about twenty-one percent lower. General psychopathology predicted nothing.

Think about what that does to enrichment strategy. If you design a relapse-prevention trial and select for severity in the usual undifferentiated way, you are mixing two populations that move in opposite directions and partially cancelling your own event rate. The clinical reading is worth sitting with too: residual positive symptoms at withdrawal mark instability, while a predominantly negative presentation marks something closer to a stable, if impaired, state. These are placebo arms, so this is prognosis under no active drug rather than treatment prediction, and it needs prospective validation. But the asymmetry is clean and the sample is large for this question.

From prognosis to anatomy. A group has produced the first high-resolution spatiomolecular map of the human habenula, combining single-nucleus multiome sequencing — gene expression and chromatin accessibility in the same nuclei — with spatially resolved transcriptomics.

For a structure this central to the depression and addiction literature, almost everything we know about its molecular organization comes from rodents and zebrafish. This resolves four medial and five lateral habenular neuronal types in humans and places them anatomically. Two things stand out. They identify two putative inhibitory populations in the lateral habenula — which matters because whether habenular cells described as GABAergic actually release GABA has been genuinely unresolved, and an inhibitory population inside a structure modeled as uniformly glutamatergic changes the circuit you'd draw. And when they test which cell types are enriched for psychiatric genetic risk, the strongest enrichment is medial, not lateral.

That second point is what I'd carry away, because the field's attention is overwhelmingly on the lateral habenula — burst firing, aversive prediction error, the ketamine story. The medial habenula is the nicotine-withdrawal structure, high in mu-opioid receptor, projecting to the interpeduncular nucleus. If common-variant risk concentrates there, the therapeutic map is less lateral-centric than current work assumes. This is a resource paper and the enrichment analysis is correlational, but it's the kind of resource that redirects where people look.

Now a mechanistic result I liked for its willingness to report two measurements that disagree. A Michigan-led group recorded locus coeruleus neurons and downstream norepinephrine release simultaneously during hyperthermia-induced seizures in a Dravet syndrome mouse model, using electrocorticography plus in vivo imaging with a fluorescent norepinephrine sensor.

Population somatic activity in the locus coeruleus was inhibited during seizures. Downstream norepinephrine release was dramatically elevated. Those are the opposite sign, from the same event, in the same animals.

The reconciliation is the Glutamate Amplifies Noradrenergic Effects model — GANE — which holds that glutamate spilling out of excitatory synapses acts on receptors on noradrenergic axon terminals and drives release locally, independent of what the cell body is doing. The authors show that activating a downstream region is by itself sufficient to evoke local norepinephrine release, partly through glutamate receptors, and that ictal release generalizes across other downstream regions, other epilepsy models and other seizure-induction methods.

The implication for pharmacology is direct. The therapeutic logic around noradrenergic intervention in epilepsy has largely assumed locus coeruleus firing is the quantity to raise. If terminals are already being driven to supraphysiologic release by local glutamate while the soma is suppressed, then somatic activation and terminal release are separate targets, and a drug acting on one is not a proxy for the other. This is one mouse model of one syndrome, and the inference about impaired consciousness is extrapolation — but the measurement dissociation is hard to argue with.

Now to the computational half, starting with the paper I think is the most consequential thing on this list for anyone building drug-discovery agents.

A team from AMD Silo AI with AstraZeneca's Molecular AI group built MAGI, a modular agent that writes and revises project objectives, launches and monitors molecular optimization, reads emerging structure-activity relationships, and changes strategy accordingly. It drives a tool layer exposing REINVENT 4, ADMET models, temporally restricted project models, Boltz-2 cofolding and an interaction profiler. Molecules come either straight from the language model or from agent-controlled REINVENT, with the same scoring stack behind both.

And then — this is what makes it matter — they tested it on nine real retrospective lead-optimization campaigns from three pharmaceutical companies: four GSK BET series, four Sanofi projects, one undisclosed AstraZeneca campaign. Replayed under fixed temporal cutoffs, the agent shown one starter and four measured analogues with everything else withheld.

The headline is that which generator you use does not determine whether the campaign succeeds. The language model acted as a focused local optimizer, staying near the known chemistry and reaching comparable or better primary activity in far fewer operations. REINVENT explored broader chemical space and demanded four to nine times more operations, before counting the hundred thousand structures inside its reinforcement-learning loop. Neither dominated on attainment. What determined attainment was whether the proposed chemistry fell inside the scorer's applicability domain. Both failing campaigns failed for exactly that reason: one had a solubility target outside its model's training range, the other a ligand-based model with weak held-out correlation.

That's an argument benchmarks structurally cannot make. Evaluate an agent on generic molecular benchmarks with well-behaved oracles and you measure orchestration. Put it on real industrial project data and the oracle becomes the binding constraint. The agent is a coordination layer, and coordination was not the bottleneck.

Two more details. In the failing solubility campaign the agent autonomously promoted thermodynamic solubility as a proxy when it noticed the kinetic scorer was extrapolating — the right instinct, and it improved the proposals. And in a blinded evaluation, four chemists could not distinguish agent-generated molecules from held-out real compounds, showed no preference between routes, and judged the agent's structure-activity reasoning plausible but incomplete. The authors' own framing of the remaining ceilings is that reasoning and scorer applicability — not autonomy. Note that every endpoint here is predicted rather than measured, with no static-objective control, so the agent's own contribution isn't isolated.

Staying in computational pharmacology for a result I found genuinely surprising. APOD — agentic population ordinary differential equation discovery — is a language-model agent that discovers population mechanistic models from data. Not just the shared differential equation structure, but the between-subject variability model alongside it, which is what makes it a population model rather than a curve fit. It proposes candidate structures, hands parameter estimation to specialized population-estimation software, and refines by reasoning over fit diagnostics, residuals, identifiability and structural plausibility.

On synthetic pharmacokinetic and tumor-dynamics benchmarks it recovered ground-truth structures in ninety-four to a hundred percent of runs, twelve-fold faster than an established library-based search. Good, but not the result that made me sit up.

They then pointed it at lutetium-177 DOTATATE radioligand therapy for neuroendocrine tumors, where treatment-induced thrombocytopenia is common and individual platelet dynamics have no established mechanistic model. From a one-sentence task description, given only platelet counts as the observable, the agent converged on the semi-mechanistic Friberg myelosuppression structure — a proliferating pool, five maturation transit compartments, feedback from circulating counts — and introduced a dose-driven drug proxy so the model has a causal handle on an actionable input. Seven differential equations, built from a single observable.

Then they validated it properly, on a temporally independent cohort of nineteen patients at the same center. Fixing the population parameters and individualizing each new patient from the first cycle alone — a median of three measurements — the ninety percent predictive intervals contained the observed platelet nadir in all nineteen, with concordance correlation point nine one. The population model without individualization explained essentially nothing of the between-patient variation, R-squared of point oh six, which is the control that makes the point. As a clinical flag at the grade-one threshold it caught eleven of twelve who developed thrombocytopenia and cleared five of seven who didn't. And because the model is mechanistic in the administered dose, you can run counterfactual schedules: halving activity from cycle two dropped one patient's predicted probability of crossing the threshold from point nine four to point five nine, while omitting a cycle entirely left it at point nine three.

The obvious skeptical question is whether the agent reasoned its way to Friberg or simply retrieved it — it's the standard myelosuppression model and it is certainly in the training data. The prompts were designed to favor structural reasoning, and the synthetic benchmarks with known ground truth are the defense. I don't think that fully settles it, and I'd want to see this on a system with no published analogue. But the temporal validation is real and the counterfactual dosing use is the actual product.

Third computational item, a clever answer to a structural problem. Frontier agents are good at biomarker hypothesis generation, but the private clinical records you'd need to evaluate their proposals can't be shared with them. A group working with Clalit Health Services — a panel of over five point four million patients — trained graph attention networks inside the data boundary to predict, for thirteen immune-mediated diseases, the case-control discriminative performance of a candidate expression built from complete blood count values. Then they released only the weights. An external agent submits a disease and an expression and gets back a predicted score. No patient data leaves.

The headline is that agent-discovered expressions improved on their literature-seeded starting points by a median of four point one eight percentage points of area under the curve.

But read the discussion, because this is one of the more self-critical papers I've covered. The authors call that number selection-bound validation yield, not autonomous clinical gain — the agent queried many candidates and validation came after selection. The reranking benchmark is cleaner: across eighty-one comparisons the scorer picked a better expression than the originating tool about sixty-eight percent of the time, but that's carried almost entirely by one cohort and flatly absent in the other, where the p-value is point nine two — attributed mostly to panels roughly twenty-five times smaller. The negative control is the most informative experiment here: swap the trained scorer for a uniform random tool, hold everything else fixed, and the mean gain collapses from five point eight four percentage points to point oh seven. The gain is the scorer, not the agent's reasoning or the search loop. And on the most demanding comparison — discovered expression against the strongest independent-tool expression — the result was negative in all ten auditable runs.

So: a real and portable method for privacy-preserving evaluation, and an unusually clear demonstration that the agentic wrapper was not where the value came from.

Which is the perfect setup for the next item, a small proof-of-concept preprint that deserves more attention than its size suggests. The authors introduce the term verification debt: the accumulation of agentic decisions, and of the research process producing them, that a human researcher cannot fully check. They ran Biomni — chosen specifically because it exposes detailed traces — on ten biological research questions spanning proteomics, genomics and drug discovery, three identical independent runs each, and scored nine decision-step metrics and thirteen research-process metrics.

Their overall verdict is favorable: the agent retrieved literature, chose appropriate databases, produced reasoning traces, caught some of its own errors, and generally made claims consistent with its evidence. But the specific failures are the useful part. Literature retrieval often ran on a search API returning only a summary of an abstract, so claims were grounded in less than they appeared to be. Assumptions weren't consistently disclosed. And analytical thresholds sometimes appeared to be chosen after seeing the data distribution, with effect sizes and confidence intervals omitted.

The finding I'd put on a slide: these failures don't occur consistently. They surface at unpredictable points and differ across identical runs of the same question. Simpler questions failed on disclosure; complex ones failed on interpretation. That is what makes verification debt a different problem from accuracy — you cannot sample a few outputs and certify the system, because the next run will fail somewhere else. Transparency is necessary for verification and nowhere near sufficient. Ten questions is a small sample and the scoring is qualitative, so treat the framework as a proposal rather than a validated instrument — but the phenomenon is clearly real.

Two shorter items to close, both neural foundation models.

The first joins two awkwardly paired modalities. Patch-seq gives gene expression and intrinsic electrophysiology from the same neuron, the natural substrate for a cross-modal model, but the datasets are small. LangPatch routes both through language: gene descriptions and verbalized electrophysiological profiles get embedded by the same frozen text encoder, with adapters aligning them contrastively. Across mouse visual cortex, mouse motor cortex and human cortex it beats the alternatives on predicting electrophysiology from transcriptome, and roughly halves the cross-modal misalignment metric against the existing Patch-seq imputation method. The result that matters is transfer: trained on mouse visual cortex, it retains about seventy percent of its correlation in mouse motor cortex and forty-seven percent in human cortex. Cross-species zero-shot transfer at that level is the argument that the alignment is capturing biology rather than dataset structure.

And finally, an efficiency result for anyone working with magnetoencephalography. MEG-Mamba replaces the transformer backbone of existing MEG foundation models with a state-space architecture, trained to predict the next token of discretized, source-reconstructed, parcellated signal conditioned on brain region and session. Against MEG-GPT on the same data it achieves higher generative fidelity while pre-training in twenty-two GPU-hours instead of four hundred, with a four-second context instead of a third of a second. A lightweight adapter can also steer it to generate realistic task-evoked responses never seen in pre-training. An eighteen-fold cut in pre-training cost alongside a twelve-fold longer context changes who can participate in this area, which is usually worth more than a fidelity increment.

That's the episode. The pattern, one more time, because today it's unusually consistent: psychedelics don't raise integration, they move it; locus coeruleus firing runs opposite to the norepinephrine release it supposedly causes; the symptom domain that reads as severity predicts relapse backwards; the drug-discovery agent isn't the ceiling, its scorers are; the biomarker gain belongs to the scorer and vanishes without it; and a fully traceable agent still accumulates debt you can't see from any single step. In every case the instrumented layer and the governing layer came apart. Back tomorrow.
