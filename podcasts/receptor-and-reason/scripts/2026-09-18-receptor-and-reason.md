## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. I'm your host, and this is your daily pass through CNS pharmacology, psychiatric and neurology drug discovery, and the computational work closing in on both. Twelve items today, and the thread is state-dependence — the repeated finding that the interesting variable is not whether a drug binds or an intervention works, but which state it engages, and that the global measure almost always hides it.

The lead is a structure paper out of Chicago that rewrites the textbook mechanism for an approved drug. Four-aminopyridine — dalfampridine, the potassium channel blocker approved to improve walking speed in multiple sclerosis — has been understood for decades as an open-channel blocker. The evidence for that was use-dependence: you incubate a channel with the drug while holding it closed and nothing happens, then you deliver a train of depolarizing pulses and the block appears. The natural reading is that the drug needs the gate open to get in.

This group determined a three-point-three angstrom cryo-EM structure of the Shaker channel carrying the classic ILT mutation, bound to four-aminopyridine, and the drug is sitting in the pore of a closed channel. ILT is the useful trick here: it stabilizes a specific partially activated closed state, one in which three of the four voltage-sensor arginines have crossed the hydrophobic plug but the fourth has not. Apply the drug to ILT channels held at zero millivolts, where they sit in that partially activated closed state, and you get inhibition with no depolarizing pulses at all. Hold the same channels at minus ninety, deep in the resting closed state, and you get nothing.

So the requirement was never opening. It was reaching the partially activated closed state, and in wild type the only way there is to activate. The structure explains the rest: the voltage sensor stays where ILT put it, but the linker connecting it to the pore shifts about three and a half angstroms, and the pore helices splay roughly one-point-eight angstroms at the intracellular side. The drug stabilizes a conformation the channel cannot leave to open. It also binds when intracellular pore access is constitutively blocked, so it is not coming up the cytoplasmic mouth — it enters through the membrane-facing fenestrations, in its neutral form, which the molecular dynamics and mutagenesis both support.

What I like most is that they then reproduce the use-dependence that motivated the wrong model in the first place. Strong depolarization drives channels open and the drug unbinds; a subsequent strong hyperpolarization carries the channel through the partially activated state too fast to rebind; so the next pulse gives a larger, faster current. Same phenomenology, opposite mechanism. For rational design on Kv channels in demyelinating disease, the target is now a specific gating intermediate and a lipid-facing access route, not a pore you have to catch open.

Staying with the idea that the receptor state is the pharmacology, a Korean group profiled sixteen synthetic cannabinoid receptor agonists — the new psychoactive substances that keep appearing in the drug supply — and asked what separates the weak ones from the dangerous ones. They measured CB1 activation in a calcium assay using a promiscuous G-alpha-fifteen readout, measured catalepsy in mice, and docked each compound into an active-state CB1 structure. Then they ran principal component analysis on the residue-level interaction profiles and tested the component scores against the pharmacology.

The result is clean in one direction and null in the other. The first principal component of the interaction pattern was significantly associated with maximal effect but not with potency, with strong contributions from activation-related residues including the phenylalanine-two-hundred and tryptophan-three-fifty-six toggle switch. Efficacy is written in which residues the ligand touches; affinity is not. That's a useful asymmetry for a triage problem where the public health question is whether a novel compound drives a full agonist response at CB1, not whether it binds tightly. The caveat they flag themselves is real: several compounds behaved differently in vitro and in vivo, and docking to a single active-state structure is a coarse instrument. But as a prioritization heuristic for compounds nobody has time to fully characterize, an efficacy-associated interaction fingerprint is worth having.

That sets up the third item almost too neatly. A group at Birmingham built a bioactivity predictor for GPCR ligands that attacks the same problem computationally, and their move is to put the Monod-Wyman-Changeux model of allostery inside the network. The architecture, called Dual-State Query, uses no 3D structures at all — active-state GPCR structures are scarce, and where they exist the two conformations are geometrically close enough to confuse standard geometric deep learning. Instead, learnable orthogonal queries pull disentangled active- and inactive-state representations out of a protein language model, and a gating module derives activation probability from the thermodynamic competition between the ligand's affinity for each state and the receptor's intrinsic energy barrier.

The headline numbers are modest — root mean squared error from one-point-zero-seven to one-point-zero-zero, R-squared from point four-five to point five-two against the best baseline. What matters is where the gain concentrates. On the agonist subset, two of three baselines have negative R-squared, meaning worse than predicting the mean, and the third manages point one. Dual-State Query gets point two-seven, and does not beat the baselines on antagonists. The claim is specific and, I think, correct: explicitly representing two conformational states recovers agonist signal that collapses when you treat the task as one scalar regression.

The honesty in the paper is the part to take seriously. They stratify by sequence identity between test and training receptors, and at low homology every model, theirs included, falls to zero or negative R-squared. The thermodynamic prior buys you better efficacy modeling inside receptor families you have data for. It buys you nothing across evolutionary distance. Anyone hoping a physics-informed inductive bias will crack orphan receptors should read that table.

Fourth item, and a change of register. In Molecular Psychiatry, a group gave a single one-milligram-per-kilogram dose of psilocybin to SAPAP3 knockout mice — the standard compulsive-grooming model of obsessive-compulsive disorder — and tested social behavior seven days later in a three-chamber task. The motivation: social dysfunction is a real and under-treated burden in obsessive-compulsive and related disorders, and nothing in the pharmacopoeia targets it.

The finding is narrow and enduring. Sociability itself — preferring a stranger to an empty cage — was intact in every group; psilocybin did nothing to it. The deficit was in social novelty: vehicle-treated knockout males spent less time with a newly introduced mouse than wild-type, and a single dose a week earlier restored that. The three-way interaction sits at p equals point zero-four-seven and the post hoc comparison at point zero-three-two, with eight to thirteen animals per group, so this is a small effect on a modest sample and I would not bet heavily on the exact magnitude. It was also male-specific; females showed no deficit to rescue.

The mechanism half is weaker than the behavior half. Psilocybin raised c-Fos in medial prefrontal cortex and hippocampus, but it did so regardless of genotype — so activation is a drug effect, not a rescue effect. Only infralimbic c-Fos correlated with the improvement in knockout males, which is a correlation in a subgroup. What's genuinely interesting is the domain specificity: one dose, one week later, one behavioral dimension moved and the others didn't. If psychedelics act by opening a plasticity window rather than by chronically occupying a receptor, that is the shape you'd expect the data to have.

Fifth, a postmortem epitranscriptomic survey in alcohol use disorder, and it is a good illustration of why global measures mislead. The group profiled methyl-six-adenosine methylation — the most abundant internal messenger RNA modification — across eight reward- and motor-related brain regions from twelve people with alcohol use disorder and twelve matched controls, a hundred ninety-two samples, using enzymatic cleavage plus microarray mapping of about ten thousand sites.

Global methylation did not differ in any region. Not one. Hippocampus showed a trend at p equals point zero-five-three, which is worth a follow-up given its role in cue-triggered craving, but nothing survived. What did appear was site-selective: fourteen hundred and three differentially methylated sites, distributed unevenly — three hundred sixty-two in prefrontal cortex, two hundred seventy-six in nucleus accumbens, two hundred forty-one in ventral tegmental area, and as few as eighty-nine in hippocampus. The methylation changes tracked expression in the direction canonical decay predicts: hypermethylated transcripts enriched among the downregulated genes, hypomethylated among the upregulated. And they checked the obvious confound — that they were just re-detecting regional expression identity — against the Allen Human Brain Atlas, and the pattern held as disease-associated.

Twelve versus twelve, uncorrected p less than point zero-five with a fold-change filter, is a hypothesis-generating catalog, not a set of validated targets. But the structural claim — that the epitranscriptome in addiction is remodeled site by site rather than globally, the opposite of what the same group's cell-culture work suggested — should change how these studies get designed.

Sixth, a longitudinal study in marmosets pairing an acute behavioral readout with a chronic structural one. Eleven animals, automated behavioral scoring, repeated cocaine at five milligrams per kilogram, imaging out to ten months. Acute cocaine produced rapid repetitive lateral head movements — head shaking — from the first exposures. Resting-state connectivity changed by one month across sensory, parietal, prefrontal, motor and hippocampal regions. Diffusion changes came much later: reduced fractional anisotropy and axial diffusivity in the splenium of the corpus callosum at ten months, with the genu spared.

Cross-species transcriptomic and proteomic comparison then converged on two axon-associated molecules, DPYSL2 and DNM3; DPYSL2 protein was reduced in cocaine-exposed marmoset brain. Alpha-B-crystallin, CRYAB, localized to callosal oligodendrocytes and rose with chronic exposure, which they read as a stress response rather than damage. And they closed the loop in mice: CRYAB knockouts had thinner corpus callosum, more immobility in forced swim, and less open-field locomotion.

Four animals carried the imaging arm, so the effect sizes are not the point. The sequence is. An acute, trivially observable stereotypy precedes functional connectivity change by a month and white-matter change by the better part of a year, in a primate, with a candidate molecular substrate attached. If that ordering replicates, head shaking is a cheap early marker for a slow structural process, which is exactly what this field lacks.

Seventh, and staying with addiction but moving to humans, a nice negative result in Neuropsychopharmacology. Nicotine salt e-liquids — the protonated formulation that reshaped the e-cigarette market — are consistently associated with higher abuse liability than free-base nicotine, and the assumed explanation is that the salt gets more nicotine to the brain faster. A group in North Carolina tested that directly: twenty adult exclusive e-cigarette users, single-blind randomized crossover, one puff of carbon-eleven-labeled nicotine formulated either fully protonated or fully free-base, with dynamic PET-CT imaging in both scan directions.

Deposition differed enormously. The salt put roughly twice as much nicotine into bronchi and lungs — standardized uptake value increases of a hundred and one and a hundred and nine percent. Free base loaded the upper airway instead, with the throat difference above two hundred percent in one scan direction. Brain uptake did not differ. Neither did puff topography. Neither did craving relief or drug liking, though liking trended higher for the salt and about twice as many participants said they enjoyed the respiratory sensations, at p equals point zero-nine.

So the mechanistic story everyone tells about nicotine salts is, at least for a single acute puff, not about the brain. That null redirects the question twice. Pharmacologically, if the salt's abuse liability is real, it is carried by something other than peak brain exposure after one puff — cumulative dosing across a session, rate of rise, or simply tolerability letting people inhale more. Toxicologically, the deposition shift is the finding: the salt delivers aldehydes and other aerosol constituents deep into the lung, where free base does not. Twenty participants, two of whom reported persistent cough after the salt, and several scans excluded as technical failures — small, but the imaging is direct and the crossover design is right.

Eighth, a pharmacoepidemiology paper that does the thing pharmacoepidemiology almost never does, which is replicate. The hypothesis is that antihypertensives differ in dementia risk according to how they leave angiotensin two type-two and type-four receptor signaling — angiotensin receptor blockers, dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers and thiazides leave it stimulated, ACE inhibitors and the rest inhibit it. That had been reported once, in a single Medicare cohort. This group ran the same new-user, active-comparator, propensity-matched design in parallel across two structurally independent sources: Medicare claims, one-point-six million beneficiaries, and Vanderbilt's de-identified electronic health record, eighty thousand patients.

Hazard ratio point nine-two in Medicare, ninety-five percent interval point nine-zero to point nine-three. Hazard ratio point nine-two in Vanderbilt, interval point eight-five to point nine-nine. Two data environments with different populations, different geography, and completely different ascertainment — claims codes versus clinical records — landing on the same number. The known confounders behaved as expected, which is the internal-validity check you want to see.

It is an eight percent relative reduction, so nobody should switch a patient's antihypertensive on this. And predominant-use designs at eighty percent of exposure days still carry confounding by indication that propensity matching does not fully remove. But this is the strongest observational case yet that the renin-angiotensin pathway is doing something in the brain that is separable from blood pressure control, and it sits alongside the losartan work we covered a couple of days ago showing acute angiotensin type-one blockade shifting approach-avoidance behavior in humans. The right next step is the randomized comparative-effectiveness trial the authors ask for.

Ninth item, and the best-framed agentic-AI paper I've read this month. The setting is a P300 speller brain-computer interface, and the question is what happens when you put a language model downstream of a noisy decoder and let it execute rather than suggest. They used one thousand sixty-five episodes from forty-seven people with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis and five language models.

The results are unsparing. Giving agents the decoder's reconstructed confidence never reduced unfaithful execution below a plain deterministic gate at matched coverage, and two models were significantly worse. Apparent safety improvements of up to twenty-two percentage points turned out to be the agent acting less often — sometimes by abstaining, sometimes by emitting invalid tool calls, which reads as safety only because nothing happened. Given the same command vocabulary as a deterministic resolver in a fair-information test, no direct agent arm improved on the resolver's risk-coverage frontier.

What did work was architectural rather than model-side. A hybrid in which the model proposes semantic corrections and an external gate keeps admission authority extended coverage beyond the resolver in five of ten models, with no observed unfaithful executions. That is the design lesson, and it generalizes well past neurotechnology: separate inference from control. Let the model do the thing it is good at — proposing a plausible repair of a garbled decode — and never let it hold the authority to commit the action. And when you see a safety metric improve, check whether the system got better at deciding or just quieter.

Tenth, the evaluation-side companion. A group asked whether language models can judge patient-facing communication, using clinical trial informed consent as the test case — demanding, because it requires conveying complex risk to lay readers under regulatory constraint. They built an eight-hundred question-answer benchmark from fifty oncology consent forms, developed a seven-criterion rubric with multi-stakeholder input, collected clinician reference ratings with good interrater reliability, then benchmarked nineteen model judges.

The split is sharp. On safety screening and document-grounded factual verification, model judges hit Spearman correlations above point eight with clinicians. On communication quality, below point six. Two findings on top of that are worth flagging. Safety-specialized guard models did worse than general-purpose models at the safety task they were built for — which should give pause to anyone reaching for a small guard model as the cheap oversight layer. And patient advocates rated communication quality lower than both clinicians and the model judges, meaning the automated judge is calibrated to the clinician's notion of clarity, and the clinician's notion of clarity is itself not the patient's.

The efficiency case is real — four seconds of model time against five minutes of clinician time for factual accuracy — so this is a deployable oversight layer for the verifiable half of the problem. Don't let it certify the half it cannot measure.

Eleventh, a large systems paper that is interesting as much for what it rejected as for what it built. A group spanning Stanford, Chinese University of Hong Kong and a startup used what they call a governed evolutionary AI Scientist to construct an immune world model — an action-conditioned model of how an intervention moves immune state across three scales at once: cell-intrinsic program, tissue ecosystem, and individual patient context. The agent searched architectures and workflows, and the resulting model was frozen before any independent evaluation, which is the methodological detail that makes the rest credible. Frozen, it generalized to unseen interventions and contexts and forecast unseen perturbation combinations.

Then the model nominated a therapeutic hypothesis — interleukin-thirty-six-gamma plus SIRP-alpha inhibition, chosen as complementary rather than redundant axes. And here is the part I want to highlight: a governed self-correction audit rejected every cytokine pair it screened. Every one. A hypothesis-generating system that mostly says no is a system with a working filter, and in a literature where agentic discovery papers report hit rates and not rejection rates, publishing the audit that killed your candidates is the more informative result. This is immuno-oncology rather than CNS, but the governance pattern — freeze before evaluation, keep an audit that has the authority to reject — is the one worth importing.

And to close, a small computational neuroscience paper with an idea that sticks. The brain has to build an internal model of the world from limited experience, and rare events are the hard case: in any finite sample their observed frequency is badly estimated. This group trained a Bayesian confidence propagation network on sequences from a Markov random walk with known event frequencies, then let it replay autonomously and compared the replay to ground truth at two levels — marginal event frequencies and conditional transition structure.

Deterministic replay systematically distorted rare events, over- or under-representing them. Adding moderate, temporally correlated noise to unit activity during replay restored both the marginal and the conditional statistics. Too little noise and you inherit your sampling error; presumably too much and you lose the structure. Moderate noise also widened the range of parameters over which replay stayed accurate, which is to say it made the system robust, not just correct at one setting.

It is a simulation, and the leap to biology is a leap. But it inverts the usual story — neural variability as a computational resource for statistical correction rather than as the thing the brain has to suppress. The clinical corollary is the interesting part: if faithful internal simulation depends on a specific amount of variability, then conditions and drugs that alter neural gain are altering the fidelity of internal models, not merely adding noise to them. A good place to stop.

That's twelve. A drug that binds the state before the one everyone was watching. An efficacy fingerprint that lives in residues and not affinity. Methylation that is flat globally and reorganized site by site. Nicotine salts that change the lung and not the brain. Two agentic systems whose real contribution is knowing when to refuse. And noise as the thing that makes the model true. Thanks for listening — back tomorrow.
