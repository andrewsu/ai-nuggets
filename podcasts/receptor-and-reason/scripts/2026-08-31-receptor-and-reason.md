## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. I'm your host, and this is your daily pass through CNS pharmacology, psychiatric and neurology drug discovery, and the computational machinery closing in on both. Eleven items today, all preprints — it's a Sunday, so the journals are quiet, but the servers gave us an unusually good week. The through-line, if you want one, is receptor state: several of today's papers are less about which target you hit than what condition the target is in when you hit it.

We open with a preprint from Tallie Baram's lab at UC Irvine that offers the cleanest mechanistic explanation I've seen for an expensive failure in psychiatric drug development. Corticotropin-releasing hormone has been a depression target since the nineteen-eighties, with all the correlational human evidence you could want — elevated CRH in cerebrospinal fluid, augmented CRH drive. Then several well-designed trials of CRHR1 antagonists failed, and the field moved on without an explanation.

The setup is the nucleus accumbens and the limited bedding and nesting model of early-life adversity, which reliably produces anhedonia-like reward deficits in adulthood. In naive mice the pharmacology behaves exactly as the textbook says. Block CRHR1 locally in the accumbens — with a truncated CRH peptide that binds without activating, or with a selective small-molecule antagonist from the class that went into the clinic — and sucrose consumption goes up. Go the other way, displacing endogenous CRH from its binding protein or chemogenetically driving the CRH-and-GABA projection from basolateral amygdala to accumbens, and consumption falls, and that suppression is abolished by the CRHR1 antagonist — so it's the peptide, not the co-released GABA.

In the adversity-exposed animals the CRHR1 antagonist does nothing. Not a smaller effect — nothing. This is where the fiber photometry earns its keep: the GRAB sensor is built on CRHR1 itself, so the readout is receptor engagement rather than peptide concentration. In controls, sucrose delivery produces a sustained drop in signal; in the adversity mice, reward cues fail to inhibit CRH signaling at all, while the acute stress response is completely intact. Then the decisive experiment — displacing CRH from its binding protein, which reliably raises the sensor signal in controls, produces no increase in the adversity animals, and not because receptor expression has fallen; they checked. The receptor is already saturated. There is no headroom in either direction.

Which reframes the target. If CRHR1 is tonically occupied, an antagonist has nothing dynamic to block, and the trial failures become predictable rather than mysterious. So they went to the other receptor. CRHR2 is lower affinity, mostly studied for peripheral cardiovascular and metabolic roles, and in the accumbens it sits on both D1- and D2-positive medium spiny neurons. Blocking it with antisauvagine-thirty does nothing in controls but makes the adversity animals worse — so it's already acting as a brake under high peptide load. And the selective agonist urocortin two does nothing in controls and fully restores palatable food consumption in the adversity mice.

That is an affinity-and-occupancy argument turned into a therapeutic hypothesis: the low-affinity receptor only becomes a target once the high-affinity one is saturated, so the target is defined by the disease state rather than the diagnosis. They have a patent filed. The caveats are that this is one mouse model, the male and female phenotypes differ, and urocortin two into the accumbens is not a drug — but as an explanation for why an entire receptor class failed in the clinic, it's the most satisfying thing I've read this month.

Second item, and staying with receptor state, a preprint from Ben Cravatt's lab at Scripps with Bina Santoro and Anna Moroni that does something structurally similar for ion channels. Activity-based protein profiling has produced covalent ligand maps for a lot of proteins, but almost entirely in immortalized cell lines and primary immune cells — the cells you can get in bulk. CNS-restricted proteins have been largely invisible to the method.

Their fix is a preparation they call brainocytes: gently dissociated intact cells from adult mouse brain, about ninety percent viable, treated with stereochemically matched electrophilic probes and read out by mass spectrometry. The comparison to conventional brain lysate is the point — of eighty-two proteins showing stereoselective engagement, a substantial fraction were liganded only in intact cells, so the interaction depends on the cellular environment. One protein was ligandable only when co-expressed with its paralog, apparently because the complex creates the pocket — a real warning about screening isolated proteins.

The pharmacological payoff is the HCN channels. A tryptoline acrylamide probe engages a conserved cysteine in the cyclic nucleotide-binding domain of HCN1, HCN2 and HCN4 with single-digit micromolar potency and clean enantioselectivity. The functional consequence is unusual: it near-completely abolishes the cyclic-AMP-induced depolarizing shift in voltage-dependent activation while leaving basal channel function alone. In HCN2, cyclic AMP moves half-activation from about minus ninety-two to minus eighty-four millivolts; with the probe on board, cyclic AMP does essentially nothing, and the mismatched enantiomer has no effect.

So this is not a pore blocker. Every clinically relevant HCN drug works on conduction; this works on modulation. And they show why you'd want that: two epilepsy-associated gain-of-function HCN1 variants that right-shift activation by ten and eighteen millivolts are partially or fully restored toward wild-type behavior. In hippocampal slices the probe reduces voltage sag and rebound depolarization in CA1 pyramidal neurons and increases firing, with no change in action potential morphology — a decent argument for on-target activity in native tissue. The honest limitation is that a covalent probe at twenty micromolar in slices is a tool compound, not a lead, and the cysteine mutants they wanted as controls were basally dysfunctional, so that control is missing. But as a template — go to primary tissue, find the allosteric cysteine, drug the regulation rather than the pore — it's excellent.

Staying at the molecular level, a computational paper on why two receptors that look almost identical behave completely differently. The adenosine A2A and A2B receptors share a highly conserved orthosteric site, and the usual explanation for selectivity is static structure. This group used funnel metadynamics for thermodynamics and adaptive infrequent metadynamics for kinetics across six ligand-receptor pairs — theophylline as the non-selective antagonist, an A2A-selective inverse agonist, and NECA as the non-selective agonist. It's validated in the right direction: the simulations reproduce known binding modes and affinities, then predict the unresolved poses at A2B. The mechanistic answer is about water and charge rather than shape. A tyrosine-to-asparagine swap keeps a secondary pocket near transmembrane helices one, two and seven more hydrated in A2B, and that extra water speeds ligand egress. A leucine-to-valine swap changes the volume of the floor of the binding site, stabilizing the A2A-selective ligand and lengthening its residence time. And the extracellular vestibule of A2B carries charged residues absent from A2A that swap partners during unbinding and reshape the exit pathway for bulkier ligands. The generalization is that the same static pocket can have different residence times, and residence time is where the selectivity lives — so if you design subtype-selective GPCR ligands from crystal structures alone, you may be looking at the wrong observable. No new experiments here, so treat the residue attributions as testable hypotheses.

From dynamics to design. A bioinformatics preprint out of Peking University introduces a closed-loop reinforcement learning framework for compound optimization, and it stands out because the loop actually closes with a wet lab. Most generative chemistry is open-loop: the model proposes, chemists synthesize and test, and the results are used for evaluation rather than to update the model. Here the reward is refreshed after every design-make-test-analyze cycle using measurements from every synthesized compound, including the inactive ones and the ones that failed on developability — exactly the data an open-loop workflow throws away.

Architecturally it's a three-dimensional structure-guided generator updated by group relative policy optimization, with a four-level reward hierarchy running from topological validity up to target-specific activity, and Pareto-frontier rather than best-score selection to preserve diversity. The prospective results are the reason to care. Three campaigns, each resolving a real medicinal-chemistry bottleneck in two to three cycles: forty-fold improvement in oral exposure of a ROR1 inhibitor with antitumor efficacy retained, twenty-fold reduction in CYP2C19 inhibition for an NLRP3 antagonist with inflammasome activity preserved, and eighteen-fold affinity improvement for an NSD3 hit. Those are property-liability rescues rather than potency-chasing, which is the harder and more useful problem.

Two caveats the authors state plainly, to their credit. The retrospective benchmark scores structural rediscovery — Tanimoto similarity above zero point nine to a known active — rather than measured activity, which fails near activity cliffs and penalizes genuinely novel actives. And it stays human-in-the-loop; chemists still judge synthetic feasibility and priority. There's no CNS target in the set, which is a gap for this audience, but the framework is target-agnostic and the pharmacokinetic and CYP objectives are exactly the ones that kill CNS programs.

Fifth item, and the machine-learning theme continues into autism genetics. Gene discovery in autism is statistically starved — the damaging variants are ultra-rare and heterogeneous, so you need enormous cohorts. This preprint asks whether a functional prior can substitute for some of that power. They built a three-point-six-million-cell brain organoid atlas, trained single-cell foundation models to predict genome-wide perturbation responses, and benchmarked seventeen of them.

The most interesting result is a negative one about model evaluation. Models trained on two-dimensional neuronal cell lines, or built on alternative pre-trained backbones, achieved competitive expression-prediction accuracy while showing no autism-relevant coherence in the perturbation space of established risk genes, and failing to recover basic neurodevelopmental structure like gene stratification by loss-of-function intolerance. Expression accuracy and useful perturbation structure came apart. That's a warning about how the field currently benchmarks these models, and it's worth more than the gene list.

The gene list is decent too. Profiling with the winning telencephalic-neuron model gave two clusters: one enriched for cortical neuronal differentiation and postsynaptic signaling, recapitulating the prevailing cortical-centric view, and a second, less expected, in early radial glia and neuroblasts spanning midbrain, hindbrain and cerebellum, built around ubiquitination genes. Both survived damaging-variant enrichment across roughly ninety thousand family-based samples, and held up after removing established autism genes — which addresses the circularity you should immediately worry about. Three hundred forty-three candidates prioritized, with recurrent support for NBEA and KLHDC10. Their framing is right: functional priors complement statistical association, they don't replace it.

Now to cannabinoid pharmacology, and a nice piece of receptor-population dissection. Incidental associations are the links you form between two neutral stimuli with no reinforcer — studied through sensory preconditioning, where pairing a light and a sound, then conditioning the light with a shock, produces a mediated response to the sound that was never itself reinforced. This group had already shown hippocampal CB1 receptors are required in an odor-taste version; here they replicate it with light and sound, in both sexes, then go after the dopamine interaction. Hippocampal dopamine activity is higher during paired than unpaired presentations, and blocking D1 receptors before each preconditioning session abolishes the mediated response, so both systems are necessary. The convergence experiment is the good part: mice lacking CB1 receptors specifically in D1-positive cells fail to form incidental associations in both protocols. Given prior work placing the requirement on hippocampal GABAergic interneurons, and the subpopulation known to co-express D1, the resolved answer is CB1 on D1-expressing hippocampal interneurons.

Then a genuinely surprising dissociation. Using a deliberately suboptimal ten pairings instead of thirty, they could rescue association formation either by inhibiting the endocannabinoid-degrading enzymes to raise endogenous tone, or by giving THC. In the conditional knockouts, the enzyme-inhibitor rescue was abolished — and the THC rescue survived. Same receptor, same behavior, different cellular substrate depending on whether you raise the endogenous ligand or flood the system with an exogenous agonist. That's a clean argument for why endocannabinoid-tone-enhancing drugs and direct agonists should not be treated as interchangeable, and it lands in a paradigm the authors explicitly tie to psychosis.

Which is the bridge to a small but pointed human imaging study from Lauri Tuominen's group in Ottawa, testing the chaotic dopamine hypothesis. The tension is old: schizophrenia is a disorder of excess tonic dopamine, and yet patients are worse, not better, at dopamine-dependent learning. The chaotic dopamine account says spontaneous release is up, raising tonic levels, while coordinated stimulus-driven phasic release is down.

They tested it with simultaneous raclopride PET and functional MRI during Pavlovian fear conditioning — a dopamine-dependent process — in first-degree relatives of people with psychotic disorders, chosen because they carry the hyperdopaminergic phenotype without antipsychotics competing with the tracer. In sixteen controls, fear conditioning does release dopamine in posterior caudate and putamen, and caudate release correlates with the BOLD response to threat cue versus safety cue — a useful result on its own, since the human evidence for fear-evoked dopamine release was thin. Then in twelve relatives, the posterior caudate release is absent, with no difference in behavioral learning or skin conductance. And lower release correlates with more frequent self-reported paranoid thinking, but not with anhedonia — the opposite of what the hypothesis predicted, since it assigned reduced adaptive release to negative symptoms.

Be blunt about the numbers: sixteen and twelve, with uncorrected p-values around zero point zero five carrying the symptom correlations. This settles nothing. But it's the first in vivo test of a hypothesis that has sat untested for years, the tracer choice and twenty-four-hour scan design are careful, and the direction of the symptom finding is interesting precisely because it contradicts the prediction.

Two neuromodulation items next, both about how you deliver stimulation rather than where. The first is a cross-species medRxiv preprint on trigeminal nerve stimulation, approved for epilepsy and depression in some jurisdictions and always vague about mechanism. The premise is that temporal pattern, not dose, is what matters. In anesthetized rats, intermittent two-hundred-hertz bursts produced persistent CA1 field potential potentiation exceeding the conventional twenty percent LTP criterion, plus prolonged firing, while continuous hundred-hertz stimulation produced nothing sustained despite delivering roughly fifteen times more cumulative charge. That asymmetry is the finding. And they nail the mechanism pharmacologically: local inhibition of the locus coeruleus abolishes both the synaptic and firing effects, whereas local D1-and-D5 blockade in CA1 only partially attenuates them. So it's coordinated noradrenergic and dopaminergic recruitment with noradrenaline load-bearing — which fits the inverted-U logic of catecholamine modulation and explains why bursts with recovery intervals beat a tonic drive.

Then the translation, which is where cross-species work usually gets thin and here doesn't. In epilepsy patients undergoing stereo-EEG, the same burst pattern evokes clear hippocampal and thalamic responses with nothing in adjacent white matter, and raises hippocampal theta-gamma phase-amplitude coupling after burst offset. And in healthy volunteers in a randomized active-sham crossover, stimulation during the consolidation window — not during encoding — improved delayed recall. That phase specificity is the strongest argument this acts on post-encoding stabilization rather than attention. Caveats they own: the benefit was selective for occupation recall and absent for names, the intracranial data come from epilepsy patients, coupling and behavior were measured in different cohorts, and follow-up is hours, not weeks.

The companion item answers a question we covered yesterday. Recall the matters-arising exchange in npj Parkinson's Disease about whether beta-based algorithms can actually pick DBS contacts, where the objection was that bipolar sensing emphasizes gradients between contacts and can't localize the beta source. This study tests the pseudo-monopolar alternative — the Electrode Identifier feature on Medtronic's sensing-enabled stimulators — in sixty-nine patients.

The pseudo-monopolar estimate predicted the clinically chosen contact above chance: forty-five percent for exact top-one, fifty-seven percent under a more inclusive rule. Reconstructed bipolar recordings only beat chance under the inclusive rule. The two methods were not statistically different from each other — and here's the part worth holding onto: the pseudo-monopolar output achieved that with essentially no post-processing, while the bipolar comparison required extensive analytical work. Equal accuracy at a fraction of the effort is a real clinical claim even without a superiority result. The disagreements are systematic rather than random: bipolar sensing favors dorsal contacts, pseudo-monopolar favors ventral ones, and their explanation is that the most dorsal contact often sits outside the subthalamic nucleus, creating an artificial dorsal gradient. They're refreshingly hard on their own tool — many recordings had to be excluded, peak frequencies still need manual inspection, and there was only a non-significant trend toward better motor outcome when the clinical choice matched the algorithm. So the answer to yesterday's question is a qualified yes on the physics and a no on product readiness.

Two shorter items to close. First, a careful electrophysiology preprint on Nav1.9 in human tissue — the tetrodotoxin-resistant channel encoded by SCN11A, where gain-of-function gives congenital insensitivity to pain, loss-of-function gives familial episodic pain, and almost everything we know comes from rodents or heterologous expression. They pharmacologically isolated native Nav1.9 currents in human dorsal root ganglion and, for the first time, trigeminal ganglion neurons.

Two findings matter. The methodological one is a warning: these currents are badly contaminated by space-clamp error, apparently from channels trafficked out along neurites that grow rapidly after plating, so clean somatic recordings required suppressing neurite outgrowth altogether. The biology is that trigeminal and dorsal root channels are not the same: inactivation is shifted about ten millivolts depolarized in trigeminal neurons, which widens the window current, and activation is faster at hyperpolarized potentials. Since Nav1.9's proposed job is setting resting membrane potential, a wider window current is a plausible substrate for why orofacial pain pharmacology behaves differently from limb pain. They also show human trigeminal channels shifted roughly twenty-five to thirty millivolts depolarized relative to rat — a reminder about which species your voltage-dependence assumptions come from.

And finally, receptor pharmacology showing up somewhere it wasn't invited — a companion in spirit to the GABA-B and glioblastoma story we covered yesterday. Neuron-to-tumor glutamatergic synapses are well established in glioma and brain metastasis. This preprint asks about meningioma, normally extra-axial and separated from parenchyma, except in the aggressive subset that breaches the boundary and forms what they call a brain-tumor interface.

They find putative neuron-meningioma synapses by electron microscopy in human specimens, more abundant in brain-invasive and higher-grade tumors, with single-cell data showing synaptic, proliferative and invasive programs enriched specifically in interface tumor cells. Glutamate evokes AMPA-receptor currents in primary meningioma cells that CNQX blocks, and drives proliferation that NMDA or AMPA-kainate blockade attenuates. In intracranial xenografts they record tetrodotoxin-sensitive spontaneous excitatory events in tumor cells, and receptor blockade reduces proliferation in vivo. The limitations are stated: the human evidence is observational, the xenograft doesn't reproduce the anatomical origin of meningioma, and nothing yet links receptor activation to the invasion machinery. But it extends neuron-cancer electrical coupling to a tumor nobody thought was excitable, and the drugs implied — perampanel, memantine — are already on the shelf. Two days, two tumors, two neurotransmitter receptor systems. Worth noticing as a pattern.

That's the episode. Eleven items, and the thread I'd leave you with is the first one: a receptor that fails as a drug target not because the biology was wrong but because the receptor was already full. That's a pharmacodynamic failure mode, and we probably have others hiding in plain sight. See you tomorrow.
