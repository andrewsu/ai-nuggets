## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. I'm your host, and this is your daily pass through CNS pharmacology, psychiatric and neurology drug discovery, and the computational machinery closing in on both. Eleven items today. It's a Sunday, so the news wire is quiet, but the preprint servers were not, and the through-line running across most of today's list is receptor pharmacology applied to diseases that were not supposed to be receptor problems.

We open with a preprint out of René Hen's lab at Columbia, and the reason it matters is partly who it comes from. Hen spent two decades building the case that adult hippocampal neurogenesis is required for the behavioral effects of SSRIs. This paper says the mechanism he's now pursuing is a different one, and that it lives in mature neurons rather than newborn ones.

The setup is chronic corticosterone to create a stressed state, then three weeks of fluoxetine, then single-nucleus sequencing across every hippocampal cell type. The response is startlingly local: essentially all of the transcriptional action is in the dentate gyrus, and within the dentate, in mature granule cells. Those mature cells re-enter a developmental-like transcriptional state. Driving it is SOX11, a transcription factor that runs neurodevelopment and axon regeneration and is normally switched off in the adult brain. Fluoxetine turns it back on, and it takes three weeks to do so — five days is not enough.

Then the necessity test, which is what elevates this from a nice observation. They built a knock-in driver line that recombines in mature granule cells while sparing newborn ones, and used it to delete SOX11 only in mature cells. The antidepressant-like effects of fluoxetine in novelty-suppressed feeding and forced swim were abolished — hazard ratio for feeding latency falls from about fifteen in controls to about two, non-significant — while neurogenesis in those animals was untouched. So you can subtract the mature-cell program and lose the drug effect with the newborn cells fully intact.

The mechanistic half is where the pharmacology gets interesting. SOX11 reactivation is gated by the extracellular matrix. Most of that literature has focused on perineuronal nets around parvalbumin interneurons, which are a small fraction of total brain matrix. Here they describe a different structure — net-like matrix wrapping clusters of axonal boutons in the dentate, which they call perisynaptic nets. Fluoxetine remodels those and leaves perineuronal nets alone. And going the other way: a Cre-dependent chondroitinase virus that degrades perisynaptic nets around mature granule cells, sparing perineuronal nets, is by itself sufficient to reactivate SOX11, raise BDNF at mossy fiber terminals, and shorten latency to feed in stressed animals. No drug required.

Two things to hold onto. First, this offers a real answer to the oldest embarrassment in antidepressant pharmacology — why a drug that saturates the transporter in hours takes weeks to work. Matrix proteins are among the longest-lived in the brain. If the rate-limiting step is proteolytic remodeling of a long-lived scaffold, weeks is the expected timescale rather than a puzzle. Second, be honest about the human claim. The abstract says they identified this form of plasticity in the adult human brain. What they actually show is that SOX11 expression in human bulk transcriptomic atlases is high during development, including in cortical regions with known critical periods, and declines across the lifespan. That is consistent with the model and it is not evidence that fluoxetine reactivates it in patients. The mouse work is excellent; the translational step is still a hypothesis.

Staying with serotonin but moving to neurodegeneration — a preprint from Hannover Medical School making the case that the 5-HT7 receptor is a druggable modifier of tau toxicity, and doing it in flies. They built a Drosophila model expressing GFP-tagged human tau, wild-type or the P301L variant, pan-neuronally. P301L reproduces the expected package: hyperphosphorylation at the AT8 epitope, sarkosyl-insoluble tangle-like assemblies, shortened lifespan, sleep loss and hyperactivity, progressive climbing decline, age-dependent vacuolar degeneration.

What makes it worth reading is the pharmacology. This group previously showed in mammalian systems that the 5-HT7 receptor drives CDK5-dependent tau phosphorylation through its constitutive activity — not through serotonin binding, but through the receptor's ligand-independent signaling. So the intervention is an inverse agonist, S-B-two-six-nine-nine-seven-oh, the standard selective 5-HT7 tool compound, fed in food at one micromolar. That reduces AT8 tau phosphorylation, reduces the fraction of brains showing degeneration, and improves climbing at thirty days. RNAi knockdown of the fly 5-HT7 receptor gives a comparable rescue, which is the control you want — it says the compound is working through the receptor rather than off-target. Roscovitine, inhibiting the downstream kinase, does the same, though it also improves climbing in control flies, so part of its behavioral effect is nonspecific stimulation.

Why this is worth your attention rather than being one more fly tauopathy paper: it's a clean argument for targeting constitutive receptor activity in a proteinopathy. The receptor isn't misregulated by ligand; it's tonically signaling into a kinase that phosphorylates tau. An inverse agonist has something to do there that a neutral antagonist doesn't. The 5-HT7 receptor is highly expressed in human hippocampus and cortex, exactly where tauopathy hits. The catch is that neither knockdown nor drug affected control flies, which reads as good tolerability but also means the assay can't distinguish a disease-specific effect from a floor.

Sticking with tauopathy and GPCRs, a bioRxiv preprint takes the M1 muscarinic receptor into the vascular compartment. The premise is that impaired neurovascular coupling — the local blood-flow increase that follows neural activity — is an early feature of Alzheimer's disease, and that tau, not just amyloid, drives it. They used P301S mice at ten months, measured whisker-stimulation-evoked cortical blood flow by laser speckle imaging, then gave a single intraperitoneal dose of a selective, orally bioavailable M1 positive allosteric modulator from the Vanderbilt series and re-measured every fifteen minutes for an hour.

The tau mice have blunted neurovascular coupling at baseline. The allosteric modulator restores it, peaking around thirty minutes in females and forty-five in males. And critically, it does nothing in wild-type animals of either sex. That disease-specificity is the pharmacologically interesting part and it's what you'd predict from a positive allosteric modulator: it amplifies existing cholinergic tone rather than driving the receptor itself, so where signaling is intact there's nothing to amplify. It's also the mechanistic justification for the allosteric approach over orthosteric agonists like xanomeline, which produce the peripheral cholinergic burden that has dogged that class.

The caveats are real — four to five animals per group, a single acute dose, and cognitive deficits that were characterized but never treated — this shows a vascular rescue, not a cognitive one. The sex effect is a difference in latency to peak, not in whether the drug worked. Still, adding a cerebrovascular readout to the M1 story is worth something, because acetylcholinesterase inhibitors are known to raise cerebral blood flow in patients and nobody has known which muscarinic subtype was responsible.

Fourth item, and the GABA-B receptor turns up somewhere unexpected. A group at the Italian National Research Council asked whether restoring inhibitory signaling changes glioblastoma progression. Glioma disrupts cortical excitation-inhibition balance and builds an immunosuppressive microenvironment, and those have mostly been studied as separate problems. Chemogenetically activating peritumoral parvalbumin interneurons transiently slowed tumor growth and preserved cortical function; silencing them raised seizure susceptibility and neurological impairment without accelerating growth.

The pharmacology comes next, and it splits cleanly. Baclofen alone, at one milligram per kilogram, did nothing to survival. Baclofen plus anti-PD-L1 produced complete tumor eradication in sixty-six percent of GL261-bearing mice, with durable protection on rechallenge. Early myeloid changes preceded radiological regression — fewer arginase-1-positive and more CD11c-positive cells within the intratumoral macrophage compartment. And human single-cell data show GABA-B and PD-L1 signaling co-varying across tumors along a myeloid-dominated communication axis.

This is unusually well caveated for a result this eye-catching, and the caveats matter. The effect is model-specific: CT-2A tumors did not respond. And there is a published report in the other direction, where GABA-B agonism enhanced myeloid-derived suppressor cell activity and promoted tumor growth in female mice. The authors' read is the right one — GABA-B activation is not intrinsically antitumoral, and the finding is about a context in which it licenses checkpoint blockade. Baclofen being an approved drug with a long safety record makes this cheap to test, and also makes it exactly the kind of result that gets over-extrapolated. The useful question isn't whether to give baclofen to glioblastoma patients; it's what defines the responsive context.

Which is a decent bridge to a sobering item about GABA-modulating drugs where they've actually been tried. A medRxiv systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized trials of GABA-modulating pharmacotherapies in autism — thirteen trials covering bumetanide, arbaclofen and valproate, with six prespecified outcome domains and GRADE certainty assessment.

The unadjusted numbers look like a modest win: global autism severity improves with a Hedges' g of minus point two five, adaptive behavior minus point zero nine, both nominally significant. Social communication, functional communication, repetitive behaviors and irritability all come out null. Then they apply a Holm-Bonferroni correction across the six domains and both surviving results drop out — adjusted p-values of point one four.

I want to give the authors credit for reporting it this way, because the tempting version of this paper is the one that leads with "GABA-modulating therapies improve global autism severity" and buries the correction. The honest conclusion is that after fifteen years of the excitation-inhibition imbalance hypothesis and three drug classes taken into randomized trials, there is no multiplicity-corrected benefit in any domain. That doesn't falsify the hypothesis — bumetanide targets chloride handling, arbaclofen targets GABA-B, valproate is a blunt instrument, and pooling them across heterogeneous outcome instruments is a coarse test. But it does mean the domain-specificity question the field keeps promising to answer has not been answered, and the trials that could answer it haven't been run.

If GABA pharmacology in autism is stalling, an alternative is to go upstream of the synapse entirely. Molecular Psychiatry published work from Zhen Yan's group at Buffalo on SMARCC2, which encodes a subunit of the BAF chromatin remodeling complex and is a high-confidence autism and intellectual disability gene. Its promoter-binding targets are significantly enriched for other autism risk genes, and SMARCC2 protein is reduced in postmortem prefrontal cortex from idiopathic autism cases — so the rare-variant gene and the idiopathic condition converge on the same protein.

Knocking it down in adolescent mouse prefrontal cortex impairs working memory and spatial memory while leaving locomotion, anxiety and sociability alone — a cognitive phenotype, matching the intellectual disability association. Underneath is coordinated downregulation of synaptic genes, reduced excitatory and inhibitory transmission onto pyramidal neurons, and reduced histone acetylation. The same synaptic gene signature appears in patient-derived stem cell neurons with SMARCC2 knocked down.

The therapeutic move is romidepsin — an approved class-one HDAC inhibitor, three daily injections at a quarter milligram per kilogram. This is the same group and the same regimen that rescued social deficits in Shank3-deficient mice back in 2018. Here it restores histone acetylation, rescues working memory, restores AMPA-receptor currents essentially to control and GABA-A currents partially, and lifts a hundred thirty-six of the six hundred fifty-three genes that knockdown had suppressed. It also works on the human stem cell neurons. The logic is clean: if losing a chromatin remodeler collapses the acetylation state at synaptic promoters, blocking deacetylation pushes back. What I'd want next is duration — three days of a systemic HDAC inhibitor producing a durable cognitive rescue is a strong claim, and the field's history with short-course epigenetic drugs is that effects fade.

Turning to clinical pharmacology, a Phase 1 trial from Brian Edlow's group at Massachusetts General on intravenous methylphenidate for acute disorders of consciousness after severe traumatic brain injury. There is currently no approved therapy for accelerating recovery of consciousness in the ICU. The rationale is dopaminergic reactivation of subcortical arousal networks, and the innovation is the route: intravenous rather than enteral, in patients whose gut absorption is unreliable.

Nine patients, three coma, three vegetative state, three minimally conscious, dosed at half, one, and two milligrams per kilogram on successive days. No serious adverse events at any dose. Peak plasma concentration inside seven to fourteen minutes. Target engagement was assessed by EEG and resting-state fMRI change-point analysis: EEG responses in seven of eight at the lowest dose, and in the one patient with usable imaging, reintegration of ventral tegmental area to default-mode connectivity.

The result that should make you sit up is the dose-response, because it runs backwards. Behavioral arousal on the Coma Recovery Scale rose within fifteen minutes in six of nine at half a milligram per kilogram, five of nine at one, and zero of six at two. The authors flag the confound — this was an open-label within-subject escalation, so dose-stacking can't be excluded, and central effects may outlast plasma clearance. But there is no signal that more drug did more, and the adverse events at the higher doses were real: insomnia, emesis, paroxysmal sympathetic hyperactivity, transaminitis. Their own conclusion is that half a milligram per kilogram is the dose to take forward. Nine patients from four hundred eighty-eight screened over four years tells you what this trial cost, and the EEG response rate exceeding the behavioral response rate is a genuinely useful observation — it suggests bedside examination is undercounting pharmacodynamic effect.

Eighth, and staying with target engagement as a concept: the PRISM-TRD trial, out on medRxiv, asking whether you can measure whether a brain stimulation dose landed, in real time, and use it prognostically. Seventy-one patients — forty-two treatment-resistant, twenty-nine ultra-treatment-resistant — got personalized left dorsolateral prefrontal targeting based on functional connectivity to subgenual cingulate, then one sham-controlled session of intermittent theta burst stimulation interleaved inside the scanner, then five days of accelerated open-label stimulation, forty sessions total.

Clinically it worked about as well as this literature has been reporting: Hamilton scores down about nine points, response forty-one percent, remission seventeen percent, maintained at twelve weeks, with the ultra-resistant group doing worse on response but not on remission. The mechanistic result is that active stimulation produced significant engagement within the subgenual-connected map used for targeting, and the magnitude of that engagement correlated with subsequent improvement in depression and anxiety — but not other symptoms — and added prognostic information beyond clinical and demographic predictors.

The reason to care is economic as much as scientific. Accelerated theta burst protocols are expensive and week-long. A single interleaved session that tells you whether the stimulator is actually moving the target circuit in this patient is exactly the assay the field is missing. The caveats are the usual ones: single center, the treatment phase was open-label, and a correlation between engagement and outcome across seventy-one people is not a validated individual-level predictor.

Ninth item, and a lighter one, though it connects to the opener. A bioRxiv preprint looking for an EEG signature of what psychedelics leave behind after the acute effects are gone. Resting-state EEG in people who had used a psychedelic within the prior three weeks — fourteen on psilocybin or LSD, twelve on 5-MeO-DMT — plus sixteen matched controls, analyzed with dynamic functional connectivity to find recurring semi-stable connectivity states rather than a static average.

A right frontoparietal beta synchrony state dominated after both the classical and the atypical psychedelic, in proportion and dwell time, and the effect scaled with days since dosing. A global theta state was prominent after psilocybin and LSD but less common after 5-MeO-DMT, which is a nice dissociation between subclasses. And Lempel-Ziv complexity — the entropy measure that reliably rises during acute dosing and has become the field's favorite neural correlate — was not altered in either group. I'll flag that I'm working from the abstract on this one, the full text hasn't rendered yet. But the headline is worth having: whatever persists in the weeks after a psychedelic dose is not the acute entropy signature persisting. It's something else, in a different frequency band, and small samples in a naturalistic retrospective design mean this needs replication before anyone builds a biomarker on it.

Tenth, from Annie Kathuria's group at Johns Hopkins, an attempt to fix how preclinical CNS drug data get read. About ten percent of drugs clearing animal testing succeed in humans and CNS programs do worse, and part of the problem is that multimodal preclinical data get evaluated assay by assay with no way to say whether a compound moved the tissue in a good direction overall. Their answer is the Neural Impact Score: a bidirectional score across four predefined categories — neurodevelopment, neuroinflammation, neurodegeneration and longevity — where positive means a desirable shift.

The substrate is a vascularized day-two-hundred multi-region brain organoid containing cortical, brainstem and endothelial lineages, and they scored five mechanistically distinct compounds — a GLP-1 agonist, bupropion, fluoxetine, fingolimod, and an Akt activator — using single-nucleus and bulk RNA sequencing, proteomics, and multi-electrode arrays. Each compound gets a category profile rather than a single verdict. Fingolimod scored highest on neurodevelopment; the GLP-1 agonist scored highest on longevity but negative on neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration.

Then the cross-species test, and this is the part to think about carefully. They applied the locked framework to rodent datasets without retraining and recovered concordant antidepressant responses — while gene-level directional agreement between the human organoid and rat brain was near chance. They present that contrast as the framework's value: category-level interpretation survives where gene-level equivalence fails. It's a reasonable claim and it's also the obvious worry. Aggregating enough disagreeing genes into four coarse bins will produce agreement almost regardless of the underlying biology, and there's no null model here telling you how often random gene sets would concord at the category level. The other thing to sit with is that a day-two-hundred organoid maps to a mid-gestational cortical window, weeks eighteen through twenty-two — so neurodegeneration and longevity are being scored in fetal-stage tissue. For a neurodevelopmental readout that's exactly right. For the other three categories it's a stretch the framework doesn't currently acknowledge.

We close on agentic AI, with a paper I found genuinely useful for reasons other than its headline. AgentFold, from a Hunan University-led collaboration, asks whether an LLM agent can autonomously improve a large, tightly coupled scientific machine learning system through executable code changes — not propose an idea, but implement it, debug it, evaluate it, and decide what to try next. The testbed is protein folding, starting from a compact ESMFold-derived codebase of over two thousand lines. A multi-agent loop proposes architectural edits, applies and debugs them, evaluates variants on standard structure metrics, and stores both successes and failures in structured memory, with a Monte Carlo tree search controller allocating compute across promising branches.

The cost first, because it's the number people skip: roughly eighty variants explored, about five thousand GPU-hours, about a hundred seventy million tokens. The return is seven and a half percent better best local-distance-difference-test score than an independent Codex-proposal baseline at matched budget, with gains concentrated in loop accuracy rather than global fold quality, on a one-block substrate trained on a thousand chains. That is a real but modest improvement on a deliberately shrunken problem, and the authors say plainly that transfer to stronger folding systems is unverified.

What I think is actually valuable is the byproduct. Because the system logged failures as structured evidence rather than discarding them, the trace yields empirical design rules: stable gains came from early soft learnable priors and gated refinement, while direct geometric perturbations and geometry-conditioned feedback tended to destabilize training. That is a form of scientific output the agent literature almost never produces, because most systems are optimized to report their best number and throw away the eighty attempts that didn't work. If the case for agentic search in scientific ML is going to hold up, I suspect it will rest less on the headline delta and more on the fact that an agent will exhaustively catalogue what fails — which is the part human researchers are worst at doing and worst at publishing.

That's eleven. Fluoxetine works by reopening a developmental program in mature neurons and the matrix is the gate; a serotonin receptor's constitutive activity is a tau target; an M1 allosteric modulator restores blood flow only where the disease has broken it; baclofen licenses checkpoint blockade in one glioma model and not another; GABA drugs in autism don't survive multiplicity correction; an HDAC inhibitor rescues a chromatin-remodeler phenotype; intravenous methylphenidate wakes people up at the low dose and not the high one; and an agent spent five thousand GPU-hours to teach us what destabilizes a folding model.
