## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. I'm your host, and this is your daily pass through CNS pharmacology, psychiatric and neurology drug discovery, and the computational machinery closing in on both. Ten items today. We open with the paper I've been waiting for somebody to write — a multi-agent system that goes all the way from molecular search to a rabbit under continuous infusion — and then move into a run of papers that share an uncomfortable, productive habit: they go back to an assumption the field has been building on for a decade, test it directly for the first time, and find it doesn't hold.

The lead is a preprint out this week describing MASCOT, which stands for Multi-Agent Search for Molecular Optimization, and the reason it matters isn't the benchmark numbers — it's that the pipeline terminates in a preclinical anesthetic candidate with animal data.

Start with the design choice, because it's the right one. Most agentic chemistry puts the language model in the generation loop, asking it to propose structures. MASCOT does the opposite: molecular editing, scoring, constraint enforcement and acceptance all stay inside an explicit, auditable Monte Carlo graph-editing engine, and three language-model agents sit above it controlling only how the search proceeds. A trade-off agent reallocates weights across competing objectives; a strategy agent adjusts one number, the fragment-addition probability, which tunes exploration against refinement; a reflection agent turns delayed decision-outcome records into a compact shared memory. Every agent output is schema-checked and clipped to bounds, and a malformed call just retains the previous value. The agents never touch a molecule.

The ablations are what convinced me. Against a no-agent control the full framework hit its target metric about fifty-five steps earlier and converged higher, and five reduced controllers each captured only part of the gain. But the control I want to see become standard is the shuffled-memory condition: keep the reflection agent's call schedule, prompt structure and lesson count identical, then randomly re-pair each decision with the wrong outcome before showing it to the model. Same volume of feedback, same interface, causality destroyed. That separates genuine online learning from the benefit of merely adding another agent to the diagram, and it's the ablation most multi-agent papers quietly skip.

Then the pharmacology. They fixed the scaffold of remimazolam — the ultrashort-acting benzodiazepine anesthetic — restricted edits to side chains, and optimized for shorter predicted metabolic half-life, greater blood-brain barrier penetration and lower predicted toxicity. From about twenty-seven hundred candidates the system prioritized one, and it held up experimentally: microsomal half-life down from roughly twenty minutes to six and a half, median effective anesthetic dose in mice down from thirty-eight to twenty-four milligrams per kilogram, higher brain exposure, therapeutic index up from six and a half to about ten.

The best part is what happened next, because it's a lesson about proxies. Side-chain derivatization produced six more compounds, and the ones with the shortest half-lives were the worst anesthetics — three cleared in under three minutes and were useless. The winner, RM-7, has an intermediate half-life of about twelve minutes. It cut the effective dose to about fifteen milligrams per kilogram, shortened time to resumed walking after loss of righting reflex from five and a half minutes to two, potentiated GABA-A currents more strongly than the parent, stayed fully flumazenil-reversible, and reached a therapeutic index of twenty-four. In rabbits, recovery after continuous infusion out to six hours was faster and more stable at comparable EEG depth, with smaller cardiovascular swings and less QT perturbation.

So the finding I'd carry out of this is the nonmonotonic relationship between metabolic half-life and functional recovery. The optimizer was pointed at "shorter half-life" and the compound it actually needed balanced the exposure window against potency and brain penetration — precisely what no single oracle encodes. The authors say so themselves, and are clear that the oracles are surrogates rather than clinical endpoints and that synthesis, candidate selection and interpretation stayed expert-directed. That honesty is what makes it credible: not an autonomous discovery system, but a search controller that made a medicinal chemistry campaign converge faster, with a human program carrying it the rest of the way.

Second, a systematic pharmacology paper on GPR139, out this week in the British Journal of Pharmacology, and it is the kind of paper that should change how you read your own assay data. GPR139 is one of the more interesting orphan receptors in CNS drug discovery — brain-restricted expression concentrated in habenula and interpeduncular nucleus, endogenous candidate ligands in the aromatic amino acids tryptophan and phenylalanine, and enough interest in it as a negative-symptom and anhedonia target that Takeda pushed an agonist, TAK-041, into clinical development. What nobody had done properly was map its transducer coupling.

The group profiled dissociation of fifteen G alpha subtypes by BRET in HEK293A cells, then repeated the key measurements in CRISPR knockout lines missing individual G protein families. In the direct BRET assays GPR139 coupled broadly — the inhibitory G proteins, the G-q family, and G-twelve — which puts it in a club of roughly a dozen class A receptors with that kind of promiscuity. What it did not couple to, in the proximal assay, was the stimulatory G protein. And yet it stimulates cAMP, which should not happen through inhibitory coupling.

The resolution is the interesting part. Knock out the stimulatory G protein and the cAMP stimulation disappears entirely; knock out G-q or the inhibitory family and it survives; in cells stripped of everything, only reintroducing the stimulatory subunit restores it. So the receptor has functional stimulatory coupling that is invisible to a proximal readout — the authors call it hidden coupling. Better still, once the stimulatory arm is gone, an inhibitory-mediated cAMP inhibition emerges that was there all along, masked by the larger opposing signal. Two opposing pathways converge on one second messenger, and the sign of the net answer depends on which G proteins your cell happens to express.

Why this matters practically: seven structurally diverse agonists were run head to head against the Janssen reference compound, and while relative potencies were broadly consistent, TAK-041 and the two amino acids showed reduced maximal responses in arrestin-3 recruitment and in cAMP stimulation — a partial-agonist signature confined to particular branches. If you screened GPR139 agonists in one recombinant line on one readout, and most programs did, you may have been ranking compounds on an arm of the pharmacology that isn't the one operating in habenula. The authors don't claim this explains any specific clinical outcome, and the physiological validation genuinely isn't done. But it is a concrete mechanism for how a target with good genetics and good expression yields a compound series that doesn't translate.

Third, the same treatment applied to an anatomical assumption rather than a pharmacological one. Everyone knows there is no direct projection from prefrontal cortex to hippocampus. The standard workaround, which appears in essentially every model of prefrontal–hippocampal interaction and in a large fraction of the working-memory and contextual-inference literature, is that the nucleus reuniens — the ventral midline thalamic nucleus — carries prefrontal signals to hippocampus disynaptically. That model has an implicit requirement: prefrontal afferents have to actually synapse onto the reuniens neurons that project to hippocampus. This preprint tested that requirement, and it fails.

Dual retrograde tracing showed that prefrontal-projecting and hippocampal-projecting reuniens neurons are almost entirely separate populations — only two percent of cells project to both. Then, using input-output tracing, they labelled the monosynaptic inputs to each population specifically. Prefrontal-projecting reuniens neurons receive dense input from anterior cingulate, prelimbic and infralimbic cortex, as expected. Input from medial prefrontal cortex onto the hippocampal-projecting neurons was almost completely absent. And then the physiology, which is the cleanest part: channelrhodopsin in medial prefrontal cortex, retrobeads labelling both projection classes, and sequential whole-cell recordings from neighbouring cells in the same field of view under identical optical stimulation. Robust excitatory currents in the prefrontal-projecting neurons; small to undetectable in the hippocampal-projecting neighbours, with the gap widening as light pulse duration increased.

So reuniens is not a relay. It is parallel projection-defined circuits that happen to share an address. The dominant input to the hippocampal-projecting population comes from posterior and lateral hypothalamus instead — a plausible indirect route, since prefrontal cortex innervates those regions, but a different circuit with different latency and content, likely carrying internal-state and motivational signals rather than a copy of a prefrontal representation. If you have been reading a reuniens manipulation as a clean test of prefrontal control over hippocampus, that interpretation needs rebuilding.

Fourth, and staying at the synapse, a nice piece of NMDA receptor subtype pharmacology. GluD1 is the delta-type glutamate receptor — it doesn't gate a conventional ionotropic current, and its main job appears to be as a trans-synaptic organizer coupling presynaptic neurexin–cerebellin complexes to the postsynaptic side. It has been repeatedly implicated in schizophrenia and intellectual disability, and it was known to modulate NMDA receptor responses in hippocampus, but not which subtype. This group used CRISPR to knock out GluD1 in cultured hippocampal neurons and found a selective loss of GluN2B-containing NMDA receptor responses, with GluN2A-mediated responses and GluN2A puncta size untouched — only GluN2B puncta shrank.

In slices, at the ventral CA1 to subiculum synapse, GluD1 loss reduced GluN2B-containing currents, accelerated NMDA receptor decay kinetics, and impaired LTP induction — and region-restricted GluD1 deletion in ventral subiculum disrupted long-term contextual memory in vivo. The honest limitation is stoichiometry: pharmacological blockade and decay kinetics cannot cleanly separate diheteromeric GluN2B receptors from triheteromeric GluN2A-GluN2B receptors. But the concept is the takeaway — GluD1 is a subtype-selectivity switch. If you want to shift the GluN2B fraction at a specific synapse without a subunit-selective antagonist, this is an upstream handle, and the ventral subiculum localization puts it in an emotionally relevant circuit rather than a generic one.

Fifth, a review in Molecular Psychiatry on the therapeutic potential of COMT inhibition, and I'm including it because it is the most useful synthesis of an inverted-U pharmacology I have read in a while. The anatomical selectivity is the foundation: COMT accounts for roughly sixty percent of dopamine turnover in rat frontal cortex versus about fifteen percent in striatum, because cortex clears dopamine enzymatically while striatum has the transporter. So inhibiting COMT raises frontal dopamine without touching striatal dopamine — confirmed by microdialysis under tolcapone, by knockout mice, and in humans by the Val158Met dependence of prefrontal but not striatal D1 receptor PET binding.

Layer the inverted-U on top and you get a lawfully predictable drug. Tolcapone improves working memory in Val homozygotes, who sit on the rising limb with lower prefrontal dopamine tone, and does nothing or impairs performance in Met homozygotes near the peak. What I hadn't appreciated is how far the effects extend past working memory: tolcapone increases preference for larger delayed rewards, particularly in more impulsive individuals; it reduces symptoms in pathological gambling, most prominently in Val carriers; and two studies show reduced drinking in alcohol use disorder. In rats it reduces ethanol intake and disrupts the prefrontal–accumbens synchrony associated with drug seeking.

The constraint is real and the review is blunt about it: tolcapone is the only brain-penetrant licensed COMT inhibitor, it carries rare severe hepatotoxicity requiring continuous liver monitoring, and the target populations — Parkinson's, schizophrenia, addiction — are exactly the metabolically compromised patients you least want to give it to. No new brain-penetrant COMT inhibitor has reached the clinic. Worth flagging too: several of the rodent behaviors attributed to COMT are hippocampal rather than frontal tasks, and COMT's role outside frontal cortex is essentially unexplored despite robust hippocampal expression. That's an open medicinal-chemistry problem with a fully worked-out rationale behind it, which is not a common combination.

Sixth, a neuromodulation first. Transcranial ultrasound has been used to target grey matter for years, but nobody had shown you could selectively modulate a human white matter tract. The mechanistic rationale is decent — low-intensity ultrasound activates mechanosensitive channels including the two-pore potassium channels TREK-1 and TRAAK, which are heavily enriched at nodes of Ranvier — but the human demonstration requires a physiological readout of the sonicated pathway plus comparison sites, and that's what this study built.

Fifteen healthy adults, within-subject crossover, eighty seconds of 5-hertz repetitive ultrasound targeted at the left corticospinal tract at the superior internal capsule, with two comparison conditions: the homologous right tract, and ipsilateral motor cortex. TMS provided the readout. Left tract stimulation increased corticomotor excitability and intracortical facilitation site-specifically, exceeding both comparisons and still separating at fifty minutes with moderate-to-large effect sizes. Motor cortex stimulation did something different — it reduced short-interval intracortical inhibition, which the tract conditions did not. Two signatures, two targets, dissociated.

Two caveats matter. About thirty percent of the thresholded acoustic field fell in grey matter, because the focal volume is larger than the cross-section of the posterior limb of the internal capsule — the tract was the principal but not the only exposed tissue. And TMS indexes the corticomotor pathway as a whole, so this cannot localize the change to the sonicated white matter; a transient conduction change driving lasting plasticity at cortical synapses fits equally well. What is genuinely new is that fractional anisotropy moderated the pressure–response slope — the effect scaled with how well organized that person's tract was. That is the first quantitative argument for anatomy-informed ultrasound dosing rather than one protocol for everybody.

Seventh, a pain pharmacology paper that reframes a target the field mostly gave up on. Anti-NGF antibodies work, then run into safety problems, and the standard mental model of NGF-driven pain is TrkA on nociceptors sensitizing TRPV1. This group asked whether the different pain modalities separate by receptor and by cell type, and they do, cleanly. Intraplantar NGF in mice produced acute nociception, heat hyperalgesia, mechanical allodynia and cold hypersensitivity. Cleavage-resistant proNGF — which binds p75 preferentially — produced only the mechanical allodynia and the cold hypersensitivity, with no acute nociception and no heat hyperalgesia at all.

A TrkA inhibitor or a TRPV1 antagonist blocked the acute nociception and heat hyperalgesia and left mechanical and cold sensitivity untouched. Those two require p75 signaling in Schwann cells, not in neurons. And the downstream cascade is druggable: p75 activation triggers calcium release, then TRPA1 activation, mitochondrial reactive oxygen species and NOX1-dependent oxidative amplification, in a feed-forward loop. Blocking ROS, blocking TRPA1, or deleting TRPA1 specifically in Schwann cells markedly reduced mechanical allodynia and cold hypersensitivity while sparing the other two modalities.

This tidies up a genuinely confused literature — the contradictory reports on whether p75 blockade affects thermal hyperalgesia resolve once you specify which thermal modality and which cell. Strategically, it says the persistent mechanical component of NGF-driven pain is reachable in a non-neuronal cell, which is a different safety profile from anything acting on nociceptor TrkA.

Eighth, and a direct counterpoint to the lead: the largest careful benchmark of generative molecular design I've seen, and the headline is one every computational chemist already suspects but now has numbers for: docking score does not predict prioritization success. Twelve generation and optimization methods across 176 curated protein–ligand systems, spanning pocket-conditioned three-dimensional generation, diffusion and flow models, autoregressive construction, reference-conditioned optimization, and synthesis-aware design.

The first useful finding is that chemical validity is a dead metric — every model emitted 99.8 to 100 percent valid molecules. What differs enormously is usable yield. Pocket2Mol and FlowMol delivered at least a hundred valid molecules for ninety-eight percent of targets or better. TargetDiff completed almost every target and hit that quota for one percent. So report execution success and molecular yield separately, because they come apart.

The second finding is the trade-off, and it's stark. On the 65 targets all twelve models covered, one flow model achieved the best median docking performance and cleared the docking threshold for ninety-nine percent of targets — and had a two percent median pass rate once you also required drug-likeness and synthetic accessibility. FlowMol had comparatively weak normalized docking and the highest joint pass rate, at forty-four percent. Advantages at one stage almost never persisted to the next, including across ADME and toxicity endpoints. As a positive control the pipeline did recover rentosertib, the AI-designed TNIK inhibitor now in phase three for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis — retrospective and single-compound, so a sanity check rather than validation. The real deliverable is the guidance: deploy these models stage-specifically, and stop reporting single-metric leaderboards. Read alongside the lead, the two papers agree — the value is in the controller and the staging, not in any one generator.

Ninth, and closer to your clinic than any of the above: a scoping review in npj Digital Medicine on the mental health harms of large language model chatbots. A hundred and nineteen articles, sorted post hoc into five categories — conceptual work, mental health support use, cognitive overreliance, AI dependence, and what the literature is now calling AI psychosis. Context worth having: survey estimates put the fraction of chatbot users who use them for mental health somewhere between twenty-four and forty-nine percent.

The review's real contribution is an evidence audit, and the verdict is bracing. The two mechanisms everybody worries about — hallucinated clinical information and sycophancy — have been discussed at length and studied for actual impact on symptoms essentially not at all. The evidence base is dominated by vignette studies with simulated users, which the authors classify as first-tier foundational work; only three studies in the entire corpus reached the second tier of actual patient-chatbot interaction. And the vignette design has a specific artifact: simulated queries start from a fresh session with no history, whereas real users bring accounts, chat histories and personalization, and response quality is known to degrade across turns within a session.

On AI psychosis specifically — seven articles, six of them conceptual — the mechanism is coherent: sycophantic models validate delusional content, a benchmark study found implicitly expressed delusions were reinforced more readily than overt ones, and one group frames it as bidirectional belief amplification, where user input feeds model memory and the model adapts toward the delusional frame. Coherent, and almost entirely untested prospectively. If you're asked to advise, the defensible position is that the mechanisms are well-characterized and the incidence and causality are not.

We finish with something small and honest. A mouse study of kava — Piper methysticum — reformulated to remove flavokavains A and B, the constituents implicated in its hepatotoxicity. The question is whether you can get an anxiolytic without sedation, and specifically whether it helps in the context of nicotine, since anxiety and smoking are bidirectionally risk-enhancing. Male and female mice on a supplemented diet went through two blocks of every-other-day nicotine at half a milligram per kilogram with open field testing, then challenges.

The good news: kava increased center time consistently, including in a novel open field two weeks after the last injection, and increased rather than decreased locomotor activity — anxiolytic-like without sedation, which is the harder half of the ask. The bad news, and the reason I like the paper: the effect was clearest in saline-treated animals, and kava did not prevent nicotine's suppression of exploratory behavior. The authors also note they didn't replicate nicotine locomotor sensitization under this schedule. So an anxiolytic signal that doesn't extend to the state you'd actually be treating — a negative result reported as one.

That's the episode. The pattern worth holding onto is that four of these papers earned their value by going after a load-bearing assumption instead of adding a data point on top of it — which G protein your receptor really uses, whether the relay you've been drawing exists, whether your metric predicts the thing you care about, and whether the harms everyone is discussing have ever been measured.
