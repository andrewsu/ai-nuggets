## Script

Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Saturday, October thirty-first, twenty twenty-six.

There's a thread running through today's list, and it's about the reference. Not the measurement — the thing the measurement was held up against. A placebo arm that improved just as much. A landmark finding that won't replicate, with the new point estimates pointing the other way. A baseline with no chemistry in it that beats every tool built from chemistry. A train-test split that moves a score by a quarter of its range while the architecture stays fixed. A number that is real, sits in the agent's own files, and came from a different experiment than the one the sentence claims. In every one of these, the data collection was competent. What decided the answer was what sat on the other side of the comparison.

Let's open with the most interesting negative result of the week, a pilot secondary analysis out of the Johns Hopkins Center for Psychedelic and Consciousness Research, posted to medRxiv. Twenty-five participants with co-occurring alcohol use disorder and major depression, drawn from a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of psilocybin — thirteen on placebo, twelve on psilocybin. Five cognitive tasks before dosing and again about a week after, mean six-point-seven days. The battery was built to span the stability-flexibility continuum: the Multi-Source Interference Task for inhibition, probabilistic rule switching for feedback-driven shifting, cued task switching, a computational model of reward and punishment learning, and operation span for working memory.

Two predictions were on trial. The first is the component-specific account: once acute effects wash out, psychedelics selectively enhance flexibility while inhibition and working memory return to baseline. The second is PRISM, which says the post-acute change is motivational rather than capacity-based — psilocybin lowers the perceived cost of effort, so people become more willing to exert control, not more able to. PRISM nominates the Demand Selection Task, where participants freely choose between easy and hard versions, as the direct test. And here's the thing worth saying out loud: the first author is the author of PRISM. This is a group testing its own model, prospectively, and reporting that it didn't hold.

Psilocybin slowed cued task switching rather than speeding it — learning-phase reaction time, Cohen's d of one-point-oh-one, Bayes factor seven-point-seven; switch trials, d of one-point-one-two, Bayes factor seventeen. That's the opposite of the flexibility prediction. Across the other thirty-four retest measures the Bayes factors sat between zero-point-four and two-point-four, which is the numerical way of saying the data don't distinguish anything, and nothing survived false-discovery correction. Demand avoidance, the one measure PRISM actually named, showed no detectable drug effect — a Bayes factor of two in favor of the null. And the finding that should reorganize how you think about mechanism here: drinking fell in both arms, and on fifteen of seventeen stability-flexibility measures, the people whose drinking dropped most had moved toward stability, not flexibility. Stability composite, Spearman rho of minus zero-point-six-four. Lower baseline working-memory span predicted larger drinking reductions. Depressive symptoms fell more after psilocybin, d of minus zero-point-eight-eight, but that improvement tracked nothing cognitive at all.

Twenty-five people is a pilot and the authors say so. But the structure is exactly right. The placebo arm wasn't a formality — it carried the drinking reduction too, and once it did, the cognitive correlate pointed away from the mechanism the field has been building toward.

Second, a conceptual replication from Karolinska, also on medRxiv, and a clean one. Thirty-four unmedicated people in a recurrent major depressive episode, carbon-eleven U-C-B-J positron emission tomography, which indexes synaptic vesicle glycoprotein two-A as a proxy for presynaptic density. The target was the Holmes finding: depression severity inversely associated with SV2A binding in frontolimbic regions. In dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, anterior cingulate and hippocampus, they got nothing — correlations of zero-point-one-eight to zero-point-two-six, all p at or above zero-point-one-five. And every one of them positive in sign, with confidence intervals that exclude an effect of the magnitude originally reported. The original sample was transdiagnostic, mixing major depression, post-traumatic stress disorder and comorbid cases, which gives you an obvious candidate for the discrepancy.

Then the exploratory half, and this is where I'd be careful. Duration of the current depressive episode was negatively associated with binding across all three regions, r of minus zero-point-three-six to minus zero-point-three-seven, p values clustered between zero-point-oh-four-one and zero-point-oh-four-six. Those are uncorrected and sitting right on the line at an n of thirty-two. The adjusted models look far stronger, p of zero-point-oh-oh-four and Bonferroni-surviving — but notice what else those models produce. Once you adjust for episode duration, the severity association flips significant and positive. The same adjustment that creates the chronicity finding creates a severity finding in the opposite direction from the one being replicated. That's a classic suppression pattern and it should temper how hard anyone leans on either half. There's also no healthy control group, so none of this can say whether synaptic density is low in depression at all. The honest headline is the failed replication; the chronicity story is a hypothesis for a longitudinal design.

Third, into circuit pharmacology, from Copenhagen and Lund on bioRxiv. Chemogenetic activation of motor-cortical pyramidal tract neurons — targeted with Chrna2-Cre, an excitatory DREADD, and the agonist deschloroclozapine — in intact mice and in the unilateral six-hydroxydopamine model. In healthy animals, driving pyramidal tract output suppressed spontaneous locomotion, so the net effect is motor-inhibitory. In lesioned animals it did not improve parkinsonian deficits, which is the useful control: whatever it's doing isn't generic sedation, or the deficit would have deepened. What it did do was attenuate contraversive rotations driven by the D1 agonist SKF-38393, and reduce the axial component of levodopa-induced dyskinesia.

The mechanism is the appealing part. Acute D1 stimulation drove robust c-Fos in the dopamine-depleted striatum, and pyramidal tract activation significantly cut it — p of zero-point-oh-oh-six-five — with c-Fos and enkephalin co-labeling confirming the activity was overwhelmingly in D1 spiny projection neurons. Meanwhile delta-FosB, the chronic marker that accumulates over weeks of levodopa, was untouched. So this is an intervention that dampens acute D1-driven striatal overactivity without reaching the maladaptive plasticity underneath. That dissociation is genuinely informative about what cortical stimulation could and couldn't be expected to do in patients.

Where I'd push back is the word "selectively." The overall abnormal-involuntary-movement score was not significantly reduced — p of zero-point-one-five. The significant effect was at one of five twenty-minute observation periods, the eighty-minute mark, at p of zero-point-oh-three-nine, and the axial-only claim rests on limb and orolingual dyskinesia being null. Selectivity inferred from where the nulls fell, inside a design with five timepoints and three domains, is a weaker claim than the abstract makes it sound. The c-Fos data are the real result.

Fourth, a piece of classical channel pharmacology from Buffalo, Minnesota and N-Y-U on bioRxiv, asking whether the two subunits of the neuronal ATP-sensitive potassium channel can be separated functionally. Kir6.2 is the pore; SUR1 is the sulfonylurea receptor that confers Mg-A-D-P and diazoxide sensitivity. They made intrathecal AAV-Cre conditional knockouts of each in dorsal root ganglion and lumbar cord, and compared excitability, potassium flux and morphine antinociception.

The answer is that they can't be separated. Loss of either subunit raised D-R-G firing, loss of either blunted diazoxide-induced potassium flux, and loss of either attenuated morphine antinociception — for mechanical, though not thermal, stimulation. Baseline mechanical and heat thresholds were unchanged in both. The sharpest detail is that knocking out Kir6.2 reduced diazoxide sensitivity, even though diazoxide acts on nucleotide-binding folds in SUR. And Kcnj11 message fell in the SUR1-recombinant animals as well. So these aren't two independent manipulations; they're two ways of destabilizing one obligate complex, and the trafficking interdependence means neither knockout isolates its own subunit. The title says the two produce "comparable" effects, but no direct SUR1-versus-Kir6.2 contrast is reported — the comparability is read off two separate knockout-versus-control comparisons, which is not the same test. Practically, though, the message for anyone hoping to build a Kir6.2-selective peripheral analgesic is useful and a little deflating: in vivo, you probably can't address one subunit without moving the other.

Fifth, staying with Parkinson's, a large myeloid atlas from Mount Sinai on medRxiv. Nearly a hundred and seventy thousand brain myeloid cells and nuclei from ninety-six donors spanning the Lewy body continuum, eighty-six thousand of them from substantia nigra, integrated with the Accelerating Medicines Partnership Parkinson's data for just under three hundred thousand profiles total. Across eight conserved states, alpha-synucleinopathy is associated with a bidirectional program — glycolytic and proteostatic stress genes up, lysosomal and mitochondrial pathways down — and microglia shift from P2RY12-high sensome cells toward an HSP90AA1-high proteostatic stress state that intensifies at late Braak stages. Immunohistochemistry backs that up: HSP60-positive proteostatic microglia rise as nigral neurons are lost.

Two findings carry the genetics, and both deserve an asterisk. A rare NUCKS1-high state carried the strongest polygenic risk loading — and it's about one percent of myeloid cells, so that's a strong signal in a thin slice. And the protective allele result has a direction worth sitting with: rs3732765-A is a microglial expression QTL that lowers P2RY12 expression, yet it associated with a higher proportion of P2RY12-high microglia and higher lysosomal activity. The authors flag it as nominal, and it should stay nominal until someone reconciles why an allele that reduces the transcript enriches the cell state named for it. The descriptive atlas is the solid contribution; the genetic convergence is a nomination.

Sixth, from Boston University and Colorado, on bioRxiv — what early-life adversity does to the expression of genes we already know confer schizophrenia risk. Limited bedding and nesting as the stressor, twenty-four-plex single-cell multiplexed fluorescence in situ hybridization registered to the Allen atlas, one-point-two-nine million cells across four postnatal ages, profiling the ten original SCHEMA genes plus complement C4b and activity markers. Whole-brain risk-gene upregulation peaked at postnatal day twenty-one, with hypothalamus especially prominent. Sp4 rose at P21 and fell at P90 in hypothalamic oligodendrocytes; Cacna1g fell at P10; C4b upregulation spread across neurons, oligodendrocytes and O-P-Cs. Across regional cell types, P10 C4b effects correlated positively with P21 Grin2a effects.

The scale is impressive and the developmental-window framing is the right one — if gene-by-environment interaction in schizophrenia is real, it should be visible as adversity moving the very transcripts that rare variants disrupt, and in a specific window. But with twelve genes, four ages, dozens of regions and multiple cell classes, the significant cells are selected from an enormous grid, and that last cross-gene, cross-age correlation is a derived quantity built on top of effect estimates rather than a measurement. Take the P21 window as the hypothesis. The individual gene stories need replication.

Seventh, and the one I'd put in front of anyone building predictive toxicology, from Matterworks on bioRxiv. They assembled five hundred sixty-nine approved drugs from ChEMBL, labeled toxic if carrying a black-box warning or withdrawn, and gated for novelty — every compound absent from invitroDB and below zero-point-seven ECFP4 Tanimoto similarity to its nearly nine thousand reference compounds. Then thirty-five model readouts: fingerprint QSAR, fine-tuned foundation encoders, and purpose-built toxicity tools.

Two results. Among models fit on the panel by cross-validation, a raw ECFP4 fingerprint with logistic regression hits an area under the curve of zero-point-nine-one-oh, and no learned representation matches it — Uni-Mol's 3D encoder gets zero-point-eight-five-two, MolFormer zero-point-eight-oh-oh, Chemprop zero-point-seven-four-five. So representation sophistication is not the bottleneck. Second, and harder: among the toxicity-specific tools applied frozen, as released, most fall below chance, only one clears it, and none beat a regression on first approval year alone — zero-point-seven-eight-five from a predictor containing no chemistry whatsoever. No drug-induced liver injury tool beats chance on the panel, and the external set where several do succeed is one where approval year does most of the separating: in LiverTox-314 the positives predate the negatives by twenty-two years.

Now, the obvious objection — that approval year works because black-box warnings accumulate with time on market, making it a label artifact rather than an insight — is one the authors anticipate, and their panel narrows the year gap to six years, positives at 2012 against negatives at 2018. They also ran a similarity-binned check on nineteen hundred forty-five drugs where mean area under the curve stays between zero-point-five-two and zero-point-six-oh across every bin and does not rise with similarity to training chemistry. That control is what turns this from a complaint into a finding. The conclusion I'd draw is the authors': the input is wrong, not the model. Structure alone doesn't carry exposure or metabolism.

Eighth, to clinical multi-agent systems, from Drexel and Michigan on arXiv, and the title does the work — disparity laundering. A full factorial of five model families crossed with four aggregation topologies, on a hundred twenty real emergency cases from MIMIC-four under counterfactual demographic swaps. All twenty cells committed to the direction of their constituent agents' independent answers, assigning Black patients more acute triage. Every model has a topology that amplifies its own baseline gap, but which topology varies by model — so there is no design that is reliably safe and none reliably harmful. The disparity gets relocated, never removed.

What makes this more than another bias audit is the failure of the fixes. Fairness instructions did nothing. Demographic masking did nothing. Per-agent self-debiasing made it worse, because by the time deliberation starts, the bias is already encoded in the agents' ordinary clinical estimates rather than in any statement about demographics. And the argument that follows is the one to take seriously: auditing per model, per output, per version certifies the components but never the committed decision, which is where the harm lives. The one intervention that works — a diverse-perspective ensemble that makes counterfactual versions visible inside the system and pools them through deliberation at every round — cuts the gap by sixty-seven to ninety-four percent, and notably fails on the single topology that never pools all five perspectives. That consistency is what makes the mechanism credible rather than incidental.

Ninth, the same lesson in a different domain, from Auckland University of Technology on medRxiv. Five published seizure-prediction pipelines held completely fixed, from a power-spectral-density plus linear-discriminant-analysis baseline up to a transformer, evaluated on the CHB-MIT scalp E-E-G database, with only the evaluation design varying. Hold out whole subjects across twenty repeated partitions and every pipeline lands between zero-point-five-two-seven and zero-point-five-five-three — and the five architectures differ from each other by less than each differs across partitions. Within patients, the spectral baseline reaches zero-point-eight-one-five when windows are allocated at random, and zero-point-five-seven-two when preictal blocks are kept intact and later seizures are held out. Tracing every window to its recording showed index-based within-patient splits let raw samples cross the train-test boundary in all ten eligible cases. Thresholds tuned for a one-point-six-seven percent false-positive rate on validation didn't hold on new subjects, and at those thresholds only two to six percent of preictal windows were detected. Zero-shot transfer to the Siena database: zero-point-four-eight-oh. They release the window manifests, partitions and predictions, which is the part that makes it actionable rather than merely discouraging.

Tenth, and in the same spirit, a careful piece from Al-Ahliyya Amman University and Binghamton on arXiv about what actually goes wrong in papers written by autonomous research agents. They re-analyzed all five hundred forty released judgments from a public benchmark covering nine agents and fifteen open research problems, at the level of individual rubric criteria, then hand-labeled two hundred fifty-six numerical claims from eighteen submissions. The distinction they draw is the contribution: a claim with no supporting source anywhere in the artifacts, versus a value that is real but drawn from the wrong experiment, the wrong metric or the wrong run. Roughly four in five unsupported numbers fall in the second bucket. The failure mode is not fabrication. It's misattribution — the agent did the work, the number exists, and the sentence around it points at a different experiment.

Their fix is a deterministic claim-to-evidence checker that asks whether surrounding context identifies the right source, and it clears ninety percent precision against blinded human annotation with inter-annotator kappa of zero-point-eight-nine. The honest limitation, which they state, is coverage: it certifies about twenty-three percent of evaluated claims. Recall is deliberately sacrificed. That's the right trade for an integrity check, but it means three-quarters of the numbers in an agent-written paper remain unadjudicated, and the single-L-L-M-judge verdicts this work critiques are still what covers the rest.

And to close, something smaller and more cheerful, from Michigan Tech on arXiv — a controlled study of what you randomize in low-data molecular generation. Train an autoregressive sequence model on a thousand seed molecules, which is the regime where chemical language models mostly learn to recite their training set. Compare canonical SAFE — Sequential Attachment-based Fragment Embedding, where fragment order can vary as well as atom traversal — against randomized SAFE and against randomized SMILES at matched corpus size and compute. Randomizing SAFE raised the yield of genuinely novel molecules more than four hundred fifty-fold. The two axes act jointly, not additively: atom order alone gets you a tenth of the full effect, fragment order alone a seventh, so together they deliver roughly four times the sum of their parts. And at matched corpora, randomized SAFE returns two-point-eight times the genuine yield of randomized SMILES. It's a modest study with a clean design and a line worth keeping: in the low-data regime, what you randomize, not how much, decides whether augmentation gives you new molecules or just new spellings of the ones you already had.

That's the episode. The through-line, one more time: eleven papers, and in nearly every one the decisive move was choosing the comparison — the placebo arm that improved anyway, the covariate that creates the effect it explains, the year of approval with no chemistry in it, the split that moves a score by a quarter of its range. Have a good weekend.
