## Script

Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Thursday, October fifteenth. Ten items today. bioRxiv throttled direct requests from the first call, so most of today's preprints came through a reader proxy, and the Nature family filled in around them.

The thread I kept pulling on today is attribution. In almost every one of these papers, the variable that turns out to be doing the work is not the variable named in the headline. A drug's cognitive cost does not sit where the field has been looking for it. A dopamine manipulation does not behave like less dopamine. A chemistry model's answer is not a function of the molecule you asked about. Keep that in mind and the day holds together.

We open with the most interesting psychedelics paper of the week, from a group at University College London and Monash. The question is what happens to language systems during ego dissolution, and the design is unusual enough to be worth unpacking. Participants listened to music and watched moving clouds, once at baseline and once after nineteen milligrams of psilocybin. Rather than asking how any individual's brain changed, the analysis asks whether two people's brain activity became more similar to each other at the same moment of the same stimulus, and whether that similarity scaled with how much ego dissolution the pair reported on the Ego Dissolution Inventory — the standard self-report instrument for loss of self in psychedelic studies.

That inversion is the point. The intuitive prediction is that losing your sense of self makes your brain activity more idiosyncratic. What they find is the opposite. Under psilocybin, the component cluster whose between-subject similarity scales most strongly with ego dissolution is a distributed, left-lateralized frontotemporal network that matches the Neurosynth language map at a correlation of point three eight, against a maximum of point two one for the elementary visual imagery comparator and point one two at baseline. It peaks in dorsal pars opercularis near the inferior frontal junction and runs out through the frontal operculum, insula, and left lateral temporal cortex. The more two people dissolved, the more alike their language-associated cortex looked — while visual, auditory, executive, and limbic clusters all appeared too, but none of them tracked the language map anywhere near as well.

There is a resting-state follow-up: that network decoupled from posterior visual cortex and coupled more strongly to thalamic and frontal targets, and a contrast score built from those two directions correlated with ego dissolution at rho point four six and with elementary imagery at rho point zero one. So among the experiences they measured, this is specific to loss of self, not to perceptual intensity generally. The authors' reading is that language-associated systems are what stitch your semantic and autobiographical context into ongoing experience, and that disrupting them is part of what ego dissolution is. The caveat is that intersubject similarity is a strange dependent variable — convergence across people is not the same as any individual's representation changing — and the resting-state analysis is explicitly exploratory. But the stimulus-driven synchrony checks behaved sensibly, and the effect is specific in exactly the way a confound would not be.

Second, a cross-sectional study from the Bipolar-Schizophrenia Network on Intermediate Phenotypes, which asks where the cognitive cost of psychotropic medication actually lives. In one thousand sixty-seven people with psychotic disorders, the authors built personalized receptor-engagement profiles — for each patient, the prescribed dose of each of forty-nine psychotropic drugs mapped through known binding affinities onto thirty-two targets, then summarized into seven correlated pharmacological clusters. Gradient-boosted models with nested cross-validation predicted cognition, and the contribution of pharmacology was read out with out-of-sample Shapley values and tested by within-diagnosis permutation.

The result that matters is what did not come out. The dopamine D2-containing profile — the one every clinician and every regulator reasons about — was not associated with cognitive performance after correction for multiple testing. What was associated, and negatively, was a profile combining alpha-2 adrenergic, D4 and D5 dopaminergic, and several serotonergic receptors. Muscarinic modulation was separately associated with worse verbal memory and motor speed, which is the anticholinergic-burden finding everyone expects but rarely gets at the level of estimated engagement rather than a crude drug count. And dopamine transporter engagement was associated with better cognition. So in a population where the entire pharmacological conversation is organized around D2 occupancy, the receptor profiles that track cognition are the off-target ones. This is observational and cross-sectional, prescribing is confounded by illness severity in the obvious direction, and the affinity-times-dose model is a coarse proxy for occupancy. But as a hypothesis generator for cognition-sparing prescribing it is far more actionable than another chlorpromazine-equivalent regression.

Third, and staying in psychosis, a virtual brain modelling study of treatment resistance. Forty-three healthy controls and thirty-three patients with schizophrenia, stratified into treatment-resistant and non-resistant, with large-scale dynamics simulated from each person's structural and functional MRI using the Wong-Wang model. That model gives you four interpretable network parameters: global coupling, local inhibitory coupling, local excitatory N-methyl-D-aspartate coupling, and recurrent excitation.

Compared with non-resistant patients, the treatment-resistant group showed higher local NMDA coupling and lower recurrent excitation at the whole-brain level, with network-specific patterns on top — higher NMDA coupling and global coupling in the dorsal attention network, lower global coupling in the limbic network. And the parameters carried symptom information: inhibitory coupling in the default mode network predicted negative symptom severity, while NMDA coupling in limbic and ventral attention networks predicted total symptom scores and disorganization. That is a satisfying story for the glutamatergic account of treatment resistance, and the authors are refreshingly direct about why you should not believe it yet. Medication exposure, and specifically clozapine in the resistant group, cannot be disentangled from intrinsic disease differences in a cross-sectional design. These are generative-model parameters, not measurements — indirect proxies for excitation-inhibition balance, not glutamate or GABA. And there is no independent validation cohort. The honest framing is that this is a hypothesis about which networks to interrogate with magnetic resonance spectroscopy or PET, not a biomarker.

Fourth, a perspective piece in Molecular Psychiatry from the Cardiff group on something genuinely under-recognized: transition from ADHD to psychotic disorders. The odds ratios are between four point seven and eight point nine, which is large, and it happens in a minority, which is why it stays invisible. The piece works through the candidate explanations — that early ADHD features are prodromal psychosis, that treatment causes it, that ADHD-evoked environmental exposures mediate it, that both arise from shared neurodevelopmental processes — and finds the direct evidence thin for most of them.

The pharmacology section is the part worth carrying around. Registry data give an incidence rate ratio of one point zero four for psychosis in the twelve weeks after starting methylphenidate versus before, so therapeutic methylphenidate does not appear to raise psychosis risk, and a Finnish cohort hints it may be protective. Amphetamines look different: a meta-analytic odds ratio of one point five seven versus methylphenidate, rising to five point two eight at high dose. The clinical recommendation that follows is specific and, I think, correct — where there is a family or personal history of psychosis, prefer methylphenidate first line and monitor. And the authors make the counterweight explicit, because the field tends to forget it: the risks of not treating ADHD are substantial and better evidenced than the risks of treating it, including depression, suicidal behavior, criminality, and premature mortality, all reduced by medication.

Fifth, a mechanistic paper that is an unusually clean dissociation. Striatal cholinergic interneurons can trigger dopamine release locally, at the axon, through beta-2-containing nicotinic receptors, without any involvement of midbrain cell body firing. Whether that local control matters for behavior has been genuinely contested. This group conditionally deleted beta-2 nicotinic receptors from substantia nigra neurons in adult mice, which abolished cholinergic-interneuron-evoked dopamine release while leaving electrically evoked, beta-2-independent release intact.

The behavior is where it gets interesting. On a progressive ratio schedule, knockouts pressed less — roughly five hundred seventy presses against twelve hundred fifty — and quit at a lower response requirement. That reads like reduced effort, and you could stop there. But in extinction, the same animals were more persistent, suppressing responding by thirty percent over the session against sixty-four percent in controls. And in a choice task pitting a three-pellet variable-ratio lever against a one-pellet fixed-ratio lever, when the big reward required more effort, controls progressively shifted away from it, dropping preference by about seventeen percent across sessions, while knockouts dropped by two percent. The trial-by-trial analysis localizes it precisely: knockouts switched away from the costly high-reward option far less often, while switching toward it was completely unaffected. No side bias, no general switching deficit, no difference when rewards were matched.

The reason this is more than another effort paper is the direction. Broadly reducing mesolimbic dopamine biases animals away from costly options. This manipulation does the opposite — it leaves animals stuck on the expensive one. So disrupting nicotinic control of dopamine release is not a weaker version of reducing dopamine. It is a different thing, a failure to update away from a costly action with experience. That distinction matters for anyone thinking about nicotinic drugs in Parkinson's or addiction, where the mental model is usually "more dopamine, less dopamine."

Sixth, a GPCR signaling paper that pushes back on an idea most of us have internalized. The phosphorylation barcode hypothesis holds that ligand-dependent phosphorylation across many receptor residues encodes which transducer gets engaged. This group tested that at the melanocortin-4 receptor, the central regulator of energy homeostasis, by systematically substituting phosphorylatable residues in helix eight and the C-terminal tail.

What they find is not a barcode but a small number of strategically placed residues doing spatially segregated jobs. Substituting serine three-oh-six in helix eight disrupted beta-arrestin-2 recruitment, early endosomal trafficking, and ERK activation, and visibly shifted G-alpha-s-cAMP signaling across variants. Distal C-terminal substitutions barely touched arrestin coupling or trafficking — so those regions are not redundant copies of each other. And the G-q and G-twelve-thirteen-linked readouts stayed largely constrained no matter what they mutated, which is pathway-specific control rather than global tuning.

The translational hook is setmelanotide, the approved melanocortin-4 agonist used for rare genetic obesity. Against alpha-MSH, setmelanotide shifted the cAMP half-maximal effective concentration from about one point nine nanomolar to point zero six nanomolar at wild type — and more importantly, it remodeled the mutants. Where alpha-MSH revealed reduced capacity, setmelanotide produced greater potency than wild type at most variants and higher maximal cAMP responses at all but two. But that rescue was pathway-selective: it enhanced arrestin and ERK potency without producing the same inversion, and it did not restore the G-q and G-twelve-thirteen deficits at all. So the chemotype determines which parts of the regulatory landscape are even accessible. For anyone reasoning about variant-specific response to melanocortin-4 agonists, that is the practical finding — rescue is real, and it is partial in a way that depends on the pathway you happen to be measuring.

Now the computational half, and the attribution theme gets sharper.

Seventh, MolHop, from Xiamen and the Shanghai AI Lab, on scaffold hopping. The framing is a distinction worth making: optimizing a molecule and finding a structurally distinct replacement for it are different search problems, and LLM molecular design agents almost always do the first while claiming the second. MolHop organizes about one point six million scaffolds from commercial molecules into a coarse-to-fine hierarchical action space, so the agent navigates between scaffold regions and edits within them, with trajectory memory for long searches.

The number I care about is the harness ablation. Taking frozen models and changing only the search environment, Grok four point six went from forty-one and thirty-six percent joint success to sixty-four and sixty-nine percent on the drug and commercial tasks, and DeepSeek-Flash from fifteen and seventeen percent to fifty-two and sixty percent — with scaffold similarity going down at the same time, so it is not buying success by hopping less. Then they trained policies in that harness; the nine-billion-parameter model reaches fifty-five and seventy-three percent, beating every one-shot generation baseline, but only by two points on the drug task against the best generator. So the honest summary is that most of the gain here is the structured action space, not the model, and a thirty-seven-point swing from changing the environment around a frozen model is the result, not the fine-tuning.

Eighth, and this one should be read by anyone using an LLM for property prediction. The authors ask whether high benchmark scores on chemical prediction reflect structure-property reasoning or something else, and they built a clean test for it. Instead of giving the model the target molecule, they give it a source molecule and an explicit structural edit — replace this hydroxyl with an amine — so the target is fully determined but its identity is never stated. The models reconstruct the target structure correctly between eighty-six and one hundred percent of the time, including chirality, so they know what molecule is being asked about.

Then the results fall apart in a very specific way. When source labels are supplied, performance splits by whether the source and target happen to share the property. In twenty-two of twenty-four model-task combinations the label-consistent group wins, often catastrophically: one frontier model hits one hundred percent accuracy on the label-consistent half of BACE and HIV and zero percent on the label-inconsistent half. On a chirality-change odor task, F1 scores of point nine nine against point zero six. And the intervention closes it: holding the target molecule, the edit, and the ground truth fixed and swapping in a false source label flips ninety-one percent of one model's binary predictions toward the false label, with the others between thirty-six and fifty-nine percent. In yield prediction, sixty-one to ninety-eight percent of predicted values moved in the direction of the substituted source value. The target never changed. The model is predicting the source's property and reporting it as the target's.

Ninth, a paper from Penn on agentic scientific claim verification that asks why verifier disagreement with experts is so hard to debug. Natural-language scientific claims are underspecified — their running example is an alloy claim where "forms a single solid phase below seven hundred kelvin" could mean at some temperature below, or at every temperature below, and the expert and the agent silently chose differently. The proposal is a Claim Card: before gathering any evidence, the verifier writes its interpretation as nine fields, each marked stated, inferred, or default.

They ran an agent five times each on sixty expert-labeled materials science and biomedical claims, with a card, without one, and with a frozen card — nine hundred runs. Two results, and they pull in opposite directions. First, over forty percent of card fields were not marked stated, which tells you how much interpretation is happening invisibly. But adding the card cost about ten percent more tokens and time and did not measurably improve agreement with expert labels. Second, and this is the one that justifies the paper, deliberately editing a single field of the frozen card — making a quantifier universal, or tightening the support threshold — changed the verdict on about a third of claims, against about one in seven expected by chance. On the materials claims run with computational tools, the stricter threshold also changed what the agent asked the tools to compute. So the interpretation is load-bearing even though writing it down does not help accuracy, which is a genuinely uncomfortable combination. The authors' recommendation — report verdicts with their interpretations, and have benchmarks publish the interpretation behind each gold label — follows directly.

Closing on the lightest item, and a cautionary one. PRISMATIC is a multi-agent prescribing system for multimorbid patients, built on a blackboard architecture where specialist agents generate and refine a regimen, with two coordination mechanisms — one adjusting agent influence weights from historical case outcomes, one resolving disagreements within a case. Evaluated on MIMIC-IV records, it reaches a zero-interaction ratio of ninety-nine point two percent, meaning essentially no drug-drug conflicts, where baselines struggle past ninety-five.

Read that next to the other number in the same table. The exact-match score against the actual prescribed regimen is thirteen point five eight percent. The ablations are consistent — removing components drops exact match to seven or eight percent, and conflict-freedom to seventy-nine percent — so the architecture is doing something real. But a system that is conflict-free ninety-nine percent of the time and right about one time in seven is telling you that the easy metric saturated and the hard one did not. Safety here means the absence of a flagged interaction, which is a property of a lookup table, not of the prescription being correct. I would rather see the thirteen percent in the abstract, because that is the number that would have to move before any of this is near a patient.

That is today. The common thread, one more time: before you believe a result, check that the thing being measured is the thing being named. Back tomorrow.
