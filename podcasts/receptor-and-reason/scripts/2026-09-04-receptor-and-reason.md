## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. I'm your host, and this is your daily pass through CNS pharmacology, psychiatric and neurology drug discovery, and the computational machinery closing in on both. Nine items today, and most are versions of one claim: the effect does not belong to the molecule. It belongs to the molecule plus a context — a patient's pre-existing circuitry, a stress history, a channel's subunit partners, a receptor's unresolved tail, a documented interface. Wherever today's papers found something surprising, they found it by varying the context and holding the drug fixed.

We open in Geneva and Lausanne, with Olaf Blanke's group at the Swiss Federal Institute of Technology and neurologists at Geneva and Bern, taking on one of the oldest unresolved arguments in Parkinson's pharmacology: does dopamine replacement therapy cause hallucinations? Longitudinal studies say yes — higher levodopa-equivalent dose predicts structured visual hallucinations, and dose reduction often makes them go away. Against that, patients hallucinated before levodopa existed, drug-naive patients hallucinate today, no consistent dose-response relationship has ever been demonstrated, and when Goetz and colleagues gave intravenous levodopa directly and watched, nothing happened.

The methodological problem is that hallucinations are measured by asking people about events from hours or weeks earlier, while the standard pharmacological design is a twelve-hour on-off withdrawal window in which you're unlikely to observe a spontaneous hallucination at all. So this group brought a provocation test. Their robotic paradigm induces a presence hallucination — the sense that someone is behind you — through somatomotor conflict: the patient pokes a front robot, a rear robot delivers the corresponding touch to their back, and a delay is inserted between the two. Ten pokes per trial, delays of zero, a quarter second or half a second, and after each trial the patient reports whether they felt a presence. Twenty patients, all with motor and neuropsychiatric fluctuations, tested on and off medication on separate days in randomized order — eleven who hallucinate in daily life and nine who don't, indistinguishable on age, disease duration, sex, daily dose, motor severity and two cognitive batteries.

The result is a clean dissociation. Delay drove the illusion in everyone, as it should. But the three-way interaction between medication state, delay and hallucinatory phenotype was significant, and unpacking it, medication raised sensitivity in the patients who already hallucinate and did nothing at all in the patients who don't — point zero zero eight against point nine one eight. Off medication the two groups were identical, so this isn't a baseline difference dressed up as a drug effect. Resting-state imaging then found, both in a predefined hallucination network and in a fully data-driven whole-brain analysis, that the medication-linked rise in sensitivity tracked hyperconnectivity: within striatum, between striatum and cortical hallucination network, and across default mode, somatomotor and salience networks.

What I like is that it reconciles the literature rather than picking a side. Dopamine isn't uniformly hallucinogenic; it interacts with an intrinsic vulnerability some patients have and some don't, which is exactly why dose-response studies in unstratified cohorts kept coming up empty. Twenty patients is the limitation, which the authors own, and an overnight washout doesn't remove the long-duration response to chronic levodopa, so the off state isn't drug-free. The prospective study you want is whether provocation sensitivity in de novo patients predicts who goes on to hallucinate. If it does, this stops being a warning in a package insert and becomes a stratification tool.

Same logic, different transmitter, much further upstream. From Florence Kermen's group at the University of Copenhagen, a preprint asks why serotonergic anxiolytics work inconsistently, and answers with something that isn't about the drug at all: whether serotonin is involved depends on the intensity of the stress the anxiety came from. Larval zebrafish, graded hydrocortisone baths during development at zero, half and one micromolar, then a swimming plus-maze — the fish analogue of the elevated plus-maze.

Raising the glucocorticoid dose didn't simply produce more anxiety. The lower dose produced anxiety in the standard maze; the higher dose produced none in the standard maze but did once they brightened the lights and made the test more aversive. Two phenotypes, one context-general and one context-dependent. Chemogenetic ablation of the dorsal raphe rescued the low-dose anxiety in both conditions and left the high-dose anxiety untouched. Whole-brain activity mapping across two hundred ninety-three atlas regions, filtered for regions that tracked the behavioural rescue, converged on a dopaminergic cluster in the posterior tuberculum and hypothalamus. The pharmacology follows — the D1 antagonist S-C-H two-three-three-nine-zero abolished the anxiolytic effect of raphe ablation, haloperidol did not, and neither antagonist changed anxiety in fish with an intact raphe. A D1 blocker that does nothing alone but cancels an anxiolytic manipulation is telling you the dopaminergic arm is permissive downstream, not a tonic anxiety brake.

Be straight about the statistics, though, because the abstract is more confident than the tables. The stress-by-ablation interaction didn't reach significance, and the rescue rests on planned contrasts at point zero two five and point zero eight eight. The methylphenidate experiment — where a subtherapeutic, environmentally relevant concentration reduced anxiety in unstressed fish — has a non-significant omnibus test and a post-hoc contrast at point zero six with sixteen fish per group; treat that as a hypothesis. The framing still earns its place in trial design: two patients with the same anxiety phenotype from different stress histories may have a load-bearing serotonergic system in one case and an irrelevant one in the other, and you'll never see that in an unstratified randomization.

Now a drug that works while doing the opposite of what the mechanism predicts. From the Lyon Neuroscience Research Centre, cannabinoid receptor type two activation during epileptogenesis in a juvenile rat lithium-pilocarpine model of temporal lobe epilepsy. Anti-seizure medications don't touch the cognitive impairment and psychiatric comorbidity that make this disease disabling, so the target here is epileptogenesis, not ictogenesis. And since CB2 in myeloid cells is understood as a brake on leukocyte recruitment, the intuitive prediction is that an agonist blunts the inflammatory surge after status epilepticus.

It doesn't. Mapping the time course first, the pro-inflammatory cytokine peak is sharp and gone within a day, and the monocytes that arrive are not the villains of the standard account — at twenty-four hours they carry six-fold less tumour necrosis factor alpha, twenty-fold more interleukin ten and ninety-two-fold more arginase one than resident microglia. They then gave G-P-one-a, a selective CB2 agonist they validated at a dissociation constant near one point two nanomolar, from three hours after status epilepticus out to two weeks — inside the epileptogenic window, weeks before seizures emerge. It did not reduce the pro-inflammatory index. It doubled monocyte infiltration, which a second agonist reproduced. It didn't touch astrocyte or microglial markers at all.

And the outcomes improved. Long-term potentiation in CA1, which fell from about a hundred sixty-five percent of baseline in controls to a hundred nine percent after status epilepticus, came back to a hundred fifty percent with treatment. Reversal water-maze learning improved, novel object recognition was preserved where vehicle animals were impaired, and time to a first handling-induced seizure was pushed out — all vehicle animals had seized by the twentieth session against sixty-five percent of treated animals. Since dosing stopped weeks before onset, this is disease modification, not an anticonvulsant effect. The honest limitation is the endpoint: they deliberately avoided implanted electrodes because a pilot showed implantation itself drives monocyte infiltration, so the seizure data are behavioural counts, not electroencephalographic verification.

From a drug that works unexpectedly to a tool compound that doesn't work at all — and this is the quietly most useful paper on the pile. A group at the Hertie Institute in Tübingen wanted a precision-pharmacology tool for K-C-N-A-two channelopathies, the epilepsy, intellectual disability and episodic ataxia syndromes caused by variants in the K-V one point two potassium channel subunit. The ideal experiment is to overexpress a variant subunit carrying a second mutation that confers resistance to a subunit-selective blocker, so you can pharmacologically subtract everything else and read out the variant alone. So they synthesized a scorpion toxin peptide, B-M-K eighty-six P-one, reported to be K-V one point two selective.

Three things went wrong, each instructive. The peptide did selectively block K-V one point two homomers and spare K-V one point one, but its potency came in below published values, with the authors' own candidate explanation being incomplete disulfide oxidation — exactly the failure mode that makes disulfide-rich peptides hard to use as reagents. Second, it largely lost efficacy against K-V one point two co-assembled with K-V one point one, and in real murine neurons it changed nothing measurable: no effect on passive properties, action potential shape or firing, and only minimal changes in spontaneous excitatory currents. Since these channels assemble predominantly as heteromers in vivo, a homomer-selective blocker is not a physiologically useful one. Third, and this is the sting: the resistance mutation they identified, valine three eighty-one to tyrosine, does confer resistance — but the same substitution shifts activation to more hyperpolarized potentials, so the channel starts behaving like K-V one point one. The escape mutant is not the same channel. If your strategy requires the resistance mutation to be pharmacologically consequential and biophysically silent, you have to demonstrate the second half, and here it isn't true.

That theme, that the residue you thought was inert is doing the work, scales up in the next item, from Stéphane Laporte's group at McGill with Nagarajan Vaidehi's computational team. Where does G protein coupling selectivity actually live in a promiscuous receptor? The assumption in the field, and behind most biased-ligand design, is the ligand-binding site or the G protein interface. They tested it with near-complete mutagenesis of two vasopressor receptors — the angiotensin two type one receptor and the prostaglandin F-two-alpha receptor — over three hundred single-point substitutions each, reading out Gq-eleven and G-twelve-thirteen coupling with resonance-energy-transfer biosensors in G-protein knockout cells, or with the Gq inhibitor Y-M two-five-four-eight-nine-zero.

Roughly three-quarters of the functionally important residues in both receptors sit outside both the binding site and the interface. Among residues selectively important for one pathway and not the other, about eighty-three percent in each receptor were allosteric rather than at a contact surface, and direct co-dependencies between binding-site and interface residues accounted for only five to six percent of the total. The structurally unresolved regions carry much of this: in both receptors, mutations that enhanced G-twelve-thirteen signalling clustered in the carboxyl terminus, meaning the tail actively restrains that pathway in the wild-type receptor. The Bayesian network analysis adds the mechanistic flourish — a receptor's preferred pathway corresponds to a densely interconnected residue network while the non-preferred pathway runs on a sparse one, and is therefore much more easily broken by a single mutation. That is a concrete explanation for why coupling promiscuity is robust and coupling bias is fragile.

Three computational items now, and the first is the one I'd most want a psychiatrist to read. A paper on arXiv asks whether depressive symptoms are represented separably inside a language model's activations, rather than collapsed into the single severity score clinical practice runs on. Working with the twenty-seven-billion-parameter pretrained Gemma three model, the authors built a corpus of symptom descriptions from validated instruments — the diagnostic manual, the Hamilton and Montgomery-Åsberg scales, the nine-item patient questionnaire — annotated into mood, somatic and suicidality groups. Sweeping all sixty-three layers with a permutational multivariate analysis of variance, the groups separate maximally at layer twenty-one on several independent distance metrics. They then form a symptom vector as each group's activation centroid and project held-out text onto it — and because the axes are severely collinear, with a Gram matrix condition number over fourteen thousand, they decorrelate with a pseudoinverse rather than projecting naively. That decorrelation step is the real methodological contribution.

The held-out test is naturalistic first-person and clinical narrative — Styron's memoir, a depression handbook, an annotated Reddit corpus, a hundred forty-one excerpts per group. On each axis the matching group scored highest, which is what they claim, and it's real. But read the coefficients rather than the abstract. On held-out text, mood-annotated excerpts scored higher on the suicidality axis than on the mood axis. So the ordering holds between groups within an axis and does not hold within a passage across axes — meaning you could not use this to say which symptom a given passage is about, which is what a clinical tool would have to do. The valence gate has the same shape of problem: a single depression direction separates depressive from happy text with an area under the curve of point seven eight nine on held-out data, but the cosine values sit in a narrow band with heavy overlap, and the highest-scoring supposedly-depressive outliers from the happiness corpus were emotionally neutral sentences — "their sore throat cleared up", "they went shopping". They're candid that this is a population-level separator, not a per-excerpt classifier. Add the confound they disclose, that every passage was rewritten into third-person prose by another language model, and the honest summary is that the geometry is there and the instrument is not. Their closing limitation is the right one: agreement with clinician annotation is not clinical validity, and nothing here has been tested against diagnosis, treatment response or trajectory.

Then a bioinformatics agent paper that lands as a near-perfect counterpoint to the neuroscience agent system we covered yesterday. Bioinfoysis is a multi-agent harness in which every request becomes a persistent, artifact-grounded analysis run: a planner keeps an executable checklist and revises pending steps using structured handoffs that bind each intermediate result to the step and plan generation that produced it, so stale evidence can't be silently reused after replanning, and a runtime validates generated scripts, tables and figures before anything downstream consumes them. On the two-hundred-five-question BixBench benchmark it reports eighty-two point four percent, ahead of the next-best published agent by seven points.

Ignore the leaderboard number and read the ablation table, because that's the result. Swapping the underlying model across three frontier families moves accuracy by five point four points. Removing persistent memory costs twenty-two. Removing the forty-eight domain-specific skills costs nineteen, and removing the remaining thirty general skills costs another eighteen — thirty-seven points total attributable to the skill library. Replacing adaptive replanning with a fixed plan-then-execute loop costs under seven. So the model is nearly fungible, the orchestration logic is worth little, and memory plus a curated procedure library is the entire product. That's the same conclusion yesterday's system reached from the opposite direction, where stripping out the pretrained model library left orchestration scoring below single-shot code generation. Two independent teams, same lesson: the scaffolding that matters holds domain procedures and durable state, not control flow. One caveat on the comparison — this group audited the benchmark's reference answers first and scores runs on execution correctness, artifact generation and traceability in addition to answer match. Defensible evaluation, but not the one the competitors were scored under, so the margin isn't apples to apples.

Which pairs naturally with a preprint from Xiaojie Qiu's lab at Stanford on the least glamorous prerequisite for any of this. Scientific software is locked in the language it was written in — you can call R from Python through a bridge, but you can't natively extend or build on it, and foundational single-cell tools are decaying as their dependencies move on, Monocle two among them, which is Qiu's own lineage. Bio-Babel is a multi-agent framework that rebuilds packages natively in a target ecosystem and ships each with a machine-readable contract describing how to use it. It keeps its state in files on disk rather than in the model's context, so a port survives an agent losing that context, and it works at package level: resolve the whole export surface into a dependency graph, partition into dependency-ordered slices, build and test each only after its dependencies. They did the hard version, a five-layer R stack rooted in grid — the abstract layout substrate under the grammar of graphics — climbing through the plotting grammar to Monocle and trajectory testing on top, re-rooted on the community-standard annotated-data container instead of Monocle's bespoke object system, with no runtime bridge back to R. Fidelity is reported unflatteringly, at sixty-two to a hundred percent tier-resolved parity.

The finding I'd hold onto is their two-by-two. They built a thirty-eight-task benchmark stating a goal without naming the required interface, and crossed contract access with the ability to execute code. With neither, the agent failed a hundred twelve of a hundred fourteen runs. Contract only, no execution: fifty-one percent failure. Execution only, no contract: thirty-six. Both: sixteen. Documentation and the ability to try things are not substitutes for each other, and the combination is worth twenty points over trial and error alone, while shifting effort from executing code to looking things up — fewer interaction rounds, higher per-task cost. If you're wondering why your agent flails on your own lab's tooling, that's the number to look at, and the fix is a contract, not a better model.

And to close, a small negative result that should change a guideline. A retrospective cohort from Vanderbilt looked at two hundred nineteen courses of transcranial magnetic stimulation for major depression and asked whether concurrent substance use predicted response, measured as change on the nine-item depression questionnaire. Alcohol was most common at thirty-four percent, then prescribed benzodiazepines at twenty-eight and prescribed stimulants at twenty-one. Controlling for baseline severity, age, sex and number of sessions, nothing was associated with outcome — every comparison above point zero five, effect sizes from zero to point three, and the range of plausible effects sitting below the questionnaire's minimal clinically important difference. Benzodiazepines are the interesting case, since the mechanistic worry is that enhancing inhibition should blunt the plasticity you're inducing, and the pooled literature hasn't supported that either.

The authors lead with the honest limitation: this sample used substances at low to moderate levels, only about one in ten drank more than weekly, and substance history came from a standard psychiatric consultation rather than structured instruments or urine toxicology. So it says nothing about treating an active substance use disorder. But current guidance recommends abstinence before a course of stimulation, and that recommendation now rests on a mechanistic worry that four or five independent cohorts have failed to confirm. For a treatment plenty of patients can't reach for reasons of insurance and geography, requiring abstinence you can't justify is a barrier with nothing behind it.
