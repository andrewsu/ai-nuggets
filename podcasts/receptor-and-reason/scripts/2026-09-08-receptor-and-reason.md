## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. I'm your host, and this is your daily pass through CNS pharmacology, psychiatric and neurology drug discovery, and the computational machinery closing in on both. Eleven items today, and the thread is the endogenous brake. Over and over on this week's pile, the interesting molecule is one the body already makes or one the protein already carries — a circulating antibody that behaves like a subtype-selective antagonist, a lipid pathway an analgesic needs rather than mimics, a T cell population doing the work five receptor antagonists were being credited for, and a GTPase domain restraining the kinase next to it. Same design question each time: add the ligand, or release the constraint.

We open with Molecular Psychiatry, out today, and the most interesting NMDA receptor pharmacology I've read in a while — except nobody dosed anything. The premise is that roughly five to ten percent of people carry natural anti-NMDAR1 autoantibodies by conventional cell-based assay, and that those antibodies have been reported as resilience factors in Alzheimer's disease, in schizophrenia, and for depressive symptoms, while the same target in the IgG isotype gives you anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis. This group built a quantitative luciferase-based immunoassay instead of a positive-or-negative cell assay, so what they measure is level, not serostatus, and the distribution turns out to be wide.

They took it to the Marine Resiliency Study II: one thousand and twenty-five male infantry Marines, plasma drawn a month before a seven-month combat deployment, symptoms rated before and four to six months after. Six hundred and six had a lifetime brain injury by clinical interview, eighty-six percent mild. First, the antibody levels are stable — pre- to post-deployment correlation of point eight three over ten to twelve months — so this is a trait, not a response to the injury.

Then the interaction. Antibody level did nothing in Marines with no head injury. In the injured group, higher levels predicted lower post-deployment depression scores, with an interaction p of point zero zero two and a within-group p of eight times ten to the minus five. Split at the top quartile versus the bottom three quarters, the high group's odds of moderate-to-severe depression symptoms after deployment were an odds ratio of point one four inside the injured group. Posttraumatic stress ran the same direction but weaker: the interaction held, the continuous within-group estimate was borderline at p equals point zero five one, and the categorical comparison was clean. On symptom clusters it hit numbing and hyperarousal but not avoidance. Anxiety showed nothing. And the high-antibody group endorsed significantly less psychotropic medication use after deployment, with no difference before.

Here is why this matters beyond a resilience biomarker. Naturally circulating autoantibodies in humans are predominantly IgM, and IgM is physically too large to enter the synaptic cleft. So the proposed pharmacology is an antagonist that reaches extrasynaptic NMDA receptors and spares synaptic ones — which is precisely the selectivity the extrasynaptic hypothesis of ketamine's antidepressant action has been asking for, except tonic and persisting for over a year rather than for hours. The same epitope in IgG, small enough to get into the cleft, impairs working memory in immunized mice and drives encephalitis. Same target, same epitope, opposite clinical sign, and the discriminating variable is molecular size.

Now the honest accounting. Isotype was not measured here, which is the hole in the middle of that argument. The effect sizes are small in absolute terms — about four points of clinician-rated PTSD severity and about two on the Beck Depression Inventory — and the authors' comparison of two Beck points to an SSRI effect is generous. Screened healthy all-male cohort with floor effects, retrospective self-reported injury history, and a top quartile that is a statistical cut rather than a demonstrated biological threshold. What I'd want next is the isotype breakdown in a symptomatic cohort.

Staying on ionotropic glutamate receptors and moving into pain, Neuron has a paper that reassigns a neurotransmitter's sign. Widespread mechanical pain that survives systemic opioids is a real clinical category with no mechanism attached to it. This group in Shenzhen identify a descending projection from glycine-transporter-two-positive neurons in the rostral ventromedial medulla to somatostatin-positive neurons in the spinal dorsal horn, and show it is both necessary and sufficient for widespread morphine-resistant mechanical allodynia in mice, recruited in craniofacial pain through trigeminal and limbic inputs.

The mechanism is the part worth remembering. Glycine released from those medullary terminals activates GluN3A-containing NMDA receptors on the spinal target neurons, and those receptors are functioning as excitatory glycine receptors — glycine-gated, glutamate-independent, and excitatory in a system where glycine is the canonical inhibitory transmitter of the spinal cord. That signaling bypasses opioid suppression entirely, which is a mechanistic account of why morphine fails here rather than a pharmacokinetic excuse.

Set against the medullary mu receptor work we covered yesterday, the descending story is getting genuinely interesting: same hub, and the opioid lever it is supposed to offer turns out to be either transient or routed around. The obvious therapeutic read is GluN3A, and the obvious problem is that there is no selective GluN3A pharmacology near the clinic — unusual receptors, low calcium permeability, magnesium-insensitive, and nobody has drugged them on purpose.

Which brings us to a preprint that treats endogenous inhibition as the object of study rather than the confound, and it is the most careful receptor pharmacology in today's set. The model is repetitive restraint stress in mice: facial and hindpaw mechanical hypersensitivity that resolves and leaves latent sensitization behind, a primed state in which subthreshold triggers — sodium nitroprusside, bright light stress, prostaglandin E-two — re-establish allodynia. The standard way to demonstrate latency is to precipitate it with naloxone and conclude endogenous opioids were masking it. This group instead walked the pathways systematically with brain-penetrating and peripherally restricted antagonists, and found a division of labor in time.

In the first three weeks after stress, peripheral GABA type A and type B receptor signaling suppresses the latent cephalic state, endocannabinoids act through central but not peripheral CB1, and peripheral kappa — along with delta, notably not mu — holds chronic head pain down. Beyond three weeks central opioid signaling takes over. Blocking any single one reinstates allodynia. Separately, depleting regulatory T cells prolonged facial but not hindpaw sensitization, a selectivity nobody had shown.

Then the intervention: low-dose interleukin-two, to expand and activate those regulatory T cells. After treatment, neither subthreshold triggers nor blockade of opioid, GABA type A, GABA type B, CB1, or CB2 signaling would reinstate allodynia in either sex. The sensitization was not being masked harder — it was gone. Mechanistically it runs through regulatory T cells recruiting three peripheral cytokines, transforming growth factor beta one, interleukin-ten, and interferon gamma, and neutralizing any one of the three abolishes the protection. It works when given late, after chronic sensitization is established. And in a head-to-head, a chimeric anti-CGRP antibody prevented re-sensitization by nitroprusside but not by bright light or bicuculline, while low-dose interleukin-two blocked all triggers.

The framing I'd take is that "latent sensitization" as a construct has been measuring the brake rather than the disease, and that a treatment which removes the substrate looks categorically different from one reinforcing a single brake. The authors also draw the right practical inference: since brain-penetrating opioids and barbiturates drive medication-overuse headache, the peripherally restricted agonists and allosteric modulators at kappa, GABA and cannabinoid receptors are the ones worth testing as prophylaxis — and difelikefalin, the peripherally acting kappa agonist already approved for uremic pruritus, cross-reacts with mouse kappa and is sitting right there. Their stated caveats: cytokine sources unidentified, downstream targets uncertain, one stress model, reflexive endpoints.

A short companion item from Neuropharmacology, from Andrea Hohmann's group at Indiana, on a drug two billion people have taken without anyone agreeing on how it works. Acetaminophen suppressed mechanical hypersensitivity in post-surgical and inflammatory pain models in mice, and here is the pharmacology: two structurally unrelated diacylglycerol lipase inhibitors blocked the analgesia, and so did the monoacylglycerol lipase inhibitor J-Z-L one eighty-four. Blocking synthesis and blocking degradation of the same endocannabinoid both abolish the effect, which says the drug needs lipid flux, not a raised level. The global CB1 antagonists rimonabant and A-M two-fifty-one prevented analgesia; the peripherally restricted A-M six-five-four-five did not, putting it centrally. Broader lipidomics found acetaminophen raised corticosterone more than two-fold, cut prostaglandins more than five-fold in brain and paw skin, and shifted up to thirty-nine percent of the signaling lipids screened. The standing story has been the metabolite A-M four-oh-four at TRPV1 and CB1; this pushes the entry point upstream to diacylglycerol-lipase-dependent two-AG production. That corticosterone rise is a confound I'd want controlled first.

Moving from pain to cognition, a preprint from Benno Roozendaal's group at Radboud with Lars Schwabe, on how stress hormones break inference. Mice learned two sets of auditory-visual associations, then one visual cue was paired with reward, and inference was probed by whether they sought reward to the auditory cues alone. Controls were selective. Corticosterone before test made them seek reward to both cues — and note the shape of that failure. They still responded, and they still went for the previously rewarded visual cue, so this is not a retrieval deficit; it is a loss of precision in the inference itself.

The mechanism has two dependencies. Corticosterone raised noradrenergic activity, yohimbine reproduced the deficit, and propranolol prevented it — so the glucocorticoid effect requires concurrent beta-adrenergic signaling, the same coincidence logic that keeps propranolol in reconsolidation trials. At the tissue level, corticosterone reduced CA3 pyramidal c-fos while raising c-fos in GABAergic cells of CA3 stratum radiatum, with increased phosphorylation of the GABA type A receptor beta-three subunit, and intrahippocampal bicuculline prevented the behavioral impairment. The lesion is over-inhibition of CA3, which is where pattern completion lives — and when they split animals by performance rather than by treatment, the CA3 reduction tracked accuracy rather than treatment assignment. That is the analysis that makes the circuit claim credible. It is still c-fos rather than physiology, and bicuculline in hippocampus is a blunt instrument.

Stay with depression treatment, because there is a medRxiv pilot that directly addresses the deployment problem in everything we said yesterday about Stanford Neuromodulation Therapy. Targeting matters — response tracks how close the coil is to the dorsolateral prefrontal region most anticorrelated with subgenual cingulate — and finding that region per patient normally takes individual resting-state functional MRI. This Columbia group instead used structural MRI alone, registered the Human Connectome Project multimodal parcellation to each patient's reconstructed cortical surface, and targeted anatomy: the peak anticorrelated region falls at the junction of parcels forty-six, nine-forty-six-d and a-nine-forty-six-v.

Fifteen patients with treatment-resistant depression, standard ten-hertz rTMS, twenty-seven sessions over six weeks, open label. In the eleven targeted at the junction, mean depression score fell fifty-eight percent, with response in eight of eleven and remission in six of eleven. The four targeted instead at the middle of parcel forty-six got thirty-eight percent, one responder, no remitters.

Two things to hold. The good news is real: if a structural scan plus an atlas gets you where individualized functional imaging gets you, the main barrier to connectivity-guided rTMS in ordinary clinics disappears. The odd news is the biomarker, which ran opposite to the targeting rationale — remitters were the patients with near-zero prefrontal-to-subgenual anticorrelation at baseline, not the strongly anticorrelated ones. With eleven patients and reported areas under the curve of point eight eight for response and one point zero for remission, that is overfitting, and eleven-versus-four is a post-hoc contrast being asked to demonstrate millimetre sensitivity. Feasibility result and hypothesis.

To psychedelics, and Translational Psychiatry has a paper aimed at the field's central medicinal chemistry problem: separating the plasticity from the trip. The approach is phosphoproteomic. Chemically diverse serotonergic psychedelics produce a coordinated reorganization of phosphorylation across many proteins in neural cells, and inside that global response is a signature separating hallucinogenic compounds from structurally close non-hallucinogenic analogues. As biological follow-up they take phosphorylation of FOXK2, a transcription factor regulating glycolysis, and show the hallucinogenic compounds but not their non-hallucinogenic counterparts increase lactate accumulation.

If that holds, it is a cell-based surrogate for a property currently assayed behaviorally by head twitch in rodents or indirectly through biased-agonism panels — and everyone designing a non-hallucinogenic serotonin two-A ligand needs one. The authors are appropriately modest, framing it as separable signaling architectures under defined in vitro conditions and releasing the dataset as a resource, which is the right claim level. A phosphosite signature in cultured cells is a long way from a behavioral prediction in a human.

A pharmacogenomics item, also in Translational Psychiatry, and a rare one because it is a drug-response GWAS rather than a disease GWAS. The Swiss PsyMetab cohort: eight hundred and twenty-nine patients with cholesterol fractions and triglycerides measured before and up to a year after starting a psychotropic. Seven loci reached genome-wide significance for treatment-induced lipid change, and they are drug-specific — three for total cholesterol on aripiprazole, on quetiapine, and on a pooled quetiapine-olanzapine-clozapine group; two for HDL, on lithium and on mirtazapine; one for triglycerides on risperidone; one drug-agnostic. There is no replication cohort, which the authors acknowledge, substituting sensitivity analyses across post-treatment intervals; the mirtazapine HDL variant held most consistently.

Take the individual loci as leads — eight hundred patients split by drug is thin, and the field is littered with unreplicated pharmacogenomic hits. But the direction is right and underserved. Almost everything actionable in psychiatric pharmacogenomics today is a cytochrome metabolizer allele that predicts exposure rather than outcome, and metabolic side effects are among the leading reasons antipsychotics get stopped. A per-drug predictor of who gains the lipids is a bigger clinical lever than another efficacy signature.

Two computational items to close, starting with a nice one from Kevan Shokat's lab at UCSF on LRRK2 — and I flag up front that this is a short preprint that reports groundwork, not a drug. LRRK2 variants are the commonest cause of autosomal dominant Parkinson's disease and the prevalent ones raise kinase activity, so the kinase domain has been the target for a decade, and clinical progress has been held up by on-target peripheral toxicity from strong kinase suppression. LRRK2 is one of only two human proteins carrying a Roc-COR GTPase domain in tandem with a kinase domain, and that GTPase intramolecularly regulates the kinase output. So they sensitized LRRK2 chemical-genetically to existing GTPase inhibitors and showed that engaging the Roc-COR domain in cells decreases, but does not fully inhibit, LRRK2-driven phosphorylation of Rab10 at threonine seventy-three.

Partial is the point. When your therapeutic window closes because full target suppression is toxic, a mechanism whose ceiling is partial inhibition is a feature rather than a disappointment. And the lineage is worth naming: this is the group that built the analog-sensitive bump-and-hole approach for kinases and later the switch-two pocket work on KRAS, so a sensitized allele plus a repurposed inhibitor on a GTPase is them running their own playbook on a new domain. What it does not tell you is whether a real ligand for the wild-type Roc-COR pocket is findable, which is the whole question.

Finishing with generative chemistry, and a preprint called OmniSyn that makes an architectural argument I think is right even though the validation is thin. The dominant paradigm in target-aware molecular design conditions on a three-dimensional pocket and treats synthesizability as a filter applied afterward. OmniSyn is a protein-sequence-conditioned mixture-of-experts language model with a synthesis-action decoder, so every molecule it generates arrives with an explicit synthesis trace, and de novo generation, synthesizability projection and hit-to-lead optimization are one model with shared representations and reinforcement-learned expert routing.

On a real-world drug discovery benchmark with unseen targets it beats a panel of structure-based generators on target awareness and active-molecule rediscovery without ever seeing a pocket, and independent retrosynthesis scoring gave sixty-eight percent success for de novo molecules and seventy-two percent for hit-to-lead — sixty-one and one hundred eighty-four percent better than the strongest baselines. They then ran it across more than twenty-one thousand human proteins and released two point seven billion target-specific molecules, each with a synthesis trace.

The genuine insight is twofold. Target relevance and makeability become a joint optimization instead of a sequential filter, so the model only explores chemistry it knows how to build. And the sequence-conditioning result argues that predicted structure is one lossy encoding of sequence constraints with its own errors to propagate — a real problem, if it replicates, for the assumption that structure-based design is the ceiling.

Two things temper it. Retrosynthetic success as judged by a retrosynthesis planner is one model grading another, and the authors concede a valid trace says nothing about conditions, yields or purification. More importantly there is no wet lab anywhere in this paper — nothing synthesized, no affinity measured, so a two-point-seven-billion-molecule library with zero measured actives is a hypothesis generator of unknown hit rate. And their own stated limitation is the one I'd lead with: accessible chemical space is bounded by the reaction vocabulary and building-block inventory, which is precisely the constraint that makes the retrosynthesis numbers look good.

Two short pieces to end on, both about what the machines should be pointed at. Drug Discovery Today has a review arguing for what it calls strategic recovery, distinct from repurposing and from rescue: clinical-stage assets shelved for portfolio and business reasons rather than scientific failure. The claims are that non-scientific terminations are a rising share of late-stage kills, that the patent cliff is forcing pipeline refills faster than de novo discovery can supply, and that the largest untapped reservoir sits in China, where trial registries are poorly indexed against Western ontologies. The proposed job for agentic AI is multilingual trial-corpus mining. Nothing is built, so this is an opinion piece — but the framing is worth stealing. A shelved asset is a de-risked clinical-stage molecule whose bottleneck is bibliographic rather than chemical, which makes it a far more tractable target for a language-model agent than designing a new molecule, and essentially nobody is funding it.

And as the counterweight, Clinical Pharmacology and Therapeutics has published a cautionary guide for machine learning in medical research, which I mention because of where it appeared rather than what it says. Seven pitfalls: data drift when coding systems or clinical practice change, features that learn operational proxies instead of biology, accidental leakage that inflates performance and then collapses on deployment, competing risks and differential time-at-risk manufacturing spurious safety associations, collider bias in health-record studies, metrics that overstate performance on rare outcomes, and pooled multi-trial analyses that extract study artifacts rather than signal. None of it is news to a statistician. All of it is news to a lot of people currently shipping models — and it now sits in the journal read by the clinical pharmacologists who will be asked to sign off on them.

That's the show. If there's one thing to carry out of today, it's that on four separate occasions this week the useful pharmacology turned out to be a constraint the body was already imposing — and that the drug's job was to be redundant with it, not to imitate it. Back tomorrow.
