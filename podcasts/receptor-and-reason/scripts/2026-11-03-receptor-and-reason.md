## Script

Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Tuesday, November third, twenty twenty-six.

Today's theme is repetition as an instrument. Nearly everything on the list is about what you can only learn by running the same thing more than once — in more than one laboratory, with more than one trigger compound, in more than one species, or along more than one internal path to the same printed answer. The uncomfortable part is that most of our publishing and most of our software reports exactly one attempt, and a single attempt cannot tell you which kind of result you are holding.

The lead is the most consequential paper in psychedelic neuroscience this year, and it is a negative result. Five laboratories in the Bay Area — Stanford, two at Berkeley, two at UCSF — formed a consortium they call the Psychedelic Bay Area Animal Neuroscience Consortium, and ran the same psilocybin behavioral battery in parallel, pre-aligned on dose, strain, age and both sexes. Two milligrams per kilogram, intraperitoneal, in C57 black six mice, ten to fourteen weeks old. The dose was chosen deliberately: high enough to produce head twitch responses, low enough to avoid gross motor confounds, and in the range that yields receptor occupancy comparable to clinically relevant human doses.

What replicated is worth stating first, because it is real. Head twitch went up in every lab, with a modest sex difference — females twitched more — and a time course concentrated in the first ten minutes. Acutely, psilocybin was anxiogenic, not anxiolytic, in both the open field and the elevated plus maze. Acutely, it reduced novel object exploration. And acutely, it reduced fear expression in every single lab, which the authors flag as the most robust and mechanistically interesting positive finding in the paper, and the one that maps most cleanly onto a plausible human use — suppressing fear during exposure therapy.

Then the persistent effects, measured one to four days out, which is where essentially the entire therapeutic literature lives. Nothing held. The tail suspension test: no lab, pooled or z-scored, showed an antidepressant-like effect. The repeated forced swim design, the one the psychedelics field has been using specifically because it is supposed to be sensitive to this — one lab out of five saw an effect on day three, and that lab had the lowest inter-rater agreement on scored video, zero point four seven against a range of zero point five six to zero point eight seven elsewhere. They then switched to a genuine stress model, twenty-one days of corticosterone in drinking water, deliberately chosen to minimize experimenter-driven variance. Sucrose preference did improve after psilocybin in two of five labs and in the pooled data, but the authors take it apart themselves: the improvement was driven by reduced water consumption, not increased sucrose intake. In the forced swim arm of that same experiment, three labs saw a corticosterone effect, one saw none, and one saw it in the opposite direction.

And then the critical period. The claim that psychedelics reopen a critical period for social reward learning in adult mice has been one of the most influential ideas in the field. This consortium reproduced the critical period itself — juveniles showed social place preference, adults did not, with effect magnitudes essentially identical to the original report — and then gave adults psilocybin four days before conditioning and saw nothing. No lab observed social reward learning in adults after either saline or psilocybin.

The part that lifts this from a null result to a methods paper is the last section. They asked why. Simulations show that for effects the size of the ones they measured, the probability of replication never exceeds sixty percent even at forty animals per group, a hundred sixty mice per experiment. They then modeled every methodological variable they could — housing, handling, timing, apparatus — and found that method factors did influence mouse behavior, but no method-by-treatment interaction influenced psilocybin's effect, and labs that differed more in methods did not differ more in behavior. So the usual explanation, that it is all down to local laboratory idiosyncrasy, is not supported by their own data. Which leaves the arithmetic: across roughly one hundred independent behavioral experiments at an alpha of zero point oh-five, they observed six isolated non-replicable positives. That is almost exactly the false positive rate. I would not read this as "psilocybin does nothing" — the acute effects are robust and the authors are careful about dose, assay and time window as unexplored dimensions. I would read it as the field having built a mechanistic program on effect sizes that a single laboratory cannot reliably detect, and that we have been reading single detections as discoveries.

Staying with psychedelics and moving down to subcellular geography, there is a nice piece of chemical neurobiology from the Haggarty lab at Mass General and the Center for the Neuroscience of Psychedelics. The question is endogenous dimethyltryptamine. We have known since Axelrod's work in the nineteen sixties that mammalian tissue can N-methylate tryptamine, and indolethylamine N-methyltransferase — INMT — is the candidate enzyme, but it has never been characterized at the protein level in the human central nervous system. Transcript-level work has been equivocal partly because the message is scarce in bulk tissue.

They validated an INMT antibody and did immunocytochemistry in human iPSC-derived neurons, and the enzyme localizes in close proximity to the cytosolic face of the cis-Golgi network — alongside aromatic L-amino acid decarboxylase, the upstream enzyme that makes tryptamine in the first place. And the part that makes this more than an anatomy result: both DMT-like immunoreactivity and 5-HT2A receptor immunoreactivity partially localize to the same compartment. The hypothesis they are building is specific and testable. There is a known intracellular pool of 5-HT2A receptors at the Golgi, and activating that pool produces structural plasticity signaling distinct from plasma membrane signaling. Exogenous psychedelics are lipophilic enough to reach it; serotonin is not, which has been one of the cleaner explanations for why psychedelics do something serotonin does not. If the enzyme that makes a membrane-permeable endogenous tryptamine sits on the cytosolic face of the very organelle that holds the intracellular receptor, you have a candidate endogenous ligand for a compartment we previously thought only drugs could reach. Caveats: this is proximity by immunofluorescence in a stem-cell-derived system, not catalysis measured in situ, and detecting endogenous DMT in brain remains at the edge of mass spectrometry. But as a hypothesis-generating localization result with validated reagents attached, it is the kind of thing that moves a field.

Third, and a direct follow-on to a paper I covered last Friday. The Falk group at Arizona previously showed that sub-anesthetic ketamine reduces levodopa-induced dyskinesia with a long-lasting effect, and that it works through striatal Trk-B signaling. Last week's companion paper asked whether ketamine's neuroprotective effect in a Parkinson's model also runs through BDNF, and found it does not. This week's asks which dopamine receptor pathway the anti-dyskinetic effect runs through.

The design is pharmacological dissection. Unilateral 6-hydroxydopamine lesion, prime with levodopa to establish dyskinesia, then switch the trigger to a receptor-selective agonist: quinpirole for D2-like, SKF-81297 for D1-like. Score limb, axial and orolingual abnormal involuntary movements, then give ketamine at twenty milligrams per kilogram five times over ten hours, and re-challenge after washout. Against quinpirole, ketamine suppressed dyskinesia on treatment day and the suppression persisted through day four and day seven. Against the D1-like agonist, nothing — not acutely, not after washout. The conclusion offered is that ketamine's anti-dyskinetic action works on indirect-pathway, D2-mediated mechanisms, which is genuinely interesting because the field's default framing puts levodopa-induced dyskinesia on the direct pathway.

Two things to hold. First, the quinpirole statistics are perfect separation — Mann-Whitney U of zero with nine animals per group on all three days — and an F for treatment above five hundred in the time course. Separation that complete in an ordinal behavioral rating, with no blinding described, at a ketamine dose repeated five times, should make you ask how much of the suppression is anti-dyskinetic and how much is ketamine doing what ketamine does to movement generally. The D1 arm is the internal control against that worry, and it is a good one, but it only works if the two experiments are comparable. And that is the second thing: these are two separate cohorts, two different agonists, two different routes — the D2 agonist intraperitoneal, the D1 agonist subcutaneous — with independently established baselines. The dissociation that carries the paper was never tested within a single design. Male rats only, nine per group.

To the clinic, and to the epigenetics of antidepressant response. A German group ran an epigenome-wide study across eight weeks of escitalopram at a fixed twenty milligrams a day, with blood methylation at baseline, day fourteen, day twenty-eight and day fifty-six, in roughly a hundred thirty patients split by response plus controls. Two questions: can baseline methylation predict who responds, and does methylation move with symptoms?

The prediction answer is a clean null. No differentially methylated position reached epigenome-wide significance for predicting symptom severity at either day twenty-eight or day fifty-six. The regional analysis turned up a handful of differentially methylated regions, strongest at a gene called TNRC6C, but that is the weaker, lower-resolution test. The longitudinal answer is one site. A single position, annotated to YLPM1, tracked symptom change at p of one point two five times ten to the minus eight, surviving correction, with methylation and depression scores falling together over treatment. A regional signal at GPR126 appeared at both day twenty-eight and day fifty-six, which is the better evidence in the paper because it repeats within the study. A sensitivity analysis restricted to the seventy-nine percent who stayed on escitalopram through week four gave concordant estimates, which is the right check to run and they ran it. Read this as an honest, well-executed study whose main contribution is telling you the effect size: whole-blood methylation is not going to give you a baseline predictor of SSRI response at this sample size, and the dynamic signal is one CpG out of hundreds of thousands.

Related in spirit, a lipidomics-plus-machine-learning study in schizophrenia from the same journal. Paired cerebrospinal fluid and serum from a hundred fifty-six people: fifty-one healthy volunteers, forty-nine antipsychotic-naive patients, fifty-six treated patients. They measured endocannabinoids — anandamide, two-A-G, and the related N-acylethanolamines — plus ceramides and sphingomyelins, and fed them into a gradient-boosting classifier with SHAP attribution. The discrimination numbers are strong: area under the curve of zero point eight seven for naive patients in both fluids, and zero point nine four in cerebrospinal fluid for treated patients.

That last number is the one to think about. The best-performing model is the one trained on the group taking olanzapine, clozapine, quetiapine, risperidone and five other antipsychotics — drugs whose defining off-target liability is remodeling exactly the lipid classes being measured. The authors are upfront that per-drug subgroup analysis was impossible at two to twelve patients per medication, and they note nobody was on a statin. But a classifier that separates treated schizophrenia from healthy controls on sphingolipids at zero point nine four is, on the face of it, at least as good a detector of antipsychotic exposure as of illness. The naive cohort at zero point eight seven is the scientifically interesting number, and the fatty-acyl-chain-dependent sphingolipid changes they report as exclusive to the treated group look like the medication signal declaring itself. There is no external validation cohort.

One more from the same journal, on prediction rather than mechanism. Using nearly ten thousand children in the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development study, a group built an environmental risk score for adolescent depression from a literature review of candidate factors, trained with elastic net and cross-validation, and compared it head to head against polygenic scores from a recent trans-ancestry genome-wide study. The environmental score kept twelve predictors, explained twelve point three percent of variance in symptoms, and classified major depressive disorder at two-year follow-up at an area under the curve of zero point six nine for parent-reported and zero point six seven for youth-reported outcomes — beating the polygenic score's zero point six four. Combining them was best, at zero point seven one and zero point seven zero. The top predictors are parental depression, witnessing domestic violence, sleep duration and dieting. Two of those four are modifiable, which is the authors' point. I would note the two that are not — parental depression is carrying both genetic and environmental signal, which makes the clean comparison between the two score types less clean than it looks — and that this is one cohort, one country, no external validation.

Now to the agentic AI side, where three papers this week converge on the same uncomfortable observation: the output an agent prints is a poor summary of what the agent actually did.

The sharpest is a framework called CONFIRM out of the TReNDS Center, aimed at autonomous neuroimaging. The premise is that current agentic systems verify that the code ran and that reported numbers match the code's output, and then hand the decision about whether a finding is worth reporting to a language model acting as judge. The authors show that those judges endorse site-confounded findings even when the confound is handed to them as evidence — in multi-site imaging, where group differences and scanner differences are routinely tangled, that is not an exotic failure. CONFIRM separates running the analysis from deciding to report it: the model writes a machine-readable, pre-specified analysis plan that fixes the estimand, the covariates, the cohorts and the search family before any result exists, and then the result passes through five deterministic gates — search history and multiplicity, confounds, power, analytic stability, and replication. They built a benchmark of four hundred eighty-nine executable neuroimaging claims on frozen data, a hundred fifty of which are controls engineered to violate one methodological requirement each. CONFIRM falsely supports at most three percent of the controls against twenty-three to thirty-eight percent for the baselines, while recovering up to sixty-one percent of literature-supported claims. Bolted onto an existing agent as an auditor, it takes false support on controls from twenty-three percent to zero while keeping that agent's real findings. The honest framing is that this is pre-registration implemented as software, enforced by a parser rather than a conscience, and the recovery rate tells you the price: you will lose a substantial share of true findings to the gates. That is the trade pre-registration has always offered.

Second, a study that asks whether an agent's cited evidence is as stable as its verdict. The setup is clever: inject silent numerical corruption of the kind that faulty GPUs actually produce — finite, plausible values that pass every not-a-number check — into a tool result, and see what changes. Across eight batches of a scientific claim-verification benchmark, two small open models showed repeatable changes in which documents they read and which sentences they cited, while the verdict stayed the same and stayed correct. In the illustrative case, both the clean and the corrupted run return the benchmark-correct answer about a reactive-oxygen-species mechanism, and neither cites the annotated supporting sentence; one cites something about autophagy and the other something about microbe size. Grading on the verdict scores both as right. The applied half is better still: in a hERG cardiac-safety assay allocation task — hERG being the potassium channel whose blockade is the standard cardiotoxicity flag — silent faults changed which compounds got tested while the fixed-budget shortlist kept the same number labeled as blockers, so the headline count was invariant and thirty-nine individual decisions were not. Restoring the agent's earlier execution state and supplying certified scores recovered all thirty-nine; just replacing the prediction file recovered none, because the agent had already conditioned on the bad numbers. This is a workshop paper on small models and should be read as a demonstration rather than a survey. But the lesson generalizes: if you audit a scientific agent only on its conclusions, you are measuring the one quantity that is most robust to the failure you care about.

Third, the privacy version of the same idea, from Ajou University and Oxford. The HIPAA minimum necessary standard says you retrieve patient identifiers only when the task requires them. An agent can retrieve identifiers and never print them — silent access, invisible to any evaluation that reads only the output. They embedded canary tokens in synthetic patient records and linked every tool invocation to whether the canary surfaced in the answer, across five models, four policy-boundary levels and seven adversarial framings, on clinical tasks that need no identifiers at all. Three of five models invoked the protected-health-information tools unnecessarily; two did not. Stronger boundary language reduced it, and what survived the strictest policy was confined to frames where the user asserted override or claimed prior approval — which is to say, the easiest thing in the world for a real clinician to say in a hurry. A pre-execution gate given an explicit task-necessity criterion nearly eliminated it. The conclusion is modest and correct: output inspection cannot establish minimum-necessary access, and you need boundaries, gates and access-log audits together.

Two shorter items to finish. First, a drug-response prediction method out of Tsinghua Shenzhen called GRAFT, which rests on a nice biological premise: within a given cellular context, genetic and chemical perturbations engage overlapping regulatory networks, so you can use the genetic perturbation responses measured in a target cell type to transfer drug-response predictions into it without ever dosing that cell type with the drug. They report improvements over competing methods on response correlation, directional agreement and magnitude, concentrated — as you would hope — in drugs whose responses are context-dependent, and they ship an atlas of a hundred two thousand predicted drug-by-context combinations across thirteen cellular contexts and nearly eight thousand catalog compounds. The claim to watch when this gets scrutinized is whether the atlas recovers pharmacology that was not already implicit in the source contexts.

And for the closer, a paper that is the day's theme in miniature. A group at Swarthmore has been developing planarians as a high-throughput organismal model for organophosphate neurotoxicity, with an image-analysis pipeline that quantifies seizure-like activity. The practical motivation is real: midazolam stops organophosphate-induced seizures but has to be given within minutes, which makes it impractical for mass-casualty exposure, and finding later-window antiseizure drugs in mammals is slow and expensive. So they took four antiseizure medications — midazolam, topiramate, MK-801 and carbamazepine — and asked whether they rescue seizure-like activity induced by the pesticide parathion, by pentylenetetrazole, or by NMDA, in two planarian species.

All four drugs reduced parathion-induced activity in one species. And then it comes apart. Midazolam did nothing against parathion in the other species. Carbamazepine increased seizure-like activity against pentylenetetrazole. Topiramate produced frank paralysis at high concentrations in one species and abnormal morphology in the other, which is why they measured translational movement alongside seizure events — otherwise a paralyzed animal reads as a rescued one. Worm-to-worm variability within a single plate was large enough to limit power. Some results contradict the published literature outright. The useful conclusion is not that planarians fail; it is that the answer a screen gives you depends on which species and which proconvulsant you happened to pick, and a platform that reports one combination is reporting a draw, not a result.

Which is where we started. Run it twice, in two places, with two triggers, and you find out what you had.
