## Script

Good morning. This is Receptor and Reason for Saturday, November twenty-eighth. Twelve items today, and we open with a paper that turns a single phosphorylation site on the serotonin transporter into a sex-specific social phenotype — and then rescues it with a drug you can buy in any pharmacy.

The Blakely lab, now at Florida Atlantic, has been arguing for years that the serotonin transporter is not a static clearance pump but a regulated one, and that protein kinase G acting on threonine two seventy-six in the second intracellular loop is the switch that raises transport capacity on demand. What they've done now is ask what happens to an animal that can't throw that switch. The knock-in mice carry alanine at that position, so the transporter is otherwise normal but cannot be phosphorylated there. These are not transporter knockouts; expression and baseline function survive. What dies is the regulated increase.

The behavioral result splits by sex, and splits cleanly. Mutant females lose social preference and lose sustained interaction with juveniles. Mutant males keep their sociability entirely but back down in the tube dominance test — they retreat where wild-type males hold their ground. Biotinylation in striatal slices shows why the lesion is real: kinase activation raises surface transporter in wild-type tissue of both sexes and does nothing in the mutants. Fiber photometry in nucleus accumbens then localizes the behavioral consequence with some precision — extracellular serotonin rises during social interaction in mutant females on first encounter just as in controls, but the response on repeated interactions is gone. It's the sustaining of the signal that fails, not its initiation.

The mechanism they land on is the one I'd have bet against, and it's the most interesting part. Patch recordings from dorsal raphe show that serotonin neurons from mutant females are less excitable — and blocking 5-HT1A autoreceptors restores that excitability, which means the mutants are sitting under tonic autoreceptor inhibition. The transporter defect, in other words, has raised somatodendritic serotonin enough to clamp the cells that make it. So the prediction is that a 5-HT1A partial agonist should help the mutants and hurt the controls, and that's exactly what buspirone does. In wild-type females, buspirone abolishes social preference — standard autoreceptor agonism in an animal with efficient somatodendritic clearance. In mutant females, it restores social preference, restores interaction bouts, and raises accumbens serotonin responses. In mutant males, it restores dominance. One drug, opposite signs by genotype.

I want to flag the caveat that the restoration is partial — bout number comes back in mutant females but total active interaction time does not, which suggests buspirone is reinstating the initiation of social approach rather than the maintenance of it. Still, this is the cleanest demonstration I've seen that transporter regulation, as distinct from transporter abundance, is a behaviorally load-bearing variable. And it reframes what an autoreceptor-targeting adjunct is doing: not tuning synaptic serotonin directly, but relieving an inhibitory brake whose strength depends on how well the cell body region clears its own transmitter.

Staying with pharmacology that changes meaning depending on the state of the animal — a paper in Nature Neuroscience from the Benchenane group at ESPCI in Paris identifies a physiological state I don't think had a name before, and then shows a benzodiazepine works by promoting it.

Mice learn to avoid a shock arm in a two-arm maze. During conditioning they go immobile in two distinct ways, and breathing rate separates them completely: fast-breathing immobility near the threat, which is ordinary freezing, and slow-breathing immobility — two to four hertz, low and variable heart rate — in the safe arm. The slow-breathing state is not sleep and not quiet wakefulness; it has its own physiological signature, and the amount of it an animal produces predicts how stressed that animal is afterward, measured by thigmotaxis, heart rate, insomnia and REM proportion the following night.

Then the causal half. Hippocampal sharp-wave ripples occur during the slow-breathing state and reactivate representations of the aversive location — the animal is sitting in the safe zone replaying the dangerous one. Closed-loop disruption of those ripples reduces the slow-breathing state and increases post-task stress. And diazepam, at a dose that is anxiolytic without sedation in the elevated plus maze, increases the duration of the slow-breathing state specifically, leaves fast-breathing freezing untouched, and lowers the stress score.

Here's the part I'd put in front of anyone working on anxiolytics. Diazepam promotes the recovery state while suppressing sharp-wave ripples in every behavioral state measured. So the drug does not work through the ripple pathway — it appears to bypass it, inducing the endpoint directly without the mnemonic processing that normally produces it. If ripple-dependent reactivation is how the animal learns where safety is, a benzodiazepine gives you the physiological recovery while skipping the learning. That is a mechanistically specific version of a clinical worry people have had about benzodiazepines and exposure-based therapy for decades, and it's now a testable one.

Third, a selectivity paper that deserves wide reading because of how much published work rests on the assumption it overturns. In eLife, a group shows that Yoda1 — the small molecule used across the field as the selective PIEZO1 agonist — also agonizes PIEZO2.

The reason the field believed otherwise is historical and specific. Yoda1 was characterized as PIEZO1-selective because it fails to evoke spontaneous calcium influx in PIEZO2-expressing cells and barely slows inactivation of poking currents. Nobody tested stretch currents, largely because PIEZO2 was thought insensitive to membrane stretch until recently. When you do test them, Yoda1 increases PIEZO2 single-channel open probability and stretch sensitivity about as efficaciously as it does for PIEZO1. The efficacy profile differs across readouts — spontaneous calcium entry only appears under non-physiological conditions, osmotic stress or actin destabilization — but the agonism is real.

Two things fall out of this. The useful one: Dooku1, the Yoda analog with no effect on PIEZO1, does modulate PIEZO2 inactivation, which makes it a starting point for a genuinely PIEZO2-selective agonist — and PIEZO2 is the one that matters for mechanical allodynia and proprioception. The uncomfortable one: a decade of in vivo and in cellulo work attributing effects to PIEZO1 on the strength of Yoda1 now has a confound, particularly in tissues expressing both channels and particularly where endogenous mechanical force was present. The structural story is clean — the more potent analog Yoda2's benzoic acid group forms a transient salt bridge with a conserved arginine in the binding site of both channels, which is why it reaches nanomolar potency — but the practical message is that tool-compound selectivity established on one assay readout is not selectivity.

Now to psilocybin, where someone has finally asked what the entropy measurements actually measure. A group at Penn and Maryland took fifty-seven paired scans from the PsiConnect cohort and quantified brain entropy both across space within a frame and across time within a voxel. Both increase after psilocybin, alongside reduced local synchrony and shorter intrinsic timescales. That much replicates what the field has been reporting since the LSD entropy papers.

The contribution is the surrogate analysis. They generated synthetic data preserving specific statistical properties and asked which property is sufficient to reproduce the effect. Surrogates retaining only the zero-lag covariance between voxels — destroying the original temporal order entirely — reproduce the spatial entropy increase. Surrogates preserving each voxel's power spectrum while independently randomizing phase reproduce the temporal entropy increase. And a distance-only model, which throws away pair-specific correlations and replaces them with a fitted function of how far apart two voxels are, recovers ninety-seven percent of the spatial effect.

Read that carefully, because it's deflationary in a specific way. The increased spatial irregularity under psilocybin is largely explainable by a change in the spatial autocorrelation structure — how similar nearby voxels are to each other — not by anything requiring a network-level or repertoire-level interpretation. The temporal increase is explained by weaker serial dependence. Entropy is not a free-standing measure of richness of conscious content; it is a downstream readout of coordination and persistence, and this paper tells you which one each estimator is tracking. Anyone citing brain entropy as an index of the psychedelic state should now specify which entropy and against which surrogate.

Fifth, dopamine and the structure of a cognitive computation, from Narayanan's group at Iowa. The task is interval timing — estimate several seconds — and the striatal signature is medium spiny neurons that ramp up or ramp down across the interval. They recorded ensembles in dorsomedial striatum alongside dopamine release with a fluorescent sensor, and the asymmetry they find is the result: dopamine dynamics predict the slope of down-ramping ensembles specifically, not up-ramping ones, and that relationship does not appear in Pavlovian tasks with no temporal demand. Inactivating substantia nigra dopamine then disrupts timing and disrupts down-ramping neurons while leaving up-ramping neurons intact.

The translational step is what makes it an episode item rather than a circuit curiosity. They fit a drift-diffusion model in which only the down-ramping component is perturbed, and that single perturbation predicts the timing behavior both of dopamine-inhibited mice and of humans with Parkinson's disease. That is a quantitative bridge from a cell-type-resolved manipulation in rodents to a cognitive deficit in patients, through a model with one perturbed parameter. If it holds, down-ramping dynamics become a candidate marker for the cognitive, as opposed to motor, consequences of dopamine loss — which is the part of Parkinson's with the fewest treatments.

Next, a transcription factor arriving in schizophrenia from the immune side. Aiolos, encoded by Ikzf3, is an Ikaros-family zinc finger protein characterized almost entirely in lymphocytes. A group has now mapped its brain expression — region- and stage-dependent, settling into principal neurons in adulthood — and found it upregulated both in peripheral blood mononuclear cells and in post-mortem hippocampus from people with schizophrenia. The specificity check matters: in major depressive disorder, Aiolos is unaltered in both compartments.

Modeling that dysregulation in mice produces behavioral phenotypes spanning positive-, negative- and cognitive-symptom domains, and hippocampal Aiolos elevation raises complement protein abundance with microglia-associated loss of synaptic material. So the proposed mechanism is complement-mediated synaptic elimination — the same axis the C4 schizophrenia genetics pointed at — reached from a new upstream regulator. I'd hold the causal claim loosely; the human arm is correlational and small, and a transcription factor that broad will have many targets. But the convergence on complement from an independent direction is the kind of thing worth tracking, and Aiolos has the unusual property of being druggable through the molecular glue chemistry developed in myeloma.

Seventh, a psychiatric-genetics paper in Molecular Psychiatry that is really an argument about whether the additive model is hiding subtypes. The method is coordinated epistasis, extended here from quantitative to binary traits. The idea is elegant: if risk alleles come in synergistic sets that each drive a distinct subtype, then polygenic scores built from random partitions of the genome will show an interaction whose sign tells you whether you've sampled within a set or across sets. You don't need to know what the sets contain.

Applied to five disorders in the Danish iPSYCH cohorts, polygenic scores are underpowered to detect this, but family genetic risk scores — liability estimated from the diagnoses of relatives — are not. Those detect significant negative interactions in ADHD, autism and schizophrenia, meaning multiple distinct sets, meaning genetically distinguishable subtypes within each disorder. The cross-disorder tests then find that synergistic sets do not span disorders: comorbidity liability is not an additive blend of two disorders' risk, and comorbid cases are genetically enriched for one disorder or the other. For bipolar and depression specifically, that gives genetic support for the clinical convention that someone meeting both criteria is classified as bipolar.

The caveat the authors state plainly is the one to carry: nearly all the positive findings come from family-based scores, which capture familial environment and assortative mating alongside genetics. They argue those contributions are small and the test is robust to them. For a field that has spent a decade reading high genetic correlations between disorders as evidence that diagnostic boundaries are arbitrary, this is a direct and well-constructed counterargument.

Eighth, a negative result on an accelerated neuromodulation protocol. Stanford Neuromodulation Therapy delivers prolonged intermittent theta burst stimulation and achieves real antidepressant efficacy, but nobody knows what a single session does acutely. A randomized sham-controlled crossover in forty healthy men, with EEG recorded between stimulation trains and TMS-evoked potentials before and after, gives a mixed answer. Left frontal theta power does follow a different trajectory under active stimulation — suppressed in the first two blocks, then rising significantly into the third — which is a genuine online signature. But the direct comparison of pre-to-post changes in TMS-evoked potentials found no significant clusters in any of the standard component windows, and neither working memory nor sustained attention moved.

I read this as a useful calibration of expectations. The cortical-reactivity measures that the field hoped would serve as a single-session biomarker for an accelerated protocol did not separate active from sham in healthy volunteers. That doesn't impugn the clinical efficacy — healthy brains aren't depressed brains and one session isn't fifty — but it does mean the inter-train theta trajectory, not the evoked potential, is the thing to chase if you want an early-response marker.

Ninth, pharmacogenomics with a methodological sting. A pharmacometrics preprint asks what interindividual variability structure does to the prior in a Bayesian population model, using a codeine-to-morphine model with CYP2D6 built in, re-estimated four ways across nearly a thousand patients. Allowing variability to differ by metabolizer phenotype rather than pooling it improves fit substantially under both continuous activity-score and categorical parameterizations. But the variance estimates for poor metabolizers come out wildly different depending on which parameterization you chose — and shrinkage exceeds ninety percent in poor and ultrarapid metabolizers, meaning the data in those groups are barely informing the individual estimates at all.

The consequence is concrete: in controlled simulations, phenotype-specific variability roughly triples the coefficient of variation of predicted morphine formation clearance in poor metabolizers under one parameterization and raises it about forty percent under the other. Same data, same drug, same gene — different implied prior for the group the genotyping was supposed to protect. The authors are careful to say better fit does not establish biological validity, and that's the line to remember. Model-informed precision dosing inherits whatever variability structure the modeler assumed, and for the rarest phenotypes that assumption is doing most of the work.

Tenth, and pairing with that, a phased-haplotype survey of tuberculosis-treatment pharmacogenomics across African populations. Six hundred sixty-one high-coverage genomes from five continental-African and two diaspora populations, examined at NAT2, SLCO1B1, CES2, HLA-B, NR1I2 and UGT1A1 — the loci governing isoniazid and rifampicin disposition. NAT2 acetylator status was inferrable for about ninety-four percent of individuals, and core-allele matching was essentially complete.

The finding is in the background. Within the same named NAT2 star allele — the same call a clinical panel would return — the surrounding non-core haplotype structure differs significantly between African populations. The allele definitions that pharmacogenetic practice treats as the unit of inference are sitting on genuinely different genomic backgrounds depending on where the sample came from. That doesn't invalidate the call, but it means regulatory variation and linkage structure outside the defining sites vary in ways no current panel captures. Pair it with the previous item and you have the same worry from two directions: our genotype-to-phenotype mappings are more population- and model-dependent than the clinical tooling admits.

Eleventh, something for the computational side. A preprint asks a blunt question about single-cell foundation models: do they need hundreds of dimensions to represent one expression count? The answer, in a controlled transformer where you can separate the value pathway from the gene-identity backbone, is no. A four-dimensional value track retains nearly all of the masked-prediction gain, and replacing the learned value curve with a straight line costs little in transfer.

More pointed: clamping the values of detected genes — reducing expression to little more than detected-or-not — retains most of the gain on cell-type annotation and surface-protein prediction. Which says the models are largely learning from gene detection, not from expression magnitude, a result that makes sense given how many detected transcripts in droplet data carry a count of one. They also show that gene offsets plus three linear cell-level scalars approximate what masked prediction is computing, with sequencing depth leading in the controlled model and cell type leading in scGPT. And their interpretability controls demonstrate that attribution, attention and probe readouts can all reflect sequencing depth or gene frequency rather than anything biological. The lesson generalizes beyond single cell: if your explanation method hasn't been run against a channel-uninformative control, you don't know what it's reading.

And to close, a small paper about a coding convention that quietly inverts a national statistic. The federal treatment episode data set records drug use at discharge, and the specification says that when the discharge substance is "None," the frequency field is not applicable. In the public-use files from 2018 onward, every such record has its frequency coded as missing or unknown — so a documented report of no drug use becomes indistinguishable from a record with no information.

These records are twelve and a half percent of discharges in 2024, and two states, Massachusetts and New Jersey, contribute over eighty percent of them because their discharge forms use "None" to mean exactly what it says. Drop them, as a researcher defining use from the frequency field would, and New Jersey ranks forty-ninth of fifty-one jurisdictions on no-use-at-discharge at under seven percent. Count them as no use, and it rises to fifty-two percent and eighteenth. One published national analysis excluded ninety-four percent of New Jersey's completed episodes, almost all of them these records.

I include it because addiction-treatment outcome research runs on this file, and the gap between the six-point-eight percent and the fifty-two percent is not a measurement question or an effect size — it's a decision about one field in a codebook. Worth knowing which side of it any abstinence figure you read was computed on.
