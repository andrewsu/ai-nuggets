## Script

Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Tuesday, November eleventh. Today's episode has a thread running through it, and it's one of the oldest problems in our business: almost nothing we measure is the thing we actually care about. We measure a receptor we built in a cell line, not the one in a patient's cortex. We measure a prescribed dose, not a plasma concentration. We measure whether a model can recite a guideline, not whether it can use one. Most of the time the stand-in is fine. Today's papers are, nearly all of them, careful accountings of exactly when it stops being fine.

Start with a piece of ion channel pharmacology that makes the point better than any editorial could. The lurcher mutation is a classic — it swaps the third alanine in the highly conserved SYTANLAAF gating motif for a threonine, and it has been characterized across delta, NMDA, kainate and AMPA receptors for twenty-five years. It is famous for producing an overactive channel. What's changed recently is that it stopped being a curiosity: lurcher has turned up as a recurrent mutation site in GRIA1 and GRIA3 in patients with neurodevelopmental disorders, which makes its pharmacology a clinical question rather than a biophysical one.

A new preprint works through the mechanism and, crucially, the subunit dependence. In homomers, the effect is enormous. Put lurcher into GluA2 homomers and deactivation slows roughly fifty-fold, desensitization slows about seven-fold, and desensitization is essentially abolished. Schild analysis says the mutation works by changing gating efficacy — the efficiency with which glutamate binding opens the channel — rather than by changing affinity for glutamate, which is a cleaner mechanistic answer than the field has had. And the effect size is subunit-specific: in GluA1 homomers the desensitization block is about fifty percent, where in GluA2 it is near total.

Now the part that matters. Receptors in brain are not homomers. The authors built the physiologically plausible complex — two mutant GluA1 subunits with two wild-type GluA2 — and everything shrinks. Then they ask the question a clinician would ask, which is whether perampanel still works. Perampanel is the one widely licensed AMPA receptor drug, a non-competitive antagonist, and its binding site sits near but not on the lurcher residue. In GluA2 homomers, the mutation shifts the perampanel IC-fifty from about point four nine micromolar to fifty-two micromolar — roughly a hundred-fold loss of potency. If you stopped there you would write a paper saying perampanel is useless in these patients. In the heteromer, the shift is two-and-a-half-fold. And when you add stargazin, the TARP that actually accompanies these receptors in brain, the IC-fifty is point two micromolar for wild type and point four six for the mutant — a roughly two-fold shift, with the mutant still in the submicromolar range.

So the homomer didn't lie, exactly. It reported a real property of a receptor that doesn't exist in anyone's head. The honest caveat the authors raise themselves is that stargazin is not the only auxiliary subunit — most GluA1/GluA2 heteromers in rodent brain run with gamma-eight and CNIH2, and CNIH2 is known to reduce perampanel potency. So the answer isn't "perampanel is fine," it's "the auxiliary subunit, not the mutation, is the variable that sets the drug's potency," which is a much more useful thing to know and a much harder experiment to have done.

Staying on excitability and on anti-seizure drugs, there's a modeling preprint that does something I liked a lot: it attacks the word "hyperexcitability" itself. The field uses it across epilepsy, Alzheimer's, ALS and chronic pain without a shared mechanistic definition, and the authors point out that it quietly conflates two different questions. Whether a quiescent neuron will activate is one question. Whether a neuron that just fired will fire again is a completely different one — and within a single neuron, successive spikes take different post-spike trajectories, with after-depolarization allowing an early return to firing and depolarization block prolonging silence.

Their move is to make this measurable with the firing hazard, and in whole-cell recordings from midbrain dopamine neurons the subthreshold voltage trough resolves a non-monotone aggregate hazard into physiologically distinguishable components. They then model these as competing return pathways in a population renewal framework. One is an immediate-return pathway that skips stages of recovery and drives population hyperexcitability without any change in activation from quiescence. The other is a sequestration pathway that diverts recently active neurons into a prolonged non-recruitable state — which is depolarization block, or use-dependent blockade.

And here's the payoff, which is pharmacological. The selectivity of phenytoin and carbamazepine — the long-standing puzzle of why sodium channel blockers suppress pathological firing while sparing normal activity — falls out of this as redistribution among return pathways at each renewal. Fast-cycling pathological regimes simply offer more opportunities for renewal, and therefore more opportunities for eventual sequestration. The drug isn't selective for a pathological neuron; it's selective for a pathological rate. That's a mechanistic restatement of use-dependence, and it means the aggregate firing rate you measure is a proxy that can hide the thing the drug is actually acting on.

Third item, and it's a drug-repurposing story with an unusually sharp lesson. A Drosophila preprint on C9orf72 hexanucleotide repeat expansion — still the most common genetic cause of ALS and frontotemporal dementia — looks at the autophagy-lysosome pathway and finds something specific: lysosomal and V-ATPase genes are transcriptionally suppressed at a presymptomatic stage, while autophagosome formation is largely preserved. So this is not "autophagy is broken," it's "degradative capacity is broken while the upstream machinery keeps running."

What that produces is counterintuitive. Genetically inhibiting the residual lysosomal degradation made toxicity worse, as you'd expect. But knocking down autophagy genes, and particularly secretory autophagy genes, suppressed toxicity. In vitro, disabling lysosome function — not inducing autophagy — increased neuronal release of dipeptide repeat proteins, and those released DPRs were taken up by neighboring neurons and glia in vivo. The pathway meant to clear the toxic species, when its endpoint is blocked, starts exporting them instead.

The pharmacology is where it gets pointed. Valproic acid, given at one millimolar throughout development, markedly reduced retinal degeneration and rescued motor phenotypes — but only when supplementation started at the presymptomatic stage. Treat at the symptomatic stage and the improvement was not statistically significant. The effect required Mitf, the fly ortholog of TFEB, which drives lysosomal biogenesis. Now, valproate has already been through two ALS trials in broadly recruited cohorts without clear efficacy. This preprint's implicit argument is that those trials tested the right drug in the wrong window and the wrong population — the mechanism is a genotype-specific, early event, and by the time patients enroll, Mitf trafficking may be too compromised for the drug to have a substrate to act on. I'd hold that lightly; it's flies, and "our mechanism explains why your trial failed" is the most seductive sentence in translational biology. But the stage-dependence within the model is real data, not a hand-wave.

Switching to systems neuroscience, and the stand-in here is social rather than molecular. A new Nature paper asks whether learning about aggression by watching produces the same thing as learning by fighting. The framing goes back to Bandura's Bobo doll work — observation can shape behaviors we think of as part of an innate repertoire — and the modern version is that aggression mirror responses have been reported in one hypothalamic population, the estrogen- and progesterone-receptor-expressing VMHvl neurons.

What this group did is record across the broader social behavior network — the conserved subcortical hypothalamic, amygdalar and midbrain circuitry — during both direct experience and observation, and then test both groups in a novel aggression context. Both experienced and observer mice showed the same behavioral signature: more initiated attacks, shorter latency to attack, more time in aggression-preparatory postures, and less time in distant inattentive states where they'd be vulnerable. The authors read that as a shared "be ready" strategy, and the network-level change that accompanies it is a relaxation of inhibition across the core hypothalamic aggression circuit that persists into the new context.

Two details keep this honest. First, the equivalence is not complete — behaviors involving direct social contact changed more after direct experience than after observation, so observation substitutes for experience in some dimensions and not others. Second, the dopamine result complicates the obvious interpretation. Nucleus accumbens dopamine responded vicariously to observed actions and distinguished the aggressor's behavior from the subordinate's, and the response during a simulated attack was substantially reduced when the subordinate was absent. So the observer isn't simply adopting the winner's point of view. The authors argue for something closer to goal emulation across both participants' outcomes, which is a more interesting claim than mirroring and a harder one to pin down. The habenula signal, meanwhile, was indifferent to what was observed and tracked familiarity with the individual instead.

Now to a paper that is entirely about a stand-in, and knows it. In schizophrenia research we routinely compress antipsychotic treatment into one number, and the available summaries disagree with each other — chlorpromazine equivalents, the WHO defined daily dose, and percentage of licensed maximum will rank the same patient differently. That matters most for clozapine, where the dose-to-concentration relationship varies several-fold with sex and smoking, through CYP1A2.

A new index called Antipsychotic Load 2026 expresses each daily dose as a percentage of a reference dose and sums without a cap, and the interesting part is that the clozapine reference is individualized for sex and cigarettes per day rather than being dose-proportional. They tested it against the thing the number is supposed to stand for — plasma clozapine, across more than thirteen hundred assessments in two hundred fifty-five patients. The individualized reference tracked plasma concentration better than any dose-proportional measure: marginal R-squared of point two five versus point one seven, a difference of about point zero eight with a confidence interval from point zero four to point one two. And the scheme with the larger smoking correction fitted better.

I want to be precise about what that is and isn't. Point two five is not a lot of variance explained; most of what determines a patient's plasma clozapine is still not in the formula. But the comparison is the point — the index that accounts for sex and smoking beats the one that doesn't, and it was validated against a biomarker rather than against expert consensus. The authors are commendably clear that only the clozapine term has that validation; the reference for other antipsychotics is, in their own words, a calibrated convention. They also found that percentage of licensed maximum was the least consistent of five candidates with external criteria, which is worth knowing given how often it's used.

Sixth, and this one is a caution. A postmortem proteomics study of prefrontal cortex in borderline personality disorder — thirty-six cases, forty-three controls, label-free proteomics — reports dysregulation of mitochondrial, immunological and neuroplasticity pathways, and a co-expression module enriched for secretory and synaptic processes. It then runs druggability and drug-repositioning analyses and concludes that the module is druggable with SSRIs, and separately nominates VEGF signaling as a pharmacologic target.

Read the methods and the SSRI claim dissolves. It comes from a database annotation on SRP19 — a signal recognition particle protein — being listed as an SSRI interactor. That is a drug-gene interaction database artifact, not pharmacology, and to their credit the authors themselves note that SSRIs are often prescribed in borderline personality disorder and show limited effectiveness. So the paper contains both the claim and its own refutation. The VEGF and neuropilin signal is more consistent — it shows up in both the protein-protein interaction subnetwork analysis and the signature-based repositioning — but "appears in two analyses run over the same differentially expressed proteins" is not independent replication. The part I'd actually keep is the convergence result: integrating the borderline signatures with PTSD and major depression datasets showed strong cross-disorder overlap, with STX7 and RTN4 conserved across them. That's a real finding about shared molecular pathology, and it does not require any of the repurposing scaffolding.

Seventh, and this connects directly to yesterday's closer on resting-state fMRI foundation models. A preprint called ASD Neuro Agentic puts language model agents to work on the autism fMRI classification problem, and the design choice is the item. The agents propose features — drawing on the literature and on researcher input — but every calculation is performed by fixed tools, with leakage control enforced by the framework rather than by the model. The authors are explicit that the field's reported accuracies are inflated by data leakage, so the architecture is built around not trusting the agent with the measurement.

Across a thousand and twenty-eight participants from seventeen ABIDE sites, the system tested seventy-one pipelines and settled on a ComBat-harmonized ensemble of tangent-space connectivity models across six parcellations. Leave-one-center-out AUC was point eight one four, beating the ASD-DiagNet and ASD-SAENet baselines. Point eight one four is not a diagnostic test, and nobody should pretend otherwise. But set it against yesterday's result — nine fMRI foundation models adding nothing over baseline clinical measures — and the pairing is informative: the gains here came from disciplined search over classical pipelines plus honest cross-site validation, not from representation learning. The agent's contribution is auditable search, which is a modest and genuinely useful role.

Eighth, the cleanest statement of today's thread, from a Chinese-language clinical benchmark called Med Evidence Bench. Fifteen hundred thirty-six physician-constructed items across three levels — guideline knowledge, standardized clinical scenarios, and real-world laboratory scenarios — and the design feature is that every scoring criterion is linked to a versioned span of source text. The metric, the evidence trace score, is the proportion of available rubric points earned, with any safety-critical error zeroing the item.

The numbers do the work. Across ten systems, including two medicine-specific ones, the evidence trace score on guideline knowledge ran from point nine seven five to point nine nine seven. On standardized scenarios it fell to between point two nine and point six six. On real-world scenarios, point one eight to point four five. And decomposing the real-world items by stage shows exactly where it goes: identifying the relevant clinical information scored point seven seven on average, clinical calculation point three nine, matching the evidence point one six five, and formulating a recommendation point one six. Critical-error detection stayed high, around point nine five five, so these aren't models behaving dangerously — they're models that can find the facts and recite the guideline and then cannot connect the two to this patient.

Rankings also reordered across levels, which is the practical warning. A model that tops a guideline-recall benchmark is not thereby the best model for point-of-care use, and the field has been inferring one from the other. The authors are appropriately restrained, noting these are Chinese-language materials and that nothing here establishes clinical effectiveness.

Ninth, the same confusion one level of abstraction up, in Nature Machine Intelligence. Compositional cell foundation models reuse frozen unimodal encoders and combine them through a learned interface, which is the sensible response to paired multimodal data being scarce while unimodal data is abundant. The question these authors pose is when fusion genuinely adds information beyond the strongest single modality, and when it just repackages redundancy.

Their theoretical claim is a spectral ceiling. In the frozen-encoder regime, with linear probes doing the evaluation, standard alignment objectives — canonical correlation analysis, contrastive losses under whitening constraints — reduce to maximizing linear cross-covariance. They are therefore biased toward recovering the linear redundancy already present in the pretrained experts, and have limited capacity to expose synergistic information that isn't linearly expressible once aligned. They benchmark ten fusion interfaces over matched histopathology and spatial transcriptomics patches with frozen UNI-2 and Nicheformer encoders, so any difference is attributable to the interface, and they introduce a synergy diagnostic to separate the two regimes. The conclusion is that strong benchmark performance can arise while the model is effectively relying on one dominant modality. The scope limit is stated clearly — this is about frozen backbones and linear evaluation, not end-to-end CLIP-style training — and alignment remains the right objective for retrieval. It's the word "integration" that's been doing unearned work.

Tenth, a benchmark that exists because the ground truth moved. TrialDesignBench takes on statistical design selection for early-phase trials — which is, as the authors note, a step with almost no AI progress despite being the step that determines how doses get tested in humans. The problem is that you cannot learn it from the historical record, because the FDA's Project Optimus redefined what counts as a reasonable design and rendered much of the back catalogue obsolete. So the overwhelming majority of recorded trials can't serve as training data or ground truth.

Their answer is six hundred seventeen Phase One and One-Two oncology dose-finding trials, each paired with an expert-validated reference design across eleven design method classes, generated by formalizing the expert design process under current guidance. Five frontier models, none matching the reference. The ablations are the useful part: supplying the latent design determinants that aren't recorded in the trial source raises class-wise balanced accuracy by point two two, and imposing structured decision-tree reasoning adds point two four. So the failure splits roughly evenly between not inferring unstated context and not reasoning in the structured way an expert does — two different fixes, one of which is a data problem and one of which is a scaffolding problem.

And to close, something light that is itself a small lesson in proxies. DrugCoding is a scientific programming agent for drug discovery out of a Shanghai startup lab — task planning, adaptive model routing, multi-agent collaboration, code generation across molecular design, property prediction and virtual screening. The headline is an average session-level pass-at-one of ninety-seven point three five percent on their benchmark, beating the strongest baseline by about nine points, and ninety-seven point nine on what they call the challenging subset.

When the challenging subset scores higher than the full benchmark, and the number is ninety-seven either way, what you're looking at is a measurement of whether the tool-calling plumbing works, not of whether the science is any good. Pass-at-one on tool invocation is a perfectly reasonable engineering metric. It is not a drug discovery result, and the gap between those two things is the whole subject of today's episode. That's the thread: every one of these papers is about a number that was standing in for something else, and the good ones are the ones that went and checked.
