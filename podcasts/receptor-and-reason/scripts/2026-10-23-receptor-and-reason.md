# Receptor & Reason — 2026-10-23

## Script

Good morning. Today's episode has a theme that runs through almost every item, and it's a slightly uncomfortable one: the instrument is in the answer. Not as a caveat at the end, but as the thing doing the work. A blocker that isn't selective. A model that had to be rebuilt before the effect it was testing could exist. A reliability metric that a personality paragraph maxes out. In each case the finding is real in the sense that it reproduces — and the reason it reproduces is the apparatus, not the biology.

We start with the sharpest version, from the Kavli Institute in Trondheim, and it's a genuine piece of pharmacological archaeology. Hippocampal Cajal-Retzius cells are the transient, reelin-expressing cells of the early postnatal hippocampus, and for about twenty years the settled fact about them has been that they receive an exclusively GABAergic drive — local inhibitory input in, glutamate out onto pyramidal cells, and glutamate going the other way does nothing to them. This preprint does monosynaptic rabies tracing from a Cajal-Retzius-specific intersectional line and finds that wrong at the anatomy level: presynaptic cells in at least seven extrahippocampal regions, medial entorhinal cortex the largest pool, contralateral input only from layer three. Then the physiology confirms the paradox rather than resolving it — twelve and a half micromolar gabazine abolishes a hundred percent of spontaneous events, at minus sixty millivolts and at plus forty, at postnatal day fourteen and at day four. No glutamatergic current anywhere.

The good part is what the authors do with that. They point out that gabazine at that concentration, and bicuculline above thirty micromolar — the doses the entire Cajal-Retzius literature has used — also antagonize glycine receptors, and specifically the alpha-two and beta subunit receptors that these cells are reported to express exclusively. So "complete block by a GABA-A antagonist" is evidence about chloride conductances, not about GABA, and a twenty-year consensus may be a readout of a reagent's selectivity profile. Their own positive proposal — that these long-range terminals signal through co-packaged glycine — is much weaker than the demolition. Three cells for strychnine, four for taurine, no washout, no time control, no glutamate-receptor antagonists ever applied, and a caesium chloride internal that puts the chloride reversal at plus one millivolt — which makes GABA-A and glycine currents indistinguishable by reversal potential in the very recording meant to distinguish them. The anatomical apposition evidence is one animal and, in their phrasing, more than ten receptors within three hundred nanometres, imaged with a z-step of three hundred and seventy nanometres — larger than the criterion being adjudicated. They also call raphe and septal inputs glutamatergic because they were GAD-sixty-seven negative, while showing in the same figures that those cells are serotonergic and cholinergic. Take the confound, leave the mechanism.

Staying with the idea that the tool decides the finding, there's a medRxiv preprint from the Mahidol-Oxford unit on rosuvastatin dosing with ticagrelor in elderly patients carrying the ABCG2 four-twenty-one C-to-A variant. The pharmacology is genuinely worth knowing. Ticagrelor inhibits BCRP; the variant already cripples BCRP; so the drug interaction has less room to act in the carrier than in the wild type. Run it forward and you get an inversion of the pharmacogenomic reflex — under ticagrelor, it's the wild-type patient who needs the bigger dose cut, not the variant carrier, because the inhibitor has converted everyone to the poor-function phenotype and the only person whose exposure changes is the one who wasn't already there. The authors draw the CYP2C19 analogue themselves: fluvoxamine roughly quadruples lansoprazole exposure in extensive metabolizers and barely moves poor metabolizers. That principle generalizes straight into psychopharmacology, and it's the part to keep.

The part not to keep is the dose table, and the reason is instrumental. The published model they started from couldn't produce the genotype effect at all — dialling BCRP activity down a hundredfold moved rosuvastatin peak concentration by about ten percent, against a clinically observed hundred-and-thirty-one percent increase. So they raised BCRP turnover roughly eighteenfold and moved its Michaelis constant from two to about seventeen micromolar, until the transporter was load-bearing enough for a genotype knob to matter. Everything downstream follows from that choice by construction. The elderly model, the genotype model and the interaction itself each rest on a single clinical study arm, and the Methods contain the phrase "further optimized and verified" — same data, same clause — which the Discussion later upgrades into an independent check proving the model isn't overfit. Two of their four dose recommendations miss their own prespecified bioequivalence window.

That same question — is the result a property of the patient or of the analysis — gets its cleanest demonstration in a medRxiv preprint from Tongji University called ORIGIN-PD, a whole-blood transcriptomic classifier for Parkinson's disease. It's a thirty-five-input penalized logistic regression on PPMI baseline RNA-seq, three hundred and seventy participants, and it reports a locked-test-set area under the curve of zero-point-nine-four-four. Two things about that number. First, the only genuine external validation, in an independent public cohort, is zero-point-six-six-seven with a confidence interval running from zero-point-four-five to zero-point-eight-eight — it contains chance — and sensitivity of one in three. That number is not in the abstract. Second, and more instructive: the locked test set is not locked, and the admission is in a figure legend. The Methods say it was used exclusively for final evaluation. Figure one's legend says it was not used for coefficient estimation or threshold fitting, but that historical results from this cohort informed model-version selection. Three model versions were evaluated on seventy-four people and the best one shipped.

The deeper beat is the interpretability layer. The paper offers a molecular taxonomy — it sorts each patient's genes into eight hand-labeled modules and ranks which dominates. The final model differs from the previous version by one swap: HSPA8 out, NPM1 in. Those two genes correlate at a Spearman rho of zero-point-eight-four-five, and the swap changed the test AUC by exactly zero — the paired difference is zero-point-zero-zero-zero-zero. But HSPA8 was filed under proteostasis and NPM1 under dopaminergic neuron identity. So proteostasis went from appearing in the top five of sixty-three percent of patients to six percent, and in the final model it is a top-three contributor in zero of two hundred and fifty-five patients. The dopaminergic module's predominance, which is the paper's entire biological story, is a consequence of which folder one gene was dropped into. The authors say this in the results and then build the discussion on it anyway. In a linear model, a module score is a property of your annotation file, not of the patient. Worth adding that the largest single contributor by SHAP is a composite of three hundred and twenty-two low-abundance transcripts, half of them unassigned, which is explicitly excluded from the interpretation layer before any module is ranked.

Here's a smaller, sharper version of the same phenomenon, from a Yale biostatistics group. The method is called StochIKM and it reconstructs individual patient data from published Kaplan-Meier curves. The good idea is that it returns a set rather than a point — fifty admissible datasets, each reproducing the printed numbers at risk exactly and staying within one pixel column of the drawn curve — because the map from figure to data is not one-to-one, and the spread across that set is an honest estimate of what the figure left undetermined. In one trial the median varies by about four-tenths of a month across the fifty, which is exactly the quantity nobody currently propagates into a network meta-analysis.

The vignette that makes it worth your time is a failure of the incumbent method. In the fulvestrant-plus-anastrozole trial, the standard reconstruction places every event of an interval at the single drop where it became visible, which gives sixty-five distinct event times in one arm, one of them carrying twenty-four events. A step that large can take the survival curve across one half in a single jump, so the Brookmeyer-Crowley lower limit hits one half at the same event time, the interval collapses onto the median, and the published figure is drawn with no left whisker. A confidence interval with one end missing, manufactured entirely by a tie-placement convention. Spreading events within their own windows gives two hundred and sixty-four distinct times in the same arm and an interval with both ends. Be skeptical of the accuracy table, though — reproducing the risk table in every arm is constraint zero of the method, so that row scores the method against its own acceptance criterion, forty-two of ninety-seven arms needed the criterion relaxed, and nowhere in the paper is a hazard ratio recovered.

From there to multi-agent clinical reasoning, where a very well-designed study lands a direct hit on a metric several deployed systems rely on. A group from Columbia, Swansea and Aston simulated a seven-agent spine surgery department discussing twenty published cases of degenerative cervical myelopathy. The clever part is that the case reporting and risk assessment stages carried no persona and saw none of the later discussion, so they were generated once per case and replayed verbatim in all three hundred and sixty runs. The only thing that varies is a short prompt prefix on the chair and its deputy: neutral, consultative, autocratic, or avoidant. On byte-identical evidence, the share of runs ruling operate-now goes from fifty-seven percent under a neutral chair to ninety-three percent under an autocratic one and two percent under an avoidant one. Tests ordered per run swing from zero-point-seven-three to five-point-one.

Now the part that matters. Run-to-run agreement rises from fifty percent of cases to eighty-five and ninety — which looks like a reliability win, and several clinical decision-support papers have used exactly that measure, including one in Nature Medicine this year where consistency across runs was reported as the strongest signal of a correct diagnosis. But chance-correct it and Fleiss kappa does not rise; it falls, from zero-point-three-one to zero-point-two-zero. Observed agreement exceeds chance by about four points in the autocratic arm against twenty-five in the neutral one. The output distribution collapsed; the system did not become more reliable. One case is worth the whole paper: a seventy-five-year-old man with a hydrocephalus picture alongside dynamic cervical cord compression, where the neutral chair reached three different conclusions in three runs, and the autocratic chair chose the same fusion all three times without any of its rulings mentioning the hydrocephalus. In the published report the fusion was done, gait kept deteriorating, and a shunt was eventually placed. The caveats are real — twenty cases, no ground truth anywhere, and persona prefixes five times longer than the neutral one with no length-matched control — but the construct-validity hit stands.

Two more items on language models in clinical settings. A group at the University of Minnesota built ChronoEvo out of seventeen hundred verified recommendation-level revisions mined from successive versions of the same clinical practice guideline. The design choice that matters: the model gets the old recommendation plus evidence excerpts from the newer guideline. That is oracle retrieval — right document, right passage, handed over — so this is not a training-cutoff result. And the finding is a consistent gap between recognizing that a recommendation changed and actually carrying the change through. Their primary model recognizes the revision in ninety-one percent of cases and fully incorporates it in fifty-nine. The strongest frontier model tested recognizes it in ninety-eight percent and fully incorporates it in seventy-eight. Every model shows the gap; scaling within a family does not close it; the medically specialized models are not better and in two cases are worse.

The structure of the failure is the part that transfers. Adding a new recommendation is easy — superseded content survives in only three percent of those. Modifying an existing one is where stale guidance persists: an adjusted odds ratio of four-point-four for revisions that change strength or certainty, and around three-and-a-half for revisions that widen or narrow the eligible population. That is the exact shape of most psychiatric guideline churn — not a new drug, but this is now second-line, this no longer applies under twenty-five, monitor at a different interval. And the intervention they tried is instructive: explicitly telling the model to prioritize the updated evidence cut overt carryover of old text nearly in half and moved the recognition-to-incorporation gap by one-tenth of a point. Suppressing the old is not the same operation as propagating the new. Two honest caveats. Not fully incorporated is not the same as wrong — in the clinician-adjudicated sample, seventy-nine percent were clinically correct, so the actionable error rate is closer to twenty percent than fifty. And the primary model was asked to answer concisely, capped at two hundred and fifty-six tokens, and then scored against a completeness rubric — and the two strongest predictors of failure are recommendation length and old-new overlap, which is exactly what a length artifact would look like.

The companion item is from UC Irvine, and it's an unusually self-critical attempt to build a clinician-authored guardrail layer for mental-health chatbots — four checks compiled into Boolean predicates over sixteen typed facts, with a theorem prover verifying the rule base offline and no solver at inference. The result they intend is auditability: when the guardrail misbehaves, you can name the fact and the rule that did it. The result they deliver is a lesson about defaults. Any fact the extractor can't determine is filled in with its more cautious value — undetermined distress becomes high. On five hundred deliberately benign health prompts, that policy appends a referral line to three hundred and sixty-four of them, three hundred and fifty-nine purely because a fact couldn't be graded. Fifty-six pass untouched. And the authors write that the fail-closed rule was initially an implementation default rather than a clinician-authored choice, with no clinical rationale on record. They then put three options to their clinicians, with maximum caution as the default if no answer came back, and both clinicians kept it. The safety policy of the whole system is a programming convention that was never overruled.

Their comparison arm is worth sixty seconds on its own. The same model, same quantization, same rule text, run as a free-form judge, agrees with the compiled layer on thirty-five percent of two hundred and ninety-four conversations — and it is perfectly deterministic across repeats, so the disagreement is systematic, not noise. Of sixty-five replies that literally contain the digits nine-eight-eight or nine-one-one, the judge demands a crisis resource from fifty-five of them, while the same model, asked as a fact extractor, confirms a resource is present in fifty-four of those fifty-five. The paper's line is that no reading of the rule text licenses those verdicts. Caveats: two clinicians, both co-authors, kappa around zero-point-five on the check that drives most outcomes, synthetic single-turn conversations, no guard-model baseline, and psychosis isn't a category at all.

Briefly on a pharmacovigilance resource, because the negative result is the useful part. A Harvard-Mayo-Michigan consortium built a knowledge graph out of twenty-two million adverse-event reports, a hundred and fifty thousand publications, seventy-six thousand trial records and four million hospital allergy entries — more than two and a half million drug-adverse-event edges. Against three standard annotated corpora it recovers about a third of the relations; against the corpus built from FDA drug labels, four-point-eight percent. One in twenty of the adverse reactions already printed on the label. The confidence-scoring framework the abstract advertises for every edge is skipped for the hospital allergy-list edges on the grounds that physicians already validated them — ninety-six percent of the graph, and precisely the assumption the penicillin-allergy delabeling literature exists to refute. Their best statistical prioritizer reports an F-one of zero-point-eight-four with recall of ninety-nine-point-four percent — back out the precision and it's about zero-point-seven-three, against a pharmacist agreement base rate of seventy-three-point-two percent. A classifier that says yes to everything scores slightly higher. The resource isn't released anyway; what transfers is the arithmetic you should run on any prioritization score before believing it.

Two quick computational-biology items to close. The first is in Nature Biotechnology from the Melnick lab at Weill Cornell, a multimodal latent neural ODE called DynaVelo that learns one global vector field over single-cell multiome data — expression and transcription-factor motif accessibility as two coupled state variables — and because it has an explicit functional form, you can differentiate it for cell-state-specific regulatory edges and intervene on it to predict knockouts. The genuinely new thing here, and it is rare in this literature, is that they took the model's rescue nominations in an Arid1a-haploinsufficient germinal-center B cell system and built two entirely new double-heterozygous mouse lines to test them, and the dark-zone-to-light-zone ratio was restored in both. That is a real prospective test.

But read the Methods. The vector field is supervised on scVelo's velocities at a loss weight of ten thousand, on the few hundred genes scVelo will produce velocities for, then extrapolated to several thousand genes and two hundred motifs — so the standing identifiability critique isn't answered, it's inherited with a smoother surface. Motifs were selected by taking, for each transcription factor, the motif most correlated with that factor's RNA expression, and the paper then argues motif accessibility carries information beyond RNA. And the line that belongs in every methods seminar: they tested four values of the KL weight and found one thousand good for learning velocities and latent times, and one good for perturbation prediction. A thousand-fold swing — meaning the trajectory figures and the perturbation predictions do not come from the same fitted model. Their rescue rates in the most disrupted cell type are thirty-eight, sixty-four, fifty-one and forty-six percent, where the metric is a sign agreement and chance is fifty, with no permutation null anywhere.

Staying in that territory, there's a bioRxiv preprint from Oldenburg and the EMBL-EBI group describing hvantk, an agentic multi-omics framework for variant annotation. The architecture is the right-sized claim for this field right now. The language model writes and maintains the data-provider plugins — twenty-one public resources, schema-validated, with a scheduled job that fingerprints each upstream source and opens a draft pull request when it changes — and their sentence is that the analytic runtime itself remains deterministic and independent of large language models. The agent writes the pipes; it never touches the number. Annotation drift is real and deeply unglamorous, and this is a credible use of a coding agent. The evaluation of that half is thin: forty-five runs across three plugin tasks, scored by the authors' own unit tests, with no non-agentic baseline of any kind.

The science half gives us today's best deflation. They re-rank gene burden statistics in congenital heart disease, epilepsy and schizophrenia cohorts by adding multi-omics features, and they report their baseline honestly. The four published gene-prior scores — GeVIR, DOMINO, s-het and pHaplo — reach areas under the curve of zero-point-six-two to zero-point-six-seven individually, and zero-point-six-five in combination. Publication count alone reaches zero-point-six-six-three. Their words: how often a gene is mentioned in publications, a count that grows as genes are studied, ranks these genes about as well as the evaluated gene-prior scores. Their own gains over that baseline are real but shrink as the cohort gets bigger and more independent — the headline twenty-six-to-forty-nine-percent recovery is their in-house cohort, while the largest cohort gains three points — and the model does not transfer: every valid cross-cohort comparison underperformed the best published prior in the target cohort. To their credit, all of that is in the paper rather than hidden.

And finally, a short one that's really a lesson about splits. A bioRxiv preprint from Shanghai proposes an immune world model with multi-agent orchestration for peptide vaccine design — a ten-compartment ODE with twenty-nine unfitted priors in arbitrary units, and a multi-agent layer the Methods define as five role prompts executed sequentially in one session, with no comparison to not doing it. The useful datum is in their transfer table. A ranker given donor and adjuvant context scores zero-point-four-eight under donor holdout, beating bare HLA features at zero-point-four-zero. Hold out the source peptide instead and it collapses to zero-point-three-three — below the bare features, and below the off-the-shelf predictor it was meant to improve on. Same model, same data, two split definitions, and one of them is measuring memorization.

If there's one habit to take from today, it's to ask of any result what would have to be true of the apparatus for this to come out the other way — the antagonist's selectivity, the folder a gene was filed in, the convention for placing tied events, the default that fills an undetermined field. In every one of those cases the finding reproduced perfectly. That's the point. Reproducibility tells you the apparatus is stable; it doesn't tell you what the apparatus is measuring. Thanks for listening, and I'll see you tomorrow.
