## Script

Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Sunday, November fifteenth. Today's thread is averaging — specifically, what disappears into a mean. Several papers on the list report a group average that is flat, unremarkable, or exactly what you expected, and then show that the thing worth knowing was in the shape of the distribution, or in the timing, or in which cell type the effect lived in. In a couple of cases the average is not just uninformative, it points the wrong way.

The lead is a cross-species characterization of striatal dopamine across the lifespan, and it takes a swing at a story most of us have been repeating for twenty years. The standard account of adolescent risk-taking is that dopamine availability peaks in adolescence, before prefrontal inhibitory control catches up — a transient dopaminergic surge meeting an immature brake. The problem is that nobody had measured release capacity directly across a full lifespan in a primate. This group did, in macaques from two to seventeen years and in mice from three weeks to fifty-eight weeks, using electrically evoked dopamine transients in dorsal striatum.

The trajectory is an inverted U in both species, which is not surprising. What is surprising is where the top of the U sits. In macaques, release capacity peaks at an estimated seven point one years. In mice, at twenty-nine point six weeks. Normalize to average lifespan in captivity and those two numbers land at twenty-eight point five percent of the lifespan in both species — essentially identical, and the shapes are statistically indistinguishable. That is well past adolescence. Aligned to biological milestones rather than to chronological age, the macaque peak coincides with skeletal maturity and the mouse peak is later still. So dopamine release capacity keeps maturing into young adulthood, and the adolescent-dopamine-peak model, at least in its simple form, does not survive contact with a direct measurement. This actually agrees with human imaging work that has been quietly reporting a young-adult peak for several years.

Then they asked what builds the peak, and this is where the averaging theme shows up. Two obvious candidates: less GABAergic restraint, or more cholinergic facilitation. GABA-A and GABA-B antagonists potentiated evoked dopamine by about fifty-one percent in macaques and twelve percent in mice, and that potentiation got stronger with age in both species — so inhibition tightens as the animal matures, which works against the peak rather than creating it. For acetylcholine, blocking alpha-six-containing nicotinic receptors with alpha-conotoxin PIA suppressed release by roughly twenty-seven percent in both species, but the developmental pattern diverged: in mice it climbed steadily across the lifespan, while in macaques it was at its smallest during exactly the post-pubertal window where release was highest. So the cholinergic facilitation cannot be driving the primate peak either; if anything it is at its weakest there. The honest conclusion is that the maximum emerges from several regulatory processes pulling against each other, and that the species share a trajectory while disagreeing about the mechanism behind it. One more detail worth flagging for anyone who works on transporters: clearance got faster with age in macaques, from a decay constant of about point three-four seconds to point two-nine, which is the opposite of what declining dopamine transporter expression would predict. Function and expression are coming apart.

From dopamine terminals to the neurons they land on. A group working on TSHZ3, an autism-linked gene, has a conditional knockout restricted to cholinergic neurons, and those mice show repetitive behaviors with reduced firing of striatal cholinergic interneurons. The question here is what that does downstream, at the spiny projection neurons. Cholinergic transmission through the muscarinic M1 receptor onto those neurons is reduced, with no change in M1 expression or in the receptor's functionality — they checked with a selective positive allosteric modulator, VU-0357017, whose effect scales with basal receptor occupancy. So it is tone that is lost, not the receptor.

Here is the part that makes the paper. If you measure spiny projection neuron excitability as firing frequency to a depolarizing step, the mutants and the controls are the same. Identical means. But the distribution of those frequencies changes from bimodal to unimodal — it homogenizes. Going to triple transgenics to separate the D1-expressing from the D2-expressing population, the normal difference in excitability between the two pathways is simply gone. That is a real pathophysiology hiding inside an unchanged average, and it is mechanistically the right kind of lesion for repetitive behavior: the direct and indirect pathways select and suppress competing motor programs, and that requires them to be different from each other. Flatten the difference and selection degrades. The translational hook is that acute donepezil, an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor already approved in people, significantly alleviates the repetitive behaviors. Acute dosing in a mouse model is a long way from a clinical claim, and donepezil trials in autism have a mixed history, but the mechanistic case for putting cholinergic tone back is unusually clean here.

Staying with autism and with cell types, another group has a non-cell-autonomous route to parvalbumin interneuron dysfunction in the cntnap2 knockout. Their finding is that oligodendrocyte precursor differentiation is transiently impaired in early postnatal development, producing delayed myelination in somatosensory cortex and corpus callosum, altered myelin lipid composition, and sheaths that wrap poorly, alongside transient microgliosis and increased oligodendrocyte death. The oligodendrocyte transcriptome shows interferon and immune pathways up and GABAergic synaptic genes down, which points at disrupted communication between interneurons and precursor cells — a conversation that is known to exist but rarely shows up as a disease mechanism.

The consequence is selective: parvalbumin interneuron axons are hypomyelinated, parvalbumin cell maturation is impaired, excitation-inhibition balance shifts, gamma oscillations degrade. And early treatment with a promyelinating agent — either clemastine, the antihistamine repurposed as a differentiation driver, or pranlukast, a leukotriene receptor antagonist — restores precursor differentiation and myelination, rescues the parvalbumin phenotype including PGC-1-alpha expression, normalizes the circuit measures, and improves social behavior. The word doing the most work is "early." This is a developmental window argument, and it says the interneuron deficit everyone has been treating as the primary lesion may be downstream of a glial one that closes on a schedule.

The averaging theme gets its most literal treatment in a study of Fragile X synapses. Most molecular work in this disease has used bulk homogenates or crude synaptic fractions, which by construction report an average molecular identity across synapse types. This group used fluorescence-activated synaptosome sorting with both RNA sequencing and mass spectrometry to separate cortical excitatory from inhibitory synapses in Fmr1-deficient mice, quantifying roughly seven thousand transcripts and four thousand proteins per sample.

Only a minority of the dysregulated molecules are shared between the two synapse types. The substantial majority are specific to one — and, crucially, FMRP loss often produces opposing protein changes at excitatory versus inhibitory synapses. Average those together and you get a number near zero for exactly the molecules that matter most. They also find that messenger RNA and protein changes are poorly coordinated, consistent with heavy post-transcriptional control, and they implicate CAND1-dependent protein degradation as one node where the coordination breaks in a cell-type-distinct way. There is a dosage result too: comparing hemizygous males to heterozygous females, FMRP dosage is coupled to reciprocal regulation of the neurodevelopmental risk gene Trpc4 in both synapse types. The broader point for anyone designing a Fragile X therapeutic: if your readout is a synaptic fraction from whole cortex, a compound that fixes excitatory synapses while worsening inhibitory ones looks identical to a compound that does nothing.

The same resolution argument, applied to a drug. A single-nucleus atlas of 5xFAD and wild-type mouse brain, twenty-nine thousand six hundred and sixty-four nuclei across sixteen annotated cell populations, in a two-by-two design with vehicle or rolipram — the PDE4 inhibitor that reliably produces procognitive effects in Alzheimer's models without anyone having pinned down the cellular substrate. Thirty days of subcutaneous pump at point-zero-three milligrams per kilogram per day.

Disease-associated synaptic gene loss is broadly distributed when you look at individual transcripts, which is the familiar bulk result. But at the pathway level it concentrates almost entirely in VIP-Reln disinhibitory interneurons — two hundred eighty-nine genes down, with gene ontology enrichment in synapse organization programs at p-adjusted values around ten to the minus fourteen. That enrichment is not reproduced at comparable statistical depth in parvalbumin or somatostatin interneurons, or in excitatory neurons. And the rescue follows the same geography: VIP interneurons yield seventy-two genuinely rescued genes and astrocytes forty-two, with the remaining fourteen clusters showing substantially fewer. The nicest single data point is Pde7b, a cAMP-specific phosphodiesterase, which is reduced in 5xFAD VIP interneurons and restored by rolipram, with wild-type expression unchanged — a disease-context-specific drug effect at a gene in the pathway the drug targets. The caveat is sample depth: that VIP-Reln cluster is one hundred forty-nine nuclei. But the design is right, and the claim — that a broadly acting cAMP drug has a narrow cellular address — is exactly the kind of thing bulk sequencing cannot tell you.

Switching registers to molecular pharmacology. A structure-based campaign against GPR119, the orphan class A receptor expressed in pancreatic beta cells and enteroendocrine L cells that has been a diabetes target for fifteen years without producing a drug. The group ran V-SYNTHES2, a synthon-based docking approach, against roughly three trillion compounds of Enamine's on-demand chemical space. The interesting methodological choice came first: they had six receptor models available and benchmarked them against twelve hundred known ligands and six thousand one hundred property-matched decoys. The best model for prospective screening was the one bound to the endogenous lipid oleoylethanolamide, not the ones bound to potent synthetic ligands. When they restricted the benchmark to the most potent known ligands, the synthetic-ligand structures won — because those pockets recognize analogs of their own cognate ligand. The lipid-bound pocket is less restrictive and generalizes to new chemotypes, which is what you actually want from a screen.

The campaign delivered full and partial agonists in the low micromolar range, with two leads at one point six and one point three micromolar, and cryo-EM structures of three hits in active-state complexes confirming the docking poses. Micromolar is a starting point, not a drug. But two observations travel beyond this target. First, screening an active-state agonist-bound structure yielded mostly agonists — which is the hard direction, since virtual screens usually hand you antagonists — yet it also produced competitive antagonists, including one that is a close analog of an agonist with a nearly superimposable predicted pose. The activation-related changes in this orthosteric pocket are too subtle to sort agonists from antagonists reliably. You can enrich for functional direction; you cannot select for it. Second, the SAR splits the pocket into a conserved aromatic anchoring region, where the chemistry is constrained, and a permissive extracellular head-group region, where it is not — which is a useful map for anyone optimizing against this class.

A different GPCR, and a question about where signaling happens. Many receptors keep making cAMP after internalization, even with beta-arrestin bound, which ought to be impossible given that arrestin binding is supposed to occlude the G protein site. A cryo-EM structure of a stabilized PTH1R–beta-arrestin-1–G-alpha-s complex was recently reported, defining a direct arrestin–G-alpha-s interface. This group asked whether that assembly actually exists in living cells, and where.

They used three spatial resolutions. Confocal imaging with a nanobody selective for nucleotide-free G-alpha-s puts the activated G protein, arrestin, and agonist-occupied receptor on the same endosomes. Single-molecule localization microscopy, with illumination modes that separate plasma membrane from endosomal membrane within one cell, shows that agonist recruits both G-alpha-s and arrestin to both membranes. But FRET detects a genuine interaction only on endosomes — and a G-alpha-s inhibitor restricted to the endosomal compartment abolishes the sustained cAMP phase without affecting receptor internalization. That last control is the methodological advance. Every previous approach to this question blocked endocytosis, which compares a receptor that internalizes with one that does not; this one leaves trafficking intact and removes the signaling at its putative site. Finally, weighted-ensemble simulations show the complex is metastable, releasing G-alpha-s through a large rigid-body rotation while arrestin tightens its grip on the receptor — so this is an intermediate on a path, not a stable signaling machine. For drug discovery that is the actionable part: an assembly that exists transiently in a specific compartment is a different target than a receptor conformation.

One more mechanism paper, this one about state dependence. Ultrasound can suppress neural activity, and the clinically appealing claim has always been that it preferentially quiets hyperactive tissue. Nobody had a molecular transducer. This group identifies a complex between caveolin-3 and the sodium-calcium exchanger NCX1, and shows that prolonged ultra-low-intensity stimulation engages NCX1-mediated calcium extrusion in hyperactive neurons and astrocytes while sparing cells at basal activity. NCX1 is necessary and sufficient, and disrupting the caveolin-NCX1 interface abolishes the effect. In vivo, targeted stimulation suppresses fear-evoked hyperactivity and freezing in an NCX1-dependent way, and attenuates neuropathic-pain-associated hyperactivity, allodynia, and hippocampal epileptiform activity. The reason this belongs on a pharmacology show rather than a device show is that identifying the transducer converts a stimulation parameter into a drug target: if the selectivity for hyperactive cells comes from a calcium-dependent exchanger, then the state dependence is a property of the molecule, and it is in principle addressable without the ultrasound.

Two items on machine intelligence, and they make a nice pair because they point in opposite directions. The first is a randomized experiment in npj Digital Medicine asking whether physicians can actually catch an AI system that breaks. Forty-eight physicians classified forty heart sounds across four blocks, told the system had ninety-six percent accuracy, while reliability was covertly degraded block by block — ninety percent, sixty, fifty, and finally zero, with deliberately mismatched audio and visual cues in the last block. Half the participants were in a cognitive-forcing condition requiring an independent judgment before seeing the recommendation.

Three results. In the high-reliability block, combined human-AI performance was lower than the system alone — the human made it worse. Under degrading reliability, humans partially compensated, beating the system but falling below the unassisted human baseline. And cognitive forcing did not help. The number that should stick is that only twelve point five percent of participants spontaneously reported noticing that reliability had declined, and even when explicitly asked, only thirty-seven and a half percent said they had. In the forced-independent-judgment group, confidence in their own unassisted judgment fell across blocks while their unassisted accuracy did not — they concluded that they were getting worse rather than that the system was. Forty-eight physicians in a lab on an isolated subtask is a narrow experiment, and the degradation to literally zero accuracy is not a realistic failure mode. But human oversight is the regulatory backstop in essentially every framework now in force, and this is direct evidence against its load-bearing assumption.

Against that, a model trained to resist the human. Neuro-VL is a vision-language model built at Michigan on nearly a million neuroimaging studies from routine clinical care. It beats frontier and specialized models on diagnosis, localization, description and report generation, which by now is the expected headline. The part worth your attention is the red-teaming. They deliberately trained on premise-correction examples, then tested whether the model would invent pathology when the prompt assumed it. On three hundred twenty studies reported as normal, Neuro-VL held at ninety-five to ninety-seven percent across neutral wording, wording that assumed an abnormality, and wording that assumed an abnormality at a named location. The comparison model fell from eighty-eight percent to sixty-four to fifty-one percent over those same three conditions. On studies with annotated glioma or infarct, supplying an incorrect diagnosis in the prompt dropped the comparison model from ninety-nine point six percent to forty-four point five percent, while Neuro-VL went from ninety-five to ninety. The reported failure examples are specific and recognizable: describing an empyema-like collection on a normal scan after a febrile history, reinterpreting an infarct as a mass after being told about new seizures. Put the two papers side by side and the picture is uncomfortable. The clinician does not reliably catch the model's error, and the general-purpose model enthusiastically adopts the clinician's. Premise correction as an explicit training objective is one of the few concrete things anyone has proposed about that.

To close, something modest and useful. A group at the Paris Brain Institute has released Open-BEATBox, an open-source autonomous home-cage operant chamber. The animals live in the testing environment, so behavior is self-paced and continuous rather than parceled into short daily sessions requiring handling and, usually, food or water restriction. On a visual reversal-learning task, continuous autonomous testing produced roughly ten times more completed trials during reversal than conventional session-based protocols, with reduced experimenter workload and no evidence of adverse effects on the welfare measures assessed. Circadian activity patterns are preserved. The platform is modular, takes other paradigms including uncertainty-based decision tasks, and is designed to integrate with chronic recording.

It is a fitting end for today's thread, because the thing it fixes is a sampling problem. Short sessions under restriction give you a small number of trials collected at a time of day that suits the experimenter, in an animal whose motivational state you have manipulated to make it cooperate — and then you average over that. A tenfold increase in trials in a freely behaving animal does not just tighten error bars. It makes it possible to see the shape of a distribution at all, which, as several papers on today's list suggest, is usually where the finding was hiding.
