## Script

Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Wednesday, October fourteenth. Eleven items, and both preprint servers cooperated today, so the mix is heavier on bioRxiv than it has been all week.

The thread running through today is about vocabulary. Several of these papers take a word the field has been leaning on — integration, complexity, autonomous, satisfying — and show that the word was carrying more weight than the measurement underneath it could bear. The best of them replace the word with something you can count.

We open with the most conceptually ambitious paper of the week, and probably the most uncomfortable one if you work on psychedelics. A group has applied integrated information decomposition to source-localized magnetoencephalography and electroencephalography data from five separate studies, covering LSD, psilocybin, ketamine, DMT, and five-methoxy-DMT, about eighty subjects in total. The goal is to ask what we actually mean when we say psychedelics increase integration in the brain.

The framework matters, so let me anchor it. Integrated information decomposition takes the past state of two brain regions and the future state of those same regions, and decomposes the information flowing between them into redundant information, held identically by both regions; unique information, held by one and only one; and synergistic information, which exists only in the joint state and nowhere in the parts. Allowing information to move between those three formats over time gives you ten qualitatively distinct modes, and the authors give each of them a name — Oneness, Preservation, Copy, Erasure, Migration, Unification, Dissolution, Decryption, Encryption, and Cooperation.

Here is where it bites. The authors show that the standard measures we rely on are not primitive quantities at all — they are sums of these atoms, and the atoms can move in opposite directions and cancel. Signal complexity, which almost every psychedelic neuroimaging paper reports via Lempel-Ziv, is the mirror image of active information storage; across all five drugs those two track at correlations of point eight-eight to point nine-one. But active information storage is itself the sum of four distinct modes, which can vary independently. Functional connectivity — plain Pearson correlation, the single most common dependent variable in this literature — turns out to be a reasonable approximation of exactly one of the ten modes, Oneness. So every claim about network desegregation built on a functional connectivity matrix, however elaborate the downstream algorithm, is a claim about one mode out of ten.

Directed connectivity via transfer entropy fares worse. It is the sum of Copy, Migration, Decryption, and Dissolution — and the authors find that Decryption and Dissolution are the most strongly altered quantities globally across these drugs, but they move in opposite directions, so transfer entropy shows you almost nothing. The signal was always there; the instrument was summing it to zero.

What survives when you look at the atoms individually is a different story about psychedelic action. Erasure and Copy fall significantly under all five compounds. Preservation — the ability of a cortical population to privately hold its own information — is the largest numerical effect, though it only reaches significance for LSD and ketamine. And crucially, five-methoxy-DMT does the opposite of the other four on both Preservation and Oneness. The authors' summary is that the psychedelic brain is one in which cortical populations become alike, but not necessarily together — content available everywhere, depending on nowhere.

Then they close the loop with a generative model, and this is the part I'd push back on hardest if I were reviewing. They reproduce the whole pattern with neural populations that simply have reduced self-coupling — no change in coupling between regions at all. Which means a decade of interpreting altered inter-regional integration as evidence about communication between brain areas may be recoverable from a purely local parameter. That is either a profound simplification or an identifiability problem, and the paper does not fully distinguish them. The honest limitations are stated: LSD, psilocybin, and ketamine are magnetoencephalography while the two tryptamines are electroencephalography, the five-methoxy-DMT study had no saline comparator, and all of it is resting state.

Which makes the second item an almost perfect companion, from the other end of the methods spectrum. Nature Neuroscience published simultaneous PET-MRI during LSD effects in healthy volunteers — as far as I can tell the first study to put molecular and functional imaging in the same scanner during a psychedelic state. Eleven participants, oral LSD from twenty-five up to two hundred micrograms, with serotonin 2A receptor occupancy measured by a carbon-eleven labeled agonist tracer.

The dose-occupancy curve is the deliverable the clinical development world has been waiting for. Fitting occupancy against plasma concentration gives a half-maximal concentration of about one point nine nanomolar and a maximum occupancy of ninety-seven percent. Against dose, a half-maximal effective dose of thirty-one micrograms; adjusted for body weight, the fit tightens considerably, to about point four micrograms per kilogram. Three phase three trials of LSD are now running in anxiety and depression, and until today nobody had a human occupancy curve to dose against.

Two findings complicate the tidy version. First, plasma levels and subjective intensity trace an anticlockwise hysteresis loop in nearly every participant — for a given plasma concentration, the experience is weaker on the way up than on the way down. And one participant at seventy-five micrograms still had seventy-four percent occupancy hours later, with subjective intensity down from eight and a half out of ten to one and a half. Occupancy and experience come apart in time, which is hard to reconcile with simple occupancy-driven pharmacodynamics.

Second, and this is the headline: LSD increased global cerebral blood flow by nineteen percent, with occipital cortex up twenty-six percent, and internal carotid artery flow up twenty-eight percent while the artery's diameter barely moved. Psilocybin does the opposite — the best prior study found global blood flow down twelve percent with the carotid actually constricting about seven percent. Two drugs, same primary target, opposite cerebral hemodynamics. The paper's own occupancy correlations support the interpretation: global connectivity decreases tracked serotonin 2A occupancy closely across almost the entire neocortex, while blood flow changes showed essentially no relationship to occupancy at all. So the connectivity effect looks on-target and the perfusion effect looks like something else — LSD's nanomolar affinity for a long list of other monoamine receptors being the obvious suspect.

The practical warning is for anyone running blood-oxygen-dependent imaging on these compounds. If the drug moves perfusion by nineteen percent in one direction and a close pharmacological relative moves it twelve percent the other way, baseline assumptions about neurovascular coupling are not transferable between psychedelics.

Staying with brain states and treatment, a group has combined precision functional mapping in densely sampled individuals, large cross-sectional cohorts, and direct intracranial recordings to argue that hippocampal connectivity alternates between two discrete and opposing states on a timescale of minutes. One state is dominated by default mode coupling, the other by sensorimotor coupling. In major depressive disorder the equilibrium is skewed toward the default-mode-dominant state. In the densely sampled participants, hippocampus-to-default-mode coupling tracked symptom fluctuations over time and normalized after electroconvulsive therapy — specifically by driving switches away from the default-mode-dominant state, with the magnitude of that shift linked to treatment response. The intracranial recordings found analogous fluctuations in hippocampal-to-default-mode theta synchrony tracking symptoms over hours and days.

The appeal here is that it is a dynamic account rather than a static biomarker. A skewed equilibrium between two states that both exist in healthy brains is a more plausible object for a treatment to act on than a region that is simply too big or too active. The caveat is the usual one for precision mapping work: the convincing within-subject findings come from very few subjects, and the inference that electroconvulsive therapy works partly by restoring switching dynamics is correlational.

Now to straightforward receptor pharmacology, and a nice one. A Czech group asked what happens to channel-blocking NMDA receptor antagonists when the magnesium block is broken. Voltage-dependent magnesium block is the defining feature of these receptors, and there are patients with mutations at the GluN2A asparagine-615 position that impair it — a genuine NMDA receptor hyperfunction setting. The comparison is a dizocilpine-derived compound called K2060 against memantine and ketamine.

The core result is a clean statement about drug-channel interaction. At wild-type receptors, magnesium reduces the apparent potency of all three blockers and slows their onset — the physiological magnesium block is competing with the drug for the pore. At the mutant receptors that competition largely disappears, so the same drug behaves differently in the patients it is meant to treat. K2060 showed slower unbinding and greater trapping than memantine, and in knock-in mice it protected against audiogenic seizures at one milligram per kilogram where memantine and ketamine needed five, with no behavioral disruption and no additional impairment of prepulse inhibition at the higher dose. More trapping would normally worry you about tolerability, and here it did not, at least on these measures. It is an early compound with no pharmacokinetic or safety package yet, and the authors say so. But the general lesson generalizes beyond this molecule: when a mutation removes a physiological constraint on a channel, it also removes a constraint on your drug, and potency measured in wild-type systems will mislead you.

One more pharmacology item, out of cardiovascular but mechanistically interesting to anyone who thinks about allosteric sites. A Cleveland Clinic group reports hit-to-lead optimization of the first negative allosteric modulator chemotype at the angiotensin two type one receptor. The clinical problem is specific: in several autoimmune conditions the receptor is driven chronically by pathogenic autoantibodies binding an extracellular surface site, not by hormone binding the orthosteric pocket — and standard angiotensin receptor blockers, which work at the orthosteric pocket, are strictly contraindicated in preeclampsia. Their compound binds a site distinct from both the hormone and the blockers, reduces hormone-induced calcium signaling, arterial contraction, and blood pressure in infused mice, and — the point of the exercise — blocks autoantibodies from patient serum in preeclampsia and dilated cardiomyopathy from engaging the receptor. It is proof of concept, not a drug. But it is a clean demonstration that when the pathology and the physiology use different binding sites, the right pharmacology is site-selective rather than pathway-selective.

Turning to the computational half, and the single result most likely to provoke an argument in your group meeting. A team evaluated whether large language model agents, running autonomously, can develop molecular modeling methods competitive with the best human-developed ones — across four settings: the Therapeutics Data Commons ADMET tasks, two OpenADMET prediction challenges, and the Practical Molecular Optimization benchmark. The agents followed the prescribed splits and metrics in each case.

The numbers are not subtle. On the ADMET suite, the best agent-developed score beat the leaderboard reference on eighteen of twenty-one tasks, and beat the combined leaderboard-plus-peer-reviewed reference on seventeen. On the ExpansionRx challenge, agent configurations landed at the equivalent of fourth and sixth place against the published final leaderboard. On the pregnane X receptor induction challenge, a five-agent setup slightly beat the best published entry. And on Practical Molecular Optimization — which is the hardest one conceptually, because it requires designing a search policy under a limited oracle budget, not just fitting a model — the agent-developed optimizer beat existing methods under the benchmark's standard conditions.

Two things keep this honest, and the authors raise both. The first is the contamination problem, which they state plainly: every one of these benchmarks and the published methods for them were public at the time of the experiments, and unlike a human researcher, an agent's exposure through pretraining cannot be audited. So this measures competence under retrospective conditions, not independent rediscovery. The second is that they did not run enough independent replicates to say how reliably an agent arrives at these solutions — and in one experiment three of five agents terminated on capacity errors. There is also a result worth carrying forward on its own: more agents did not reliably help. Single-agent Claude Code beat its five- and ten-agent counterparts on seventeen and fifteen ADMET tasks respectively, while on ExpansionRx the five-agent setup won on eight of nine endpoints. Team size is a task-dependent hyperparameter, not a dial you turn up.

Compare that to a paper from Ohio State describing MADDIE, a multi-agent drug discovery engine with an orchestrator and four workers covering hit identification, lead optimization, synthesis, and evaluation, composed into flexible rather than predefined workflows. Across fifty protein targets it reports delivering one hundred molecules per target that jointly satisfy every property requirement — docking score, drug-likeness, synthetic accessibility, mutagenicity, cytotoxicity, hERG, Lipinski, and a synthesis route — against the strongest agentic baseline managing twenty-eight satisfying molecules on forty-nine targets.

A hundred percent on every criterion on every target should set off your instincts, and it should. The quota is the stopping condition: the system generates until it has a hundred molecules that pass its filters, so the pass rate is close to definitionally one. The real number in the paper is the efficiency one — about one thousand forty-four molecules generated to deliver a hundred, roughly three and a third hours and two minutes per delivered molecule. That is a meaningful engineering result about search efficiency. It is not evidence that the molecules are good drugs, because every criterion here is a computed property, and the stack of filters they are passing is exactly the stack that medicinal chemists complain correlates poorly with what happens in a cell.

Which is why the next paper is the useful contrast — the same ambition, but with wet-lab readouts. A group reports a generative framework that encodes deterministic reaction logic directly into a generative flow network, so that every molecule it proposes is constructible on automated synthesis hardware. They evaluate ten million candidates to navigate an eleven-trillion-compound make-on-demand space, jointly optimizing physicochemical properties alongside synthesizability. Prospectively, against a cryptic interprotomer interface on a glutamine transporter variant — a site conventional screening handles badly — they report a ninety-six percent synthesis success rate, twenty-two of twenty-three compounds, and a fifty percent functional hit rate, yielding two sub-micromolar leads with a workable window between on-target activity and nonspecific cytotoxicity.

Twenty-three compounds is a small prospective set and the writing is more promotional than the data warrant. But the structural insight is right and transfers: the binding constraint on generative design has stopped being whether the model can propose a potent molecule and has become whether the molecule can be made this week by the robot you already own. Encoding the hardware's reaction logic into the generator, rather than filtering for synthesizability afterward, is the version of that insight that actually changes the hit rate.

If you want a harder-nosed way to evaluate any of this, there is a new benchmark worth watching. Autonomous Science Bench poses six budgeted design-build-test-learn campaigns grounded in published experiments across biology, chemistry, and materials science. The agent picks batches of designs, gets measurements back, and refits, over several rounds. What makes it better than most is the grading: it requires passing two separate gates. A discovery gate asks whether the designs the agent actually measured were good, and a learning gate asks whether the model it hands you ranks held-out designs correctly. In a pilot of eight agent-model configurations across a hundred forty-four trials, the best configuration succeeded in sixty-seven percent, and the hardest campaign was solved three times out of twenty-four. The valuable observation is that the two gates dissociate: some agents build accurate predictive models but measure too few promising designs, others find good designs but cannot predict which unseen ones will work. A single success rate hides exactly that distinction — which is, again, today's theme about measurement vocabulary.

Two shorter items to close. First, a group at Anthropic deployed Claude Code instances to survey reverse transcriptase loci across one point nine billion protein clusters, and surfaced a novel family — jumbo-phage reverse transcriptases coupled to an array of roughly two-hundred-nucleotide repeat units plus a dedicated partner gene. The arrays are highly expressed and appear as discrete units during staphylococcal phage infection, suggesting a reverse transcriptase system directed by a repertoire of distinct RNAs. What makes it more than a press release is the provenance claim: the session transcripts show the model identified the family by reading the DNA sequences directly and noticing the atypical repeats, which is anomaly detection rather than retrieval of something it already knew. Obvious caveat, the authors are the model's vendor and have filed on it.

And the lightest item, which I enjoyed most. Harvard researchers asked whether language models can supply perceptual supervision for olfaction. Most odor work studies single molecules; natural odors are mixtures of tens to hundreds of volatiles. They took a database of more than twelve hundred volatile-compound-in-food mixtures spanning over eight thousand components, and used language models prompted for chemosensory descriptions as a scalable stand-in for human perceptual ratings of each mixture. Then they learned low-rank molecular embeddings that predict held-out mixture similarity.

Three results make it more than a stunt. The low-rank model reaches a held-out correlation of about point five-five, well above a diagonal weighting at point three-seven or raw component overlap at point two-nine — and it holds at point four-three on mixture pairs that share no molecules at all, where overlap-based measures are zero by construction. Second, chemosensory prompts beat prompts for other sensory modalities, so the model is not just reading off generic semantics. Third, and the one that matters: although no single-molecule labels were used in training, the learned molecular geometry aligns significantly with the independent human odor-descriptor space, rising to point five-seven when restricted to the most stably estimated molecules. A representation trained purely on language-derived mixture descriptions recovered something about single-molecule human olfactory perception that it was never shown. Whether that reflects real structure in odor space or structure in how people write about food is a question the paper raises and cannot settle — but it is a genuinely clever use of a language model as a measurement instrument rather than as a reasoner.
