Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Wednesday, September thirtieth. Twelve items, and a thread that runs through most of them: the gap between a mechanism and the outcome it was supposed to produce. A receptor mechanism that moves without the seizure moving. A container that turns out not to be what produced the accuracy. A desensitising scaffold that turns out to sustain signalling. A drug that fixes the chorea and accelerates the part of the motor exam that actually tracks function. One housekeeping note before we start. Let's start with a follow-up, because it is the most useful thing I read this week and because I owe you a revision of emphasis. Five days ago I covered PMxAgent, the open-source agentic platform for pharmacometrics published in CPT: Pharmacometrics and Systems Pharmacology — Docker orchestrating an R-based API server alongside a Python model-context-protocol server, five tools, and a benchmark in which its non-compartmental analysis workflow reached ninety-eight point three percent accuracy against a commercial reference across a hundred eighty-two drugs and eighteen hundred and twenty simulated subjects, matching or beating four frontier agents which solved the problem by writing fresh code every run and therefore produced a different answer every time. My reading then was: if you want an agent's output to be auditable, don't let it author the analysis, let it call an analysis somebody already validated.

A group in the pharmacology department at the All India Institute of Medical Sciences in Jodhpur has now tested the half of that sentence I didn't think to question — whether the validated thing has to be a server. Their write-up is a letter to the editor in preparation, but the work itself is already fully public: the harness, the skill files frozen and hashed before any run, the scorer, a per-run ledger, and every raw agent transcript archived with checksums. They re-implemented the paper's own metric, which is the percentage of subject-level parameters landing within one percent of the commercial reference, averaged across seven parameters, and they calibrated their scorer until it reproduced the paper's published numbers exactly. Then they ran five arms on the identical benchmark, each run as a fresh headless session in a fresh worktree, no resumed conversations, memory off, web access off.

Arm zero, the coding agent with no tools at all: ninety-three point two percent. Arm one, the actual PMxAgent container over model context protocol: ninety-eight point two. Arm two, no server at all, just the method conventions written down as a skill file in prose with no scripts attached: ninety-eight point seven. Arm three, the same skill file plus a tested R wrapper around PKNCA: ninety-eight point six. Arm four, arm three on a small cheap model: also ninety-eight point six, identical to four significant figures.

Two things fall out of that. The first is that the entire accuracy gap lives in one place. Peak concentration and time to peak are a hundred percent correct in every arm, including the arm with no tools. What separates them is everything downstream of the terminal slope: area under the curve extrapolated to infinity, half-life, clearance, volume. The untooled arm scored eighty-six percent on half-life and eighty-three on volume of distribution, where every tooled arm sat just under ninety-six on both. So the model was never bad at the arithmetic. It was guessing at a convention — how many terminal points to fit, whether to exclude the peak, how to break ties on adjusted R-squared, what to do with below-quantitation values on either side of the peak. Writing that convention down, in about a page of prose, recovered essentially all of it.

The second is where the reproducibility came from, and this is the part that revises what I told you. It was real, but it belongs to the script rather than to the server. Arms three and four produced identical parameter values on every one of ten runs, standard deviation zero. The prose-only arm had the single highest mean accuracy of the five and still varied run to run, because the model was still writing the code. The container arm was deterministic as well, at three and a half times the cost per run and five times the wall clock of the scripted skill. What the container actually buys you is deployment governance — pinned image digests, build provenance attestation, one environment for a whole team — which is a genuine thing to want and is not the same thing as accuracy or determinism.

The caveats are honest and they are in the repository rather than buried. The container arm has three runs against the others' ten, planned that way on cost and time. Its wall clock is inflated because the amd64 images were running under emulation on Apple silicon. Cost is the agent's own list-price estimate from token counts on a subscription, not an invoice. Runs that died on subscription usage limits were excluded under a rule stated before any score was looked at, and they are enumerated. Only the non-compartmental task has actually been run; the messy-spreadsheet, below-quantitation, unit-handling and oral-dosing tasks are written but not executed, and those are precisely where a purpose-built server might earn its keep. It is one laboratory and it is not peer reviewed. But this is the most valuable genre of agentic-AI paper in circulation right now, which is somebody taking a published framework and asking whether the framework is the part that worked.

Second, and staying with language models doing drug discovery but at the far upstream end, a preprint from MD Anderson introducing a target-indication prioritisation framework called PRIORITI. The design is the argument. Given nothing but a target gene and a disease indication, a domain-instructed language model synthesises human genetic evidence across prespecified channels, and it is explicitly drug-agnostic and never sees the outcome label. That synthesis is embedded, combined with static context from gene summaries and disease definitions, and handed to a supervised model trained on sixty-seven hundred and eighty-four temporally curated historical target-indication outcomes. On a strictly post-cutoff out-of-time set of seven hundred and sixty-five pairs it reports an area under the receiver-operating curve of point eight one seven, a precision-recall area of point three five seven, and expected calibration error of point zero four four — beating both a static-context baseline and direct zero-shot forecasting by the same language model. That last comparison is the informative one: what the model contributes is reading genetics, not judging outcomes. Hold onto the precision-recall number rather than the AUC, because against a skewed base rate an AUC in the low eighties still means most of what you flag will fail. And the honest limit of any leakage-resistant claim here is that the design can hide the drug and the label, but it cannot fully hide what the model absorbed during pretraining.

Third, from Exeter and Imperial, a secondary analysis of the KARE trial of adjunctive ketamine for alcohol use disorder — and it is a fascinating document to read as much for what it is as for what it shows. KARE randomised ninety-six adults with severe alcohol use disorder, already detoxified, in a two-by-two design to three weekly ketamine infusions at zero point eight milligrams per kilogram or saline, each crossed with either mindfulness-based relapse prevention or alcohol psychoeducation. What the authors have done is re-express the data on endpoints regulators actually accept: percentage of days abstinent, and the proportion of patients with no heavy drinking days at all. Across pooled weeks seventeen to twenty-four, ketamine plus psychological support gave seventy-eight point five percent days abstinent against fifty-seven point eight in the least intensive arm, a difference of twenty point seven percentage points at a nominal p of point zero three two; adjusted for baseline, twenty-six point two points. The no-heavy-drinking-days endpoint moved in the same direction and did not reach significance — fifty percent against twenty-six percent at weeks twenty-one to twenty-four, p of point one three. Heavy drinking days made up ten percent of days against twenty-two, a fifty-five percent relative reduction across the pooled window — though that one only reached a p of point zero six, and it was at weeks seventeen to twenty that it cleared, at a sixty-seven percent reduction.

Then they put that next to the incretins, and this is the part with legs. Relative reductions in heavy drinking days: fifty-five percent in KARE, against twenty-five for depot naltrexone, minus seventeen for exenatide, forty-six for semaglutide, and thirty-five for pemvidutide — where the pemvidutide number comes from a company topline press release and rests on an assumed baseline. The argument is that these are comparable in magnitude, but ketamine's was measured four to six months after a four-week course finished, while the incretins are given indefinitely and their effect depends on continued exposure. If effect sizes across mechanisms really are similar, then the discriminating comparison is persistence after stopping, not peak effect.

Now the caveats, which are large enough that you should hold the comparison loosely. This contrasts the most intensive arm against the least intensive arm of a factorial design, so it is a comparison of whole treatment packages and cannot separate the ketamine from the psychotherapy — the main effects are in the primary publication, not here. It is post hoc, observed-case, unadjusted for multiplicity, and the two arms compared have twenty and twenty-three participants with diary data. Everyone improved, because everyone entered detoxified, so placebo-arm heavy drinking was one point six days a week in KARE against an estimated four point three in the pemvidutide trial, which makes the absolute rates non-comparable and the authors say so. And the senior author is chief scientific officer of the company running the phase three, with the first author consulting for it. None of that makes the analysis wrong. It does make it a positioning document for MORE-KARE, and it should be read as one.

Fourth, staying with alcohol use disorder and psychedelics, a pilot randomised controlled trial from Nimes and Louvain asking a mechanistically specific question instead of an outcome question. The premise is that the social-cognition deficits documented in severe alcohol use disorder — usually framed as theory-of-mind impairment — may be more parsimoniously explained by egocentric bias: not an inability to infer what someone else thinks, but an inability to suppress your own perspective when it conflicts. The visual perspective-taking paradigm they use decomposes that cleanly. You judge what an avatar can see while your own view conflicts, which indexes egocentric interference; you judge your own view while the avatar's conflicts, which indexes altercentric interference; and the difference between the two cancels the shared interference-resolution component.

Thirty patients with severe alcohol use disorder and persistent depressive symptoms, detoxified within the previous two to eight weeks, were randomised two-to-one to twenty-five milligrams of psilocybin or an active placebo of one milligram, double blind, inside a structured preparation-and-integration framework, and did the task at baseline and three weeks after dosing. In the high-dose group, egocentric bias and egocentric superiority on reaction times both dropped significantly; in the low-dose group neither moved; the between-group difference in pre-post change carried a Cohen's d of about minus one for both indices. Altercentric bias did not budge, and nothing moved on accuracy. So the claim is specific and the specificity is the point: this looks like less egocentricity rather than more sensitivity to others.

The limitations are as large as the effect. Twenty-five patients completed the task, and the analysed groups are eighteen against six, which is a comparison you should treat as a signal generator and not an estimate. There is no healthy control group, so we cannot tell whether this is normalisation of an alcohol-related deficit or a general psilocybin effect on conflict processing. There is no long-term follow-up on the task. The exploratory link to subjective intensity reached significance on exactly one of four indices, and the five subjective dimensions were so intercorrelated — Cronbach's alpha of point nine seven eight — that no dimension-specific claim about ego dissolution can be supported. What I like about it anyway is the design logic. If you think psilocybin works in addiction partly through self-related processing, this is how you test that, rather than by measuring drinking and inferring the mechanism.

Fifth, the ketamine mechanism item, and this one is a genuinely clean piece of human neuropharmacology from the Krystal and Anticevic groups at Yale, published in Biological Psychiatry. The standing hypothesis from animal recording and cortical microcircuit models is that NMDA receptor antagonism impairs spatial working memory by broadening the spatial tuning of individual neurons — making each cell less specific about which location it represents. You cannot record neurons in humans, so the question has sat untested at the level that matters. What they did instead was combine a pharmacological challenge with computational fMRI in forty healthy adults and model population-level neuronal representations rather than the usual blood-oxygen activation magnitude. Ketamine broadened neural spatial tuning, broader tuning was linked to worse spatial working memory performance, and — the detail I find most useful — the tuning change was more consistent across individuals and across brain regions than the change in activation magnitude was. That is the first human evidence tying altered neuronal tuning specifically to NMDA antagonism and to the working memory deficit, and it is a reminder that the right dependent variable is often not the one the scanner hands you.

Sixth, and staying with serotonergic psychedelics, a preprint applying a new class of dynamical measure to DMT. The authors introduce two vorticity-based metrics — spiral vorticity and its spatial dispersion — to quantify how rotational, vortex-like the organisation of large-scale cortical phase dynamics is, and how spatially heterogeneous that rotational structure is. Applied to within-subject fMRI under placebo and DMT, both measures rise under drug and temporal irreversibility falls, with the largest changes in default mode, visual, frontoparietal and dorsal attention networks. Both track the time course of subjective intensity. The finding I would flag is the one that cuts against the obvious prediction: the strongest reorganisation of spiral dynamics emerged during offset and recovery rather than at peak intensity. If that holds up, it argues that the interesting neural event in a short-acting psychedelic is not the peak but the return, which is also where most of the clinically relevant integration is supposed to happen.

Seventh, the negative result of the day, and one worth more attention than negative results usually get. Systemic 5-HT2A antagonism has been reported to reduce absence seizures, and enhanced tonic GABA-A inhibition of thalamocortical neurons is an established driver of those seizures, so the obvious hypothesis is that endogenous thalamic 5-HT2A signalling feeds the pathological tonic inhibition. A group tested it directly in Genetic Absence Epilepsy Rats from Strasbourg. In patch-clamp recordings from ventrobasal thalamocortical neurons, the selective 5-HT2A antagonist MDL eleven thousand nine hundred thirty-nine had no effect on tonic current in non-epileptic controls but significantly reduced the elevated tonic current in the epileptic strain — a clean, disease-dependent mechanism. Then they delivered the same antagonist bilaterally into the ventrobasal thalamus of freely moving epileptic rats by reverse microdialysis, and the seizures did not change. The cellular mechanism moved and the disease did not. The parsimonious conclusion is that whatever systemic 5-HT2A blockade does to absence seizures, it is not doing it through thalamic tonic inhibition, and the site is somewhere else. This is exactly the dissociation that usually gets quietly dropped from a paper, and the fact that it is the headline here is the reason to read it.

Eighth, opioid receptor pharmacology at whole-brain scale. Using reporter mice that permanently mark active neurons through the c-Fos locus, combined with tissue clearing and light-sheet imaging, a group mapped brain-wide activation after acute morphine at ten milligrams per kilogram, after an escalating twice-daily regimen producing dependence, and after Ro sixty-four sixty-one ninety-eight, a selective non-peptide agonist at the NOP receptor. The first result is the paradox: all three raised global activation relative to vehicle, despite mu and NOP both being Gi/o-coupled and inhibitory — the expected consequence of disinhibiting GABAergic neurons, but striking to see at this scale. Acute morphine engaged the most regions, all increased and none decreased. Sex differences were prominent: the global increase reached significance only in males, and morphine engaged mainly pain- and reward-related midbrain circuitry in males against hypothalamic circuitry in females. Dependent animals showed lower activation across several regions yet more widespread co-activation, so reduced recruitment coexists with tighter coupling. NOP agonism produced a far weaker profile overall, which is worth knowing for anyone hoping a NOP agonist behaves like a mu agonist with the bad parts removed.

Ninth, and the most surprising mechanistic result of the week, a structural paper on beta-arrestin one. The textbook role of beta-arrestins is to terminate G protein signalling, and yet internalised receptors are known to sustain arrestin-dependent Gs and cAMP signalling from endosomes — unresolvable until now because every receptor-arrestin-G protein assembly described so far places the two transducers on opposite faces of the receptor with no contact between them. Working with the parathyroid hormone type one receptor, this group shows that beta-arrestin one binds Gs directly, engages its nucleotide-free state, and does so far more strongly than it engages the beta-gamma subunits. A cryo-EM structure at about three point one angstroms shows how: the arrestin anchors to the phosphorylated receptor C-tail, leaving the transmembrane core free for Gs, then reorients to clamp the Switch One, Switch Two and alpha-three and beta-five regions of Gs with its finger, C- and back loops. Disrupting either side of that interface abolishes assembly and selectively collapses the internalisation-dependent phase of cAMP production — and the same interface governs arrestin-Gs coupling at the GLP-1 receptor. So the desensitiser is also a positive regulator, holding Gs in a signalling-competent state. For anyone designing biased agonists, the implication is uncomfortable: arrestin recruitment is not a clean proxy for switching signalling off.

Tenth, the incretin-and-addiction item, short because the preprint is short. Following cocaine self-administration, male and female rats received daily semaglutide across a thirty-day abstinence period and were then tested for cue- and cocaine-induced reinstatement. Semaglutide attenuated cue-induced reinstatement and cocaine-induced stereotypy — but operant responding was elevated after a cocaine prime. The split matters. Cue-induced reinstatement is the model of relapse triggered by environment, which is the clinically relevant one; a drug prime is a different pharmacological challenge. A compound that blunts the first while not blunting, and possibly amplifying, the second is an interesting profile rather than a clean win, and it lines up with the pattern emerging across this literature, where incretins look better against cue- and habit-driven seeking than against acute drug reward.

Eleventh, a clinical prediction item. First-line antipsychotics fail roughly forty percent of people with first-episode psychosis and there is no deployable way to know in advance who. A group recorded resting EEG in fifty-six minimally treated first-episode patients before treatment started, plus thirty-four matched controls, and clustered it into six microstates including the canonical four. Rather than testing individual microstate parameters, they built a normative reference — the first principal component of microstate properties in the healthy controls, explaining thirty-five percent of variance — and projected patients onto it. Baseline distance from that norm predicted response, defined as better than fifty percent symptom improvement at twelve weeks, with an adjusted odds ratio of point six three, reaching an area under the curve of seventy-two percent in leave-one-out cross-validation when combined with clinical information. What makes this more than another small-sample EEG classifier is that they rebuilt the normative component from an entirely independent control cohort of ninety-two people and it still predicted response. Transportability of the normative reference is the thing that usually breaks first in this genre, and they tested it rather than asserting it.

We'll close on a prescribing question that is easy to state and hard to answer. Vesicular monoamine transporter type two inhibitors are the only FDA-approved therapy for the motor features of Huntington's disease, and the trials that got them there measured chorea. Using six hundred and twenty-two participants from the Enroll-HD platform, a group modelled rates of change in the hyperkinetic and hypokinetic subscores of the unified motor assessment before and after a patient started one. The inhibitors reversed the progression of hyperkinesis, as advertised — and significantly increased the rate of change in the hypokinetic subscores. Antipsychotics, analysed the same way, also reversed chorea progression but did not significantly worsen hypokinesis. Now, this is observational with a within-patient before-and-after design, so you should worry about indication drift: clinicians start these drugs when chorea is prominent, and disease stage is not static. But hypokinesia in Huntington's rises progressively and correlates with functional impairment and global decline far more tightly than chorea does. If the drug you use to quiet the chorea accelerates the part of the exam that actually tracks function, then the right framing is not whether it works but which motor domain you are choosing to treat. That is a conversation worth having at the point of prescribing rather than after.
