## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. I'm your host, and this is your daily pass through CNS pharmacology, psychiatric and neurology drug discovery, and the computational machinery closing in on both. Twelve items today, and the thread running through almost all of them is resolution — the question of what unit a signal actually belongs to. Which micrometre of tissue. Which neuron in the field. Which animal in the cage. Which copy of the transducer on which protomer of the receptor. Which facet of a clinical construct. Several papers today get a result that was invisible at the coarser grain, and one or two get a result that dissolves when you look closer.

Start with the best paper of the week, a preprint out of the Max Planck Florida Institute that makes the locus coeruleus behave in a way the textbook says it cannot. The textbook position is that neuromodulatory nuclei are broadcast systems: a small number of cells, axons everywhere, transmitter spilling into extracellular space and bathing whatever is nearby. Volume transmission, by design non-specific. That framing sits underneath a great deal of how we think about noradrenergic drugs.

These authors trained head-fixed mice on a virtual-reality navigation task where the animal has to integrate self-motion to estimate when it has reached an unmarked reward zone, then lick. Recording in the locus coeruleus with optogenetic tagging of dopamine-beta-hydroxylase-positive cells, they find that about two-thirds of tagged neurons fire a brief phasic burst at run onset. Not a generic locomotion signal — the response was larger when run onset marked the start of a timing computation than when the animal resumed running mid-trial at matched speed.

Here is the part that matters. Imaging the dopamine sensor dLight in hippocampal C-A-one while simultaneously visualizing labelled locus coeruleus axons, they see dopamine transients — from the canonically noradrenergic nucleus — that decay with a spatial constant of about three micrometres and a time constant of about one and a half seconds. Three micrometres. And only about a fifth of axon-containing regions produced a transient at run onset at all, with those regions no more spatially clustered than chance. So the release is confined both to a tiny radius and to discrete axonal segments.

Within that radius, the dopamine preferentially amplified one functional class of pyramidal neuron — the population whose activity ramps up at run onset and decays over the following seconds, which this group had previously shown is required for task performance. Brief optogenetic locus coeruleus activation at run onset evoked local dopamine, enhanced those ramping dynamics, and delayed the animal's first anticipatory lick by about four-tenths of a second, with no change in running speed or reward rate. The same stimulation mid-trial did nothing.

The pharmacology is the load-bearing control. Bilateral intra-hippocampal infusion of the D1-like antagonist S-C-H two-three-three-nine-zero degraded task performance and, at a lower dose that left behaviour intact, still reduced the prevalence of ramping neurons. The alpha-one antagonist prazosin and the beta antagonist propranolol did neither. So this is a dopaminergic effect from a noradrenergic nucleus, acting at D1-like receptors in hippocampus.

And the modelling is the conceptually satisfying part. They show that the selectivity for ramping neurons falls out for free if you assume only two things: dopamine is available locally, and D1-like signalling preferentially amplifies cells that are already strongly active. No dedicated wiring, no molecular targeting. The neuron gets modulated because it happened to be busy when the transmitter arrived. That is a mechanism for how a broadcast system can act like a targeted one, and it reframes what you should expect a systemic noradrenergic or dopaminergic drug to do — the drug's effect will be conditioned on what each local circuit was doing at the moment of exposure. The honest caveat is that dLight retains some sensitivity to norepinephrine, and their controls narrow but do not fully exclude a noradrenergic contribution to the imaged signal.

Staying with dopamine at high spatial resolution but moving to the amygdala, a preprint from the German Center for Neurodegenerative Diseases records dopamine release in the basolateral amygdala of freely moving mice with fibre photometry. The interesting design choice is that they didn't only look at cued learning — they looked at self-initiated exploration, building an analysis framework that treats dopamine as a function of the animal's orientation and proximity to a target rather than as a function of experimenter-delivered events.

Dopamine rose during rearing, head dips, and stationary investigation — the animal's own information-seeking acts — and scaled with proximity to the target. It attenuated across repeated exposures to an object, consistent with novelty decay, while responses to a conspecific held up better. In fear conditioning, the shock-evoked signal declined across pairings as the cue-evoked signal grew, and it tracked cue relevance through extinction.

The claim they build on that is that basolateral amygdala dopamine is neither an aversive-teaching signal nor a reward-prediction-error signal but a state-dependent salience signal that flags behaviourally relevant moments — including ones the animal generates itself — and primes the circuit for plasticity. The exploration data is the novel part, because almost every amygdala dopamine study in the literature is built on experimenter-timed cues, and the self-generated case is where a salience account and a prediction-error account come apart. This is photometry, so it is bulk release, not per-terminal — exactly the resolution the locus coeruleus paper just showed can hide structure.

From dopamine to a neuropeptide system that gets far less attention than it deserves. A preprint applies touchscreen tasks to the melanin-concentrating hormone system, and the reason to care is reverse-translation: the visual discrimination and reversal paradigms used here have close human analogues, so a result about behavioural persistence transfers more cleanly than most rodent findings.

They gave mice the selective MCHR1 antagonist SNAP-nine-four-eight-four-seven during either acquisition or extinction of an appetitive touchscreen response. Blockade during acquisition did essentially nothing. Blockade during extinction produced a pronounced early drop in responding once reward was withdrawn. In reversal, the antagonist improved attainment of criterion and overall performance — but without reducing correction trials per error, so the benefit was in acquiring the new contingency, not in suppressing perseveration after mistakes. The clean reading is that MCHR1 signalling matters specifically when a contingency changes, not while it holds.

Then they chemogenetically activated lateral hypothalamic MCH neurons, expecting the mirror image, and did not get it. Activation had no detectable effect on extinction. It did alter reversal learning — and, oddly, the effect persisted into a second reversal run days later with no chemogenetic drive at all, where previously activated mice learned the new contingency faster but showed greater persistence after individual errors. So receptor blockade and neuronal activation are not two ends of one axis. That is a useful warning for anyone reading MCHR1 antagonists as anti-obesity or antidepressant candidates: the receptor and the cell population are doing dissociable things, and a compound that blocks the receptor is not simply subtracting MCH-neuron activity.

Now down to the receptor itself, and a Nature Communications paper that will matter to anyone designing biased ligands for family C receptors. Essentially everything we know about how beta-arrestins couple to receptors comes from monomeric family A receptors, where the picture is a tidy one-to-one complex in one of two modes: tail-only, or tail plus core. Metabotropic glutamate receptors are constitutive dimers, which immediately raises a question nobody had answered — what stoichiometry does a dimer use?

The group built a single-molecule pulldown assay: tagged receptor captured on glass, tagged beta-arrestin in the other channel, colocalization counted molecule by molecule, and photobleaching steps used to read out how many copies of each are in the complex. The assay first reproduces known biology — the vasopressin-two receptor and mGluR-eight form stable complexes with wild-type beta-arrestin-one, while the beta-two adrenergic receptor and mGluR-three only pull down a truncated, constitutively active beta-arrestin. That is the classic class A versus class B distinction, recovered from a biochemical readout.

Then the new result. Dimeric mGluRs form both two-to-one and two-to-two complexes. Combinatorial mutagenesis of the C-terminal tails and intracellular loops maps out contacts in cis and in trans — one beta-arrestin's tail contact on one protomer with its core contact on the other. And they find megacomplexes: receptor dimer bound simultaneously to beta-arrestin-one and beta-arrestin-two, or to a beta-arrestin and a G protein at once. Overlaying their companion cryo-EM structure shows why some combinations are forbidden — two core-bound arrestins clash, and a core-bound arrestin clashes with a G protein on the adjacent protomer. All told they propose more than twenty distinct coupling modes for mGluR-eight against two for a family A receptor.

Why this matters practically: biased agonism at family C receptors has been discussed as if the arrestin arm were a single output you could dial up or down. If the arm has twenty configurations with different trafficking and signalling consequences, "arrestin bias" is underspecified as a design goal for an mGluR drug. One limitation they state plainly: these are non-equilibrium pulldowns, so the colocalization percentages report relative complex stability, not affinity.

Two items on drug discovery machinery now. The first is a preprint applying the direct-to-biology strategy to cereblon-targeting molecular glue degraders. The premise is that the rate limiter in glue discovery is not design, it is purification — you make a compound, you purify it, you assay it, and that loop is slow enough that libraries stay small. Direct-to-biology skips the purification: run the synthesis at nanomole scale in a plate, and put the crude reaction mixture straight into the cell assay.

They used amide coupling because it is mild and tolerant, screened seven condition sets to find one with complete conversion and no cytotoxicity, then built three hundred thirty-six compounds from six immunomodulatory imide cores and fifty-six carboxylic acids on a single 384-well plate, reading out degradation of casein kinase one alpha and Helios in HiBiT knock-in cells. The validation that makes the paper is a purity titration: they spiked known degraders against the unreacted building blocks at defined ratios and showed how potency and maximal degradation read out as a function of purity. Crude mixtures scored comparably to purified counterparts.

No new degrader of consequence comes out of it, so this is a methods paper. But the constraint it removes is real, and for CNS degrader work specifically — narrow property window, many linkers and caps to explore — compressing the make-test loop by a purification step is worth more than it sounds.

The second discovery item is computational and asks the inverse question: given a compound, what does it hit? A framework in Briefings in Bioinformatics builds proteome-wide target profiles using evolutionary chemical binding similarity — the idea being that if you want to predict whether a compound binds a target with sparse data, you should borrow ligands from evolutionarily related targets in the same family rather than training each target in isolation. They assemble nearly six million binding measurements from ChEMBL and BindingDB and build target-specific models across six thousand two hundred proteins.

The test case is ginsenosides, which is a smart choice — a structurally diverse natural product class with an enormous, mostly uninterpretable pharmacology literature and very few confirmed molecular targets. And then, unusually for this genre, they went to the bench: a direct binding assay confirms interaction with protein kinase C isoforms, previously unreported, with phenotypic evidence consistent with activity at the SERCA calcium pump. The limitation is the one the authors state — similarity-based prediction is bounded by training data, and natural products are exactly where that data is thinnest, so the regime where you most want the method is the regime where it is least calibrated. Still, a direct binding confirmation is more than most papers in this space attempt.

Now a translational pharmacokinetics item that I think is quietly important for anyone doing first-in-human dose selection. A preprint asks whether whole-body PET microdosing data can inform a physiologically based pharmacokinetic model well enough to extrapolate from monkey to human, using dolutegravir as the case study because they had both radiolabelled microdose PET and conventional-dose data in nonhuman primates.

The workflow is sequential: build a whole-body model, fit it to three hours of organ-level PET time-activity curves, re-fit against plasma and tissue concentrations from a conventional intravenous dose, then simulate a virtual population on the standard human oral regimen. The result that makes the paper is a negative one embedded in a positive one. Microdose-derived parameters alone systematically underpredicted human exposure; combining microdose and conventional-dose data predicted steady-state exposure and peak concentration within the observed clinical range, with a slight underprediction of trough.

The reason the negative matters is that the whole appeal of microdosing is that you can do it early and cheaply. This says the microdose gives you excellent early distribution kinetics — which is genuinely what PET is uniquely good at, minute-by-minute organ uptake you cannot get any other way — but it does not give you the nonlinearities that emerge at therapeutic dose, here saturation of glucuronidation and a shift in unbound fraction. Use the microdose to inform distribution priors, not clearance. Brain and bile profiles were poorly captured, which for a CNS audience is precisely the tissue you cared about, and the study rests on six animals with sparse sampling. Proof of concept, appropriately labelled as such.

Three items on agentic and language-model tooling. The first is a single-author framework in Briefings in Bioinformatics that addresses a specific and real gap: language-model agents and expression foundation models cannot talk to each other. The agent has no access to the transcriptomic representation; the foundation model has no handle on natural language.

The system pairs single-cell GPT expression embeddings with BioBERT semantic retrieval, and routes each query through a classifier — a gene signature goes to marker scoring, a natural-language concept goes to semantic matching, and a mixed query goes to reciprocal rank fusion of both. All of it runs on the embeddings, never touching the original count matrix, which has obvious implications for working with data you are not permitted to move.

Benchmarked across six datasets spanning lung disease, cancers, organoids, healthy tissue and neurodevelopment, it beats the closest comparator and a lexical baseline on cell-type retrieval, with the largest gains on gene-signature queries — an effect size so enormous that I read it as "the comparator has no real mechanism for signature queries" rather than as a measure of quality. The more honest claim is the architectural one: explicit routing beats implicit contrastive fusion when your query types are genuinely different modalities. And it is one person's work on donated compute, which given what it competes against is its own kind of result.

The second agentic item is a behavioural phenotyping framework that uses a language model in a role I have not seen before. The hard problem in social behaviour quantification is identity: during contact and occlusion, pose estimates get assigned to the wrong animal, and that error propagates into every downstream social variable. This framework couples multi-animal pose estimation with identity-preserving tracking, then uses a language model to mine candidate behavioural factors — kinematic, postural and social descriptors — which are then quantitatively screened rather than accepted.

That intermediate position is the interesting one. Hand-designed features are interpretable but don't scale; fully unsupervised embeddings scale but produce factors you cannot name. Letting a language model propose the feature space and letting statistics adjudicate it keeps interpretability while widening the search. Applied to social defeat stress, it recovers reduced approach behaviour and a multivariate profile separating susceptible animals from controls, and the outputs synchronize with miniscope calcium recordings for joint behaviour-and-activity analysis. For anyone running social phenotyping in a psychiatric model, the identity-resolution part alone is the reason to look.

The third is a short piece in npj Digital Medicine arguing about architecture rather than models. Responding to a study that found a single language model could generate acceptable fact-recall exam questions but failed at higher-order clinical reasoning items, these authors argue the failure is structural rather than a capability ceiling — human high-stakes item development is inherently multi-stage, with drafting, independent review from multiple perspectives, and editorial adjudication, so asking one generative pass to do all of that is the wrong comparison. They split the task into drafting, critique and iterative adversarial refinement agents, and in blinded expert evaluation against China's national medical licensing standards the multi-agent outputs drew fifty-eight percent of expert preferences against forty-two for the single-model baseline. A modest margin from a correspondence-length study, but the framing generalizes: when a single-pass model underperforms on a task humans do in stages, test the staged architecture before concluding the model can't do it.

Two clinical items to close. The first is a study in Translational Psychiatry on interoception in borderline personality disorder that does something the field needs more of, which is to refuse to treat a construct as one thing. They separated interoceptive accuracy — measured objectively with a heartbeat discrimination task — from metacognitive awareness of that accuracy, from self-reported interoceptive attention, from neural responses during an interoceptive attention task, in fifty-five patients and thirty-one controls, before and after four weeks of residential dialectical behaviour therapy.

The result is genuinely selective. Patients reported reduced interoceptive attention, and that reduction tracked interpersonal problems. Multivariate imaging showed that in insula and dorsal anterior cingulate, the activity patterns evoked by attending to the heartbeat and attending to an external stimulus were more similar in patients than in controls — a loss of representational distinctness between inside and outside, which is a nicely specific version of a claim the clinical literature usually makes vaguely. But there was no group difference in objective interoceptive accuracy or in metacognitive awareness; accuracy was impaired only in the more symptomatic patients. And after four weeks of dialectical behaviour therapy, the only thing that moved was self-reported attention.

That last point deserves emphasis, because it is the kind of finding that gets written up optimistically. A four-week intervention improved the self-report measure and left every objective measure unchanged. The charitable reading is that attention is the facet that responds first and accuracy needs targeted training in severe cases, which is what the authors propose. The uncharitable reading is expectancy — with an unblinded treatment and a waiting-interval control, I would not treat the self-report change as evidence of altered interoception until an objective measure follows it.

And to finish, a large pragmatic trial from Australia that is about workforce rather than mechanism. Three hundred fifty-eight adults with major depressive disorder or bipolar depression at moderate-to-severe symptom levels were randomized to either a multicomponent lifestyle programme — diet and exercise, delivered by accredited dietitians and exercise physiologists over telehealth — or a brief structured course of cognitive behavioural therapy delivered by psychologists. Primary outcome was blinded interviewer-rated depression severity at eight weeks, with a prespecified non-inferiority margin of one-point-six points.

Non-inferiority held in both intention-to-treat and per-protocol analyses. Both arms improved substantially — about eight and a half points on the depression scale with lifestyle therapy and just under seven with cognitive behavioural therapy — and the lifestyle arm also improved diet quality, sleep, and alcohol and substance risk, independently of weight change. The important caveat is attrition: primary outcome data were available for two hundred sixty-seven of three hundred fifty-eight randomized participants, a quarter of the sample missing at eight weeks, and non-inferiority designs are the ones most flattered by dropout. There is also no usual-care arm, so the shared improvement could include a good deal of regression to the mean.

Still, I think the finding is the right kind of pragmatic. There is no version of the mental health workforce shortage that gets solved by training more psychologists fast enough, and this is evidence that a different and already-large allied health workforce can deliver a comparable eight-week outcome in moderate-to-severe mood disorder. That is a delivery result rather than a mechanism result, and it is the one more likely to reach patients this decade.

That's the episode. A broadcast neuromodulatory system that turns out to act within three micrometres and selects its targets by who happened to be firing; amygdala dopamine tracking self-generated exploration rather than delivered cues; a neuropeptide receptor that only matters at the moment a contingency changes, and a cell population that isn't its mirror image; a dimeric receptor with twenty ways to bind arrestin instead of two; crude unpurified reaction mixtures that screen as well as purified degraders; evolutionary similarity finding a kinase target for a natural product class nobody could deconvolve; PET microdosing that informs distribution but not clearance; an agent that routes queries by modality instead of fusing them; a language model proposing behavioural features that statistics then adjudicate; a multi-agent pipeline beating a single pass at writing exam questions; interoceptive attention in borderline personality disorder moving on self-report and nowhere else; and dietitians matching psychologists on an eight-week depression endpoint.
