## Script

Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Sunday, October eighteenth. Eleven items today, weighted toward medRxiv — bioRxiv was throttling me for most of the morning and the Nature-family journals that would otherwise have contributed were mostly paywalled, so the shape of the episode is partly a shape imposed by what I could read.

The thread through today's set is measurement. Over and over, the result that matters is not a new effect but a correction to the instrument used to look for effects — the outcome scale, the anatomical parcel, the query wording, the model's own prior about what success looks like. In several of these papers the finding only becomes visible after someone fixes the thing they were measuring with.

We start with the item most likely to change how someone in drug development spends their week, from Insilico Medicine. They have built a pair of gradient-boosted models that predict whether a clinical trial will meet its registered primary endpoint, trained separately for efficacy endpoints and for safety and toxicity endpoints, because those are genuinely different questions with different failure modes. Thirty-two thousand trials with resolved outcomes, split strictly by start year — trained on trials begun between two thousand and twenty twenty, tested on trials begun in twenty twenty-one or later. Every feature is computed from information available at registration: target and mechanism precedent, whether the asset has been tried before and how far it got, modality, first-in-class status, design rigour, biomarker use, planned enrollment, site count, competition for the same patient pool, and a manufacturing-risk proxy derived from modality. On the held-out recent trials the efficacy model reaches an area under the curve of about zero point eight three and the safety model about zero point eight one.

The leakage discipline is worth a sentence. The target-choice feature — a learned summary of how promising a given target-plus-indication pairing has historically been — is built on a frozen biomedical language model whose pretraining stops in twenty nineteen, so it cannot have absorbed the outcome of any trial in the test set, and the supervised head on top is cross-fitted within training.

But the genuinely interesting result is the benchmark they built around it. They assembled two thousand and forty ongoing trials with expected completion between October of this year and the end of twenty twenty-seven — outcomes unknown by construction — and asked five frontier language models, all with web search enabled, to call each one. The trials have not read out, so nobody is right yet. Two things are already visible. First, the statistical model predicted success for about half the trials, close to the historical base rate; every one of the five language models predicted success for roughly three quarters or more. Second, the five language models agreed substantially with one another — pairwise chance-corrected agreement in the range of zero point four to zero point six seven — while the statistical model's agreement with any of them was near chance, about zero point one.

I want to be careful about what that does and does not show. It does not show the model is right and the language models are wrong; that is precisely what the benchmark is designed to find out over the next year. What it does show is that inter-model agreement among frontier systems is not evidence of accuracy. Five models trained on overlapping corpora, asked the same question, converge on a shared optimism prior and a shared view of what a promising trial looks like. If you were planning to use a panel of language models as a consensus oracle for anything resembling a probability-of-success estimate, this is the result to sit with.

Second item, and it is the one that would actually change a trial protocol. A group working on Parkinson's genetics has imaged striatal dopamine and glutamate release in LRRK2 G2019S and VPS35 D620N knock-in mice using the genetically encoded sensors dLight and iGluSnFR, across adult ages and in both sexes, with and without the selective LRRK2 kinase inhibitor MLi-2. LRRK2 inhibitors are in human trials now on the logic that LRRK2 hyperactivity is a shared feature of familial and idiopathic Parkinson's. What has been missing is any account of what inhibiting the kinase does to circuit function, and in whom.

Three findings. In wild-type mice, ongoing LRRK2 activity is already shaping dopamine release, and it does so differently by age and sex — acute inhibition reverses the baseline sex difference in aged animals and potentiates dopamine release more in middle-aged females than males. In the G2019S knock-ins, dopamine release declines progressively after full adulthood, and it declines in males first. And the therapeutic window is narrow in a specific, testable way: at six months, the age at which the deficit first appears, two hours of MLi-2 exposure restores release in both sexes, confirmed against reduced phosphorylation of the downstream substrate Rab12. At twenty-five months, the deficits in both the G2019S and the VPS35 mutants are resistant to acute inhibition entirely.

The implication is uncomfortable. If the reversible phase is early and the late phase is refractory, then a LRRK2 inhibitor trial enrolling established, diagnosed patients may be testing the drug in exactly the window where the mechanism no longer responds — and the age and sex composition of the cohort is not a nuisance covariate but a determinant of whether you see anything. Slice physiology in mice is not a trial, and release deficits are not degeneration. But this is the kind of preclinical result that should feed back into enrollment criteria rather than sitting in a discussion section.

Third, staying with Parkinson's and moving to measurement proper. A longitudinal proteomics effort across eight hundred and fifty plasma and twelve hundred cerebrospinal fluid samples from the Parkinson's Progression Markers Initiative set out to find biofluid markers of disease burden and disease activity. The finding I keep returning to is not any individual protein. It is that they had to rebuild the outcome before the biomarkers would appear.

The motor rating scale, which remains the regulatory gold standard, is noisy enough that half the variance in one-year change may be measurement error, and it is contaminated by symptomatic therapy. So they adjusted scores for the daily dose of each of a dozen antiparkinsonian medications and then applied isotonic regression to enforce monotonicity within each participant. About a third of observations were unaffected; roughly three percent moved by more than half a standard deviation. That recalibrated score correlates far better with every independent measure of disease — most strikingly, correlation with dopamine transporter imaging binding ratio went from zero point three four to zero point five three. The gain came from fixing the outcome, not from a better assay.

Two further results matter. Symptomatic dopaminergic therapy both perturbs and normalises the biofluid proteome — early-disease protein changes often abated after medication initiation, which means a naive longitudinal study would read treatment as disease stabilisation. And among the strongest plasma predictors of motor score is leptin, the adipocyte hormone, outperforming neurofilament light. That is a warning about what a proteomic burden marker can actually be: in a population where disease burden tracks body composition, an adiposity signal will out-predict a neurodegeneration signal. Several markers were also non-monotonic — oxytocin elevated early and very low in advanced disease — which breaks the assumption that a progression marker moves in one direction.

Fourth, and still on the theme that the parcel you choose determines the answer. A normative modelling study in nearly eighty-two thousand UK Biobank participants aged forty-five to eighty-seven asks whether the basal forebrain cholinergic system is anatomically uniform in its vulnerability, by building age- and sex-specific reference models of grey-matter volume separately for the nucleus basalis of Meynert and for the septal and diagonal-band compartments, then scoring each individual's deviation from the norm.

It is not uniform, and the asymmetry is striking. The nucleus basalis declines across the entire observed age range; the septal compartment is flat until the late fifties and then falls. Across diagnosed neurological conditions, the deficit concentrates in the nucleus basalis — in Parkinson's disease the nucleus basalis deviation is clearly negative while the septal compartment is, if anything, slightly positive. Prospectively, in a sample followed to incident diagnosis, lower baseline nucleus basalis volume predicted both dementia and Parkinson's, with hazard ratios around zero point four five and zero point five six per unit of normative deviation, surviving adjustment for deprivation, education, smoking, alcohol, body mass index, and polygenic risk. For dementia, adding hippocampal and cortical deviation attenuated it to about zero point six two but did not abolish it; for Parkinson's, adjusting for nigral susceptibility left it essentially unchanged. The septal compartment, entered alone, predicted dementia — and that association vanished completely once the nucleus basalis was in the same model.

The incident event counts are small — a hundred and twenty-nine dementia and seventy-six Parkinson's diagnoses — and the nucleus basalis effect on dementia violates proportional hazards, strongest near the scan and attenuating over follow-up, which is what you would expect from a marker of imminent rather than remote risk. But for anyone designing a cholinergic trial or a cholinergic imaging endpoint, the message is that "basal forebrain" is the wrong unit of analysis, and that common genetic risk explains very little of this.

Fifth, a short and genuinely important piece of receptor pharmacology with an immediate regulatory edge. Six-methylnicotine — also sold as metatine — is a structural analogue of nicotine being added to vape solutions and oral pouches, including some marketed as nicotine-free, and because it occurs only in trace amounts in tobacco it is currently unregulated in the United States, though several European countries and Australia have moved. Earlier this year the German federal risk assessment institute concluded that a health risk assessment was not possible, because almost all the available data were industry-funded. This paper is the independent pharmacology.

It is not reassuring. Binding affinity at alpha-four beta-two is essentially the same as nicotine's — but at alpha-three beta-four, six-methylnicotine binds four to five times more tightly, in heterologous cells and in native tissue alike. Functionally the gap is much larger. In a calcium-reporter assay it is thirteen-fold more potent than nicotine at alpha-four beta-two and ten-fold more potent at alpha-three beta-four, with the same maximal response, so this is a potency shift, not a partial agonist story. Whole-cell recordings show peak currents roughly four-fold larger at alpha-four beta-two and ten-fold larger at alpha-three beta-four, with slower deactivation. It also desensitises more potently — three-fold at alpha-four beta-two and fifteen-fold at alpha-three beta-four, which flattens nicotine's usual selectivity between those subtypes. And two weeks of oral administration to rats at one milligram per kilogram per day raised cortical receptor binding by about twenty-five percent, the same upregulation nicotine produces.

The alpha-three beta-four numbers are the ones I would not skip past. That subtype dominates autonomic ganglia, adrenal chromaffin cells, and the habenulo-interpeduncular pathway implicated in withdrawal aversion. A compound that is ten-fold more potent there than nicotine, and that loses nicotine's subtype preference in desensitisation, is not a nicotine substitute with a different name. It is a different drug, and it is in products sold on the claim that they contain no drug at all.

Sixth, the week's psychedelics paper, and it makes an argument about context that the clinical field has asserted for years without electrophysiological backing. This is the largest psilocybin EEG dataset assembled — fifty-six participants with matched pre-drug baselines — and crucially, recordings were made in four different conditions: eyes-closed rest, movie viewing, guided meditation, and music listening. Source activity was estimated with minimum-norm estimation across six frequency bands.

At rest the findings replicate what you would expect: reduced alpha power, increased gamma-range power, an elevated alpha peak frequency, and reduced alpha-band connectivity within and between higher-order networks implicated in top-down control. The new result is the interaction. Relative to eyes-closed rest, structured sensory environments attenuated — and in places reversed — the drug's alpha-band connectivity changes. The direction and magnitude of the drug effect depended on what the person was doing.

This is more than a methodological footnote about resting-state recordings, though it is certainly that, and it should make anyone comparing psychedelic imaging studies across paradigms nervous. The substantive claim is that "setting" is not a clinical nicety layered on top of a fixed pharmacological effect — the cortical signature of the pharmacological effect is itself a function of the sensory environment. If you are building a psilocybin therapy protocol, the music playlist is part of the intervention in a way that shows up in the alpha band.

Seventh, a mechanistic palate cleanser from fly sleep research that nonetheless reframes a concept everyone in this field uses. Sleep-promoting neurons projecting to the dorsal fan-shaped body are the best-characterised sleep-pressure integrator in any organism — they accumulate a record of sleep need as oxidation of a cofactor on a potassium channel beta-subunit, driven by lipid peroxidation downstream of mitochondrial electron leak. The question here was what those neurons actually do with that record.

Two-photon calcium imaging in vivo shows rhythmic transients at roughly half a hertz. Individual cell bodies are largely uncorrelated with each other, and a handful of pacemaker-like cells account for most of the compound dendritic signal. Bilateral imaging then shows the left and right hemisphere populations alternating out of phase, with crosscorrelation minima at zero lag — the signature of two mutually inhibitory half-centres, the same circuit motif that drives swimming in molluscs and central pattern generation in vertebrate brainstem.

The conceptual consequence is what I would hold onto. Sleep pressure is not encoded as a switch that flips when a threshold is crossed. It is encoded in the amplitude of a continuously running oscillation that keeps going during wakefulness — an analogue signal broadcast to the action-selection circuitry, which then has to perform its own analogue-to-digital conversion to produce a discrete sleep-wake transition. Whether the mammalian homologue is a half-centre or something else is open, but the framing — pressure as oscillation amplitude rather than accumulated scalar — is worth importing.

Eighth, two genetics papers that pair well, starting with rare variants. A study in the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development cohort and the All of Us programme asks what constraint-weighted rare variant burden does in population samples rather than clinical ones. Rather than counting variants, they sum the selection coefficients of the affected genes, separately for protein-truncating and damaging missense variants, which weights each hit by how intolerant its gene is to loss.

In thirty-five hundred children, one additional typical constrained gene carrying a truncating variant is associated with cognition about a fifth of a standard deviation lower and with autism at an odds ratio of one point eight. In three hundred thousand adults, the same load associates with autism, schizophrenia at about one point five, and intellectual disability at about two — rising to nearly ninefold odds of intellectual disability for the most constrained genes. The population framing is the striking part: carriers of a constrained truncating variant are twelve percent of the adult cohort but thirty-one percent of intellectual disability cases, twenty percent of schizophrenia cases, and seventeen percent of autism cases. And this load is essentially uncorrelated with polygenic score — it is a separate axis, not a rare tail of the common-variant signal.

The result I would flag for anyone thinking about clinical translation is portability. In African-ancestry participants, the truncating-variant load retained seventy-one to a hundred and twenty-five percent of its European-ancestry effect size, against twenty-three to sixty-two percent for matched polygenic scores. The failure of polygenic scores to transport across ancestries is a property of linkage structure and allele frequency, not of biology — and a constraint-weighted rare variant burden, which indexes gene function directly, largely sidesteps it.

Ninth, the common-variant companion, and a more cautionary read. A laboratory-wide association scan in the Penn Medicine Biobank tested polygenic scores for twenty-five phenotypes against seventy-eight laboratory tests and six vital signs, in thirty-five thousand European-ancestry and ten thousand African-ancestry participants. In the European sample, psychiatric polygenic scores partition into coherent physiological profiles: anxiety, attention-deficit disorder, major depression and post-traumatic stress disorder share a metabolic and inflammatory signature — higher body mass index, triglycerides and glucose, lower high-density lipoprotein, higher pulse and monocytes. Anorexia nervosa, obsessive-compulsive disorder and schizophrenia show the opposing body-composition pattern, and bipolar disorder sits apart with a renal and electrolyte profile.

That is a satisfying picture, and I would resist reading it as biomarkers. The odds ratios for the psychiatric scores run between one point zero two and one point zero eight — population-level physiological correlates of genetic liability in a clinical biobank with a mean age of sixty-four, not anything that discriminates a patient. And the ancestry gap is stark: twenty-two of twenty-five scores had a significant lab association in European-ancestry participants, four of nine in African-ancestry participants — the previous item's portability problem on the other side of the ledger.

Tenth, a small and admirably honest paper on language-model-assisted drug repurposing. The tool takes a plain-language query and assembles candidate drugs from Open Targets, ClinicalTrials-dot-gov, UniProt and PubMed, filtering for drugs that modulate every implicated target in the right direction, with every candidate traceable to a specific source — a database record, a trial, or a language-model proposal. They then ran a retrospective test on clemastine, the antihistamine that was pursued as a remyelinating agent in multiple sclerosis.

The results are the kind that usually get softened. Clemastine was retrieved in every replicate when the query was worded mechanistically, and in none when it was worded as a disease. No structured query retrieved it at all with the language model disabled. And the ranked output reflected the evidence supporting clemastine but not the later evidence of harm in progressive disease, reachable only through an on-demand literature search. The authors' own conclusion is that the tool is better at tracing evidence around a hypothesis than at generating one — an accurate description of what retrieval-plus-filtering does, and a fair description of this whole category. The dependence on phrasing is what I would generalise: a system whose recall depends on whether you said "remyelination" or "multiple sclerosis" is not searching a space, it is following your framing.

Closing light, a clean engineering result on where language models should and should not be doing the work. Medical calculators require extracting the right variables from a free-text note and then executing a scoring rule exactly, and prompting a model to do both at once entangles rule recall, extraction, arithmetic and output formatting in a single generative step. The framework here retrieves the calculator specification, generates a reusable executable tool from it, uses a schema-constrained extractor to map the note to calculator inputs, and then computes deterministically. On the MedCalc benchmark it reaches about eighty-seven percent final-answer accuracy, twenty-four points over one-shot prompting and seventeen over supervised fine-tuning alone.

The error analysis is the useful part. The remaining failures are concentrated in clinical-semantic extraction — reading the note correctly — and in benchmark-specific output conventions, not in arithmetic. Which is the right division of labour made visible: the model's job is to understand clinical language, and the moment a rule can be written down, it should be executed by code rather than generated. That principle is the quiet common thread with the first item too. The trouble starts when we ask a language model for a number it has no mechanism to compute.
