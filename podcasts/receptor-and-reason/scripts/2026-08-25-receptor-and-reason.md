## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. I'm your host, and this is your daily pass through CNS pharmacology, psychiatric and neurology drug discovery, and the computational machinery closing in on both. Twelve items today, and the lead settles an argument that has been running for twenty-five years — though not in the direction either side wanted.

The question is whether the adult human hippocampus makes new neurons, and whether that has anything to do with depression. A group has now put a multiomic atlas on it in Nature Medicine: single-nucleus transcriptomes and chromatin accessibility from the same nuclei, spatial transcriptomics at two resolutions, and regional proteomics, on postmortem hippocampus from unmedicated people who died with major depressive disorder and neurotypical controls. Four hundred ninety-five thousand nuclei, eleven cases and nineteen controls after quality control. The unmedicated part matters — it takes antidepressant exposure out of the picture.

They recover a neurogenic lineage, and they recover it the hard way, from two modalities at once — quiescent stem cells through activated stem cells, intermediate progenitors, doublecortin-positive neuroblasts, and two immature granule cell states. Trajectory inference gives a bifurcation: one branch to mature granule cells, one to a persistently immature state. And there's a path running backwards, from mature granule cells into that immature state — dematuration, previously described in marmoset. Those immature-marker cells sit in the granule cell layer rather than the subgranular zone, which is what you'd expect if mature cells are de-differentiating rather than young cells lingering.

Now the depression result, which is more specific than the usual story. Neurogenesis in the depressed hippocampus is not depleted. It's stalled. There are more quiescent stem cells and fewer neuroblasts, so the pipeline is jammed at the front rather than emptied — confirmed with protein: fewer subgranular zone cells expressing nestin and Ki67, fewer doublecortin-positive cells, and, the control that makes the argument, no change at all in the persistently immature population. If this were tissue-wide degeneration you'd expect that population to move too.

Two things in the mechanism are worth carrying home. The first is that the gene module rising earliest in the depressed trajectory, in quiescent and activated stem cells, is interferon signaling. Not a stress program, not a metabolic program — an antiviral one, in the compartment that's supposed to be dividing. The second is serotonergic, and it's the kind of detail that changes what you'd do with a drug. HTR4 and HTR1E are down in intermediate progenitors, HTR7 is down in immature granule cells, and 5-HT2A and 5-HT2C are down across the spatial subfields. So the receptors an SSRI is meant to work through, indirectly, are themselves downregulated in exactly the cells the neurogenic hypothesis says are the target. There's also a population nobody had described in human hippocampus — a medium-spiny-neuron-like GABAergic interneuron with high proenkephalin — which turns out to be the most dysregulated cell type in the dataset, with elevated immediate-early genes suggesting it is trying to clamp down on a stress-hyperactivated dentate gyrus.

Staying in the hippocampus, and staying with antidepressants, a small preprint that I like out of proportion to its sample size. Four Long-Evans rats, silicon probes in intermediate hippocampus, and a single ten-milligram-per-kilogram dose of psilocybin, with saline sessions the day before and the day after as within-animal controls. The readout is sharp-wave ripples — the hundred-fifty-to-two-hundred-fifty-hertz events in CA1 that carry replay and that we know causally support consolidation.

Psilocybin does not change ripple rate. What it changes is ripple shape. Duration goes up and peak frequency goes down, consistently, in all four animals. Sharp-wave amplitude goes up in three of four, which points at the CA3-to-CA1 limb specifically, since the sharp wave is the CA3 population burst depolarizing CA1 dendrites. And the sleep result is the interesting part. The animals stopped moving but did not sleep: non-REM was sharply reduced, latency to the first sustained non-REM bout was long in every animal and infinite in one, and instead the recordings filled with high-voltage spindles — the waveform you normally see for a few minutes at the wake-to-sleep transition. Psilocybin parks the animal at that transition and leaves it there for hours.

Why that combination matters: long-duration ripples are what you get after novelty and after a memory task, and optogenetically prolonging ripples improves memory. So the drug produces, in the awake animal, the ripple signature that normally requires either learning or sleep — a mechanistically concrete version of a claim the psychedelic field usually makes at the level of network connectivity, and a possible neural handle on why set and setting predict outcome. The honest limits are four animals, and a confound the authors flag themselves: the first saline session also had almost no sleep, because it was the animals' first full injection, so the second may be showing rebound rather than baseline. For contrast, LSD in a previous study lowered ripple frequency too, but cut ripple rate and slightly shortened duration. Different psychedelics, different ripple signatures.

Third, ketamine, and a study designed to isolate a specific psychological target rather than a diagnosis. This is a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled functional MRI trial in Translational Psychiatry — seventy-five healthy volunteers enrolled, sixty-two analyzed, randomized one-to-one-to-one to placebo then placebo, placebo then ketamine, or lamotrigine pretreatment then ketamine. The analysis uses coactivation patterns, which is a way of asking what discrete network configurations the brain visits and how often, rather than what correlates with what on average.

At baseline, self-reported rumination tracked the occurrence rate of one particular configuration: default mode network and frontoparietal network active at the same time. That's odd on its face — those two are normally anticorrelated, one for self-referential processing and one for cognitive control — and the natural reading is a failure to disengage one while engaging the other. Ketamine acutely cut the time spent in that state relative to both baseline and placebo, and the size of the reduction scaled with how much the individual ruminated. Twenty-four hours later it was gone. Lamotrigine pretreatment, which blocks presynaptic glutamate release and is the standard tool for asking which ketamine effects run through the glutamate surge, nominally attenuated the effect but not significantly.

The caveat is structural: these were healthy people, so the antidepressant relevance is an inference, and the authors are careful about it. They also point at a real problem in the field — a recent study in treatment-resistant depression found a frontoparietal-dominant pattern normalizing after serial ketamine, which sounds like the same finding and probably isn't, because the patterns were derived differently. Data-driven state decompositions are not portable across papers, and we talk as though they are.

Fourth, and moving from mood to substance use, a Nature Neuroscience paper on how a social variable becomes a pharmacological one. Fourteen days of social isolation escalates alcohol drinking in male mice and reduces it in females. The circuit is basolateral amygdala neurons projecting to medial prefrontal cortex, recorded from retrogradely labeled cells: isolation raises their intrinsic excitability in males and lowers it in females. That sex-opposite direction is the strongest thing in the paper, because in both sexes the excitability tracks the drinking. Isolated males are more excitable and drink more; isolated females are less excitable and drink less. Same circuit variable, same relationship to behavior, opposite response to the same experience.

They then close the loop in both directions. The projection-specific signal encodes alcohol better than undifferentiated amygdala activity and predicts intake; driving it increases drinking; inhibiting it during isolation reduces drinking. And isolation potentiates downstream prefrontal responses to alcohol, which driving the projection reproduces. The authors flag that the excitability shifts are modest and concentrated near threshold — though near-threshold shifts are exactly where recruitment gets decided. One more finding worth having: social rank predicted alcohol intake in both sexes, subordinates drinking more, and it did not predict water intake, which argues the effect is on reward rather than territorial drinking.

Which is a decent segue into two pipeline items from the same week's neuropsychiatric news. The first is cannabis use disorder, where there is still no approved pharmacotherapy for more than nineteen million affected people in the United States, and where withdrawal is the thing that drives relapse. PleoPharma has taken a six-and-a-half-million-dollar NIDA grant to move a once-daily oral candidate into phase three for cannabis withdrawal. The mechanism as described is combined CB1 pathway modulation plus correction of mesolimbic neurotransmitter dysregulation, which is vague enough that I'd want the actual composition before commenting. What's real is a phase 2b that hit its primary endpoint with a dose-response signal and no serious adverse events, plus Fast Track designation. Withdrawal mitigation is a sensible place to start because the endpoint is short and measurable — but it also means the trial won't tell you much about sustained abstinence.

The second is more interesting pharmacologically. FDA has accepted Teva's new drug application for ecopipam in pediatric Tourette syndrome, with priority review and a target action date late in the first quarter of 2027. Ecopipam is a selective D1 and D5 receptor antagonist, and if approved it would be the first novel mechanism in Tourette syndrome in more than fifty years. Everything we currently use goes at D2 — antipsychotics, or VMAT2 inhibitors upstream of it — with the movement-disorder and metabolic burden that implies in children. Coming at tics from the D1 side is a genuinely different bet about where in the basal ganglia the pathology sits. The supporting data: a phase 2b with a significant improvement in Yale Global Tic Severity Scale total tic score at twelve weeks, durability in the open-label extension, and a phase three randomized-withdrawal design showing a fifty-three percent reduction in relapse risk over twelve weeks. Randomized withdrawal is reasonable for a fluctuating disorder, but it does mean the pivotal claim rests on enriched responders rather than an all-comers comparison.

Which brings us to the dopamine paper of the day, and it's the one that reframes the others. There has been a genuine tension in striatal physiology: slice work says dopamine raises direct-pathway excitability through Gs-coupled D1 and lowers indirect-pathway excitability through Gi-coupled D2, but in vivo, optogenetically moving dopamine barely budges striatal activity, and disrupting phasic dopamine release doesn't impair basic movement. A preprint resolves it with a measurement nobody had made: triple-color spectrally resolved fiber photometry in dorsolateral striatum in freely moving mice, recording dopamine, glutamate release, and pathway-specific projection neuron activity simultaneously, with unsupervised behavior classification on top.

The finding is that dopamine is not a movement signal. Its correlation with speed and turning is near zero, and it actually rises after locomotion stops. What dopamine predicts is the ratio — the projection neuron's output relative to the glutamate input arriving at it. Higher dopamine, higher input-output efficacy in direct-pathway neurons; higher dopamine, lower efficacy in indirect-pathway neurons. And the coupling is confined to a window from roughly two hundred milliseconds before to a hundred milliseconds after the glutamate peak, which is the right timescale for a G-protein cascade and not for anything faster.

Then they move it pharmacologically in both directions. Methylphenidate pushed direct-pathway gain up and indirect-pathway output down, and biased behavior toward mobile and turning states; reserpine, depleting vesicular stores, did the reverse and produced immobility. There's even a satisfying asymmetry — methylphenidate moved the direct pathway more, reserpine the indirect pathway more, consistent with D2's higher apparent affinity leaving it closer to saturated at baseline. The take-home for anyone reading dopamine as a code: it isn't one. It's a gain term on somebody else's code, which is exactly why manipulating it looks weak when you measure activity and strong when you measure the transformation.

Eighth, and the last of the wet items, another Nature Medicine paper — and one where the surprising result is that more of a good thing is worse. The resource is the Lothian Birth Cohort of 1936: people IQ-tested at age eleven, retested from age seventy every three years, with postmortem brain donation. Eight hundred sixty-six had follow-up testing, and essentially all of them declined; the authors stratified by rate of decline into none, mild and severe, then went to the tissue.

In severe decliners, myelin integrity is lost — but not in the direction you'd guess. Large-diameter axons, above a micron, carry abnormally thick myelin, the myelinated axon population shifts toward smaller calibers, and oligodendrocyte density is increased. All of it held independent of postmortem interval, brain pH, and Alzheimer's and small-vessel pathology. Since myelin thickness and axon caliber together are predicted to account for eighty-five percent of the variance in human conduction velocity, thicker myelin on the big axons is not benign.

The mechanism they land on is NRF2. Oligodendrocyte NRF2 expression is reduced in severe decliners, and deleting NRF2 from oligodendrocytes in mice reproduces the whole phenotype — thicker myelin, a shift toward myelinating smaller axons, and a blunting of the cognitive improvement aged mice normally show with repeated testing. That's the part that turns an association into a claim. And it hands you a repurposing hypothesis with an approved drug attached: dimethyl fumarate is an NRF2 activator, it improves cognition in rodents and in multiple sclerosis, and until now nobody could say which cell type was carrying that. The broader lesson is uncomfortable for the learning-and-myelination literature: in aging, oligodendrocytes are not reliably supportive. They can go past the point where more myelin helps, and myelinated axons are more vulnerable to degeneration than bare ones.

Now the computational half, and I want to take three papers together because they're all really asking the same question — how do you know an AI system did the science, as opposed to describing it?

Start with the optimistic one. A preprint introduces GUIA, a research-agent network built on the agent-to-agent protocol, and the point is interoperability rather than capability. Instead of one monolithic scientific agent, you expose specialized agents — theirs include a computational biologist, a medicinal chemist, a structural biologist and a critic, with ninety-seven tools between them — behind standard HTTP endpoints with capability cards, then plug in other people's agents without integrating their codebases. They wire in Pathology-o3 for whole-slide images, ChemCrow for chemistry, CRISPR-GPT for editing design, and Eubiota, itself a four-agent microbiome system, some of it running on separate hardware.

The benchmark numbers are the tell, and they cut in a direction the paper underplays. On a seven-hundred-twenty-question biomedical benchmark aligned with the in-house agents' expertise, GUIA gets seventy-eight percent against fifty-six for the bare model. Fine. On a microbiome benchmark about drug effects on microbial growth, GUIA alone gets forty-five percent — barely above the bare model's thirty-five — and GUIA plus Eubiota gets ninety-three. That forty-eight-point jump is not the network reasoning better. It's the specialist's tools and databases arriving. And on medical question answering, where GUIA was already strong, adding a cross-server clinical agent produced what the authors themselves call a subtle improvement. So interoperability buys you coverage where you had none and almost nothing where you already had competence — still worth having, and the case studies are more than a demo. The system ran a virtual screen against the potassium channel KCNK9 down to nineteen hits, proposed a spatial subtyping of non-small-cell lung cancer from imaging mass cytometry that separated survival, and nominated CASZ1 as a novel androgen receptor coregulator, which the humans then validated by knockdown, chromatin immunoprecipitation at androgen receptor target genes, and a proliferation assay. That last one is an agent-generated hypothesis with wet-lab confirmation, a higher bar than most of this literature clears.

Which makes the second paper the necessary corrective. K-Bench takes a hundred seventy-eight first-turn requests verbatim from live traffic on a commercial scientific-agent platform — underspecified, with attachments, no reference answers — and runs each under nine frontier models in identical sandboxes. Sixteen hundred runs, forty thousand scored judgments, three identity-blinded judges against an eight-dimension rubric, and critically, the judges get read-only access to the files each run left on disk rather than just the transcript.

On a rubric whose top-band anchor means a domain scientist would accept the work with minor edits, no model clears the line under all three judges. The best pooled mean is eight point oh four with a confidence interval straddling the threshold, and two of the three judges rank a different model first. But the two numbers I'd put on a slide are these: scientific accuracy trails communication by one point one one within every single one of the nine models, in the same direction each time; and forty-eight percent of runs finished with no file on disk at all. Overclaiming is the leading failure tag, at thirty-one percent of assessments. The authors' conclusion is that the ordering of systems reproduces across judges and the absolute level does not, so report the ranking and treat the number as a property of your instrument. Two caveats: this is vendor-authored on that vendor's own traffic, and the judges are language models scoring language models. But "eloquence is not a deliverable" is the right frame, and the accuracy-versus-communication gap is a finding no leaderboard position would have surfaced.

The third one puts human raters where the rubric was, and the two disagree almost completely. A preprint describes MIND, a domain-specific model retrieval-grounded on patient-education material from authoritative psychiatric organizations, evaluated on fifty common patient questions about escitalopram against ChatGPT and against OpenEvidence. MIND answered thirty-eight and declined twelve, which is the behavior you want. On the automated rubric, MIND won everything — accuracy, clarity, completeness, nuance, safety, referral appropriateness, all at p below point zero zero one.

Then ten board-certified psychiatrists rated the same responses, and it inverted. Accuracy: seventy-two percent for MIND against seventy-nine for ChatGPT, significant in ChatGPT's favor with a negligible effect size. Safety: identical, eighty-four percent each. MIND's only real win was completeness, seventy against fifty-six. And asked which they preferred, the psychiatrists chose ChatGPT fifty-eight percent of the time — seventy-two percent among early-career raters, while the two late-career raters leaned the other way. The proposed mechanism for the accuracy inversion is the useful part: ChatGPT gave shorter, less complete answers, and shorter answers are less likely to contain anything a rater can mark wrong. Completeness and rated accuracy trade off. Two details worth keeping. MIND, trained exclusively on patient-facing material, answered at an eleventh-grade reading level against ChatGPT's ninth — because the source corpus itself averaged tenth grade, well above the American Medical Association's sixth-grade recommendation. And the psychiatrists said eighty-four percent of these questions needed a clinician, while neither model's referral behavior correlated with that judgment at all. Small, unblinded, no clinician-written control arm, slight inter-rater agreement. But if your evaluation is a rubric and your deployment is a clinic, this is the size of the gap to assume.

And to close, a short perspective in npj Digital Medicine that follows straight on. The argument is that the first reader of a paper is now frequently a machine, and the field's response has been to wait for better machines — when the cheaper fix is for authors to state explicitly what may and may not be concluded. The evidence is good: a study of roughly five thousand summaries across ten models found more extreme generalizations than the source supported even when the prompt asked for accuracy, and a nonsense phrase introduced by an AI summary has now been repeated in more than twenty peer-reviewed papers. Their proposal is a peer-reviewed, machine-readable metadata block constraining what an ingesting model can infer, plus a plain-language claims-and-scope label for humans, worked through on the diet-soda-and-stroke paper everyone read as causal. Whether those specific artifacts survive is beside the point. Reporting standards formalized how we describe what we did; nothing yet formalizes what we're entitled to say about it, and that's where the losses are happening.

That's twelve. A stalled rather than emptied neurogenic pipeline in depression, psilocybin producing the ripple signature of learning without sleep, and three separate reminders that an agent's prose and an agent's output directory are different objects. See you tomorrow.
