## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. I'm your host, and this is your daily pass through CNS pharmacology, psychiatric and neurology drug discovery, and the computational machinery closing in on both. Ten items today, and the thread through most of them is about choosing the right dependent variable — in the lead paper the drug effect is invisible if you measure firing rate and unmistakable if you measure how the spikes are bunched. Later on, two attempts to make AI act on biology rather than read about it, and we close with a quiet deflation of a very influential behavioral-phenotyping result.

We start with the largest single-unit psychedelic dataset I have seen. A collaboration between the Allen Institute and the translational depression group at UC Irvine recorded more than forty-six thousand single units across thirty-five mice using simultaneous multi-region Neuropixels probes, along with scalp EEG, pupillometry and locomotion, through the acute hours of psilocybin. The pharmacological dissection uses ketanserin, the 5-HT2A and 2C antagonist that abolishes the perceptual effects in humans and the head-twitch response in rodents.

The headline is a dissociation. Psilocybin barely moved mean firing rate — a modest decrease in olfactory areas and in layer 5 of somatosensory cortex, and nothing at all in thalamus, hippocampus or striatum. What it reconfigured was bursting, bidirectionally, with a clean anatomical logic. Reticular thalamic burst rate rose hard, effect size around 0.8, and anterior thalamic nuclei rose comparably; first-order somatomotor relay nuclei rose more modestly. First-order sensory geniculate nuclei went the other way, down. In hippocampus the proportion of bursting units fell across CA1, CA2 and CA3, most dramatically in CA3 at an effect size of minus 1.4. Striatal burst rate rose. Nearly all of it was abolished by ketanserin pretreatment.

Bursts matter here because they drive postsynaptic targets far more reliably than isolated spikes and are specifically required for burst-dependent potentiation, so this is a result about a coding channel rather than about excitability. And the direction pattern maps onto receptor pharmacology. Reticular and anterior thalamus are densely innervated from dorsal raphe and express 5-HT2A postsynaptically; at the retinogeniculate synapse, by contrast, serotonin acts presynaptically to suppress glutamate release from retinal terminals, reducing exactly the afferent drive that triggers relay-cell bursts. Opposite directions, two loci, one transmitter.

The framework the authors reach for is Crick's searchlight hypothesis, in which reticular inhibition does not merely suppress relay cells but switches them into burst mode, so bursting marks which thalamocortical channel is transiently amplified. Psilocybin looks like a pharmacological co-optation of that gate, driving the reticular nucleus into burst mode without the corticothalamic feedback that normally recruits it. That is a concrete circuit story for the bottom-up versus top-down rebalancing that predictive-processing accounts of psychedelics have been asserting at the level of metaphor.

Two caveats, both stated plainly in the paper. The cortical effects, including the deep prefrontal result you would most want given where the receptors are, did not survive correction across layers; the authors report uncorrected values and call them trends. And the mesoscale story runs opposite to the spiking story: psilocybin suppressed high-gamma and resting occipital alpha, and ketanserin did not abolish that — if anything it amplified it. So the EEG signature people treat as a psychedelic biomarker is, in this dataset, not 5-HT2A-dependent.

From pharmacology to stimulation, with a neurochemical readout and a genuinely useful negative result. A randomized, sham-controlled, double-blind crossover proton spectroscopy study in seventy people with alcohol use disorder, out of the Medical University of South Carolina. Two protocols, each against its own sham: continuous theta burst to the frontal pole of medial prefrontal cortex, and ten hertz stimulation to dorsolateral prefrontal cortex. Four metabolites measured under the coil — glutamate, glutamine, glycine and GABA.

Continuous theta burst to medial prefrontal cortex significantly lowered glutamine, glutamate's direct metabolic precursor, and glycine, the co-agonist required for NMDA receptor channel activation. Glutamate itself was mixed — not significant overall, but a marginal effect qualified by a marginal interaction with the delay between stimulation and scan, such that the reduction appeared only in participants scanned sooner. GABA, nothing. Ten hertz to dorsolateral prefrontal cortex produced nothing at all on any of the four, and electric field modelling rules out the boring explanation, since the dorsolateral fields were the higher of the two.

So one protocol shows single-session neurochemical target engagement under the coil and the other shows none. The authors flag the apparent paradox themselves: untreated alcohol use disorder is characterized by lower levels of these same metabolites, and here is a candidate treatment pushing them lower still. Their answer is that medial prefrontal theta burst has separately improved outcomes in a sham-controlled trial — which also means nobody should read these metabolites as a direction-of-benefit biomarker. What this study delivers is dosimetry: a way to find out whether a protocol did anything measurable in tissue before you spend three years discovering clinically that it did not.

Staying with alcohol and glutamate, a conditional knockout preprint inverts an assumption. Tamoxifen-inducible deletion of STAT3 from adult astrocytes asks what astrocytic STAT3 does in a healthy adult brain rather than in injury or neurodegeneration, where it is normally studied as a reactivity switch.

Losing it reduced GFAP, as expected from a known STAT3 target, but also reduced glutamate transporter transcripts including the one encoding GLT-1, and GLT-1 protein itself in male prefrontal cortex. Prelimbic pyramidal neurons showed larger spontaneous excitatory currents, consistent with impaired clearance. And the behavior: no change in locomotion or anxiety-like measures, but male knockouts drank significantly less ethanol in a binge paradigm.

That is the inversion worth flagging. The field's default is that impaired astrocytic clearance and a hyperglutamatergic prefrontal state drive drinking; here you get the hyperglutamatergic state and less drinking. Small, male-only, one model, modest transporter changes — but if the direction holds, astrocytic STAT3 is permissive for alcohol intake rather than protective, and it sits upstream of a transporter people have spent years trying to drug. Somebody should try to reproduce the sign before building on it.

Now to pain, and the most therapeutically pointed paper of the day, from Susan Ingram's group at Oregon Health and Science University, open access in Neuropsychopharmacology. Inflammatory hyperalgesia in rat hindpaw, and the question is how that hyperalgesia resolves.

The answer is that it does not fully resolve — it gets masked. Levels of 2-AG in ventrolateral periaqueductal gray stay elevated to at least day twenty-one, when hyperalgesia has behaviorally resolved, with correspondingly enhanced CB1-dependent suppression of GABA release. Give rimonabant, the CB1 inverse agonist withdrawn years ago for psychiatric adverse effects, as a single injection on day ten or twenty-one, and the hyperalgesia comes straight back. So this is endocannabinoid-mediated latent sensitization: ongoing 2-AG tone at CB1 receptors is actively holding the pain down, and the upstream driver is corticosterone at glucocorticoid receptors in the periaqueductal gray, which stimulates that 2-AG synthesis in the first place.

The pharmacological sting is in the tail. Presynaptic CB1 receptors normally resist desensitization, holding up against exogenous agonist for hours and against three weeks of elevated endogenous 2-AG here. But in the inflamed state, corticosterone or a CB1 agonist layered on top of that endogenous tone does desensitize them, and blocking GRK2 and 3 restores CB1-mediated suppression of GABA release. So the endocannabinoid system in persistent inflammation is already near its ceiling, and a cannabinoid analgesic on top pushes the receptor into desensitization rather than further analgesia. That is a mechanistic account of why cannabinoid analgesics keep producing statistically significant but clinically small effects in chronic pain trials, and it predicts a narrow therapeutic window rather than a dose-response you can climb. It also nominates kinase-targeted co-therapy as the way to widen it.

From peripheral signaling to a genuinely surprising intervention. Maya Opendak's group at Kennedy Krieger and Johns Hopkins asked whether the biological embedding of adverse caregiving runs through bile acids — cholesterol-derived endocrine signaling molecules that also influence neuronal function and gene expression centrally.

Two rat models. The deconstructed adversity model separates adverse social experience from stress alone: pups receive repeated shock either alone or in the presence of a maternal-odor-producing dam. Social adversity, not non-social adversity, produced the deficit — delayed approach to the dam's ventrum, the affiliative region where nursing and grooming happen, with normal time spent there once they arrived. So the impairment is in the decision to initiate affiliative contact, not in maintaining it. Serum metabolomics showed sex-, age- and adversity-type-dependent changes in bile acid and tryptophan metabolism, and basolateral amygdala sequencing showed remodeled co-expression modules rather than large global shifts.

Then the test. In the limited-bedding scarcity model, daily oral chenodeoxycholic acid through the adversity window rescued the ventrum-approach deficit; cholic acid, the other primary bile acid, only trended, and neither did anything in control-reared pups, so this is not a general enhancer of social approach. The prior is real: circulating chenodeoxycholic acid specifically, not cholic acid, is reduced in major depressive disorder and normalizes with remission.

The caveats matter. The rescue was significant pooled but not within either sex alone, and the sex carrying the deficit flipped between models — females in the deconstructed model, males in limited bedding. The adolescent phenotype was thin to begin with and bile acids did nothing to it. So this is rescue of an infant endpoint, not correction of a trajectory. But chenodeoxycholic acid is approved, the pathway is peripheral and therefore accessible during exactly the window you would want to reach, and an oral bile acid is a very different intervention shape from anything on offer for early-life adversity.

Two dopamine items now, and the first introduces a molecular player I had not seen implicated. Anoctamin 2, the calcium-activated chloride channel also called TMEM16B, is known for sensory transduction, but it is expressed in prefrontal cortex, striatum, substantia nigra, ventral tegmental area and cerebellum, and nobody had asked what it does there. CRISPR knockouts showed age-dependent hyperactivity, impaired inhibitory behavioral control, altered risk-related responding and selective motor deficits, with spatial learning preserved. The neurochemistry is the part worth remembering: tyrosine hydroxylase expression and phosphorylation both fell, dopamine fell, the transporter was unchanged, and receptor changes were region- and subtype-dependent. So the lesion sits upstream, at synthesis, rather than at reuptake or at dopaminergic identity. A calcium-activated chloride channel modulating dopamine biosynthesis is not a mechanism I would have predicted, and if it replicates it is a new node for the inhibitory-control phenotypes that cut across attention deficit disorder, substance use and mania. It is a constitutive knockout, so developmental compensation is unexcluded.

The second dopamine item is a careful piece of behavioral pharmacology in two macaques. The task requires extended sequential evidence sampling: a stream of briefly presented shapes, each carrying probabilistic evidence about which target will pay out, with the animal deciding when to stop. That loads working memory, and the psychophysical modelling shows it — decay, primacy, recency and priming all shape choices, with decay the dominant limit. A recurrent circuit model fit to the behavior attributes the individual differences in decay to differences in recurrent self-excitation, a nice bridge from behavior to a biophysical parameter you could in principle manipulate.

Then mid-session systemic injections of SKF 81297, a selective D1 agonist, or SCH 23390, a selective D1 antagonist. The agonist increased the subjective weight assigned to each cue and reduced priming. The antagonist reduced the weight scale and, counterintuitively, reduced working memory decay. Both with large effect sizes.

But the result I would take home is that task performance did not change. Under D1 blockade the monkeys sampled about one additional cue and finished at the same accuracy. A large, receptor-specific change in the internal parameters of evidence accumulation was fully absorbed by a strategy adjustment — a caution worth generalizing to every pro-cognitive program in psychiatry that reads a null on summary accuracy as failure of target engagement. The drug hit the mechanism; the animal compensated. If your outcome is percent correct, you learn nothing about either.

Two agentic AI items, and the first is the one I would point people to, from Philip Romero's lab at Duke with Harvard. The premise is that biological AI is almost entirely observational — models extract statistical patterns from static data, which lets them interpolate inside the observed regime but never generates new information about how a sequence change alters function. So they closed the loop.

An agent built on ProteinNPT, a protein language model that learns sequence-function relationships on top of pretrained evolutionary priors, proposes variants using an upper confidence bound acquisition function that trades predicted function against predictive uncertainty. A robotic laboratory does gene assembly, expression and biochemical characterization, and measurements return to the model, which updates and proposes again. Validated in silico first against deep mutational scanning landscapes used as ground-truth oracles, the agent reached top-fitness regions while evaluating under half a percent of the data, and on the GB1 landscape climbed thirty-five to forty-seven percent above the plateau every alternative strategy reached. Removing either the epistasis modelling or the uncertainty-guided exploration killed that advantage, so the two components do different jobs.

Then the real thing. Three agents with complementary objectives — glucose, xylose and mannose specificity in family 1 glycoside hydrolases — sharing one experimental memory and one learning model, running twenty-five rounds over roughly a month with no human intervention, across about 1.7 million chimeras built by recombining fragments of six natural parent enzymes. Glucose specificity improved about three-fold, unsurprising given the strong native preference; xylose improved around sixty-fold and mannose around a thousand-fold, with the authors careful to note those ratios get unstable when the reference parent sits near the assay detection limit. Purified-enzyme kinetics confirmed the shifts held.

The honest finding underneath is more interesting than the fold changes. Gains toward both non-native sugars were strongly correlated and driven mostly by losing glucose activity rather than by gaining non-native activity. The agents found the easy direction — break the native preference — which is precisely what a specificity-times-activity objective rewards. That is not a failure, it is a well-posed lesson about objective design in autonomous experimentation. And the trajectories differed by substrate: gradual improvement for xylose, where feedback was rich, versus a few discrete jumps for mannose, where most variants sat at the detection floor. Assay dynamic range shapes what a closed loop can learn.

The second agentic item is stranger, and I want to be careful with it. A small company reports a fleet of six persistent language-model agent identities that ran continuously for nearly six months on shared cross-readable memory, and describes the institutional machinery they built to stop it poisoning itself.

The problem statement is real and well put. In a bounded agent run, a fabricated claim dies when the run ends; in a persistent shared memory it becomes an inherited fact for every agent that reads it afterwards. They cite an independent audit of five autonomous research systems across seventy-five generated papers in which every system showed at least one systematic evidence-chain failure, hallucinated references reached twenty-one percent of bibliography entries, and reported scores failed independent reproduction in more than half the audited papers. If you are building anything recursive on top of language model output, that is the number that should govern your architecture.

Their response is a six-layer trust architecture: an identity-level commitment against fabrication, a write gate checking candidate facts before they enter shared memory, an output verification gate, an agent research-ethics layer, exact reporting of tool outcomes, and human co-signature for irreversible actions with the explicit rule that prior approvals do not chain. The reported numbers are large — delusion-reinforcement probe failures from ninety-two percent to zero, wrong-topic citation hallucination from ninety-six down to six and a half, and a local open-weight model going from forty-four to about ninety percent on an internal protein-design benchmark once institutional memory was attached, against sixty-four for a frontier model with no memory.

Now the skepticism, most of which the paper supplies itself. There is no external control system and no ablation of the memory write gate, and the hypothesis classification was neither blinded nor timestamp-audited. The mechanism described as memory weights updating on each read turned out, on direct query of the production store, to be exercised on only a small minority of entries — schema intent, not operating behavior. And most tellingly, the memory-augmented local model scored below its own unaugmented baseline on target classes the library did not cover, recommending a protein-backbone design tool for an RNA target, an error the bare model avoided through general reasoning. That is the honest shape of retrieval augmentation: it lifts you where the library is deep and makes you overconfident where it is thin.

The pharmacologically interesting bit is their worked case, which illustrates the ceiling. The system nominated haloperidol as a computational pharmacological chaperone candidate for a variant in a mitochondrial enzyme gene, reporting a modelled stabilization of about half an angstrom in mean residue fluctuation, after a docking counter-screen rejected two binding artifacts and a retrospective check reproduced the known CFTR delta F508 corrector mechanism. Not validated experimentally or clinically, and the paper says so unprompted. So you have a plausible unvalidated hypothesis with provenance you can audit end to end — and that last part is the actual contribution, because it is what the field is worst at.

We close with a quiet, useful deflation. The 2020 motion sequencing paper from Wiltschko and colleagues is one of the most influential behavioral phenotyping results of the last decade — nearly seven hundred mice, a large panel of neuroactive and psychoactive compounds, and a central claim that unsupervised behavioral syllables classify drug treatment better than traditional scalar features like velocity and position, by more than fifty percent.

Somebody reproduced parts of the analysis on the public dataset and found that most of that gap is attributable to preprocessing, classifier choice and hyperparameter optimization. Under alternative but comparably standard analytical choices, the advantage shrinks to about eleven percent, and it reaches significance mainly in densely populated regions of pharmacobehavioral space — where many compounds sit close together — rather than generally.

This does not say unsupervised syllables are useless; they clearly capture structure scalar features miss. It says the specific comparison that justified a lot of expensive rigs is method-dependent, and that if your question is whether a compound moves behavior at all, scalar features are cheaper, easier to interpret biologically, and quite possibly good enough. For a lab deciding where to put its phenotyping budget, that is the most immediately actionable thing in today's episode — and reanalysis is how a method earns its reputation rather than inheriting it.
