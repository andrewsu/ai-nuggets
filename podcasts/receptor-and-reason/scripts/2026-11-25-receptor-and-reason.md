## Script

Welcome back to Receptor & Reason. It's Wednesday, November twenty-fifth, and the thread running through today's episode is the gap between what a measurement says and what we assume it says. A drug that reduces intake without reducing pleasure. A genetic association that turns out to tag an allele nobody genotyped. Five computational models that agree about the biology we already knew and disagree about everything else.

Let's start with the paper I think is the most interesting of the week. Patients on semaglutide describe something they call food noise going quiet — the intrusive, repetitive pull toward food that runs in the background. It's one of the most consistent qualitative reports in the GLP-1 literature, and it has been almost impossible to operationalize in an animal. A group in Mexico City built a behavioral battery that takes it seriously, and the result is the cleanest dissociation of wanting from liking I've seen for this drug class.

The setup is a home-cage system they call a homegustometer — mice live in it, drink from instrumented sippers around the clock, and learn tasks without an experimenter in the room. That matters more than it sounds, because brief-access tests sample a few minutes of a nocturnal animal's day under handling stress, and continuous monitoring caught circadian structure those sessions miss entirely.

Onto that they layered three measurements. First, a self-paced psychophysical task where mice categorize glucose concentration against water. Second, conditioned taste avoidance versus conditioned taste aversion — and the distinction is load-bearing, so hold onto it. Third, and this is the novel piece, a lick-triggered taste alternation task. Two fixed sippers, one delivering glucose and one delivering something else — saccharin, or a lipid emulsion. After a set number of licks the solutions swap positions. Which means that sometimes glucose arrives at the sipper the mouse is already at — they call that a passive hit — and sometimes the glucose has moved to the far sipper and the animal has to get up and cross to get it. That's an active hit. Same reward, same consummatory act, and the only difference is whether the animal had to take initiative.

A single dose of semaglutide left passive hits essentially intact and cut active hits nearly in half. The transition that collapsed was the one requiring the animal to follow glucose across the cage — its probability fell from about nine in ten to about one in two, with the lost probability going straight into perseverating at the sipper the mouse was already licking. During clearance those active transitions returned to baseline. So the drug didn't make glucose less valuable at the moment of contact. It made the animal unwilling to go and get it.

The timing analysis is what sells it. They reconstructed each transition in two currencies — licks and seconds. In licks, the twelve timelines were nearly superimposable: mice emitted seven to ten licks at the origin sipper before leaving, under drug or not, and started exploiting within a lick or two of arrival. In real time, the same lick sequence stretched from about eighty seconds at baseline to over four hundred seconds under semaglutide, a more than fivefold dilation, and it lived almost entirely in the intervals — the pause before leaving the origin sipper went from about two seconds to twenty-eight. The motor program is intact. The sequence is intact. What's gone is the initiation.

And this is where the aversion distinction pays off. Semaglutide produced conditioned taste avoidance — less intake in a bottle test — without conditioned taste aversion, meaning no negative shift in palatability, and a hidden Markov model fit lick-by-lick confirmed that glucose-evoked oromotor responses were unchanged under drug. The authors are careful here, because at the dose they used, picked for maximal weight loss, rodents do show pica — so there is almost certainly nausea. Their point is that nausea is not sufficient to produce a frank taste aversion, and the behavioral signature is motivational, not hedonic.

One more detail with a clinical echo. As the drug waned, mice regained weight by selectively overconsuming chow over glucose, and glucose over lipids — a macronutrient hierarchy in the rebound that persisted to the end of the experiment. If that generalizes, the composition of post-discontinuation regain may be structured, not random.

Limitations are real and the authors state them: a single acute dose, one compound, no dopamine recording. But as a behavioral framework, the active-versus-passive hit contrast is the kind of assay design that gets borrowed, and I'd expect to see it reused.

Staying with motivation and the accumbens. A group has been building the case that the small GTPase RhoA drives dendritic atrophy in D1 spiny projection neurons of the nucleus accumbens, and that this atrophy underlies negative affective states. The open question was sufficiency, and specifically sufficiency in a defined projection rather than the structure as a whole.

They used a retrograde Cre virus in the ventral tegmental area with Cre-dependent RhoA overexpression in the accumbens, which restricts the manipulation to accumbens neurons that project back to VTA — predominantly D1 cells. That alone produced social avoidance, reduced open-arm exploration, less grooming, and longer latency to approach food. The motivational readout is the one worth noting: the animals learned a food operant task normally, so this isn't an acquisition or a motor deficit, but their responding and their breakpoint on a progressive ratio both dropped, and persistent sucrose seeking fell. Then the complementary half — chronic stress induced RhoA message selectively in that same accumbens-to-VTA population.

So you get sufficiency and stress-induced recruitment pointing at the same projection. The cautionary note is the usual one for overexpression: elevating a GTPase above physiological levels tells you a circuit is capable of producing the phenotype, not that endogenous RhoA signaling there is the mechanism in a stressed animal. The in situ data narrow that gap but don't close it.

Third item, and we stay in reward but move to primates. There's a paper in Neuropharmacology characterizing a selective nociceptin-opioid-peptide receptor agonist — a compound designated ATDP33605 — in rhesus monkeys self-administering cocaine. I'll note up front this one is a tail item for me: the body is behind Elsevier and isn't in Europe PMC's full-text corpus, so I'm working from a self-contained abstract.

The in vitro profile is clean: sub-nanomolar affinity at human NOP, about two-hundred-forty-fold selectivity over mu-opioid, full agonist efficacy comparable to the endogenous peptide. In four male monkeys, acute intramuscular dosing dose-dependently reduced self-administration of a peak unit dose of cocaine, and the doses that did so had little or only transient effect on food-maintained responding — the control that distinguishes a reinforcement-specific effect from general behavioral suppression. The effect held across a range of cocaine unit doses.

Why this matters: NOP has been the perennially interesting opioid-family target that keeps not producing a drug, and most of the supporting behavioral work is rodent. First evidence that a selective NOP agonist suppresses intravenous cocaine self-administration in a nonhuman primate is a genuine step up in predictive weight. The honest caveats are in the abstract itself — four animals, all male, with real variability between individuals.

That brings us to cocaine from a completely different direction. There's a medRxiv preprint on the CYP2D6 locus that is, I think, a good object lesson in what genome-wide association actually hands you.

The background: this group previously reported a four-SNP haplotype at CYP2D6 associated with cocaine use disorder and with altered CYP2D6 expression in brain. The question is what molecular variation that haplotype is actually tagging, and you cannot answer it with short reads, because CYP2D6 is one of the hardest loci in the genome — extensive allelic diversity, structural complexity, a neighboring pseudogene, copy number variation.

So they did targeted long-read sequencing on nineteen individuals of European ancestry carrying zero, one, or two copies of the haplotype, supplemented with pangenome assemblies, splice quantitative trait loci, protein structure modeling, and — the detail I'd underline — the actual probe sequences from the SNP arrays.

The result is that the haplotype tags known star alleles. Four of five individuals lacking it carried two canonical reference alleles; nearly all homozygotes carried noncanonical ones, specifically star two and star four. Star two carries two missense substitutions; star four carries four plus a loss-of-function splice-site mutation, and star four is the classic poor-metabolizer allele.

The reframe is the point. An association that reads as a novel psychiatric risk haplotype may in fact be a tag for well-characterized pharmacogenetic function that was invisible to the genotyping platform. For anyone doing pharmacogenomics in psychiatric cohorts, the practical lesson is that array-based CYP2D6 calls are structurally unable to resolve this locus, and what you conclude about a brain phenotype may be an artifact of that. The sample is nineteen people, so treat the specific allele frequencies as illustrative rather than estimates.

Sticking with substance use genetics, there's a nicely executed negative from All of Us on rare copy number variants in alcohol use disorder. Fifty-six thousand participants with short-read whole-genome sequencing — about fifty-four hundred cases — analyzed within European-like, African-like, and Admixed American-like groups and then meta-analyzed.

Nothing survived correction. No genome-wide burden metric, no individual rare CNV region above ten kilobases. The gene-set results that did come up were almost all duplications associated with reduced risk across biologically unrelated sets, which the authors read — correctly, I think — as a global duplication dosage effect rather than pathway-specific biology. Two things nudged above threshold: deletion count in a stringent neurofunction gene set at an odds ratio near one point zero four, and a fifty-eight-base-pair deletion at sixteen-p-twelve-point-one, a known dosage-sensitive neuropsychiatric locus, at an odds ratio near three without significant heterogeneity.

I'm flagging this because it's exactly the kind of result that doesn't get written up and should. The field has a standing prior that rare structural variation is a hidden reservoir of risk for every psychiatric phenotype, and here is a well-powered look that says, for this phenotype and this detection technology, probably not much.

Next, antidepressants and metabolism, from UK Biobank. This is a metabolome-wide analysis of two hundred fifty-one nuclear magnetic resonance biomarkers in about two hundred twenty-three thousand people, with external validation in two independent cohorts and a longitudinal arm of roughly fifty-eight hundred followed about four years.

A hundred fifty-two metabolites associated with current antidepressant use after correction, and the signature splits in an interesting way. On one side, elevated triglycerides and very-low-density lipoproteins — the adverse cardiometabolic direction you'd expect. On the other, higher omega-three fatty acids and a lower omega-six-to-omega-three ratio, both of which have themselves been associated with lower depression risk. Profile scores distinguished users in the external cohorts with areas under the curve from about point five-eight to point seven-three, and antidepressant initiation was nominally associated with increases in nine lipid biomarkers.

The analytic value here is confounding by indication, the problem that eats every observational antidepressant study. Depression itself has a metabolic signature, so a cross-sectional association tells you very little, and the longitudinal initiation analysis is what gives this traction. Effect sizes are small and NMR panels are coarse compared to mass spectrometry. But if the fatty-acid arm is real and drug-driven rather than behavioral, that's a mechanistic lead sitting right next to the cardiometabolic liability we already counsel patients about.

Now a methods paper that solves a problem I suspect a number of you have run into. Metabotropic glutamate receptors are class C GPCRs that work as obligate dimers, and some of the physiologically interesting combinations are heterodimers. The trouble is you cannot easily express a heterodimer in isolation, because the receptors prefer to homodimerize, so any pharmacology you measure is contaminated by homodimer signal and the response of a heterodimer to a selective ligand is consequently hard to predict.

The fix is elegant. They put an endoplasmic-reticulum retention sequence in the receptor carboxy-terminal tail, flanked by halves of a split intein. A receptor carrying one intein module is held in the ER. When it meets a partner carrying the complementary module, the inteins associate, protein trans-splicing excises the retention signal, and only then does the receptor reach the plasma membrane. Surface expression becomes conditional on heterodimerization. They screened for orthogonal pairs, settled on two inteins from different organisms, and showed that co-expression gave functional responses from multiple group two and group three combinations while the individual constructs were largely silent.

Validation came from three directions: subtype-selective agonist pharmacology on the mGlu2/4 pair, glutamate dose-response curves closely matching an established heterodimer-selective complementation assay, and independent surface-expression measurements confirming partner-dependent delivery. Those last measurements also exposed a limitation — retention efficiency varies by receptor, and mGlu8-containing constructs leak. But as a general trick, trafficking-gated-by-trans-splicing is portable well beyond mGluRs, to any obligate heteromer you'd like to study without its homomeric background.

From receptors to channels. A study of tetrodotoxin-resistant NaV1.8 in vagal conduction, done in both mouse and human nerve, and the human tissue is what makes it worth your time.

The question is which sodium channel isoforms carry the action potential along vagal C-fibers; prior rodent work put NaV1.7 in an essential role in roughly seventy percent of them. Here they tested two structurally unrelated NaV1.8-selective blockers on electrically evoked compound action potentials, and both reduced C-fiber responses concentration-dependently in mouse and human nerve. The knockout control is the nice part: one compound still reduced responses in NaV1.8 knockout animals, meaning its wild-type effect was partly off-target, while the other did not. That's a tool-compound selectivity audit most studies skip. And when they blocked NaV1.7 first, subsequent NaV1.8 blockade produced no further reduction — so the two isoforms cooperate within the same subset of fibers rather than serving separate populations.

The translational hook is obvious given that we now have an approved NaV1.8-selective analgesic in suzetrigine, with more in development. If NaV1.8 contributes to conduction in human vagal C-fibers, then the autonomic afferent and efferent traffic those fibers carry is a plausible site of on-target, off-indication effect. This is ex vivo nerve at pharmacological concentrations, so it is a hypothesis about where to look, not a safety finding. But it's a concrete one.

Two computational items to close, and both are about honesty in evaluation. The first asks what happens when you try to name the effector gene at a GWAS locus. About ninety percent of lead variants are non-coding, so effector-gene assignment is the bottleneck in turning an association into biology — and there is no experimentally resolved ground truth at scale, so every method is trained against quasi-true-positive sets each dominated by one line of evidence.

The authors benchmarked established methods across three independent evaluation sets and trained five models, each informed by a different biological domain. The headline is that performance does not transfer: the same method ranged from an area under the precision-recall curve of point nine-one down to point four-six depending on which evaluation set you used. They traced that not to architecture but to how differently the models weight the distance between variant and gene. Curation assumptions propagate into predictions and can become self-confirming — their example is druggability in the model informed by Open Targets, which is exactly the circularity you'd fear.

The number I keep coming back to: the five models shared only about seventeen to thirty-two percent of their effector-gene assignments. They converged on established disease biology and diverged precisely where a GWAS is supposed to deliver something new. On recovering three hundred forty approved drug targets, no combination strategy worked — averaging scores got about thirty-nine percent, joint training about thirty-seven, while the union across all five recovered fifty-two. The ensemble is worse than the disagreement. Their framework reports the curation assumption behind each assignment rather than hiding it, which is the right response: make the disagreement legible instead of averaging it away.

The second is an agentic drug-discovery paper that does something I'd like to see become standard. The problem it takes on is interaction-level selectivity — a protein with several binding partners, where you want to disrupt a specific subset and preserve the rest. The agent, called Bivexia, guides a pipeline from hit discovery and de novo design through preclinical evaluation, with decision rules fixed in advance.

Here's the part that matters. Every prospective selection the agent makes is cryptographically committed before anything is tested. They took the clinical-trial preregistration idea and applied it to compound advancement, so any subsequent change to the rules is detectable, and they released the rules alongside the measurements. In a field where agentic-discovery claims are routinely assembled after the fact from whichever runs worked, that's a meaningful structural commitment.

The demonstration target is LAG-3, the immune checkpoint receptor, which also binds tau and amyloid-beta. A compound the agent advanced bound LAG-3 with nanomolar affinity, disrupted the tau and amyloid-beta interactions, preserved binding to MHC class two and to fibrinogen-like protein one, and reduced tau and amyloid-beta burden in independent cohorts across two mouse models. Then they ported the same screening rules, unchanged, to two further targets — cellular prion protein and LRP1 — and got three hits in nine selections on one and two in nine on the other, with zero hits in matched random batches.

And now the honesty. Neither per-target comparison reached conventional significance — one-sided Fisher exact p-values of about point one-zero and point two-four. The pooled result across both targets, five of eighteen versus zero of eighteen, gives a nominal p of point zero-two-three, and the authors explicitly label it exploratory because it was not prespecified: descriptive, not confirmatory evidence that the agent outperforms. There's also a comparison against two outcome-blinded medicinal chemists, which they flag as not a fair fight, since the agent selected first.

One other finding deserves attention: retrospective cross-validation did not predict prospective performance. Both ported targets returned selective inhibitors well above library base rates despite showing no cross-validation enrichment. That is a direct warning about the retrospective benchmarks most of this field still runs on. The whole cascade rests on a single compound, and the two ported campaigns stopped at primary screening, so this is a framework demonstration rather than a validated discovery engine — but it is the most carefully instrumented one I've read.

Last item, and it's the lightest. A multi-agent system called EM-Claw for single-particle cryo-EM processing. The motivation is that difficult specimens need dataset-specific strategies no fixed pipeline captures, so the work stays stubbornly dependent on expert judgment. Instead of a predefined workflow, the system retrieves processing precedents from previously solved datasets, proposes and independently evaluates alternative strategies, actually executes CryoSPARC or RELION jobs, and revises the route based on quantitative and visual evidence — keeping successful trajectories as reusable experience, with a human able to intervene in natural language at any stage.

They tested it on public and in-house datasets spanning soluble proteins, membrane proteins, GPCR complexes, and small transporter monomers, covering the usual pathologies. Reported improvements over previously published reconstructions run from about a tenth of an angstrom to one-point-three angstroms, with little or no human intervention.

I'd read the resolution gains with care, since beating a published reconstruction can reflect better modern software rather than better agent decisions, and the comparison isn't against a current expert working the same data from scratch. But the framing is right for this corner of the field: not autonomy for its own sake, but an agent that accumulates precedent in a domain where precedent is exactly what the expert is using and exactly what never gets written down.

That's the episode. The common thread, if you want one: almost everything today got its value from a control — passive versus active hits, a knockout to audit a tool compound, a random batch alongside the agent's picks, a cryptographic commitment made before the assay ran.
