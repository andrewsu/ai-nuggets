## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. Today's papers have a habit in common. In every one of them, somebody records a number, and the number turns out not to be standing in for the thing everyone assumed it was standing in for. A plasma concentration that misses half the drug. A brain concentration that hides two opposing fluxes. A transporter that is all there on the blot and missing from the membrane. A confidence interval that covers the truth a fifth of the time. The work today is mostly about closing that gap — and in a couple of cases, about discovering how wide it had quietly become.

Start with the one I think matters most this week. A group at Johns Hopkins took chronically implanted Neuropixels probes — high-density silicon arrays that isolate single units at sub-millisecond resolution — and left them in place across a full psilocybin experiment. Eight head-fixed mice, two probes each, targeting medial prefrontal cortex, ventral hippocampus and the thalamic nuclei along the way. Saline on three baseline days, psilocybin at one milligram per kilogram on day zero, and a further recording twenty-four hours later, when the drug is long cleared. Crucially, they concatenated the sessions and spike-sorted them together, so they could follow the same sixteen hundred and ninety-two neurons from the acute drug effect into the persistent phase. Ninety-eight percent of units matched their own waveform across days rather than a neighbour's.

Acutely, psilocybin did not simply excite or simply inhibit. It pushed the population apart in both directions — twenty-three percent of units activated, fourteen percent suppressed, against sixteen and six after saline — and it did so by cell type. Narrow-waveform interneurons, the class that tends to contain parvalbumin cells, showed the clearest activation, going from sixteen percent activated after saline to thirty-one percent after psilocybin. Wide interneurons, which lean somatostatin, showed the largest jump in suppression. And the magnitude effect was a scaling, not a recruitment: the psilocybin response exceeded saline at every decile of the distribution, by a ratio of about one and a half, which is what you see when a drug multiplies the whole population's responsiveness rather than lighting up a special subset.

Then twenty-four hours later, the sign flips. Net suppression. Suppressed units rise from sixteen to twenty-six percent and activated units fall. And it is the same neurons: of the units that psilocybin acutely activated, only twenty-six percent were still activated a day later, against forty-eight percent after saline, and twenty-two percent had become suppressed. Here is the part worth sitting with. They asked which part of the acute response predicted the persistent one, and it was not the peak. The early window — thirteen to eighteen minutes, when the head-twitch response peaks and when essentially every acute psilocybin study makes its measurement — predicted nothing once you controlled for the late response. The late window, fifty-five to sixty minutes in, one half-life out, predicted day-one firing cleanly. Units whose activation was still standing at an hour stayed activated; units whose activation had decayed by then ended up suppressed.

So the acute readout the field has standardised on is measuring the wrong moment. Caveats: eight male mice, one dose, one day of follow-up, and firing rate is not plasticity — the dendritic spine story unfolds on the same timescale and may or may not be what this suppression reflects. But if you want a single-neuron correlate of psilocybin's lasting effect, this says to stop photographing the peak and start watching the decay.

Staying with the question of what a measurement omits: a group at Rutgers and the NIDA intramural programme looked at dopamine in the 3q29 deletion mouse. In humans the 3q29 deletion is typically de novo, about one and a half megabases, and carries something north of a fortyfold increase in schizophrenia risk — the single largest known molecular risk factor. The appeal of a genetic model over an amphetamine or ketamine model is obvious: you get hyperdopaminergia as a consequence of real disease biology rather than as something you imposed.

And they found it. Fast-scan cyclic voltammetry in slices showed elevated evoked dopamine in dorsal striatum — about nine hundred and twenty-four nanomolar peak against seven hundred and forty-five in littermate controls — with no significant difference in nucleus accumbens. Regionally selective, and selective in the direction the human imaging literature points, where synthesis capacity is elevated in associative dorsal striatum and tracks positive symptoms. The decay constant was slower too, which points at clearance rather than release.

Then they went looking for the mechanism and this is where the measurement problem lands. Synthesis enzymes unchanged. Degradation enzymes unchanged. But the DAT blocker GBR-12909 — a tool compound that normally raises evoked dopamine sharply — produced only a hundred and forty-five percent rise in the mutants against two hundred and thirty-nine percent in controls. Radioligand binding with tritiated WIN-35428 showed less transporter. And yet: DAT messenger RNA unchanged, total DAT protein unchanged, phospho-DAT unchanged. The transporter is all present in the homogenate and functionally absent at the membrane. That single dissociation is a plausible explanation for why the human literature is a mess — the postmortem homogenate studies and the imaging meta-analyses that found no DAT change were measuring total, and the autoradiography studies that found less were measuring surface. They propose protein kinase C beta as the trafficking culprit, which is a reasonable inference from the blunted amphetamine locomotion and the flat response to the PKC-beta inhibitor ruboxistaurin. I would hold that part loosely: ruboxistaurin is nanomolar on its target and they are using it at a hundred to two hundred micromolar in slice, so the selectivity argument is doing a lot of work. The transporter result stands on its own.

Third, a nice piece of circuit pharmacology about the median raphe. A group recorded from genetically identified populations there — serotonergic, Vglut3, and GABAergic — in freely behaving mice, and asked how they respond to sensory and cortical input across the sleep-wake cycle. The headline is that responsiveness is larger during slow-wave sleep than during waking. Quiet auditory clicks, around fifty-five decibels, deliberately too soft to arouse: in wake, they inhibited fourteen percent of serotonin neurons and excited about forty percent of Vglut3 cells. In slow-wave sleep, more than half the serotonin neurons were suppressed, often with rebound, and three-quarters of Vglut3 and eighty percent of the GABA population were excited.

Retrograde tracing turned up medial prefrontal cortex as a direct source of input, and optogenetic stimulation of that projection during slow-wave sleep inhibited serotonin neurons while robustly exciting Vglut3 neurons. So the same descending signal splits the nucleus in two directions, and does it most strongly when the animal is asleep. The implication I would draw for anyone working on serotonergic drugs: the median raphe's input gain is a state variable, and essentially all our characterisation of raphe pharmacology is done in awake or anaesthetised preparations. Whatever a 5-HT1A autoreceptor agonist is doing to this nucleus at three in the morning is not well predicted by what it does at two in the afternoon.

Now a pair of papers about brain exposure, which belong together because they make the same argument from opposite ends. The first, from Florida, is about intranasal cocaine. Insufflation is how most people start, and it produces fast central effects despite much lower plasma concentrations than an equivalent intravenous dose — which has long been taken as a hint of a direct nose-to-brain route. They measured brain and plasma cocaine by tandem mass spec in awake mice after both routes and built a physiologically based pharmacokinetic model with the nasal cavity split anatomically, blood-flow-scaled mucosal absorption, tissue diffusion resistance, mucociliary clearance, protein binding and a blood-brain-barrier permeability-surface-area term. The answer: after intranasal dosing, roughly half the cocaine that reaches the brain never went through the systemic circulation at all. Which means plasma concentration, the measurement that nearly every human pharmacokinetic and abuse-liability study rests on, is systematically blind to half the exposure for the route most users actually use.

The second is from Denali, and it takes the opposite complaint — that even measuring in the brain is not enough. Brain concentration is a net number: uptake minus clearance. They engineered antibodies that bind the transport-vehicle patch itself, which lets them block receptor-mediated uptake in vivo and read clearance directly, then fed that into a mechanistic pharmacokinetic model to reconstruct both fluxes over time across a range of receptor affinities. Transferrin-receptor targeting gives fast, affinity-dependent uptake and fast clearance. CD98 heavy chain gives slow uptake and prolonged retention. And then the finding that should change how people read these datasets: dual TfR–CD98 constructs show higher brain exposure than either single-receptor format, and the model says that extra exposure comes almost entirely from reduced clearance via CD98-mediated retention, not from better delivery across the barrier. If your therapeutic needs flux — enzyme replacement, an oligonucleotide, a degrader that has to get into cells — then the higher exposure number is telling you the wrong thing about whether you will get there.

Pair five, also two papers, is one of those accidental convergences the preprint calendar occasionally produces. Both are about Pumilio proteins in sensory neurons, both posted in the same fortnight, and they point in opposite directions at the same regulon. Pumilio one and two are RNA-binding proteins that recognise an eight-nucleotide element in three-prime untranslated regions and recruit the CCR4-NOT deadenylase complex to kill the message. A group at Wisconsin found both enriched in TRPV1-positive dorsal root ganglion neurons, with PUM2 physically bound to the transcripts for Nav1.7, Nav1.8 and Nav1.9. Depleting Pumilio in sensory neurons produced mechanical hypersensitivity and worse inflammatory pain — and that was reversed by blocking Nav1.8 but not Nav1.7, which pins the effect on one channel out of three. So they built a chimera: the PUM2 RNA-binding domain fused to a stronger repressive domain, delivered by AAV, which they call super-PUM. It cut Nav1.8 protein, reduced the tetrodotoxin-resistant and suzetrigine-sensitive sodium current — suzetrigine being the recently approved Nav1.8 blocker — and prevented mechanical and thermal hypersensitivity after spared nerve injury.

The other paper, from Rutgers, comes at the same protein from the disease side. In diabetic neuropathic pain, in both type one and type two mouse models and in human donor ganglia, a conserved circular RNA they call cRps6kb1 is upregulated. What it does is compete with the neurotrophin-3 three-prime UTR for PUM2 binding — sequestering the repressor off its real target, which raises neurotrophin-3 translation and stability, and downstream proneurotrophin-3 and the chemokine CCL2. Block the circular RNA and the hypersensitivity resolves; mimic it in naive mice and you get the pain phenotype. So one paper says too little Pumilio repression causes pain and engineers more of it; the other says a disease state produces too little Pumilio repression by decoying the protein away. Same mechanism, two diseases, arrived at independently. If I had to bet on which translates first, it is the circular RNA — a decoy you can block is an easier molecule than an AAV-delivered engineered repressor in dorsal root ganglia — but the super-PUM result is the better proof that the regulon is causal.

Two clinical items next. First, from King's College London, a study of facial emotion recognition in people at clinical high risk for psychosis — twenty-four antipsychotic-naive participants against twenty-four controls under fMRI, plus a separate randomised crossover of five milligrams of diazepam against placebo in another twenty-four. The high-risk group was slower across all emotions. And the imaging result is the interesting one: they showed lower amygdala activation for threat relative to neutral, and lower for non-threat relative to neutral — which reads like blunted threat processing until you look at what moved. Amygdala and hippocampal activation for neutral faces relative to fixation was elevated. The threat contrast is flat because the baseline came up, not because the threat response went down. A neutral face is not a neutral stimulus in this population, and every study that uses neutral as its reference condition has been quietly subtracting the signal it was looking for. The diazepam arm was close to null — no effect on sensitivity in the full sample, a p of point-zero-four-nine-nine after excluding one influential participant, which I would not build on, and a reduction in midcingulate activation to neutral faces after adjusting for global cerebral blood flow. The GABAergic hypothesis survives; it has not been supported.

Second, from Vanderbilt and Emory, the first safety estimates for prescription stimulants in pregnancy among people with opioid use disorder — a group where Medicaid programmes routinely restrict coverage and no evidence existed either way. Twelve thousand three hundred and twenty-seven Tennessee Medicaid pregnancies with opioid use disorder, three percent of whom received an amphetamine- or methylphenidate-class stimulant overlapping pregnancy. Postpartum overdose: no association. Preterm birth, low birth weight, severe maternal morbidity, cardiovascular complications, neonatal opioid withdrawal: no association. One signal did come through — NICU admission, odds ratio one point five-eight. And the authors do the right thing with it. If stimulant exposure were harming neonates, NICU admission should travel with something — gestational age, birth weight, withdrawal. It travels with none of them. The most parsimonious reading is that a stimulant prescription in this population changes how closely the infant is watched, and NICU admission is measuring surveillance rather than harm. Two exploratory signals do need replication: elevated overdose odds in people receiving both stimulants and buprenorphine, and a cardiovascular composite elevated in the ADHD subgroup.

From UCSF, a system called TALQ, which stands for text assessment with language model questionnaires. The problem it attacks is that depression and anxiety are assessed weeks or months apart with instruments covering a handful of symptoms, so almost everything that happens between visits is unobserved. They had Llama-3.1-8B rate fifty-two symptoms — compiled from a content analysis of seven depression scales — from the text of four hundred and ninety-one members of two online mental health communities, every two weeks for a year. Five repeated ratings per symptom per window, two and a half million ratings total, median intraclass correlation of point-six-four across repeats. The ratings predicted human annotations of interpersonal risk and depression severity, and collapsed into twelve replicable factors with split-half congruence above point nine-five.

Two findings. Sleep disturbance reliably preceded overwhelm two weeks later, and self-harm content preceded emotional pain, with the reverse directions not holding. And then the deflationary one: they fitted a mixture model, found eleven recognisable states, and asked how sticky those states were. The probability of staying in a state was point-two-five, against point-two-three when each person's windows were shuffled in time. People differ in which states they occupy, essentially not at all in how they move between them. Clinical intuition about a patient being "in a phase" is, on this data, mostly an occupancy fact dressed up as a dynamical one. Reddit users are not a clinical sample and an eight-billion-parameter rater is not a clinician, but the instrument is versioned and reproducible in a way no human panel is, which is the actual argument for it.

Three agentic items to close. From TCS Research in Hyderabad, a controlled comparison of orchestrator models in a tool-first multi-agent biomedical discovery platform — six specialised tool systems covering differential expression, literature retrieval, pathway enrichment, network gene prioritisation, perturbation signature matching and druggability. Tools, prompts, workflow and benchmark all held fixed; only the orchestrating model varies, across a locally deployable twenty-six-billion-parameter open-weight model, a mid-tier proprietary model and a frontier one. On a hundred and four single- and multi-turn tasks, with blinded expert review, the three were broadly comparable, and the small local model scored highest on correctness and completeness in human review. Groundedness stayed high for all three — in fact lowest for the frontier model on multi-turn. The authors' reading, which I think is right, is that groundedness in this setup is a property of the architecture, not the model: when the tools carry the domain knowledge and the model only coordinates, the thing you are benchmarking is your harness. Where capability did separate them was evidence validity on multi-turn tasks, where parameters have to be assembled across several exchanges — the small model fell to two point four against three point seven — and all three struggled with tool ordering when memory was required.

Then a benchmark from Lawrence Livermore called SciResearch-Eval, which is the sharpest thing I have read in this space in a while. The task is mundane on purpose: measure a material's thermal properties by heating it and fitting a heat-conduction simulator. The agent designs its own experiments, and the simulator it is given may be under-resolved or physically wrong. It must report each property with a ninety-five percent interval, scored properly against exact ground truth. Ninety-three held-out tasks, nine models, two and a half thousand episodes. Procedural traps, everyone handles. But when the simulator is missing physics, most models report wrong values while stating they are ninety-five percent sure — their intervals contain the truth between twenty and sixty-two percent of the time. The best performers reached ninety-four and ninety-nine percent, and they got there not primarily by diagnosing the flaw but by designing gentle experiments in the regime where the broken simulator happens to be accurate, which is what an experienced experimentalist does without narrating it. Asking for wider intervals did not help. Raising reasoning effort did help, more than tenfold for one model family, at thirteen to thirty-one times the reasoning tokens — so the capability was latent and not being spent. The framing to take away is the paper's own: a good fit is not evidence of a right answer, and a stated confidence that nobody scores is not a measurement of anything.

To end lighter. A collaboration across Zhejiang, Tsinghua and an industrial partner put up VibeSyn, which applies the vibe-coding idea to the bench — the chemist states a molecular objective in natural language and a hierarchy of agents turns intent and accumulating experimental evidence into decisions executed through computational tools and lab automation. The demonstration that earns the paper its place is a ten-step total synthesis of catharanthine, the Catharanthus alkaloid, plus structure-to-compound synthesis of cyclic peptides containing unnatural amino acids. Across those long campaigns, active human input accounted for under seven and a half percent of recorded workflow actions and simple confirmations for about five percent. Which is a real result and also, fittingly for today, a number worth interrogating: percent-of-recorded-actions is a denominator the system defines, and the handful of decisions a chemist makes in a ten-step synthesis are not interchangeable with the hundreds of routine operations they sit among. Still — ten steps, revised as evidence came in, mostly unsupervised. That is further than this was last year.

That is the episode. The through-line, if you want one: before you trust a number, ask what it is a proxy for, and whether anyone has checked lately. See you tomorrow.
