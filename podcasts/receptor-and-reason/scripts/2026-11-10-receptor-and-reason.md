## Script

Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Tuesday, November tenth.

There's a thread running through almost everything this week, and it's about summary numbers. A single score that collapses thousands of loci. A group mean that averages two opposite effects. A benchmark total that hides which half of the cohort moved. Every paper today is, in some way, about what the aggregate conceals — and in several cases the authors found the structure only because they stopped looking at the average.

Start with the best of them, out of the AURORA study, the big multisite emergency-department cohort that enrolls people within seventy-two hours of a traumatic event and follows them for months. This is a UNC-led analysis of two hundred and forty-five participants with paired blood samples at enrollment and at six months, most of them enrolled after motor vehicle collisions. AURORA has previously sorted people into latent symptom trajectories across ten domains — pain, depression, hyperarousal, sleep disturbance, mental fatigue, and so on. The new work asks what the transcriptome does along those trajectories.

The result is the inversion of how this field usually thinks. In the recovery trajectories, between thirty-five and fifty-eight percent of all tested transcripts changed significantly between the emergency department and six months. In the chronic trajectories, far fewer. Recovery is not the absence of a pathological signal. Recovery is an enormous, temporally ordered program — early innate immune activation with a neutrophil and interleukin-six signature, then homeostatic and wound-healing processes, then adaptive immune signaling — and chronicity is what you see when that program fails to run. More than three thousand genes moved in the same direction across all ten symptom domains, which means the thing being measured is not pain-specific or mood-specific; it's a shared recovery axis.

They built an RNA recovery score from that shared program and validated it externally. Higher scores tracked greater clinical recovery, higher peritraumatic cortisol, and lower cumulative lifetime stress exposure. Then they ran the recovery signature through the Connectivity Map to ask which compounds reproduce it. The strongest and most consistent recovery-mimicking class was glucocorticoid receptor agonists — dexamethasone, methylprednisolone — with protein kinase C activators and the sex steroids also appearing. The signatures that opposed recovery were dominated by mTOR and PI3-kinase inhibitors.

Now, hold that result loosely. This is L1000 data from non-neural cell lines being matched against whole-blood signatures, so what you're predicting is a peripheral transcriptional state, not a brain state. And the glucocorticoid hit sits in an uncomfortable relationship with a long, mixed literature on perioperative hydrocortisone after trauma. But the framing is the contribution, and the framing is genuinely new: if you only ever contrast chronic cases against healthy controls, the recovery program is invisible, because it's happening in the people you discarded as uninteresting.

The same lesson shows up in psychiatric genetics, from a Spanish consortium spanning CIBERSAM sites. Polygenic scores for schizophrenia are good at case-control discrimination and famously useless at explaining why one patient has an early onset and treatment-resistant negative symptoms and another doesn't. That failure is usually attributed to small effect sizes. This group argues it's an aggregation problem. They annotated common variants to five neurotransmitter signalling pathways — acetylcholine, dopamine, GABA, glutamate, serotonin — computed pathway-stratified scores in two independent cohorts of about three thousand and six hundred and forty-five patients, and assigned anyone in the top decile of exactly one pathway to that pathway's profile.

The global score showed no significant association with any clinical outcome. The pathway profiles did. The acetylcholine profile carried higher overall symptom severity, particularly negative symptoms and general psychopathology. The glutamate profile carried more severe negative symptoms, earlier age at onset, reduced three-year remission, and poorer treatment response. The GABA profile also tracked remission.

The design choice to flag is that anyone in the top decile of more than one pathway was excluded. That's what makes the profiles clean and mutually exclusive, and it's also what makes the analysis quietly optimistic — these pathways share variants, the overlap is real biology, and the people with it are exactly the ones a stratification scheme would need to handle. Treat this as a demonstration that structure exists inside the polygenic score, not as a clinical tool.

Staying with treatment response, a nice small imaging study from the Pizzagalli group, now at UC Irvine. Forty-one unmedicated adults with major depression, scanned before and after eight weeks of sertraline or bupropion, with seed-based resting-state connectivity from three anterior cingulate subdivisions. The overall effect of treatment was a decrease in connectivity between dorsal and pregenual cingulate and across default mode and frontoparietal regions. But the response-linked effect went the other way: subgenual and pregenual connectivity with temporal and inferior frontal cortex increased in responders and decreased in non-responders, and the magnitude of the subgenual-temporal increase tracked symptom reduction dimensionally.

So you have a general drug effect and a response-specific effect with opposite signs in overlapping tissue. Average them in a single group analysis and you get a muted result, or nothing at all — which may be a decent explanation for why this literature has been so inconsistent. The caveats are serious: forty-one people, no placebo arm, two different drugs pooled, and a seventy-three percent response rate that in an open-label design you cannot cleanly attribute to pharmacology. But the structural point survives.

Fourth, a piece of practical channel pharmacology from Australia — the Victor Chang Institute with the Melbourne epilepsy groups. Forty-eight percent of SCN1A missense variants in ClinVar are variants of uncertain significance, which is a real clinical problem now that sodium-channel genotype determines whether you give or withhold sodium-channel blockers, and increasingly determines eligibility for genetic therapies. They built a calibrated high-throughput automated patch-clamp assay and validated it on twenty-seven known pathogenic and twenty-five known benign variants.

Here's the part worth your attention. Seventeen of the twenty-seven pathogenic variants had current density so reduced that gating couldn't even be analyzed — the obvious readout caught those. Of the remaining ten, seven were abnormal only in the voltage gap between the midpoints of activation and inactivation. So they combined current density and that voltage difference into a graded Bayesian log-likelihood ratio, applied it to sixteen variants from an Australian cohort, and reclassified eight of sixteen — six of twelve uncertain variants became likely pathogenic, and two likely pathogenic became pathogenic. Two of the three Dravet-associated variants had current density in the normal range and were caught only by the gating measure. If your assay reports one number, those two patients stay undiagnosed.

A quick tool item next. A first-in-class fluorine-eighteen PET radiotracer for GPR39, the zinc-sensing GPCR implicated in neurodegeneration, vascular regulation and alcohol use disorder. Previous imaging used a carbon-eleven ligand with a twenty-minute half-life, which confines you to sites with a cyclotron down the hall; moving to fluorine-eighteen buys you a hundred and ten minutes and makes the tracer shippable. Pharmacological blockade cut brain uptake by up to about ninety percent in rats, knockout mice showed seventy-six percent lower uptake on imaging, and five-times-FAD transgenic mice showed about forty percent lower uptake, which is the sensitivity claim. One thing to keep in mind interpreting it: the tracer is itself a functional agonist at the receptor, with a sigmoidal response in the IP-one assay, and the blocking agents are agonists too. That's fine for demonstrating specificity and it's a real quantification question for the next step, because what a tracer like that reports is available receptor in a particular conformational state, not simply receptor density.

Two chemotherapy neurotoxicity papers landed in the same window with completely different methods, and they're worth hearing together.

The first is paclitaxel-induced neuropathy in rats, and the claim is that the sustaining lesion is in spinal cord mitochondria, not just the peripheral nerve. Eighteen days after the last injection, with allodynia well established, lumbar spinal cord mitochondria showed reduced oxygen consumption, lower membrane potential, reduced ATP-linked and maximal respiration — and a lowered calcium retention capacity, meaning the permeability transition pore opens more easily. Acute exposure of healthy tissue to paclitaxel reproduced the pore effect, and cyclosporin A, a pore blocker, reversed it. Then the interventional part: hyperbaric oxygen started after allodynia had already developed improved respiration and progressively raised mechanical thresholds, and intrathecal delivery of isolated healthy mitochondria attenuated established allodynia — and also reduced hypersensitivity in sciatic nerve crush and capsaicin models. Transplanting organelles into the intrathecal space is not a near-term clinical proposition, but as a causal manipulation it's about as direct as this field gets.

The second comes at the same problem from a German group using cisplatin in nematodes, specifically a nucleotide excision repair-deficient mutant that makes DNA-damage-driven sensory dysfunction tractable. Cisplatin lowered GABA levels, GABA supplementation rescued the sensory deficit and the morphological damage to GABAergic neurons, and carbachol — a cholinergic agonist — made everything worse. So in this model the DNA-damage route converges on an excitation-inhibition imbalance in the sensory circuit rather than on bioenergetics. Two mechanisms, two platinum-versus-taxane drug classes, two measurement modalities. Nobody should conclude they're the same disease, and the fact that two labs looking at the same clinical syndrome landed in such different places is probably the honest state of the field.

Then a clinical trial result where the summary statistic really matters. An ongoing randomized feasibility trial of gamma sensory stimulation, the lineage that goes back to the forty-hertz entrainment work. Thirty-eight older adults with cognitive impairment, eight weeks. The treatment group listened to self-selected music while viewing a thirty-nine-hertz light signal modulated by the delta and theta rhythms of the music; controls listened to podcasts with delta-band-only synchronized lights. The headline is that the treatment group improved on a face-name association task and on the ADAS-Cog, and that stimulus-driven theta-to-gamma phase-amplitude coupling increased more in the treatment group, with coupling increases tracking memory improvement.

Read the statistics carefully. On the ADAS-Cog, the group-by-session interaction was non-significant with a p-value of point seven nine six — the within-group improvement is real but the control group improved too. On the face-name task the interaction was marginal at point oh six six. So the memory claim rests on a trend-level interaction in a thirty-eight-person feasibility study, and the strongest result is the coupling-to-memory correlation, which is correlational. Also note the control differs on two dimensions at once: no gamma, and podcasts instead of self-chosen music. Self-selected music in cognitively impaired older adults is not a neutral placebo. The electrophysiology is the credible part; treat the cognition as hypothesis-generating.

Now three items on how we evaluate language models in this space, because two of them are the best methodological work I've seen on it.

The first is a large audit out of the Division of Digital Psychiatry at Beth Israel Deaconess. They asked forty-one language models the same nineteen clinical vignettes from three different footings — a clinician describing a patient, a caregiver describing a loved one, or a person describing themselves — collected thirty-five thousand free-text responses, extracted every named intervention, and separately had twenty-nine models rate the full option lists on a seven-point scale.

The clinical content barely changes; the delivery changes enormously. Models recommended consulting a professional in ninety-five percent of caregiver and patient responses versus sixty-six percent of clinician responses. Take lisdexamfetamine for binge-eating disorder, the only FDA-approved medication for that indication. When asked directly about medication, models named it to clinicians, caregivers and patients at ninety-one, eighty-three and seventy-nine percent. When the question was open-ended, it was ninety-two percent for clinicians, thirty-six percent for caregivers, and eighteen percent for patients. Twenty-five of the forty-one models raised it in every clinician-footing response and in none of their patient-footing responses. And when lay users did hear about it, a dose accompanied four of two hundred and sixty-five mentions, versus seventy of two hundred and forty for clinicians.

The second finding is sharper. Of six hundred and twenty-one commonly recommended options, eighty-three had a mean rating below zero from the models themselves — these are things the models judge poorly and recommend anyway. Benzodiazepines for generalized anxiety were recommended in nineteen to thirty percent of responses and rated minus point three to minus point six, while sertraline and buspirone were rated plus two point nine and plus two point two. But — and this is the part that keeps the finding honest — of five hundred and forty-one responses mentioning a benzodiazepine, five hundred and thirty-nine carried a dependence warning, a time limit, or an explicit not-first-line verdict. The two that didn't came from older models. So the inconsistency is largely conditional mention rather than bad advice, which you only learn by reading the text instead of scoring the recommendation list.

The companion piece comes at simulated patients, which are now used to train counsellors and to evaluate therapy agents. The argument is an information budget: if you tell a model to be a patient with moderate depression, it answers each item at the level typical of that condition and severity, which implies little variation and a full profile of typical symptoms. That's exactly what they found. Across seventeen models benchmarked against item-level data from eight human samples, simulated patients varied one and a half to eleven times less than real respondents with the same total score, and on most questionnaires endorsed nearly every symptom at a middling level. Real people — including people instructed to feign illness — answer "not at all" about as often as genuine patients, and more often than every model.

The consequence is the sentence that should worry anyone building assessment agents: patients told a severity are rated as having nearly every symptom, so an assessment agent's ability to recognize an absent symptom never gets tested. Scored against the stated severity, the agent placed a quarter of simulated patients in the right band, against about half for patients given a real symptom profile. Prompt engineering, published personas, larger models and reasoning all reduced the pattern without removing it. Having the model verbalize a real symptom profile restored half to two-thirds of the variation, which is a cheap and actionable fix.

Close with a negative result that deserves more attention than negative results usually get. Nine configurations from five families of foundation models that take regional resting-state fMRI time series, evaluated on three thousand one hundred scans from sixteen hundred and fifty-three participants across two observational and five interventional aging cohorts. Frozen representations showed no consistent advantage over plain functional connectivity for concurrent cognitive associations or diagnostic classification. Changing the readout — without touching a single model weight — improved selected associations, which tells you a meaningful share of reported performance in this literature is a property of how you extract the representation, not of the representation. Post-training and embedding alignment helped in some settings and not consistently. And in the primary longitudinal analyses, the imaging representations added nothing beyond baseline clinical measures.

That's the whole week in one finding. The benchmark number was never measuring what it claimed to be measuring, and you can only tell by taking it apart.
