## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. I'm your host, and this is your daily pass through CNS pharmacology, psychiatric and neurology drug discovery, and the computational machinery closing in on both. Eleven items today, and the thread through the first half is dose — not dose in milligrams, but how much of something you deliver, and for how long, before the sign of the effect flips on you. Two papers make that literal, one in a spinal circuit and one in a plasma concentration curve.

The lead comes from Pascal Fossat's group at Bordeaux, and it resolves something the descending-pain literature has argued about since the nineteen-seventies. Serotonergic neurons in the nucleus raphe magnus project to the dorsal horn, and depending which paper you read, they either suppress nociception or amplify it. The standard explanation — fourteen serotonin receptor subtypes live in the spinal cord, different experiments recruit different ones — is true but useless, because it doesn't tell you what determines which.

The answer here is the activity level of the raphe neurons themselves. Chemogenetic activation produced mechanical allodynia lasting two hours — the pro-nociceptive story. But chemogenetic inhibition of the same neurons also lowered paw withdrawal threshold, reversibly, over three to four hours. Both directions pro-nociceptive. Spinal electrophysiology confirmed it: silencing the descending fibers increased C-fiber-evoked spiking in dorsal horn neurons, and increased windup, the marker of central sensitization. So at baseline this pathway is holding nociception down.

Then they resolved the paradox by changing one parameter. Optogenetic stimulation at five hertz with hundred-millisecond pulses is pro-nociceptive. Five hertz with five-millisecond pulses — same frequency, a twentieth of the duty cycle, closer to what these neurons physiologically do — is analgesic: fewer C-fiber spikes, reduced windup, higher threshold. In one cohort tested under both protocols, threshold went up under one and down under the other. And whole-cell recordings ruled out the obvious alternative: these neurons are a homogeneous population by intrinsic properties, so this isn't two subpopulations recruited at different intensities. One population, two amounts of transmitter.

The receptor pharmacology maps onto that. The pro-nociceptive arm is 5-HT3, ionotropic and, by their RNAscope, preferentially on glutamatergic dorsal horn neurons — intrathecal granisetron abolished the hyperalgesia completely. The analgesic arm runs through 5-HT2C and 5-HT2A on inhibitory interneurons; low-duty-cycle stimulation raised c-Fos specifically in Pax2-positive inhibitory cells, and the analgesia was blocked by methysergide, by ketanserin, and by the selective 5-HT2C antagonist RS-102221, but not by granisetron. Tonic analgesia at rest is 5-HT2C alone — RS-102221 by itself dropped withdrawal threshold, while ketanserin did nothing.

Two things make me want to hand this to a medicinal chemist. First, 5-HT2C comes out as the single most enriched serotonin receptor transcript in the dorsal horn, in both excitatory and inhibitory populations, and that enrichment holds at comparable levels in human dorsal horn single-nucleus data. That's rare cross-species reassurance for a spinal target. Second, it reframes why serotonin-noradrenaline reuptake inhibitors give such inconsistent analgesia in neuropathic pain. If the sign of spinal serotonergic action depends on how hard you're driving the descending system, then a drug that globally raises synaptic serotonin isn't a dose you can titrate along an analgesia axis — you may be pushing patients across the inversion point. The question this paper can't answer is what a 5-HT2C agonist does in an established neuropathy model rather than a naive animal.

Second, staying on opioid targets but moving to structure, and I want to praise this one mostly for what it refuses to claim. A group at the University of Wyoming built an exact-residue atlas of opioid receptor wiring: eighty-six human opioid receptor structures — thirty-five mu, twenty-eight kappa, eighteen delta, five nociceptin — collapsed to eighty-four independent experimental maps and converted into contact graphs restricted to residues with unambiguous Ballesteros-Weinstein generic positions. Every structure lives in one comparison space instead of being interpreted individually.

The most useful number is about where conformational change actually shows up. Comparing inactive structures against active transducer-bound ones, mean contact similarity drops only about two and a half percent — but rewired contacts go up nearly thirty-five percent. Global similarity metrics barely notice a state transition that reorganizes a third more of the contact set. And of the changed contacts in maps with a resolved ligand site, only fifteen percent touch the ligand directly. Forty percent are one graph edge away, forty-five percent further. So roughly eighty-five percent of the rewiring on activation is not at the ligand interface. If you're scoring poses off the contact shell, sit with that.

The design-relevant output is two positions. Ballesteros-Weinstein two-by-sixty-three is asparagine in mu, valine in kappa, lysine in delta, aspartate in nociceptin — four chemically distinct environments at one aligned position, about as clean a subtype-selectivity vector as this family offers. And there's a kappa structure with dynorphin A orthosteric and an allosteric modulator bound simultaneously, where the peptide contacts aspartate three-by-thirty-two and the modulator contacts asparagine three-by-thirty-five. Same helix, two design axes, geometry laid out for a bitopic attempt — and a deposited water bridges those same two residues in delta, kappa and nociceptin structures, with no mu instance found.

Now the discipline. Thirty-two component-association tests, none survived global multiplicity correction. The peptide-versus-small-molecule contrast has a nominal p of point-zero-one and an adjusted q of point-three-two, and they report it as a hypothesis. They also disclose that their two headline metrics correlate at r equals minus point nine-nine-seven, so those are not two independent lines of evidence. This is what structural bioinformatics looks like when it declines to overreach, and the atlas is more useful for it.

Third, the other dose paper, in CPT: Pharmacometrics and Systems Pharmacology. A physiologically based pharmacokinetic model of fluoxetine and norfluoxetine in the elderly, verified against published data and then against a real therapeutic-drug-monitoring cohort. The finding is a timing artifact that has probably distorted the geriatric literature for decades.

Fluoxetine's half-life is four to six days in younger adults, eight to nine in the elderly, and simulated apparent oral clearance falls from about twenty-one liters per hour to eleven. Younger adults reach steady state around day twenty-five to thirty; elderly patients not until day forty to forty-five. Watch what that does to a comparison. At ten days of twenty milligrams daily, exposure to the combined active moiety differs between age groups by one-point-one-five-fold — essentially nothing. At thirty days, one-point-four-fold. At fifty days, fluoxetine exposure alone is roughly two-fold higher in the elderly. The age effect isn't modest; it's slow. Any study that ran four or five weeks and concluded age barely matters was measuring before the difference existed.

The clinical cohort backs the model. Forty-seven patients, and absolute trough concentrations were indistinguishable between over-sixty-fives and younger patients — because clinicians were already prescribing about twenty percent lower doses to the older group. Dose-normalize and it appears: thirty-two percent higher combined trough per milligram in the elderly, surviving exclusion of the sixty-milligram patients where linearity is questionable. Prescriber intuition was partially compensating for something the pharmacokinetic literature said wasn't there.

The other half is pharmacogenomic deflation. CYP2D6 phenotype moves parent fluoxetine substantially — poor metabolizers at one-point-four-one-fold of extensive metabolizers — and the model reproduces the published ratios. But because norfluoxetine formation moves the other way, total active moiety lands between point-nine-one and one-point-two-fold across all four phenotypes. So in the elderly as in younger adults, CYP2D6 genotyping tells you less about fluoxetine exposure than the calendar does. The honest caveat is that fluoxetine's exposure-response relationship isn't well established, so higher exposure isn't automatically worse outcome. But if you're attributing late-emerging adverse effects in an older patient to something other than accumulation, look again.

Fourth, from Feng Zhu's group at Zhejiang University — the group behind the Therapeutic Target Database — a system called DrugEvolve, the most ambitious version yet of a genre I've been skeptical of. The idea is autonomous algorithm evolution rather than autonomous discovery. You hand it a task, a baseline model, datasets and metrics, and three domains of language-model agents loop: a Researcher proposing architectures from a curated literature library plus a database of its own past attempts, an Engineer that implements, debugs, trains and scores, and an Analyst that writes up why the last round worked and files that back into memory.

Eleven tasks spanning target identification through clinical-trial prediction, a hundred twenty benchmark test sets, about a hundred eighty GPU-hours per task, and it improved on the baseline in essentially all of them. Some gains are incremental — cancer gene module detection went from Matthews correlation point-seven-seven to point-eight-one. Some are large: gene-disease association prediction went from area under the ROC curve point-nine-zero to point-nine-seven, across five independent splits and an external dataset. And the architectural moves aren't trivial reshuffles; in one case it replaced an attention-based fusion module with a Hamiltonian module treating gene and disease representations as coupled dynamical systems.

What I'd want is a comparison the paper doesn't run. Every gain is measured against the seed model, so "would a general-purpose coding agent given the same GPU budget have done as well" is unanswered — and that's exactly the question the computational-chemistry Perspective I covered yesterday says is now decisive. Read as a claim about superhuman architecture search, this is unproven. Read as evidence that the marginal cost of a task-specific improvement anywhere in the drug pipeline is now roughly a hundred eighty GPU-hours and no graduate student, it's a real number and a slightly unnerving one.

Fifth, Matt Thomson's lab at Caltech, and this is the most conceptually interesting computational paper of the week. The premise is that biomedicine has never had what other engineering fields have: a simulator good enough to search over designs. Their attempt is a cell interaction foundation model — a geometric graph neural network where nodes are cells carrying measured transcriptomes and spatial coordinates, trained by masking one cell's transcriptome and reconstructing it from its neighbors alone, over twenty-three million cell microenvironments from spatial transcriptomics. Then the move: run it generatively, Monte Carlo play-out where every cell is masked in turn and resampled from its current neighborhood, all cells updated together, iterated, always against a matched unperturbed simulation of the same tissue.

That gives a forward simulator for tissue-level perturbation, and they screened over a million single and combinatorial interventions across four hundred eighty-seven signaling molecules. The result I like isn't the win, it's the correction. The model reproduces the known finding that CXCL9 increases T cell infiltration — then shows it drags regulatory T cell induction along with it, exactly the emergent side effect a single-cell-type model cannot see. Searching for a combination that separates those, it nominates CXCL10 with INHBC knockdown, and the top-ranked prediction tested in vitro outperformed an anti-PD-1 combination.

Two caveats. All simulations use the hundred-million-parameter configuration despite training up to four billion, so at this data scale capacity is not the binding constraint. And a virtual perturbation here means clamping a gene's expression channel and holding it there for the whole simulation with cell positions frozen, which constrains where an exposure lands the tissue, not whether it stays there once you stop. Not a CNS paper, but if you build quantitative systems pharmacology models of neuroinflammation, the architecture is borrowable.

Sixth, briefly, the least glamorous item today and probably the one that saves someone a week. From Bin Chen's group at Michigan State, an agentic pipeline called GEOMeta that curates metadata for public transcriptomic data — splitting retrieval, extraction, standardization, ontology mapping and quality control rather than using one prompt, across about five hundred ninety-five thousand human bulk RNA-sequencing samples. Two findings to actually use. Across twenty-two frontier models evaluated prospectively on newly submitted studies, recent open-source flash-tier models matched leading reasoning models on annotation quality at about a tenth of the cost — so if you're paying reasoning-model rates for structured extraction from semi-structured text, you may be buying nothing. And the coverage gaps are a warning for anyone training on this data: perturbation and experimental setting are near a hundred percent reported, tissue eighty-seven percent, but sex is available for thirty-two percent of samples and age for sixteen. For chemical perturbation samples, dose is reported for nineteen percent and duration for twenty-seven. A pharmacology foundation model trained on public transcriptomics is, mostly, a model that doesn't know the dose.

Seventh, into wet neuropharmacology, from Robert Kennedy's group at Michigan with the selectively bred high-responder and low-responder rat lines. These lines differ heritably in novelty-induced exploration and map onto externalizing versus internalizing temperament, both of which raise substance-use-disorder risk. The question is what that difference looks like chemically in the living brain, and the method is untargeted metabolomics on microdialysate rather than tissue — extracellular, dynamic, in the animal, with probes spanning dorsomedial striatum and accumbens core and shell.

They annotated six hundred eighty metabolites in dialysate, a real expansion of what people have gotten from this compartment. At baseline, phenotype separated cleanly on stress-response, bioenergetic and amino-acid pathways, including two stress-related signaling lipids not previously linked to these lines. Then fifteen milligrams per kilogram of cocaine moved two hundred thirty metabolites, and the interesting result is directional. Cocaine acted as an equalizer, collapsing pre-existing phenotype differences in arginine biosynthesis and in alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism down to common levels. The mechanistic candidate is N-acetylaspartylglutamate — an endogenous mGluR3 agonist that restrains glutamate release and suppresses cocaine self-administration. It was higher at baseline in the high responders and abruptly depleted in everyone after cocaine. So a single dose removes a brake on glutamate transmission, and removes more of it from the animals that had more.

Alongside that, purine metabolites rose while inosine fell, consistent with cocaine impairing adenosine clearance rather than only increasing release; and antioxidants including carnosine dropped while hippuric acid, a shared marker of cocaine neurotoxicity and aging, rose. That last cluster is suggestive rather than established — one acute dose, and the aging inference rests on cross-study comparison. The equalizer finding is what I'd build on, because it says vulnerability phenotype and drug response interact rather than add.

Eighth, from Satoshi Ikemoto's group at NIDA, a satisfying twenty-year follow-up to their own observation that rats will self-administer nicotine directly into the supramammillary nucleus. Nobody had shown what nicotine acts on there. Retrograde labeling from medial septum plus RNAscope: ninety-seven percent of supramammillary neurons projecting to medial septum express the beta-two nicotinic subunit and ninety-three percent express vesicular glutamate transporter two — a glutamatergic projection carrying the subunit most implicated in nicotine reinforcement. Twenty-two to twenty-six percent also carried GABAergic markers, so some of these cells are likely co-transmitting.

The behavioral half is a methods lesson. Under a fixed-ratio schedule in mice, nicotine self-administration was indistinguishable from saline at ten and thirty micrograms per kilogram, and the hundred-microgram dose actually suppressed responding. Switch to a progressive-ratio schedule and the classic inverted-U appears, significant quadratic trend, peak at twenty-five micrograms. Mice can show nicotine dose-dependence; fixed-ratio schedules just can't see it.

Then fiber photometry, and the controls sell it. Intravenous nicotine produced rapid, infusion-locked, dose-dependent calcium increases in supramammillary glutamatergic neurons. Cocaine at three hundred micrograms per kilogram and fentanyl at six did not reliably move the signal, so this isn't a generic reward-infusion response. And nicotine pyrrolidine methiodide, a peripherally restricted nicotinic agonist, also did nothing, ruling out the peripheral-afferent route despite prior work showing that compound induces c-Fos in this very nucleus. Central, drug-specific, time-locked. It doesn't yet show the pathway is necessary for reinforcement — experimenter-delivered infusions are not self-administration, and they say so — but it identifies a non-dopaminergic substrate with a druggable subunit on it.

Ninth, a developmental piece from UNIFESP in São Paulo on insular parvalbumin interneurons, impulsivity and alcohol. Adolescent mice at postnatal day twenty-eight versus adults at day sixty-four, on an omission-contingency learning task where the correct response is to withhold a nose-poke. Adolescents were markedly worse at withholding and drank more ethanol in two-bottle choice — and kept drinking more when it was adulterated with quinine, the aversion-resistant pattern. The anatomy is a dissociation rather than a level difference. Adolescents showed higher overall Fos in anterior insula after the task but a lower fraction of activated parvalbumin interneurons, with equal numbers of parvalbumin cells. So the adolescent insula runs hotter overall while recruiting less of its own inhibitory control. Bidirectional chemogenetics in adults closed the loop: Gq activation of insular parvalbumin interneurons reduced impulsive responding, Gi inhibition increased it, and Gq activation also reduced ethanol seeking. I'd flag that the Fos fractions are tiny in absolute terms — two or three percent of parvalbumin cells in either group — so the immunohistochemistry is the weaker limb. The chemogenetics stands on its own.

Tenth, a kidney-to-brain paper out of Shenzhen People's Hospital that I'm including because the drug is already on the shelf. Neuropsychiatric comorbidity in chronic kidney disease is well documented and mechanistically unexplained. Across several mouse models, an adenine-induced model gave the most robust renal injury and, notably, no consistent baseline anxiety phenotype. What it gave instead was a lowered threshold: after subthreshold unpredictable stress, stressors that did nothing to healthy mice produced clear anxiety-like avoidance and enhanced freezing in the disease animals.

The circuit is the central amygdala — c-Fos mapping flagged it, fiber photometry showed exaggerated stress-evoked calcium responses in central amygdala GABAergic neurons, and chemogenetic inhibition of those neurons attenuated the behavior. The mechanistic candidate is angiotensin two: circulating levels were elevated, fluorescently labeled peripheral angiotensin two accumulated more in central amygdala in disease animals, and knocking down the AT1 receptor gene specifically there attenuated both the anxiety and the exaggerated calcium response. So: a disease state that primes a limbic node, a peripheral ligand with increased regional access, and a receptor for which angiotensin receptor blockers are already dosed daily in exactly this patient population. Appealing repurposing hypothesis, and appropriately hedged — how the circulating ligand reaches the central amygdala is undefined, and the brain-to-kidney arm is labeled exploratory.

And to finish, a deflationary result from Natale Sciolino's group at UConn that I think is more valuable than the positive version would have been. GABAergic neurons in the lateral hypothalamus drive feeding, arousal and reward, and the field's working assumption is one pathway, one function — each projection target carrying a distinct behavioral job. They tested that properly, comparing optogenetic stimulation of the somata against terminals in two very different targets, the dorsal pons and the lateral preoptic area, across feeding, gnawing at non-food objects, cricket hunting, place preference and operant self-stimulation.

All three conditions did essentially the same thing. Increased caloric intake in sated mice — and notably not non-caloric cellulose intake during terminal stimulation, so it isn't indiscriminate oral-motor output. Increased gnawing and shredding, increased cricket killing, and support for place preference and self-stimulation. One shared motivational repertoire, three entry points. The reason I trust the interpretation is that they name the confound themselves and supply the evidence against their own convenience. Terminal stimulation can antidromically activate the somata, and their Fos data show exactly that for the lateral preoptic field, while the dorsal pons field, further away, showed no such effect. So part of the convergence may be backpropagation, and they lay out the alternatives honestly — collateralization of single neurons to both targets, local intra-hypothalamic recruitment, or genuine downstream redundancy — with their data not distinguishing among them. Either way the practical message holds. If you were planning to gain selectivity by targeting one lateral hypothalamic GABAergic output over another, the behavioral specificity you were counting on may not be there.

That's the episode: a spinal serotonin circuit where the duty cycle sets the sign, a structural atlas that declines to overclaim, a pharmacokinetic model showing an age effect can be real and simply slow, and a hypothalamic output map less modular than anyone wanted.
