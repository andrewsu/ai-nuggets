## Script

Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Saturday, November the twenty-first. Today's thread is dissociation — the repeated discovery that a thing we have been measuring as one thing is two, or that the quantity we know how to move is not the quantity that does the work. A cortical region deactivates and we call it deactivation; a drug triples slow-wave sleep and we assume the function follows; a validator reports fewer findings and we assume the output got more correct. In every case today, someone pulled the two apart and they came apart cleanly. Twelve items.

The lead is in Nature Neuroscience, from Heidelberg, and it takes on one of the most reliable observations in chronic pain imaging: the medial prefrontal cortex goes quiet. That deactivation shows up in human neuropathic pain, it shows up in rodent models, and it has been invoked to explain both the cognitive fog patients describe and the maintenance of the pain itself. What nobody had was a cellular account of how the deactivation happens, or whether reversing it would help the sensation, the suffering, or both.

They put a gradient-index lens into the prelimbic cortex of mice expressing a calcium indicator in all GABAergic neurons, and imaged the same cells before and after a spared nerve injury. The first surprise is how selective the inhibitory neurons are at baseline — individual prelimbic interneurons respond to light touch, or to noxious pressure, or to cold, with a sharpness you would expect from excitatory cells in a somatosensory area, not from inhibitory cells in prefrontal cortex. After nerve injury the responsive cells sensitize, as the slice literature predicted, and previously unresponsive cells are recruited de novo and become polymodal. The deactivation is not a passive fading of excitatory drive; it is an active unsilencing of inhibition.

Then they take it apart by cell type. Chemogenetically silencing all GABAergic neurons reverses the injury-induced drop in Fos and attenuates mechanical allodynia at one week and at four weeks. Parvalbumin cells carry much of that, consistent with prior work. Somatostatin cells carry none of it. And a third population — parvalbumin-negative, somatostatin-negative, labelled through a Prox1 driver — also reverses allodynia. So far, so additive. The dissociation is on the excitatory side. Restoring activity in layer five Rbp4-positive pyramidal neurons strongly attenuates mechanical allodynia and does nothing to the affective side. Restoring activity in layer two-three Cux2-positive neurons, or in Ntsr1-positive neurons — which, unusually for prefrontal cortex, sit scattered in layer five rather than layer six — does nothing for allodynia at all, but drives a marked reduction in pain-avoidance behaviour, the voluntary measure of negative affect. Rabies tracing shows the wiring: parvalbumin cells preferentially contact the Rbp4 population, Prox1 cells contact the Cux2 and Ntsr1 populations, and those are the cells projecting to rostral anterior cingulate. Activating that prelimbic-to-cingulate projection, by two independent strategies, relieves the aversive dimension and leaves sensory hypersensitivity untouched early and late.

Prefrontal deactivation in chronic pain has been treated as a single clinical target, with the implicit hope that lifting it lifts everything. This says the deactivation is a composite of at least two microcircuits with non-overlapping behavioural read-outs, gated by two different interneuron classes feeding two different output pathways. A treatment that restores prefrontal output in the wrong lamina will produce a patient whose pain hurts exactly as much but bothers them less, or the reverse — and either outcome would look like a partial response in a trial that only measured one of them. The caveat is the usual one for this literature: the functional manipulations are in male mice, and chemogenetics on a transgenic driver line is a long way from a molecule.

Staying in neuropathic pain for a second item, and on a target that has been one of the field's cleanest stories. Microglial P2X4 receptors are the canonical mediator of early neuropathic pain — they are induced after nerve injury, they drive the BDNF release that shifts chloride gradients in dorsal horn neurons, and the whole model was built on the first days to weeks after injury. This group in Montpellier and Bordeaux asked what P2X4 is doing at thirty and sixty days, using conditional deletion in microglia or in neurons separately.

Deleting microglial P2X4 before injury completely prevents mechanical allodynia, as expected. The new result is that deleting it forty days after injury — in animals whose allodynia is long since established — fully reverses it, in both sexes. That matters because the sex-dependence of microglial contributions to pain has been one of the field's sharper controversies, and this is prevention and reversal in both sexes alike. Then the complication: at those late timepoints, P2X4 immunoreactivity in spinal microglia is undetectable. The receptor you just proved is necessary cannot be found in the compartment where the model says it acts. The authors suggest the sustained contribution may involve microglia outside the spinal cord, or a population below detection. Neuronal P2X4 deletion, separately, partially attenuates hypersensitivity and reduces wide-dynamic-range neuron hyperexcitability at day sixty — a modulatory role, not the main event. And global deletion prevents the anxiety- and depressive-like behaviour that emerges by day thirty in males and day sixty in females, though whether that is a direct affective role for the receptor or just downstream of less pain is left open. For anyone developing P2X4 antagonists, the practical message is encouraging — a late intervention window exists — and the mechanistic message is that we do not know where the drug would need to go.

Third, a serotonin receptor knockout that lands on bioenergetics. The 5-HT2A receptor is the target of everything from the atypical antipsychotics to the psychedelics now in trials, and the acute pharmacology is reasonably well understood: agonism is anxiolytic, and that anxiolysis runs through ventral hippocampal parvalbumin interneurons — restoring the receptor in those cells alone reinstates it in knockout animals. This group asked what chronic absence does to those same cells.

Constitutive deletion in BALB/c mice produces robust anxiety-like behaviour across four paradigms in both sexes, correcting a confusing prior result on a different background strain. Unbiased proteomics on knockout hippocampus then shows substantially reduced parvalbumin itself — the standard proxy for the activity of those interneurons — accompanied by a coordinated downshift in exactly the machinery a fast-spiking cell needs: oxidative phosphorylation and electron transport chain components, glycolysis, the tricarboxylic acid cycle, and the cytoplasmic ribosome. Fast-spiking interneurons are metabolically extravagant, so if their drive drops, their support infrastructure follows.

The dissociation is in the perineuronal net. The net normally moves with parvalbumin expression in both directions — activity deposits matrix, and genetic loss of matrix components reduces parvalbumin markers. Here, aggrecan is unchanged and HAPLN1, which is critical for net assembly, is unchanged, while the link protein HAPLN4 is substantially reduced. So the net has not disassembled; one specific link protein has moved. So the parvalbumin reduction is not secondary to matrix collapse. And the direction is pharmacologically reversible in principle: a single intraperitoneal dose of the selective 5-HT2A agonist 25CN-NBOH at one milligram per kilogram shifted the same protein sets in the opposite direction a full week later — respiratory chain, TCA enzymes and ribosomal proteins all up — at roughly a fifth the magnitude of the knockout's shift. One dose, one week of persistence, on a bioenergetic signature. That is a mechanistic hook for the durability that psychedelic trials keep reporting, and it points somewhere other than synaptogenesis.

Fourth, and still on serotonin, a structural result on the transporter. From the Forrest group at NINDS with Vienna: how does sodium binding enforce the obligatory coupling between sodium and serotonin transport? Molecular dynamics identifies an interaction network in which sodium occupancy at the second cation site stabilizes contacts between transmembrane helices five, eight and one, and those contacts gate aqueous access on both the cytoplasmic and extracellular sides. Mutate the coordinating residues, or a threonine up in helix five, and you get a transporter with reduced concentrative power and a constitutive sodium conductance. That is uncoupled sodium uniport: the ion is still moving, the substrate is no longer obligately coming with it. The coupling and the conduction are separable properties of the same protein, and the residues that separate them are identified. For anyone thinking about transporter ligands beyond simple uptake blockade — partial releasers, atypical inhibitors, the whole uncoupled-current side of transporter pharmacology — this is the structural vocabulary for what you are trying to bias.

Fifth, an old anticonvulsant in a new place. The locus coeruleus accumulates abnormal tau earlier than almost anywhere else in Alzheimer's disease, which makes it the most attractive and least tractable early target in the field. This group at the Shanghai Institute of Organic Chemistry combined electrophysiology, single-cell sequencing and sleep EEG in P301S tau mice and in wild-type mice given a local fibril inoculation into the locus coeruleus.

Young P301S mice — but not aged ones — show elevated tonic firing and intrinsic excitability in locus coeruleus neurons, alongside pre-tangle phosphorylated tau and reduced low-threshold potassium current density. The age split matters: by ten months surviving neurons fire at control rates, so a study at the wrong age sees nothing. Sequencing points at reduced potassium channel transcripts including Kcnq3, with increased nuclear REST as a candidate upstream regulator, and immunostaining shows reduced Kv7.2 and Kv7.3 protein — the Kv7.2 drop without a matching transcript change, so part of this is post-transcriptional. Local fibril inoculation reproduces the physiology in wild-type mice and the hyperexcitability depends on endogenous tau. Co-applying a Kv4 modulator and the Kv7 opener retigabine attenuates the hyperexcitability acutely; inhibiting GSK-3 beta reduces tau phosphorylation, raises the potassium current and lowers firing.

Then the part that makes it a drug paper. Mice got retigabine, ten milligrams per kilogram intraperitoneally, every two days from two and a half to four months after a unilateral fibril inoculation. It reduced AT8-positive tau burden in the locus coeruleus — bilaterally, from a unilateral seed — and was associated with higher tyrosine hydroxylase-positive cell density. Retigabine is a withdrawn drug with real tolerability problems, so this is not a clinical proposal; what it is, is evidence that an activity-sensitive component of tau pathology can be reduced pharmacologically during a defined early window, and that the window closes. Treatment was systemic, so locus coeruleus specificity is not established.

Sixth, a clean negative that should recalibrate a popular target. Slow-wave sleep has been proposed as a therapeutic lever in depression on the grounds that it serves memory consolidation and synaptic renormalization. Zurich ran the direct test: a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover in twenty-two patients with major depression and twenty matched controls, with single nocturnal doses of gamma-hydroxybutyrate — given as sodium oxybate at fifty milligrams per kilogram — or trazodone as an active comparator, or placebo, all under polysomnography, with declarative, procedural and emotional memory tested before and after sleep.

GHB did exactly what it is supposed to do: strongly increased slow-wave sleep duration and delta power, considerably more than trazodone. And memory consolidation did not improve in any domain, in either group, under either drug. The mechanistic clue is in the microstructure: GHB raised slow-oscillation density while slightly reducing spindle count, density and amplitude, which is the same dissociation previously reported for tiagabine. Slow waves and spindles have to be coupled in a particular temporal relationship for hippocampal replay to reach cortex, and a drug that inflates one while suppressing the other produces more of the stage and less of the mechanism. Both drugs promoted sleep more strongly in patients than controls, and even declarative consolidation — the one domain where patients differed at placebo — did not normalize. Slow-wave sleep quantity is not the target. Oscillatory coordination might be, and that is a harder thing to drug.

Seventh, a schedule question in neuromodulation. Accelerated TMS protocols compress weeks of treatment into days, and the clinical evidence so far suggests non-inferiority. What nobody had done was compare the neural effects of the two schedules in the same people. Vanderbilt ran a within-subject crossover in a transdiagnostic sample — thirty-six enrolled, thirty-four analysed, spanning psychosis, depression and anxiety — giving five daily sessions of continuous theta-burst stimulation to a personalized default mode network target with imaging before and after, then, after a washout averaging over a year, five accelerated sessions in a single day with the same imaging.

Across both schedules, the stimulation reduced bilateral parietal default mode connectivity, with a p value just under point oh five. Schedule had no significant effect on the magnitude of that change, and a psychosis diagnosis did not change it either. This is a small study with a modest main effect and a null on the comparison, so it cannot establish equivalence — but it is the first direct neural comparison and it sits with the clinical non-inferiority results rather than against them. If the biological effect of the dose is schedule-invariant, the argument for accelerated protocols becomes purely logistical, which is a good argument.

Eighth, a short pharmacology item with a methodological sting. A group in Belo Horizonte and Aarhus characterized nicotine in male and female mice across the elevated plus maze and conditioned place preference. The highest dose, one milligram per kilogram, reduced open-arm exploration in males — which reads as anxiogenic until you notice it also cut basal locomotion in the open field in both sexes, so the anxiety reading is confounded by the animals simply moving less. The authors say so themselves. In place preference, nicotine at half a milligram per kilogram was rewarding in males and not in females. And aripiprazole, the dopamine D2 partial agonist, prevented acquisition of nicotine place preference in males at all three doses tested, from a tenth of a milligram per kilogram up to ten. A partial agonist blocking drug reward at a dose two orders of magnitude below the top of the range is worth following; the limitation is structural, in that with no female place preference to begin with there is no way to test whether aripiprazole would block it.

Ninth, moving to the computational side, and to a question about quality control rather than capability. NeuroImgAgent is a multi-agent platform that turns natural-language requests into functional MRI workflows across humans, macaques, rats and mice. The claim is not that it preprocesses better — across two hundred and sixty-eight paired cases its metrics were broadly comparable to fMRIPrep, AFNI and RABIES, which is the right target. The claim is about knowing when it has failed. Quantitative checks plus vision-language assessment decide whether processing continues, enters bounded recovery, or stops. With GPT-5.5 behind it, it corrected a hundred and eleven of a hundred and twenty deliberately induced orientation errors. The number worth keeping: in a hundred and twenty paired challenge cases, blinded expert raters found the full configuration usable ninety-two and a half percent of the time versus seventy-six and a half with the quality-control stage ablated, and — more importantly — unusable outputs delivered without any warning fell from eighteen and a third percent to four point two. The failure mode that matters in automated pipelines is not failing, it is failing silently, and that is the number that moved.

Tenth, the same lesson in regulatory submissions, stated almost as a theorem. ClinDataBench gives a coding agent twenty-two raw trial data domains, a statistical analysis plan and dataset specifications, and asks it to produce the thirteen analysis datasets, the metadata and a program that rebuilds them offline — the work human programmers do by hand before an FDA submission. Agents run in containers whose only network route is their model API, so they cannot look up the published reference outputs. In the pilot across six models, three closed models and DeepSeek delivered all thirteen datasets in every configuration; two open-weight models delivered nothing complete. They compared a plain submission, a self-refinement pass, and a pass that gets the agent's own validator report as feedback. Four of six validator-feedback workflows had usable comparisons, and every one of them reduced its count of validator findings. None of them reduced its count of failed derivations. And the package with the fewest validator findings of all carried none of the two hundred and thirty-four efficacy summary records in the form the specifications define. Conformance and correctness are orthogonal, the optimization pressure from a structural validator is real, and it points away from the thing you care about. Anyone building agentic pipelines against an automated checker should read that paragraph twice.

Eleventh, a scaffold aimed at the same class of problem upstream, at the hypothesis. The complaint motivating EpiCompass is that research agents make claims their observations do not support, explore a narrow slice of hypothesis space, and hold onto beliefs even after acknowledging contradicting evidence. The proposed fix is an explicit epistemic state — a tree linking hypotheses, evidence, beliefs and revisions — that gets reassessed as evidence accumulates, with unresolved conflicts and unexplored directions used to steer the next hypothesis, and a final check of the conclusion against everything accumulated. Candidates are chosen by anticipated epistemic gain, counting both refinement of what is there and expansion into what is not.

Tested on three interactive discovery benchmarks spanning physical law recovery and enzyme kinetics, with three backbones, it improves the primary metric in every benchmark-backbone combination — symbolic accuracy up by fourteen and fifteen points over a ReAct baseline, trajectory error down by factors of ten to eighteen, enzyme kinetics exact accuracy from sixty-nine to eighty-five percent with the weakest backbone. The dissociation I want to flag is inside their own tables: on the physics benchmark the explanation score barely moves, sometimes drops, while the trajectory error falls by an order of magnitude. The agent is getting much better at fitting the hidden mechanism and no better at explaining it. The scaffold is model-agnostic and training-free, which is the practical appeal. But the gap between a correct executable law and a good account of it is exactly the gap that matters if the output is meant for a human.

And to close, a review in Translational Psychiatry that is really an audit, and a pointed one. The authors went through two hundred and sixty-six rodent psychedelic studies published between twenty-fourteen and this year and asked how well the experimental conditions correspond to the conditions we believe shape therapeutic outcome in humans. Set and setting, in other words, treated as a measurable property of the protocol. Only fourteen percent reported testing during the animals' active phase. Seven percent used environmental enrichment. Twenty-one percent used refined handling. Drug administration was almost universally by restraint-requiring routes. So the typical preclinical psychedelic experiment is a stressed, singly-handled, light-phase animal — close to the inverse of the human conditions whose importance the field explicitly argues for. Second, the assays: brief, constrained tests reading out isolated markers, against a drug effect that is multidimensional and evolves over hours. Third, and the one I had not thought about, almost everything is group-level and cross-sectional, so individual differences in response — the thing that dominates the clinical conversation — are invisible by design, as are social mechanisms. They recommend longitudinal tracking of individuals in enriched social settings with broad behavioural characterization. It is easy to read this as scolding. I would read it as the simplest available explanation for why preclinical psychedelic mechanism studies keep failing to replicate each other: the independent variable people think they are manipulating is not the only one that differs between labs.
