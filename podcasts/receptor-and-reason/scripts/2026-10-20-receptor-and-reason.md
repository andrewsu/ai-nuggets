## Script

Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Tuesday, October twentieth. Ten items today.

The thread running through almost all of them is a single question: compared with what? In each of these papers the headline turns on the condition the effect is measured against, and in several the honest answer is that the comparison being made was not the comparison in the title. Sometimes that reveals an artifact, sometimes it rescues a compound, and once or twice it dissolves the finding entirely.

We start with a compound changing mechanism class. CVN636 entered the literature in 2023 as an allosteric agonist at mGlu7, with a potency around seven nanomolar — a striking number for a receptor whose endogenous ligand binds in the millimolar range — and it has been of interest since because oral dosing reduced ethanol self-administration in rats. A group at Columbia and Vanderbilt has now re-run the pharmacology in two BRET assays and reports that the compound has no intrinsic agonist activity whatsoever. Alone, up to a hundred micromolar, more than seven thousand times the published potency, it produces no inhibition of cyclic AMP and no G-protein recruitment. Add an orthosteric agonist and it potentiates cleanly, shifting both potency and maximum for L-AP4 and for LSP2-9166, with a modulator potency of a hundred and forty nanomolar against each. Block the orthosteric site and the potentiation vanishes.

The explanation for the discrepancy is the part to carry forward. The original number came from a CRE-luciferase readout integrated over five hours in CHO cells. Over five hours, glutamate accumulates in the medium. An integrating reporter then reads potentiation of ambient glutamate as agonism. Exchange the medium immediately before dosing, clamp the ambient glutamate by co-expressing the EAAT3 transporter, read at five to thirty minutes instead, and the agonism is simply gone. The comparator was never zero.

The second result is the one with legs. Using CODA-RET, where each protomer carries a complementary luciferase fragment so that only a defined dimer is optically active, they find the potentiation is confined to mGlu7 homodimers. Pair mGlu7 with mGlu2, with mGlu3, with mGlu4 or with mGlu8, and there is nothing — no agonism, no potentiation of either agonist. One heterologous partner abolishes it. That hands the field something it has not had, a probe that can separate homodimer from heterodimer populations at synapses where orthosteric agonists cannot tell them apart. The caveats are substantial: entirely HEK293T overexpression with rat receptors and fused luciferase fragments, no neurons and no slices, three independent experiments per panel. The paper reports significance for the potency shifts without ever printing the actual values or the fold-shift magnitudes. And heterodimer assembly is inferred from the complementation design rather than independently quantified, so a negative at mGlu7 with mGlu4 is a negative in a system you are trusting to have built that dimer.

Second, a receptor that may not be on the cell type you think. The Price lab at UT Dallas profiled dorsal root ganglia from roughly a hundred organ donors by mass-spectrometry proteomics, single-nucleus sequencing, spatial transcriptomics, flow cytometry and patch clamp, split by documented history of opioid use disorder. They lead with a negative: not a single differentially expressed protein survives correction, twelve and a half thousand proteins per sample, no separation by condition in the principal components. What survives is pathway-level — allograft rejection, interferon alpha and gamma response, complement — and on the sequencing side individual genes do come through in neurons, immune cells and satellite glia. Flow cytometry puts CD3-positive T cells up in opioid-use-disorder ganglia at p equals zero point zero two eight, with CD11b-positive cells unchanged.

Physiologically the picture is sensitisation without spontaneous activity: more action potentials to a depolarising ramp, rheobase only trending, no change in spontaneous firing, threshold, capacitance or spike shape. That is an inflammatory signature rather than a neuropathic one. It is also thin — twenty-seven neurons from three donors against seventy-five from four — and the authors say outright that they would have needed eleven donors per group for eighty percent power on rheobase alone. The one significant comparison is also the one analysed by pooling cells rather than by donor.

The part that matters for drug discovery is a replication rather than a discovery, and the paper credits the earlier report. In human dorsal root ganglia, the kappa opioid receptor gene is predominantly expressed in satellite glial cells. Mu and delta sit in neurons; kappa sits in the glial sheath, confirmed by spatial transcriptomics and in-situ hybridisation across six donors, with only sparse neuronal signal. A cross-species atlas finds minimal non-neuronal kappa in mouse, rat, guinea pig, cynomolgus and rhesus. Every species we use to build peripheral kappa pharmacology puts that receptor on a different cell type from the one it occupies in people. Supporting the reading that the neuronal changes are indirect, transcriptional changes were about as similar between opioid-receptor-expressing and non-expressing A-fibres as between A- and C-fibres, and receptor expression itself did not change with opioid use disorder. Caveats — donors were on life support and may have been in active withdrawal, and the cohorts come from organ-donor records with polysubstance confounds.

Third, a striatal paper arguing that dopamine concentration is the wrong variable. A group at the University of New South Wales reports that during early instrumental learning, D2 medium spiny neurons in dorsomedial striatum are transiently recruited and exert a local veto on their D1 neighbours. The demonstration is the pharmacology. Raclopride raises extracellular dopamine and de-inhibits D2 cells at the same time; pile more dopamine on top with a dopamine-transporter blocker, and the D1 neurons stay molecularly silent — no ERK phosphorylation, no histone H3 phosphorylation. Chemogenetically silencing the D2 population in one hemisphere restores D1 recruitment in that hemisphere, in the same animal.

Fibre photometry locates the break precisely, and this is the nice bit. Dopamine rises stepwise. Cyclic AMP in the D1 cells pulses faithfully with each step. Protein kinase A activity is where it fails. So the block is downstream of the receptor and downstream of the cyclase, at the kinase-phosphatase step — which points pharmacologically at the DARPP-32 and phosphatase machinery rather than at D1 desensitisation. Two further observations. Reversing the drug order shows that raclopride crushes a kinase signal that is already running, so this is an eraser and not only a preventer — a plausible cellular substrate for how a brief dopamine dip becomes a lasting plasticity veto. And calcium imaging is completely blind to it: the calcium signal tracks locomotion with a half-second lag and shows none of this. An entire class of experiments would miss the mechanism by construction. Behaviourally, driving cyclic AMP in D2 cells on purpose at each reward, using a light-gated bacterial cyclase, made mice press less but with sharper devaluation sensitivity and resistance to retroactive interference — more indirect-pathway drive producing better goal-directed learning, which inverts the standard account.

I have to flag something about that paper, because it gates how much you should lean on it. There is not a single inferential statistic in the manuscript body — no sample sizes beyond one methods line, no test statistics, no p-values, no effect sizes. Every figure legend points to a supplementary table that is not in the file, and nine supplementary figures carry load-bearing results including the calcium control. The mechanism is well argued and the drug-order reversal is elegant. Nothing in it is currently quotable as a number.

Fourth, bile acid receptor pharmacology in the ageing hippocampus, from CSIR-CDRI in Lucknow with collaborators at IIT Kanpur and the MRC in Cambridge — and a different TGR5 story from the enteric one we covered last month. TGR5 turns out to be expressed in hippocampal neural stem cells, and the selective agonist TC-G 1005 releases two distinct brakes on them. Against BMP4-imposed quiescence it restores proliferation and lowers FOXO3a; against interleukin-6-driven senescence it drops p53 and p16 and restores proliferation at every dose tested. In eighteen-month-old mice, five milligrams per kilogram twice daily under the skin for four weeks expanded the SOX2-positive stem pool, reduced p16-positive cells, and restored Y-maze performance — all of it abolished when TGR5 was knocked down in the dentate gyrus by an AAV-delivered short hairpin.

Two things complicate it. The first is the paper's own most interesting negative: the drug makes no neurons. Doublecortin-positive cells were negligible in treated and untreated aged animals, and MAP2 was actively suppressed while nestin rose. The authors therefore argue the cognitive benefit comes from reducing senescence in the niche rather than from neurogenesis, and concede they never showed new mature neurons. The second is a dosing problem they report honestly and then do not reconcile. Their own pharmacokinetics put peak brain exposure around one micromolar with a brain half-life of forty minutes, while the quiescence-release effects only reached significance at ten micromolar. The senescence arm works at achievable concentrations; the quiescence arm, on their numbers, does not. Add that the four-group hairpin-by-drug design is analysed by one-way ANOVA, so the interaction carrying the necessity claim is never formally tested, that there is no antagonist and no knockout anywhere, and that the mice are all male.

Fifth, a cannabidiol trial with an unusually honest secondary analysis. This is EEG from a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover trial of broad-spectrum CBD in autistic children aged six to twelve, titrated to ten milligrams per kilogram per day over twelve weeks, with an eight-week washout. The pre-registered question was whether CBD shifts duration mismatch negativity, the standard passive index of auditory deviance detection. The answer is a near-miss: at the frontocentral site, latency fell by about thirty-two milliseconds on drug relative to placebo, nominally significant at p equals zero point zero two nine, but failing correction at an adjusted p of zero point zero five eight. Amplitude did not move at either site.

Three things make that result worth less than it looks, and I think the authors would agree. Their own simulation says they had eighty percent power to detect about forty-four milliseconds — a nominally significant effect smaller than your detectable floor is the classic signature of the winner's curse. About half the apparent effect is the placebo arm's latency going up, not the drug arm's coming down. And the electrode-by-treatment interaction is not significant, so the site dissociation is not statistically supported. Nineteen children were analysed, only ten contributed all four timepoints.

The finding that did survive correction is the one that runs against the premise of the trial. Greater parent-reported social-relating difficulty correlated with a larger mismatch negativity, around minus zero point six at both sites — the opposite sign to the attenuated-MMN model the treatment hypothesis was built on. The authors say plainly that these data caution against treating a larger amplitude or a shorter latency as inherently more adaptive. That is the more useful sentence in the paper.

Sixth, the purest "compared with what" item of the day, and my favourite. A group working across Chinese and US institutions took every placebo-controlled single-ingredient trial with posted results on the trial registry — nearly ten thousand of them, covering three point nine million participants — linked them to current US drug labels, and asked how much of each labelled nonserious adverse reaction is actually attributable to the drug. Their metric is an excess fraction; below zero point five means the frequency on drug is less than twice the frequency on placebo. The headline: about eighty percent of the nineteen thousand listed reactions across nine hundred and nineteen drugs fall below that line. The median absolute excess is zero point seven five per hundred patients. In trials that posted every nonserious event rather than only those above a reporting threshold, the excess is below one per hundred for seventy-seven percent of reactions. Several canonical label entries come out at essentially zero — headache on metformin, headache and nausea on pregabalin, headache and nasopharyngitis on liraglutide.

Two refinements keep this from being a glib result. Relative and absolute excess come apart, and the paper gives the clean example: metformin diarrhoea sits below two-fold relative to placebo and still carries an absolute excess of about ten per hundred patients. And the authors refuse the obvious inference. Because their model under-detected established reactions when evidence was sparse — it found excess for only thirty-five of fifty-four known pairs — and because demanding statistical evidence of excess would retain barely five percent of listed reactions, they argue the data do not support deleting label entries. What they support is printing the placebo column next to the drug column, which only about a quarter of labelled reactions currently carry. The honest weaknesses: for the half of reactions resting on a single trial, the data alone imply fifty-six percent rather than eighty, and the rest is borrowing across drugs and drug classes; the primary outcome was redefined after a subset had been inspected; and individual-pair probabilities are miscalibrated.

Seventh, staying with confidence as distinct from accuracy. A team at Imperial and UCL put eight language models through a hundred consultant-level neurology board questions and measured not just whether they were right but whether they knew. Accuracy ranges from forty-five percent for the smallest Llama up to ninety-two percent for both Gemini 3 models, against a human pass mark around sixty. Every model is overconfident, and most overconfident where it is least accurate. They compared three ways of extracting confidence: asking the model, asking a fresh instance of the same model to critique the first one's answer, and sampling ten times at temperature zero point seven and using agreement as the confidence. Pooled across models, consistency discriminates right from wrong best, at an area under the curve of zero point seven four against zero point six nine for self-report — the only significant difference in the paper, at p equals zero point zero four eight, uncorrected across three comparisons.

The useful result is that the ranking inverts by model. Gemini 3 Flash, one of the two most accurate models, has a consistency score of essentially chance — because a model that is right ninety-two percent of the time answers near-identically on all ten samples, which destroys the variance the method depends on. Its self-report, meanwhile, is excellent. The small Llama is the mirror image: its self-reported confidence is worse than chance, while its sampling consistency works. So the best pooled method is the worst method for the best model, and any deployment that picks one abstention rule globally picks it wrong for somebody. Caveats: a hundred questions means every interval is about ten points wide, contamination is handled by assertion rather than by test, and the clean scaling claim only holds within one model family.

Eighth, a benchmark that asks whether the language model in the loop is doing anything at all. The setup is a sequential biological screen over gene-essentiality data, where a model proposes candidates, you read out results, and it proposes again. The authors build a false-discovery certificate that survives adaptive sampling and corrupted readouts — their certified error rate is a ten-thousandth against a nominal ten percent, where naive multiple-testing correction runs at thirty-seven percent — but the certificate is scaffolding for the real question, which is a paired counterfactual: run the identical loop with the proposer returning nothing.

The answers are bracing. First, the no-model loop passes the field's standard feedback ablation on its own, because the re-selection policy re-observes promising candidates without help. Second, origin counts overstate the contribution roughly sixfold: the model originates a couple of discoveries per campaign but displaces nearly as many the fallback would have found, leaving a net gain of about zero point three five with an interval comfortably spanning zero. Third, their own headline prompt-engineering effect — telling the model what had already been tested — did not replicate on a pre-registered set of fifty fresh seeds, where no model's effect excludes zero. And fourth, a deliberately dumb non-model prior, the average essentiality of each gene across five other cell lines fixed before any readout, certifies about twelve discoveries where the best model manages three. The proposer is losing to a lookup table.

Ninth, the same question in structure-based design. A benchmark out of Toronto, Vector and Mila asks whether generative models can design a molecule that binds a target and avoids a close homolog — PARP1 against PARP2, CDK9 against CDK2, and so on. Nine models, including two trained explicitly for selectivity. On close homologs, the two selectivity-trained models sit inside the spread of models that ignore selectivity entirely, and for most methods the average difference score is negative, meaning they bind the off-target better. What the selectivity-trained models do deliver is potency — they bind harder, not more selectively. Inference-time guidance is the only thing that moves the metric substantially.

Two further findings generalise beyond this paper. The safety panel looks respectable on a panel-averaged success rate of around forty percent, and collapses to between zero and four percent once you require a molecule to beat all twenty-two off-targets simultaneously, which is the only version of the question that matters for secondary pharmacology. And the raw docking score fails its own positive control: on experimentally validated selective ligands the unnormalised difference runs the wrong way, and even after normalisation the benchmark's primary threshold scores known-selective compounds at zero. Worth holding onto when a paper reports a docking delta as evidence of selectivity.

Tenth, and briefly, as a cautionary close. A reanalysis of a single public postmortem dataset — twenty-two controls and twenty-six people with major depression, dorsolateral prefrontal cortex — reports that a glycosyltransferase pathway score couples to a microglial signature in controls and that this coupling is attenuated in depression. I would read it the other way. Every group-level comparison is null: no gene survives correction, with a minimum adjusted p of zero point seven eight, and neither the pathway score nor any cell-type score differs between groups. The correlation the title rests on is not significant by ordinary methods in either group. The attenuation claim itself carries p equals zero point one seven and a corrected p above zero point five. And the three analyses presented as converging evidence are the same point estimate re-reported under three variance assumptions. The one corrected-significant result comes from pooling four cell-type scores whose standard errors differ roughly twentyfold into one model, which is a scale artifact at least as plausibly as a biological preference for microglia. The glycoRNA idea is genuinely interesting and the mouse sialylation work it cites is real. This particular dataset does not test it.

That is ten. The lead was CVN636 losing its agonist label to a five-hour luciferase readout and gaining a homodimer-selective one. The kappa receptor sitting on satellite glia in humans and on neurons in every other species we model it in. Dopamine occupancy failing to predict D1 signalling because the neighbour sets the gate, with the break sitting between cyclic AMP and the kinase and calcium imaging blind to all of it. A bile acid agonist that de-senesces the stem cell niche without making a single neuron. A cannabidiol effect smaller than the trial's own detectable floor, alongside a correlation that survived correction and points the opposite way to the hypothesis. Four in five labelled side effects running at less than twice placebo, with the authors declining to recommend deleting any of them. Three ways to ask a model how sure it is, with the best one on average being the worst one for the best model. A screening loop where the proposer loses to a lookup table, and a design benchmark where selectivity training buys potency instead. And a null dataset wearing a finding's clothes. See you tomorrow.
