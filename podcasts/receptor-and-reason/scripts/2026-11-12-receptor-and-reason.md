## Script

Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Thursday, November twelfth. Today's papers share a problem, and it is the problem of the summary number. Every one of them reports a headline quantity — a group mean, a pooled ratio, a hit rate, a benchmark score — and in every one of them that quantity turns out to describe something other than what you would want it to. Sometimes the disaggregated version is far more interesting than the average; sometimes far less. The useful habit is asking, before you read the number, what it is averaging over.

We start with a medRxiv study that makes the point about as cleanly as it can be made. The question is whether adult ADHD symptoms fluctuate across the menstrual cycle. Clinicians hear this constantly, and the literature has been unhelpful, partly because retrospective interview reports of cyclical symptoms have a notoriously high false-positive rate — well established in premenstrual dysphoric disorder, where prospective daily ratings disqualify most people who endorse the criteria at intake. So this group did daily symptom ratings and analyzed them two ways. Averaged across the sample, the cycle effect was statistically significant and almost meaningless: under one percent of daily symptom variance. If that were the only analysis, the paper would conclude there is nothing here. Tested individually, fifty-four percent of eighty-seven participants were robustly cyclical, and among those the cycle explained a median of about half of their within-person symptom variance — a mean swing of roughly two and a half DSM-5 criteria for inattention. That is not a subtle effect. That is the difference between meeting a diagnostic threshold and not meeting it, inside one person, within one month.

Two things make this more than a statistical curiosity. The first is that most of it survived adjustment for cyclical affect and for cyclical pain — about seventy percent and eighty percent respectively — so it is not simply dysmenorrhea or premenstrual mood masquerading as inattention. The second is where the worsening fell. It most often peaked at menses and after it, not before, and no participant's cycle met prospective criteria for premenstrual dysphoric disorder. The field's default mental model, that hormone-related psychiatric worsening is a luteal-phase phenomenon, does not fit these data. Circulating hormone levels did not distinguish cyclical from non-cyclical participants — the expected answer if what varies between people is sensitivity to normal hormonal change rather than the magnitude of the change, and which also means you cannot identify these patients with an assay. The honest limits: observational, mostly one cycle per person, so this establishes within-person temporal association and nothing about mechanism. But if half of adult women with ADHD have a substantial monthly oscillation that is invisible in the group mean and does not live where everyone has been looking for it, that has consequences for how trials are timed, how symptoms are rated, and whether a dose that works one week works the next.

The same arithmetic, applied to a digital therapeutic, with a more cautionary result. Priovi is a digital therapeutic for borderline personality disorder based on schema therapy, and its average effect is small. This is a preregistered secondary analysis of its trial asking whether that small average is an average of nothing or an average of something. Using model-based recursive partitioning on five hundred twenty-eight patients at the three-month endpoint, the effect on symptom change was significantly heterogeneous, and so was the effect on clinically relevant response. Benefit was concentrated in patients with higher baseline symptom load — a representative tree split on depressive symptom score, and the more severe subgroup showed roughly twice the symptom reduction of the less severe one. So far this reads like a personalization success story.

Then the authors do the thing that makes the paper worth your time: they check whether the predicted individual treatment effects are calibrated. They are not. Quartile calibration was weak and non-monotonic — meaning that when you sort patients by how much benefit the model predicts for them, the actual benefit does not increase as you move up the ranking. Both of those findings are true at once. There is real heterogeneity in who benefits, and the model that found the heterogeneity cannot rank an individual patient reliably enough to act on. The authors say explicitly that the moderators are hypothesis-generating and should not guide patient selection without external validation. That gap — between a significant heterogeneity test and a usable prediction — is where a great deal of precision-psychiatry enthusiasm currently sits, and it is rare to see it reported this plainly by the people who own the intervention.

Third, a meta-analysis where the pooled estimate is the least informative thing in the paper. The target is a prescribing cascade: gabapentinoids cause peripheral edema as a class effect, the edema gets read as a new cardiovascular or renal problem, and a loop diuretic gets added to treat it. Nine studies across five countries, more than one point eight million new gabapentinoid users, mostly sequence symmetry analyses, which compare how often a second drug is started after the first versus before it. All five sequence symmetry analyses and both cohort studies with an unexposed comparison group pointed the same direction. The pooled adjusted sequence ratio was one point three seven. But the heterogeneity statistic was ninety-six percent — that pooled number is averaging studies that do not estimate the same thing. What should actually move you is the structure underneath: four of the five studies that examined dose found larger estimates at higher doses, which is the gradient you would predict if the mechanism is vasodilatory edema, and one chart review found that nearly a quarter of patients had a potential harm within sixty days of starting the loop diuretic. Given that gabapentinoid prescribing has expanded enormously, much of it off-label for pain with thin efficacy evidence, the actionable version is a clinical reflex, not an effect size: new edema in someone who recently started a gabapentinoid is a drug effect until proven otherwise.

Now to the bench, and a paper about which cells a receptor actually matters in. Null variants in GRIN2A, which encodes the GluN2A subunit, are strongly associated with the epilepsy-aphasia spectrum, and the standing assumption is that NMDA receptors shape excitatory synaptic kinetics and plasticity broadly. This group used GluN2A haploinsufficient and knockout rats and did whole-cell recordings in hippocampal CA1 across three cell types. In pyramidal neurons, at physiological resting potentials, NMDA receptors made no discernible contribution to Schaffer collateral EPSP kinetics, to temporal summation, or to spike recruitment — and Hebbian long-term potentiation at those synapses was preserved. Parvalbumin interneurons were likewise unaffected. The entire phenotype lived in somatostatin interneurons, where GluN2A loss prolonged EPSP decay, increased EPSP area, enhanced frequency-dependent summation, and raised action potential output during repetitive and theta-frequency stimulation, alongside increased intrinsic excitability.

That is a gene-dose-dependent, cell-type-restricted effect in a receptor subunit everyone treats as general-purpose, and it matters mechanistically because somatostatin interneuron activity is upstream of parvalbumin interneuron maturation — which had already been reported as delayed in GluN2A loss. So the proposed causal chain runs through the one cell type that showed a change, rather than through a diffuse loss of NMDA receptor function everywhere. The caveat is the usual one for constitutive knockouts: you are looking at a developed network that grew up without the subunit, so some of this is compensation rather than acute consequence. But as a demonstration that "NMDA receptor hypofunction" is not a single phenotype, it is a useful corrective — and a reminder that a drug targeting GluN2A would not be acting on the circuit uniformly.

Next, an idea in cancer pharmacology that inverts the usual optimization. The standard response to resistance is a more potent inhibitor or a mechanistically distinct combination. This group tried the opposite: several inhibitors against the same oncogenic driver, each at a dose low enough to have minimal activity on its own. In EGFR-driven models, these ultra-low-dose same-target combinations produced substantial growth inhibition in vitro and antitumor activity in vivo, despite each component being individually weak. The crucial experiment is the matched one. At comparable overall growth inhibition — around fifty percent — single-agent EGFR inhibitors produced resistant outgrowth, while a five-drug ultra-low-dose combination delayed it. Adding more inhibitors delayed it further, and with eight to fourteen inhibitors no resistant outgrowth was detected within the experimental window.

The matching is what makes this interesting. If two treatments suppress growth equally in the short run and diverge completely in the long run, then the short-term readout — the thing every screen measures — does not contain the information you care about. The authors also report that the five-drug combination retained activity against gefitinib-resistant cells, and that resistance that did arise under the combination was markedly more reversible on withdrawal than resistance arising under a single agent, which suggests a non-genetic, adaptive-state mechanism rather than selection of a hard mutation. They are appropriately careful that additivity or synergy may contribute to the growth inhibition itself; the claim that survives is about resistance behavior, which is not predicted by inhibitory strength. Whether a regimen of eight to fourteen agents is developable is a separate and hard question. But as a statement about what determines evolutionary trajectory, it is a real result.

A nicely instructive negative from Neurocrine, on gene-therapy delivery of a peptide drug. The appeal of a vectorized GLP-1 receptor agonist is obvious: one dose instead of chronic weekly injections, with adherence being the main limiter of real-world effectiveness. They built four AAV constructs — native GLP-1, a Gly-8 stabilized variant, exendin-4, and an exendin-4 Fc fusion — and tested them in diet-induced obese mice. All four secreted active peptide in a cell-based receptor activation assay, but the effective concentrations in the media spanned nearly four orders of magnitude. In the animals, exendin-4 and the Fc fusion produced body weight reductions up to about twenty-three percent out to twenty-one days from a single dose, plus reductions in food intake, fat mass, and fasting insulin; the Fc fusion also improved oral glucose tolerance and lowered liver triglycerides. The native GLP-1 and Gly-8 constructs did nothing.

Here is the part worth carrying away. Transgene transcript levels did not differ appreciably among the four constructs in either brain or liver. Measurable peptide in plasma and brain lysate, by contrast, tracked the in vitro supernatant rank order, and the two failing constructs were indistinguishable from endogenous background. So delivery was not the variable — the vector got in and was transcribed equally everywhere. Everything that mattered happened after transcription: translation, secretion efficiency, and circulating stability. The practical conclusion is that the standard vector-biodistribution readout is close to uninformative for a vectorized peptide, and that a cheap conditioned-media potency screen is a valid early filter. That is a useful piece of process knowledge for anyone building this modality, which currently has a lot of momentum.

Turning to computational pharmacology, a preprint on allosteric site prediction that begins by dismantling its own field's benchmarks. The structural premise is simple: models are trained and evaluated on ligand-bound structures, where the allosteric pocket is fully formed because a ligand is sitting in it. In prospective discovery you have the apo or substrate-bound structure, where that pocket may be contracted, distorted, or closed. The group assembled paired structures of the same known sites across states and re-ran three widely used methods. Top-three success rates fell by twenty-six to ninety-three percent in apo structures and seventy-one to ninety-four percent in substrate-bound ones. Worse, a lot of the failure happened before scoring: in the ligand-bound state, zero to fourteen percent of systems had no candidate pocket matching the site at all, and in apo and substrate-bound states that rose to between forty-five and ninety percent. The site was never generated as a candidate, so no amount of better ranking recovers it.

Their model, AllositeUltra, addresses this by not starting from preformed cavity geometry — it generates candidate regions directly from predicted per-residue allosteric probabilities rather than from a geometric pocket finder, on top of fused language-model, physicochemical, dynamic, and inter-residue-attention representations. On their cross-state benchmark that supplementation step roughly doubles success in the cases with the largest local conformational change. The validation I find most persuasive is prospective, and it has a CNS hook: applied to the PDZ0-GK supramodule of MAGI2, a synaptic scaffold protein, the model nominated a pocket at the interdomain interface away from the phosphopeptide groove. Forty fragments were screened by microscale thermophoresis, five bound, and one gave a two point two four angstrom crystal structure confirming the predicted site. That hit binds at twenty-six micromolar, so this validates a site, not a drug — but a crystallographically confirmed novel pocket on a synaptic scaffold is a genuine result, and the benchmark critique stands on its own regardless of how their model ranks next year.

Which brings us to a large foundation model for protein-ligand interactions, posted this week, and to a hit rate that needs unpacking. The model, trained on roughly three point two trillion tokens of paired protein-ligand sequences with no structural coordinates and no task-specific supervision, is reported to show three zero-shot capabilities from frozen representations: predicting mechanism of action, localizing the binding site including discriminating orthosteric from allosteric, and distinguishing covalent from non-covalent engagement. The scaling analysis is the most intellectually interesting part — across six model sizes from forty-two million to one point six billion parameters, pretraining loss falls smoothly while every capability jumps sharply only at the largest scale. If that holds up, loss-based scaling curves do not tell you when a capability arrives, which is a familiar claim from language models and a newer one here. They also train at a parameter-to-token ratio of one to two thousand, far from compute-optimal, deliberately, because paired interaction data is sparse.

The headline is a sixty-one and a half percent dose-response hit rate across nine targets in prospective wet-lab work, with structural novelty enforced — every tested compound below zero point six Tanimoto to anything in training. Read the per-target numbers and the picture is more textured. Confirmed inhibitors include genuinely impressive matter: single-digit nanomolar on JAK1, thirty-nine nanomolar on MNK2, and on HDAC6, twelve of fifteen hits lack a hydroxamic acid entirely, which is the warhead responsible for the dose-limiting toxicity that has bottlenecked that field for twenty years. But "confirmed inhibitor" in that denominator includes compounds with IC50 values of twenty-three, forty, and in one case one hundred forty-three micromolar. A hit rate that counts a one-hundred-forty-three-micromolar compound alongside a three-nanomolar one is not a quantity you can compare to anything, including the hit rates from the docking campaigns it is implicitly competing with. The useful claims here are the specific ones — novel chemotypes on targets with narrow inhibitor literatures, and activity on two targets absent from training entirely — not the aggregate.

Two papers on clinical AI where the aggregate actively misleads. The first trains multi-turn clinical agents with reinforcement learning across three constructed environments — medication refill triage, acute diagnostic workup, and dosing titration — each with hidden clinical state, explicit query costs, an exact adaptive oracle, and a deterministic outcome verifier. The agent has to decide what to ask, when to stop asking, and what to do. Training with outcome-only group relative policy optimization raises aggregate reward while degrading the procedure: the authors document premature commitment after a single finding, collapse toward asking essentially one question, and failures concentrated on rare high-risk cases. Shortening the interaction is a reward-efficient strategy and a clinically unacceptable one. Giving dense credit to questions that actually reduce diagnostic uncertainty improves held-out value and avoids the high-risk collapse across three training seeds. Separating exploration from exploitation pushes high-risk support higher still at its best checkpoint, but over-gathers information on low-acuity cases, and the advantage does not survive to the final checkpoint.

I want to flag the limitation the authors themselves put in a figure caption, because it is load-bearing: the held-out diagnostics set contains two high-risk cases. The entire claim is about behavior on rare high-risk cases, and the held-out evidence for it is two cases. That does not make the demonstration worthless — the failure modes under outcome-only training are visible in the trajectories and are the real contribution — but it means the quantitative comparison on the dimension that matters most rests on almost nothing. This is the structural problem with evaluating agents on rare events, and it is not solved by making the environments more elaborate.

The second is the most disciplined negative result I have read in a while. Scalp EEG seizure warning has a large retrospective literature reporting high sensitivity and low false-alarm rates, and a parallel literature of audits showing most of it leaks information between train and test or rewards recognizing the patient rather than anticipating the seizure. This group ran continuous video-EEG from twenty-one monitored children — thirteen hundred fifteen hours — under a written protocol fixed in advance. A statistical process control warning method detected forty-four percent of evaluable seizures at zero point four eight false alarms per hour; a random predictor got fifty-one percent. The method's statistic moved three times more with vigilance state than with seizure approach. They then redesigned it as a state-conditioned directional scheme, froze it with a hash-recorded configuration and time-stamped pre-specification, and got thirty-three percent of seizures at a comparable alarm rate, which beat analytic chance at p equals zero point zero three four. Then they checked whether a predictor using only clock time, vigilance state, and days since admission could reproduce it — and most of it, yes. The signal was confined to the last thirty minutes.

Applied once, as frozen, to two validation cohorts including the public CHB-MIT database — a hundred and one evaluable seizures — it detected nineteen percent against a twenty-six percent chance level. No secondary endpoint was met. With the child as the unit of analysis, no cohort was above chance. That is a complete, honest, pre-specified null, and the reason it is valuable is the structured chance level: the comparison is not against zero, it is against what you get from knowing the time of day and whether the child is asleep. Any scalp EEG prediction result that does not clear that bar has not demonstrated anything about seizures.

To close, something lighter and genuinely useful. A group built an automated pipeline that runs a literature query, pulls full texts, and has a language model extract hepatotoxicity findings into a fixed schema — chemical tested, biomarker, direction of change — with controlled vocabularies, deduplicating into a database that updates as new papers appear. The current snapshot holds a hundred and eighty-nine thousand findings on seventeen thousand chemicals from fifty-three thousand articles going back to 1995, covering two hundred thirty-five biomarkers each reported for at least twenty chemicals. The audit is the reassuring part: of two hundred seventeen findings manually checked across thirty-five articles, two hundred eight were fully correct, and all nine errors came from two articles. That is a real extraction tool, not a demo.

And then the use case lands exactly where this episode has been pointing. They trained structure-based QSAR models on the mined data — graph neural networks and classic descriptor baselines — and at this data scale the classic models remain competitive. Two orders of magnitude more training data than hand-curated toxicology sets, and the architecture that was supposed to need the data does not pull ahead. Whether that is a ceiling on what literature-reported biomarker directions can support, or simply a scale that still is not enough, the paper cannot say. But it is a clean demonstration that the size of a dataset and the capability it unlocks are, once again, two different numbers. That is the episode. Back tomorrow.
