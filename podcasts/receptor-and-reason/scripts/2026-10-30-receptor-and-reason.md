## Script

Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Friday, October thirtieth, twenty twenty-six.

Today's list has a thread, and it's a pharmacological one for once rather than a statistical one. Over and over in these papers, the quantity that decides the outcome is not how much signal there is. It's where the signal sits and which way it points. A higher dose that doesn't land harder. A receptor population blocked on one side of the synapse and untouched on the other. A G protein variant that passes every test of activation because its defect is in deactivation. A drug that rescues the behavior without rescuing the neurons. Magnitude is the thing we measure most easily and, in most of today's work, the thing that matters least.

Let's start with the cleanest human pharmacology of the week, a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover study out of the cannabis research laboratory at U-C-L-A, published in Neuropsychopharmacology. Forty-four healthy volunteers, balanced by design: twenty-two male, twenty-two female, and within each sex, twelve frequent users — more than four days a week — and ten occasional users. Three sessions each, smoking a paced, fixed amount of cigarette containing zero, four, or ten percent T-H-C, which works out to roughly zero, twenty, or sixty milligrams. Female participants were scheduled in the mid-to-late follicular phase and that was confirmed with blood hormones. Plasma T-H-C, eleven-hydroxy-T-H-C and the carboxy metabolite were drawn serially alongside subjective ratings, heart rate and a self-administration choice.

The design is the point: almost every prior sex-difference finding here came from pooling across trials retrospectively, and this one crosses sex and use frequency prospectively. The result inverts the prior. The preclinical literature says females are more sensitive — they discriminate T-H-C at lower doses, self-administer more, reinstate more readily. Here, females showed nothing of the kind. The group that stood out was occasional-use males, who reported significantly higher intoxication, higher sedation and impairment, and higher dysphoric arousal than everyone else, with a heart rate increase of thirty-two beats per minute at the four percent dose. And they reported more "bad effect," not more "good effect."

Now the part that makes this a pharmacology paper rather than a survey. Those occasional-use males did not have higher plasma T-H-C. The highest plasma concentrations were in frequent-use males — about fifty-seven nanograms per milliliter above the occasional-use females at the lower active strength. So the group with the most drug on board had the least response, and the group with the biggest response had unremarkable exposure. Tolerance is the obvious reading, and the authors reach for it, but occasional-use females matched on weekly frequency did not show the same sensitivity, which is awkward for a strictly tolerance-based account.

And then the strength-response curve, which barely exists. Across most outcomes there is no reliable separation between four percent and ten percent, and on some measures the ordering inverts. The explanation the authors favor is self-titration under a standardized protocol: the ten percent cigarette took twelve-point-four puffs to finish versus seven-point-eight for the four percent, consistent with shorter, shallower draws. So even a paced, fixed-mass smoking procedure doesn't fix the delivered dose. That's worth holding onto the next time you read a cannabis study that treats percent T-H-C as the independent variable. The caveats are substantial and the authors state them plainly — forty-four people split three ways, three-way interactions, exploratory post-hoc follow-up with no multiplicity adjustment, and strengths well below what's on dispensary shelves. Take the direction, not the p-values.

Second, and staying with a dose-response that stops behaving, a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover experimental medicine trial in Translational Psychiatry, out of Sweden. Thirty-one participants, sixteen heavy drinkers and fifteen light drinkers by sex-specific risk thresholds, none meeting criteria for moderate or severe alcohol use disorder. Each got placebo, thirty milligrams of baclofen, and sixty milligrams of baclofen in counterbalanced order, and the primary readout was long-interval intracortical inhibition — the paired-pulse T-M-S measure at a hundred-millisecond interval that is about as close as we get to a non-invasive index of G-A-B-A-B receptor-mediated cortical inhibition in a living human.

The result is a clean dissociation. Thirty milligrams potentiated that inhibition in both groups, to a comparable degree. Sixty milligrams potentiated it in light drinkers and did nothing in heavy drinkers — group difference at the high dose, p equals point zero zero three. And the supporting controls are the reason this is worth your time. Plasma baclofen concentrations were measured at one and two hours and were indistinguishable between groups, so this is not pharmacokinetics. Growth hormone release, which depends on pituitary G-A-B-A-B receptors, confirmed systemic target engagement in both groups, though with an altered time course in the heavy drinkers. Short-interval inhibition and intracortical facilitation — the G-A-B-A-A and glutamatergic proxies — were unmoved, so the effect is receptor-selective rather than a general excitability shift.

Two things give me pause. The group effect shows up with the categorical heavy-versus-light split but not when you put A-U-D-I-T score or drinks per week in as a continuous moderator — neither came close. A dichotomy that outperforms the underlying continuum in n equals thirty-one is a finding to replicate before building on. And baseline inhibition explained a large share of the variance in the change score, which is the usual regression-to-the-mean hazard. That said, the clinical hook is real: baclofen for alcohol use disorder has spent a decade fighting about dose, with the French high-dose practice on one side and a string of null trials on the other. This is the first experimental-medicine result I've seen that suggests the ceiling on the dose-response may itself be shifted by drinking history, which would mean more baclofen is not simply more G-A-B-A-B engagement in the people you most want to treat.

Third, and the best mechanistic paper on today's list, a preprint on anti-G-A-B-A-B receptor autoimmunity that shows what happens when you block the same receptor on only one side of the synapse. Patients with this form of autoimmune encephalitis develop autoantibodies against the G-A-B-A-B receptor and present with severe memory dysfunction and seizures. The group mapped the epitopes, and found dual reactivity — to the sushi domains of the B-one-a subunit and to the B-two subunit extracellular domain. That matters because the sushi domains are the axonal-transport determinant: B-one-a-containing receptors go presynaptic, B-one-b-containing receptors go postsynaptic.

They then passively transferred purified patient immunoglobulin into mice by chronic intraventricular infusion and recorded from hippocampal slices. Presynaptic autoreceptors: gone. The normal depression across a stimulus train was abolished, and the G-A-B-A-B blocker C-G-P five-five-eight-four-five had nothing left to do. Presynaptic heteroreceptors on glutamatergic terminals: also gone — baclofen no longer reduced excitatory currents or raised the paired-pulse ratio. Postsynaptic signaling: entirely intact. The G-I-R-K-mediated slow inhibitory potential was normal, the reversal potential was normal, input resistance and membrane time constant were normal, and the high-frequency-stimulation-induced suppression of spiking was identical to controls.

And here is the experiment that makes it. They depleted the sushi-domain-reactive antibodies out of the polyclonal patient fraction using recombinant sushi domain protein, leaving the B-two reactivity intact — and the presynaptic phenotype disappeared entirely. That is a within-sample subtraction, not a different antibody, not a different animal. It is as close to a causal assignment as you can get with polyclonal human immunoglobulin. Note also that surface expression was unchanged throughout: unlike the N-M-D-A and A-M-P-A receptor antibodies, which crosslink and internalize, these behave as functional antagonists. Downstream, the animals showed disinhibited hippocampal pathways, markedly reduced long-term potentiation, amplified C-A-one gamma power with rigid hyper-coupling in vivo, and selective impairment of long-term memory maintenance with intact acquisition — exactly the B-one-a knockout phenotype. Their model attributes the amplified gamma to unconstrained excitatory drive from the lost heteroreceptors combining with augmented feedback inhibition from the lost autoreceptors. If you want a demonstration that receptor localization, not receptor quantity, is the pathogenic variable, this is it.

Fourth, staying on the presynaptic terminal but moving from breaking it to rebuilding it. A preprint on engineered proteins that reconstitute active zone function. The background: this group previously built beta-four-Z-n, an eighty-kilodalton fusion of the calcium channel beta-four subunit, which targets the presynaptic membrane, with the R-I-M-one zinc finger, which recruits Munc-thirteen. In neurons where R-I-M and E-L-K-S have both been ablated and the active zone has disassembled, that single small protein restores vesicle docking, priming, and action-potential-evoked release without rebuilding any of the megadalton machines.

The new work swaps the payload. Beta-four-M-C replaces the zinc finger with the M-U-N and C-two-C domains of Munc-thirteen-one, so instead of recruiting the priming protein it does the priming itself. Two results stand out. First, within beta-four-M-C the M-U-N domain's function turns out to be dispensable for docking but required, along with C-two-C, for release — a clean separation of two steps usually discussed together. Second, and more consequential, the two constructs behave differently in intact tissue: in wild-type mouse and human neurons, beta-four-M-C strengthens neurotransmitter release while beta-four-Z-n does not. The recruiter is limited by how much Munc-thirteen is available to recruit; the direct effector isn't. Both restore and enhance dopamine release. Same scaffold, same targeting module, and the entire difference in what the tool can do in a healthy synapse comes from which function you hand it. As a gene-therapy concept this is early, but it's the first version that works in human neurons and at a healthy baseline rather than only in a wrecked synapse.

Fifth and sixth, a genuine coincidence worth taking together: two independent groups posted preprints this week dissecting G-N-A-O-one variants, and they arrive at complementary mechanistic classes. G-N-A-O-one encodes G alpha o, the most abundant G alpha subunit in brain, and mutations cause a rare disorder that splits into two clinical presentations — a developmental and epileptic encephalopathy, or a neurodevelopmental disorder with involuntary movements.

The first paper asks whether the split is mechanistic. Its framework: variants that sequester G beta gamma, holding it unavailable to effectors, versus haploinsufficient variants where G alpha o is poorly expressed and G beta gamma runs free. Curating published cases, sequestering variants had fifteen times the odds of presenting with the movement phenotype, consistent across sexes, with mechanism and not sex surviving logistic regression. In the R-two-oh-nine-H knock-in mouse they found sex- and cell-type-specific hyperexcitability in dorsolateral striatal spiny projection neurons — elevated input resistance in both sexes, lowered rheobase, the direct-pathway effect clear in males only. Both gallein, a G beta gamma-effector modulator, and caffeine reduced hyperlocomotion systemically, and intra-striatal gallein normalized it in females.

Here is where I'd push back. The paper's own closing claim is that patients with sequestering variants "represent a mechanistically defined population for whom increasing free G beta gamma is likely to be beneficial." But gallein blocks G beta gamma-effector interaction. The rescue runs in the opposite direction from the model that organizes the whole paper. That doesn't make the data wrong — the behavioral rescue is real and replicated across routes — but the mechanistic story and the pharmacology are pointing at each other, and the paper doesn't resolve it. The adenosine arm has a similar wrinkle: systemic caffeine works in both sexes, but the selective A-one antagonist infused into dorsolateral striatum worked in males and did nothing in females, while a prior report on a different variant read the caffeine effect through A-two-A instead. Convergent behavior, unresolved receptor.

The second G-N-A-O-one paper is quieter and, methodologically, I think the better one. It takes a subset of variants that looked completely normal in a prior screen of fifty-five — normal expression, normal receptor coupling, normal subunit dissociation, normal G-T-P binding and hydrolysis, normal thermostability. Every standard activation assay says nothing is wrong. The defect is in the off-step. Using G-I-R-K channel deactivation kinetics, a kinetic G beta gamma-G-R-K bioluminescence assay, and direct surface plasmon resonance binding to an R-G-S domain, they show these variants are refractory to regulator-of-G-protein-signaling proteins — they can't be turned off on schedule. The confirmation I liked most: these residues are perfectly conserved between human G alpha o and yeast Gpa-one, so they put all eleven mutations into yeast and got left-shifted agonist E-C-fifties and exaggerated growth arrest, phenocopying loss of the R-G-S gene. Indeed one of these exact mutations was historically instrumental in discovering the first R-G-S protein. Clinically, all the patients they could obtain data for fall on the movement-disorder, non-encephalopathy side. The lesson for anyone running variant panels: a receptor-activation assay measures the on-step, and a variant whose entire pathology is in signal termination will look wild-type all the way through your screen.

Seventh, a preprint on ketamine in Parkinson's disease that I want to flag both for its finding and for its framing. The context is real clinical development: sub-anesthetic ketamine is in Phase one for levodopa-induced dyskinesia, and this group previously showed that the anti-dyskinetic effect runs through striatal Trk-B. So the natural hypothesis for any neuroprotective effect is B-D-N-F signaling too. They tested it in the progressive unilateral six-hydroxydopamine rat model with a week of daily sub-anesthetic ketamine, with or without the Trk-B antagonist A-N-A twelve.

The motor protection is substantial — amphetamine-induced rotations down, forelimb adjusting steps restored almost completely at twenty-eight days. And A-N-A twelve did not block it. Target engagement for the antagonist is documented: it abolished the ketamine-induced rise in nigral Trk-B transcript and drove down phospho-E-R-K in both hemispheres. So the conclusion that B-D-N-F signaling isn't carrying the protective effect is well supported, and it's a useful negative result against a dominant mechanistic story.

But read the cellular data before you accept the word "neuroprotective." Stereological counts in substantia nigra showed no significant rescue of dopaminergic neurons in any group. Striatal tyrosine hydroxylase showed only a non-significant trend with ketamine alone. So a near-complete reversal of the motor phenotype sits on top of essentially unprotected neurons — which points at enhanced dopamine tone in surviving terminals, or an anti-inflammatory effect, both of which the authors raise. That's symptomatic benefit, and it's worth having, but it isn't protection. There's also a missing arm: the combination group was, on several measures, numerically better than ketamine alone, and without an A-N-A twelve-only group you can't tell whether that's noise or whether the antagonist is doing something on its own. Males only, which the authors flag.

Eighth, a shift to the computational side, and the largest clinical-AI result of the week: a Google DeepMind paper on training language models in simulation for psychiatric intake. They ran multi-turn reinforcement learning across simulated psychiatric encounters, optimizing jointly for diagnostic accuracy, patient-centered communication, and safety. The evaluation is unusually serious: a randomized, double-blind crossover, sixty matched scenario pairs, thirty-two board-certified psychiatrists role-playing patients and a separate panel of fifteen scoring de-identified transcripts. The trained agent beat its identically prompted base model in seventy-seven percent of pairs, with gains on an adapted clinical-examination rubric, on patient-centered communication — eighty-two versus sixty-five percent — and on differential diagnosis appropriateness, sixty-eight versus thirty-three. They also built two Delphi-consensus taxonomies of safety risks and relational competencies, derived two hundred fifty-six adversarial presentations, and more than halved under-triage of acute risk, from thirty-one to thirteen percent.

The comparator is the base model, not a clinician, and the absolute numbers are where I'd anchor. Comprehensive and appropriate treatment was recommended in forty-seven percent of human-evaluated encounters. At least one critical intake question was omitted in sixty-one percent of simulated intakes. Acute safety risks were still under-triaged one time in eight. The paper states every one of those plainly in its own abstract, which I want to credit. Halving a failure rate is a real result; it is not the same as a safe system, and thirteen percent under-triage of acute risk in psychiatry is not a number you deploy behind.

Ninth, a related clinical agent with a baseline problem. ClinGeX is a skill-centred agent for clinical genomic interpretation — a hundred five curated executable skills, retrieved per task and orchestrated with dependency awareness. On two hundred twenty-three temporally held-out cases it hit seventy-two percent concordance with expert reference classifications against forty-six for the strongest comparator, which is a clean benchmark result. The human study is where to be careful. Forty-five clinical genomic professionals, a hundred thirty-five cases, three conditions. With ClinGeX, raw case-level concordance ran fifty-one to sixty-four percent depending on the task. Without any assistance, it ran sixteen to thirty-one percent. Practicing genomic professionals agreeing with the reference on one case in six, unaided, tells you how strictly concordance was scored — not that the unaided workflow is that bad. Treat the within-study ordering as informative and the headline multiples as a rubric artifact.

Tenth, the strongest agentic-discovery result this week, because it has wet-lab denominators. MolRSI, from a Broad-led group, couples workflow and architecture search with persistent cross-task memory so that lessons from one discovery campaign shape the search policy in the next — Monte Carlo tree search over executable skills, with outcomes and failure repairs written to memory. In antibiotic discovery it developed a screening workflow balancing antibacterial activity, cytotoxicity and structural novelty, and achieved an experimental hit rate of fifteen-point-nine percent against eight-point-seven for a prior expert-led workflow using the same training data and the same screening library — which is the matched comparison you want. In de novo peptide-M-H-C binder design it produced eight experimentally validated hits against two targets with preferential binding over tested off-targets. The paper also reports that a general-purpose science agent failed completely on that second task — treat that as a reported observation rather than a benchmark, since prompting is doing unknown work. The claim that holds up is narrower and more useful than recursive self-improvement: carrying explicit records of what worked and what broke across campaigns beats starting each one cold.

And to close, the paper that is in some ways the opposite of the last two, and the right note to end on. A preprint reports C-H-I-DEL, a small-molecule binder of C-H-I-three-L-one — the secreted glycoprotein also called Y-K-L-forty, which drives glioblastoma progression and immunosuppressive myeloid signaling. The problem with this target is structural: it's non-enzymatic and secreted, so there's no catalytic site at which to engineer a resistance mutation, which means every prior small molecule's on-target activity has been inferred from affinity plus phenotype rather than proven.

Two details make this worth your attention. First, the screening honesty. From a one-point-four-billion-compound D-N-A-encoded library, the two most enriched features — three hundred-fold and a hundred twenty-fold over the mean — showed no reproducible binding by surface plasmon resonance when resynthesized off-D-N-A. C-H-I-DEL, enriched ninety-eight-fold, bound at about a hundred ninety nanomolar. Enrichment magnitude got the ranking exactly wrong. Second, the chemical genetics. They solved the co-crystal structure at two-point-eight angstroms, saw a binding mode centred on tryptophan ninety-nine, and used it to design W-ninety-nine-F — a compound-resistant allele that keeps native secretion, basal signaling and thermal stability, unlike the destabilizing alanine control, which is the comparison that makes the tool legitimate. In isogenic knockout-rescue cells, wild-type rescue restores both the phenotype and drug sensitivity across invasion, proliferation and a S-T-A-T-three and N-F-kappa-B signaling program; the W-ninety-nine-F rescue restores the phenotype and confers resistance. That is genetic proof that the cellular activity requires the structurally defined site, for a target class where nobody had it.

So: a higher cannabis dose that doesn't arrive, a higher baclofen dose that stops working in the people you'd treat, an antibody that spares the postsynaptic receptor entirely, a G protein variant that fails only on the off-step, a drug that fixes the behavior and not the neurons, and a library hit ranking that inverts when you actually measure binding. The amount of signal is almost never the lever. See you Monday.
