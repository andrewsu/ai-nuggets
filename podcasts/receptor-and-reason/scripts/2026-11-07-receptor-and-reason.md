## Script

Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Saturday, November seventh, twenty twenty-six.

Today's thread is direction. Not whether a lever moves the system, but which way you should push it, and in whom. Eleven items, and in most of them the sign of the effect turns out to be the contested quantity — a receptor you want to activate in one tissue and silence in another, a dose that helps one cognitive domain and does nothing for the next, a model-selection step where the direction of the comparison was chosen after the results came in.

The lead comes out of Vanderbilt, and it is the most complete behavioral pharmacology package I have seen on an M4 muscarinic positive allosteric modulator. The context matters here. M4 is now a commercially serious target — it is the receptor people believe is doing most of the antipsychotic work in xanomeline-trospium, and it is what the M4 PAM programs in industry are chasing. But almost everything we know about M4 and cognition comes from rodents or from agonists with enough M1 and M2 activity to produce the cholinergic side effects that cap the dose. This group used a selective M4 positive allosteric modulator — they call it VU8055, a compound out of the Warren Center medicinal chemistry effort — dosed orally at zero point three, one, and three milligrams per kilogram in six adult male rhesus macaques, and then ran those animals through eight separate touchscreen tasks over twenty-eight weeks.

The breadth is the point. Set shifting, visual search under distractor load, antisaccade inhibitory control, visuospatial maze learning, temporal-order object sequence learning, working memory span, and an effort-based choice task where the animal decides how many screen touches it will spend to inflate a balloon for a larger token payout. Eighty-five performance metrics. And six of the seven task categories improved. Effect sizes run from about a quarter to a half of a standard deviation — set shifting plateau accuracy and effort control at the top, attentional filtering and inhibitory control in the middle. No gastrointestinal or somatomotor adverse effects on a modified Irwin scale, stable body weight across the whole study.

The effort result is the one I would flag for anyone working on negative symptoms. The low dose made the animals willing to accept more physical effort for a larger reward — a rightward shift of about six tenths of an effort unit at the fifty percent threshold, and one point three at the twenty-five percent threshold. Critically, the model says this is not a change in how much the animals valued the reward. Subjective reward value was flat. What moved was the cost term, the effort-avoidance weight. That is a pro-motivational effect in the specific sense that matters clinically — it is amotivation, not anhedonia, and those dissociate.

Now the direction problem. There is no single best dose. Maze learning, sequence learning and effort control peak at zero point three. Visual search and set shifting peak at one. Antisaccade inhibitory control peaks at three. The task-composite score is significantly positive at the low and medium doses and flatly null at the high dose. So "broad enhancement" is really a patchwork of domain-specific optima stitched together, and if you were designing a trial you would have to pick one.

And I want to be honest about how the composite was built. It averages thirty-seven of the eighty-five metrics — specifically the accuracy-based metrics that showed a statistical effect at some dose — and working memory span was excluded from it on the stated grounds that it did not show task-specific effects. That is selecting the summary measure partly on the outcome. The authors do report the version using all sixty-seven accuracy metrics in the supplement and say it looks similar, which is the right disclosure and takes most of the sting out. But the headline number is not assumption-free, and working memory span, the one domain that did not move, is also the domain most directly indexed in the human batteries this design was built to mirror.

Next, two papers on the same receptor that want opposite things from it, which I think is the sharpest pairing of the week.

The first is a microglial aryl hydrocarbon receptor story in an amyloid model. AhR is the sensor for microbiota-derived and host-derived tryptophan metabolites — the indoles. Sustained administration of the AhR agonist indoxyl-three-sulfate made cortical amyloid deposition and plaque-associated neuritic damage worse in a knock-in amyloid mouse. Going the other way, constitutive AhR deficiency reduced amyloid burden, promoted plaque compaction, and preserved behavior, and inducible deletion restricted to microglia was by itself enough to cut plaque accumulation. Single-cell sequencing says why: losing AhR pushes microglia toward phagolysosomal and amyloid-recognition states and raises amyloid uptake — and the effect is markedly stronger in females, which lines up with sex-dependent remodeling of the gut microbiota and the tryptophan metabolite pool in the same animals. They then carry it into humans, with serum AhR-activating bioactivity in a memory-clinic cohort of about a hundred and forty, and among amyloid-positive patients higher serum AhR bioactivity tracked lower cerebrospinal fluid amyloid-beta-forty-two. So the therapeutic direction here is AhR blockade, in microglia, preferentially in women.

The second paper, from the same week, wants AhR agonism. About half of inflammatory bowel disease patients have chronic abdominal pain even in remission, and tryptophan-to-indole metabolism is reduced in those patients. AhR is expressed on gut-projecting TRPV1-positive nociceptors. In a post-colitis mouse model of visceral hypersensitivity, AhR activation with both a natural and a synthetic agonist reduced the visceromotor response in males and females, and an AhR antagonist blocked the antinociceptive effect of the indoles — so the receptor is carrying it. Mechanistically, the natural agonist indole-three-pyruvic acid, given by oral gavage, raised transcription of the inward-rectifier potassium channel Kir two point two in TRPV1 neurons and cut activation in the dorsal horn. Raise the leak conductance, raise the threshold, quiet the nociceptor. And again there is a sex signal, this time in the microbiome: control males were enriched for indole-producing Akkermansia and Alistipes relative to post-colitis males and to control females.

Put those side by side and you have a clean problem. The same receptor, the same chemical class of ligand, two tissues, opposite desired signs. A systemically available AhR agonist for visceral pain is, on the microglial evidence, a drug that pushes amyloid the wrong way — and the indole supply is partly set by the gut flora, which neither study controls in a patient. The honest version is that AhR is not a target, it is a node, and anything you do to it will need to be compartment-restricted, probably by keeping the agent out of the brain. Also worth saying: the microglial paper's human arm is cross-sectional and its comparison group was recruited independently, so the cohort contrast is doing a lot of work.

Staying with directionality, there is a nice developmental-NMDA paper. The setup is standard — neonatal MK-801, a non-competitive NMDA receptor antagonist, given to rat pups at zero point two or zero point four milligrams per kilogram, producing in adulthood the schizophrenia-relevant deficits. What they measured is social affective preference: whether a rat preferentially investigates a distressed conspecific. The treated animals, especially at the higher dose, don't. Then the circuit work, and this is where the sign flips twice. Chemogenetic inhibition of medial prefrontal pyramidal cells rescued the social affective deficit in the treated rats. Chemogenetic activation of those same cells produced the deficit in naive rats. And histology shows reduced parvalbumin interneuron density in the medial prefrontal cortex of the treated animals, which makes the pyramidal-cell result interpretable rather than arbitrary — less inhibition, more pyramidal output, broken social affect. Consistent with that, selectively activating prefrontal parvalbumin interneurons also rescued the deficit. So two manipulations from opposite ends converge on the same restored behavior. It is a coherent story, and the obvious limit is that chemogenetics is not a therapy; the translational question is whether anything pharmacological can impose that selectivity in a cortex where you cannot choose your cell type.

Fourth item, a vascular one with a hard sex restriction. Chronic hypertension drives cerebral hypoperfusion and is a primary risk factor for vascular contributions to cognitive impairment. Aldosterone acts through the mineralocorticoid receptor, and eplerenone is a selective mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist already in wide cardiology use. This group measured absolute brain blood flow and oxygenation in hippocampal CA1 and medial prefrontal cortex in spontaneously hypertensive rats of both sexes. Females had more pervasive hypoperfusion than males, interestingly without disrupting flow-metabolism coupling. Eplerenone in females restored perfusion in both regions and improved spatial working memory. In males it did nothing. And even in females the rescue was domain-specific: working memory improved, hippocampal-dependent long-term memory did not, despite hippocampal perfusion being restored. That dissociation is the useful part — it says the perfusion deficit and the memory deficit are not the same lesion, and that restoring flow buys you back some cognitive domains and not others. For a drug this cheap and this well characterized, a sex-stratified trial in hypertensive cognitive impairment is an obvious move, and the sex restriction is a feature for trial design rather than a weakness of the paper.

Fifth, a molecular mechanism for why the apolipoprotein E4 allele makes amyloid worse. In primary neurons, amyloid-beta oligomers plus E4 cut synapse density and dendritic complexity more than the same oligomers plus E3 or E2. The proposed path runs through store-operated calcium entry: the E4 combination specifically lowers STIM1 protein, not STIM2, and the store-operated calcium amplitude falls with it. Overexpressing STIM1 rescues the dendritic phenotype, so STIM1 is load-bearing. The mechanism for losing STIM1 is p62-dependent selective autophagy, and then the interesting consequence — that same autophagy activation goes on to degrade glutathione peroxidase four, the enzyme that keeps lipid peroxidation in check, which produces excess reactive oxygen species and disrupted mitochondrial cristae. So the damage is done by a degradation pathway that is normally the cell's quality-control system, running in the wrong direction on the wrong substrates, and it pulls the ferroptosis machinery into the E4 story. All of this is in culture, the rescue is an overexpression rescue, and the arrow from calcium to peroxidation is inferred rather than clamped. But as a hypothesis it is testable and it names a specific protein.

Sixth, and the one chemistry item, a structure-based campaign against Rab10, a small GTPase implicated in Alzheimer's endolysosomal trafficking and essentially undrugged. The group docked against Rab10 in its GTP-associated form, carried five compounds forward, and found something worth noting with surface plasmon resonance: micromolar apparent affinity without added GTP, nanomolar apparent affinity under GTP-containing conditions. That is nucleotide-state-dependent binding, which is the only plausible way to get selectivity across a family where the fold is nearly identical, and comparative docking against Rab5, Rab8 and Rab11 supports preferential engagement of the GTP-bound state. Functionally, their lead compound reduced GTP-bound Rab10 dose- and time-dependently, reorganized endosomes, redistributed amyloid precursor protein and reduced its carboxyl-terminal fragments in a cell model, and protected differentiated PC12 cells from nerve growth factor withdrawal with less tau phosphorylation.

Here is the direction wrinkle. Four of the five compounds reduced GTP-bound Rab10; one increased it. From the same docking campaign, against the same site. Docking predicts occupancy, not sign, and nothing in the computational pipeline anticipated that split — they found it in the cell-based assay afterwards. That is worth sitting with every time someone pitches structure-based design as end-to-end. These are tool compounds at best, the ADME is predicted rather than measured, and there is no selectivity panel beyond the Rab family. But nucleotide-state-selective small molecules against a Rab is a genuinely hard thing to get.

Seventh, a neurofeedback trial that is unusually honest about failing. Twenty-one patients with major depression did real-time functional MRI amygdala neurofeedback, with a twist on the display: instead of a thermometer bar, the feedback signal was a face whose expression changed with their amygdala activity. One group upregulated the amygdala to make a neutral face happier; the other downregulated it to make a fearful face neutral. Each group got yoked sham feedback on the opposite condition, so the sham is within-subject. The preregistered hypothesis was symptom-specific — that upregulation would preferentially help anhedonia and downregulation would preferentially help anxiety.

Both groups showed the expected neural regulation. Both groups improved on depression severity, anxiety and anhedonia. And the group-by-time interaction, the actual test, was not significant. So the directional hypothesis failed, and the improvement that remains has no control condition that could distinguish it from scanner time, attention, expectancy or regression to the mean — because every participant received real neurofeedback in one direction. A design that controls direction does not control the intervention. I give the authors real credit for saying so in the abstract rather than leading with the within-group drops, which is what most of this literature does. At twenty-one patients this is a mechanism study, and as a mechanism study the finding is that bidirectional amygdala control with naturalistic feedback is achievable — which is not nothing, but it is not a clinical signal.

Eighth and ninth belong together, because one of them is the control the other one needed. The first is a compound-response prediction paper, and it opens with an ablation that should embarrass the field. On a drug-blind benchmark built from L1000 transcriptional profiles, the authors removed the compound input entirely from seven published models — so the model knows the cell line, the plate, the experimental context, and nothing whatsoever about which drug was added. Accuracy changed by at most zero point zero one two in Pearson correlation. The published models, in other words, are predicting the assay, not the pharmacology. Their own method integrates chemical structure, annotated targets, mechanism of action and Cell Painting morphology into a kernel that tolerates missing information, and on a within-plate score that is zero by construction for any compound-blind prediction, it reaches zero point one one seven against zero point zero nine two for the strongest published competitor. Small absolute numbers — but they are small on a metric where the old numbers were zero.

The second is an agentic cellular world model that predicts single-cell expression responses, explains them against cited evidence, and updates from new measurements without retraining weights. On three hundred thirty-nine held-out conditions it beats the best baseline on differential-expression ranking by a wide margin, and the token accounting is striking — roughly a ninety-nine point eight percent reduction in language-model tokens relative to reasoning gene by gene, because it reuses one condition-level evidence interpretation across every gene readout. Credit where due: they do report the delta metric, the one that subtracts the matched vehicle control, and they explicitly note it is undefined for the control reference itself. That is exactly the discipline the first paper says is missing. Their delta correlations are modest — about zero point three three across all genes — but they are honest numbers against the right baseline, and I would rather see a modest number measured correctly than a strong one measured against a control that can win without a drug.

Last, and lightest, a careful study of what happens to a search-equipped language model agent that starts out wrong. A controlled harness, ten models, a thousand synthetic search worlds, with the question and the document collection held fixed while the starting conditions vary: result order, a record of prior failed work, and a seeded wrong hypothesis. Three findings. Final answers are more sensitive to the order evidence arrives in during active search than when the same evidence is handed over all at once — so the retrieval loop amplifies a primacy effect that is absent in the static case. A ten-step dead-end history reduces strategy switching relative to a length-matched neutral history, so sunk-cost-like persistence is about the content of the failure, not the context length.

And the third finding is the one that spoils an obvious fix. They tried instructing the agents to seek disconfirming evidence and to read both sides. For some models it worked well — one gained twenty-three points of accuracy with a twenty-two point rise in opening counter-evidence. For another, accuracy improved thirty points with essentially no change in what it opened, meaning the instruction did something other than what it said. And for a third, counter-evidence opening went up seven points while accuracy went down ten and a half. Opening the other side does not mean reading it. Which, for anyone wiring a literature-search agent into a target-triage pipeline, is the practical warning: you can verify that your agent retrieved the contradicting paper, and that tells you nothing about whether it changed its mind.
