## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. I'm your host, and this is your daily pass through CNS pharmacology, psychiatric and neurology drug discovery, and the computational machinery closing in on both. Eleven items today. If you want a thread, it's timing and state: several of today's papers are about a target whose druggability depends on when you arrive — which developmental stage, which disease stage, which conformation, which cellular compartment.

We open with a paper in Molecular Psychiatry from David Weinshenker's group at Emory, with Michael Beckstead's lab at the Oklahoma Medical Research Foundation, that gives the field its cleanest causal handle yet on the earliest stage of Alzheimer's disease. The premise is well known and badly served by existing models. Hyperphosphorylated pretangle tau appears in the locus coeruleus before amyloid plaques appear anywhere — the stage Braak labeled zero — and that period coincides with the neuropsychiatric symptoms that precede cognitive decline by years: anxiety, agitation, impulse-control problems, disrupted sleep. But every available mouse model drives tau from a ubiquitous promoter, so pathology is everywhere at once, usually alongside overexpressed mutant amyloid, and nobody has recorded locus coeruleus firing with tau restricted to those cells.

So they built exactly that: tyrosine hydroxylase-Cre mice given an adeno-associated viral vector carrying a Cre-dependent mutant human tau, infused into the locus coeruleus. The mutation is P364S, which causes frontotemporal dementia in people, chosen over the canonical P301S because it lacks seeding capacity in vitro — they wanted the consequences to stay inside the nucleus rather than propagate to its targets.

Three months later the phenotype is remarkable for what is absent. Phospho-tau is present in the somatodendritic compartments. There is no loss of noradrenergic cell bodies, no change in tyrosine hydroxylase or norepinephrine transporter immunoreactivity, no reduction in fiber density. There is a strong astrocytic response — GFAP up, larger cells, denser processes. And the neurons fire faster. Spontaneous firing is elevated, and the neurons are more excitable to depolarizing steps. Behaviorally the mice show anxiety-like and compulsive-like phenotypes: longer latency to eat in a novel arena but normal home-cage latency, which is the right control for distinguishing novelty-induced anxiety from appetite. And sleep spindles collapse in density and duration while overall sleep architecture stays intact — microstructure, not insomnia.

Then two omics layers that may matter more than the behavior. Translating ribosome affinity purification from the noradrenergic cells shows reciprocal regulation of exactly the genes you would want: the pacemaker channel HCN2 up, the inhibitory chloride channel CLIC6 down, and the alpha-2C adrenergic autoreceptor up, which reads as a compensatory brake on the hyperactivity. And they adapted cell-type-specific in vivo biotinylation — a TurboID approach — to pull a high-confidence proteome of 862 proteins from these neurons, canonical noradrenergic markers included, which is a real technical achievement in a nucleus this small. Inside it, three chaperonin subunits fall and mTOR signaling rises, both pointing at proteostatic failure.

Here is the part to hold onto. The anxiety phenotype is gone at six and nine months. At nine months the mice bury fewer marbles than controls — the anxiolytic direction. Phospho-tau burden persists, cells still don't die. So within a single model, with the pathology held constant, the behavioral consequence of locus coeruleus tau reverses sign over time. That is a direct warning about noradrenergic pharmacology in Alzheimer's. If early disease is a hyperactive locus coeruleus and later disease a hypoactive one, then any trial enrolling across stages averages over opposite effects, and the field's inconsistent results with noradrenergic agents stop being mysterious. The authors suggest atomoxetine — a norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor — as a way to dampen tonic firing through autoreceptor engagement, a nicely counterintuitive use of a reuptake blocker. Honest limitations: P364S causes frontotemporal dementia, not Alzheimer's, and was chosen precisely because it doesn't spread; they never directly manipulated locus coeruleus activity, so hyperactivity and behavior remain correlated within the model; and the sleep and omics cohorts are small, seven and three to four per group. But as a demonstration that Braak stage zero is a functional state rather than a degenerative one, this is the paper.

Staying with excitability and compensation, a preprint on something the sodium channel epilepsy field has mostly assumed away. Loss-of-function variants in SCN1A, SCN2A and SCN8A produce a wide phenotypic range — Dravet syndrome, West syndrome, autism — and the seizure-inflammation link is thoroughly described. What nobody has tested is whether neurons carrying different sodium channel deficits respond to inflammation differently. This group knocked down each gene separately in primary mouse cortical cultures with short hairpin RNA, then challenged them with conditioned medium from lipopolysaccharide-stimulated microglia.

The result is that the subtypes are not interchangeable, and the divergence only becomes obvious after the inflammatory hit. At baseline the differences are modest — the clearest is that reducing Nav1.2 costs you neurons, consistent with a developmental survival role, while reducing Nav1.1 or Nav1.6 doesn't. After the inflammatory challenge, Nav1.1 knockdown shifts synaptic transcriptional programs, while Nav1.6 knockdown produces substantial remodeling of inflammatory signaling itself. The interpretive claim is the useful one: the consequences of sodium channel dysfunction are not fully specified by the channel's contribution to excitability, because each subtype sits in a cell with a different inflammatory set point. For a field increasingly interested in subtype-selective sodium channel modulators for genetic epilepsies, that means the same drug in two patients with two different loss-of-function genes may be acting on differently inflamed tissue. Caveats are real: dissociated cultures, knockdown rather than haploinsufficiency, and readouts of survival and transcriptomics rather than seizures.

From sodium channels to a TRP channel showing up where it wasn't invited. TRPV1 is taught as a peripheral nociceptor — capsaicin, heat, protons — and its central role has never been convincingly pinned to a computation. This preprint tests whether cortical TRPV1 modulates touch, in the whisker primary somatosensory cortex of behaving mice.

Activating TRPV1 with capsaicin improved perceptual sensitivity, preferentially for near-threshold stimuli — the signature of gain modulation rather than a blanket increase in drive. In cortex, stimulus selectivity and discriminability rose across 836 tracked neurons in six mice, positive noise correlations fell, and stimulus decoding improved. Then the mechanism, which is what makes the paper: in slice current-clamp, capsaicin depolarized resting potential, lowered rheobase and increased firing at rheobase — and all of it vanished on co-application of a selective TRPA1 antagonist, and again in TRPA1 knockouts. Cortical TRPV1 does not raise excitability by itself; it works through TRPA1.

The reason to care pharmacologically is that the brain contains a long list of endogenous TRPV1 activators — anandamide and other endocannabinoids, oxidized lipids, reactive oxygen species, inflammatory mediators — all fluctuating with physiological and pathological state. If those converge on a TRPV1-to-TRPA1 axis that sets cortical gain, then channels we've treated as peripheral analgesic targets are also a lever on sensory sensitivity, which bears directly on the sensory-processing phenotypes in autism and migraine. The caution is dose: cortical capsaicin at micromolar concentrations is a tool manipulation, and the endogenous ligand levels that would recruit this axis are not established.

Two methods papers next, both solving the same underlying problem — you cannot see pharmacology happen inside a living cell — from opposite directions.

The first is a preprint on the cardiac sodium channel Nav1.5 that combines live-cell protein semi-synthesis with voltage-clamp fluorometry. The target is the DIII-DIV linker, which carries the isoleucine-phenylalanine-methionine particle that docks into a hydrophobic pocket to close the pore during fast inactivation. It's an intracellular region, and site-specifically labeling an intracellular region with a small dye in a live cell has been the blocker. Their framing line is worth keeping: the many published cryo-EM structures were determined in the absence of an electric field, so assigning them to defined conformational states is genuinely hard. What they get instead is the voltage dependence and kinetics of the linker's motion in real time, for both fast and steady-state inactivation, in a living cell. And then the pharmacology: lidocaine — the prototype local anesthetic and class one-B antiarrhythmic — changes the kinetics of that conformational change, as do auxiliary subunits. For this audience the generalization beats the cardiac result. Intracellular domains are where a lot of state-dependent drug action lives, and this lets you watch it on the millisecond scale. Point the same chemistry at Nav1.7 or Nav1.9 and you have an assay for state-dependent analgesic candidates.

The second is a preprint on subcellular pharmacology of phosphodiesterase 4. PDE4 inhibitors have a long CNS history — cognition, inflammation, and the emetic liability that killed several programs — and one persistent question is which of the many PDE4 pools inside a cell a given inhibitor is actually engaging. They built a fluorogenic analogue of rolipram, the classic PDE4 inhibitor, by conjugating it to fluorescein diacetate; the ester is cleaved intracellularly to give the fluorescent, near-neutral antagonist. Then, with molecular brightness analysis and fluorescence anisotropy imaging, they show rolipram-displaceable binding to cytosolic PDE4 and map where the binding sites sit — including untagged PDE4 at talin-positive focal adhesions in cardiomyocyte-like cells, which is a compartment nobody would have assigned by biochemistry. Be clear-eyed about the tradeoff: the probe's potency in a cyclic-AMP reporter assay is about 13 micromolar against rolipram's 0.4, roughly thirty-fold weaker, and it's a partial rather than full inhibitor. You are seeing where a related chemotype binds, not where rolipram binds at therapeutic occupancy. Still, as a template — conjugate the drug, activate it inside, image the engagement — it's a generalizable route to spatial pharmacology.

From one target to the whole proteome. A preprint from Jiaming Zhang's lab at the University of Virginia introduces CysLENS, which attacks a specific and under-discussed weakness in covalent chemoproteomics. Large fragment screens report a competition ratio for each cysteine — essentially how much a fragment blocks probe labeling — and that number conflates recognition with plain electrophile reactivity. A hot cysteine and a well-recognized cysteine look the same.

Their inputs are unusually good: 940 fragments comprising 470 matched enantiomeric pairs, quantifying over 45,000 cysteines across more than 10,000 proteins and yielding over 12,000 stereoligandable sites, 695 of them on understudied proteins. Matched enantiomers are the key design choice, because a reactivity-driven hit shows no stereopreference and a recognition-driven hit does. CysLENS then combines engagement strength with cysteine microenvironment similarity from ESM-2 embeddings, compound similarity, stereoselectivity and prior evidence, and emits ranked cysteine-chemotype signatures rather than per-site scores.

The validation I find persuasive is that it rediscovers a pharmacophore it was never told about. Among the top one percent of signatures is a cluster of glutarimide-containing fragments stereoselectively engaging zinc-finger cysteines — which is, of course, the immunomodulatory imide degrader geometry, cereblon recruiting zinc-finger neosubstrates, and the docking recapitulates the glutarimide alignment seen in the crystal structures. The second hit is a spiro-oxapiperidine series at cysteine 1478 of DNMT1, near the DNA-binding surface and distinct from where established inhibitors act, with concentration-dependent isoform-preferential engagement in lysate — half-maximal around 2.9 versus 13.5 micromolar for the two isoforms — retained engagement in live cells, and a strict requirement for the chloroacetamide over the acrylamide. It also beat competition ratio alone at prioritizing recurring interactions on an independent dataset. This isn't CNS work, but it's the infrastructure that will decide which CNS targets get called ligandable over the next five years, and a ranked signature is much closer to something a medicinal chemist can act on than a site list.

Two clinical items now, both in neurodevelopmental and psychiatric therapeutics, and both worth reading with your skeptic engaged.

First, a medRxiv preprint from Katrina Choe's group at McMaster with Kaat Alaerts at KU Leuven that takes a rodent construct into human data. Oxytocin trials in autism have produced a decade of inconsistent results, and one under-examined reason is that the outcome measures assume a global effect. The rodent literature describes a social salience network — a set of oxytocin-modulated regions including amygdala, nucleus accumbens, hypothalamic paraventricular nucleus and ventral tegmental area — but nobody had established whether that network is conserved and clinically meaningful in humans. They assembled a homologous human atlas from open parcellations and applied it retrospectively to resting-state functional MRI from a published placebo-controlled intranasal oxytocin trial in 28 autistic adult men.

There are three findings. Sub-network connectivity tracked behavior coherently — sensory-node hyperconnectivity with greater autism trait expression, disrupted social-node coupling with attachment-related anxiety — consistent with models treating atypical sensory processing as primary rather than downstream of reduced social contact. Connectivity also correlated with salivary oxytocin, a modest but welcome neurochemical anchor. And critically, intranasal oxytocin did not shift the network globally. It moved one edge, amygdala-to-somatosensory, and baseline connectivity there predicted the size of the change, with lower baseline showing greater increase.

If that survives replication, it reframes the trial failures as a measurement problem rather than a mechanism problem — you cannot detect a single-edge effect with a global readout, and if response magnitude depends on baseline, an unstratified trial is underpowered by construction. But hold the enthusiasm at arm's length: twenty-eight participants, all men, retrospective reanalysis of someone else's trial, and low baseline predicting a larger increase is precisely the shape regression to the mean produces. The proper test is prospective stratification on that edge. This paper is a good argument for running it, not evidence that it will work.

Second, a 12-week randomized double-blind placebo-controlled trial from Ningbo University of a psychobiotic added to a stable antidepressant. Sixty-two patients with major depression, all maintained on escitalopram at a fixed 10 milligrams per day for at least three months before randomization, got either Lactiplantibacillus plantarum PS128 at six times ten to the tenth colony-forming units daily or matching placebo. The design deserves credit: fixing the antidepressant dose means the placebo arm is a clean picture of ongoing selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor monotherapy, and the adjunct is tested against that.

The mechanistic package is more complete than probiotic trials usually manage. Sixteen S ribosomal RNA sequencing with functional prediction showed enrichment of the short-chain fatty acid producers Faecalibacterium and Coprococcus and suppression of the Klebsiella expansion seen under placebo; predicted bacterial B-vitamin biosynthesis up, kynurenine pathway down, co-occurrence network topology integrated instead of fragmenting, inflammatory signaling down, salivary cortisol normalized. The kynurenine direction is the interesting and testable part.

And now the reason I would not update much on the efficacy claim. The effect size at week 12 on the seventeen-item Hamilton scale is a Cohen's d around minus one, with a similar figure on the Montgomery-Åsberg scale. A standardized effect of one for an adjunctive probiotic is larger than what augmentation strategies with actual receptor pharmacology deliver — larger than adjunctive aripiprazole, larger than lithium augmentation. Second, the reported remission rate of 62 percent uses a nonstandard definition, a 70 percent or greater reduction in Hamilton score, where conventional remission is an absolute score at or below seven; those are not interchangeable and the nonstandard one flatters. Third, dropout was differential — six of thirty on placebo versus three of thirty-two on the probiotic, several for capsule dissatisfaction — and the headline analysis is per-protocol. Single-center, unblinded-adjacent attrition, generous endpoint definition, enormous effect: that is the profile of a finding that shrinks on replication. The microbiome mechanism panel is still worth having, because it specifies what a larger trial should measure.

Two computational papers to close, and they make the same argument from different corners of chemistry. The current reflex when an agent underperforms is to add search and add critics. Both of these say the learned policy is where the value is, and that the tree is doing less than advertised.

The first, out of Mila and Université de Montréal with Air Liquide, is titled "One Policy Is Enough," and it's a direct rebuttal to a published chemistry agent built on hierarchical evolutionary Monte Carlo tree search — separate policy and execution models, two learned critics, one regressed partly onto GPT-assigned scores. The replacement is deliberately plain: one model interleaving reasoning, tool calls and returns in a single left-to-right generation, warm-started with supervised fine-tuning and then trained with outcome-level reinforcement learning against a programmatic reward read directly off the gold tool-call chain. No learned critic, no language-model judge anywhere in the training loop. On a multi-tool chemistry benchmark, on both backbones the original system used, they improve tool-selection F1 by five and a half points and return F1 by nine and a half on a seven-billion-parameter Qwen, and by three point seven and three point nine on an eight-billion-parameter Llama — against that system's strongest search configuration, at one model invocation per question versus a cost that grows with the tree.

The companion, from Jean-Loup Faulon's group at INRAE on the Paris-Saclay campus, is the cleaner experiment because it freezes everything except the policy. The setting is rule-based retrobiosynthesis: a deterministic biochemical engine generates exactly the same validated transitions for every method, searching for routes that terminate in metabolites an E. coli chassis can supply. The only thing the policy does is choose which frontier molecule to expand next, through a strict choice-only interface. A LoRA-tuned seven-billion-parameter Qwen reaches 65 percent solve rate at just ten expansions on one benchmark, against 59 percent for Monte Carlo tree search, and at two hundred expansions gets 78 versus 75 on that benchmark, 88 versus 80 on a second, and 63 versus 45 on a third. Fine-tuning also consistently beats prompting the same model.

Read together: route supervision buys budgeted search efficiency without touching the chemistry, and much of what tree search was credited with is recoverable by a policy that has seen good routes. Both are honest that performance still depends on how the frontier is constructed and how reactions are ranked — the environment, not the agent. If you're building an agentic design-make-test loop, spend your effort on a verifiable programmatic reward and a fine-tuned policy before you spend it on orchestration.

And to finish, the lightest item, which is also a small piece of hygiene the whole foundation-model-for-biology enterprise needs. A bioinformatics preprint proposes evaluating single-cell foundation models with no labels at all. The principle is simple: a faithful representation should preserve its neighborhood structure under nuisance perturbations that imitate technical and sampling noise — because incomplete sampling is intrinsic to the measurement, so any structure that doesn't survive a resample was never real. Across five models — scGPT, Geneformer, scFoundation, SCimilarity and scConcept — plus a principal components baseline, over 39 datasets, models that standard benchmarks rank as near-equivalent differ by nearly twofold in local neighborhood preservation when you discard just five percent of counts. And the distortion is scale-dependent: a representation can hold its coarse global geometry while its local neighborhoods fall apart, which is exactly the failure a coherent-looking UMAP hides. Cluster-level stability under resampling tracks established biological conservation metrics at a Spearman correlation of 0.78 — the argument that this unsupervised quantity measures something the labeled benchmarks care about, without needing labels.
