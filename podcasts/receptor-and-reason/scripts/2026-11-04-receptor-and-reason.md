## Script

Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Wednesday, November fourth, twenty twenty-six.

Today's thread is about effects that turn out not to live where the measurement was pointed. A receptor whose signal was assumed to run through one intracellular store turns out to use a second one. A blood disorder of neutrophils gets its best molecular description from cells that are not neutrophils. A designed protein whose fold the model predicted almost perfectly has an interface the model partly got wrong. And a capacity we keep attributing to model scale turns out to be readable off the statistics of the training set. In each case the interesting result sits a little to the side of where the experiment was aimed.

The lead is a genuinely new piece of 5-HT-2A pharmacology, out of the pharmacology department at Oxford with a co-author from Compass Pathways. The textbook account of this receptor is that it couples through G-q and phospholipase C, generates inositol trisphosphate, and mobilizes calcium from the endoplasmic reticulum, with a non-canonical arm through beta-arrestin-2 that the field keeps arguing about. The experiment that opens this paper is almost a throwaway control. They depleted the endoplasmic reticulum store with thapsigargin, which selectively inhibits the reticular calcium ATPases, and expected the serotonin response to vanish. It did not. A substantial calcium response survived, in rat C6 glioma cells expressing the receptor endogenously and again in human neuroblastoma cells transfected with the human receptor. The control that makes this worth believing is that the response to ATP, acting through a G-q-coupled purinergic receptor in the same cells in the same minute, was abolished completely. So this is not a leaky store depletion; it is receptor-specific.

Where the rest of the calcium comes from is the lysosome. Depleting lysosomal stores with bafilomycin cut the serotonin response and the psilocin response, and left ATP untouched. They then went after the second messenger. Serotonin and a selective 5-HT-2A agonist both raised cellular levels of nicotinic acid adenine dinucleotide phosphate, measured directly by immunoassay. A membrane-permeant antagonist of that messenger reduced the calcium response in a concentration-dependent way, and — this is the control that matters — only when given as a pretreatment. Applied simultaneously with serotonin it did nothing, which rules out the simplest confound, that the compound is just blocking the receptor. For contrast, the selective 5-HT-2A antagonist volinanserin abolishes the response entirely. The two pathways are not parallel: a selective G-q inhibitor abolished the lysosomal arm as well, so G-q coupling is upstream of both stores. And with a calcium biosensor tethered directly to the lysosomal two-pore channel, they could watch the lysosomal release in single cells and show it exceeds what a cytosolic sensor reports, which is the cleanest form of the claim.

The in vivo arm is a two-pore-channel-2 knockout mouse. Psilocin-induced head twitch fell by about a third, concentrated in the first eight minutes, and psilocin's induction of the plasticity genes c-Fos, Egr-1 and Egr-2 was reduced, though Arc was not. So the pathway contributes to both of the readouts the field uses as proxies — the hallucinogenic one and the neuroplastic one — and it is required for neither. I'd flag three things. The lysosomal calcium signal is small relative to the reticular one, and the authors are candid about that; the cellular work is in glioma and neuroblastoma lines rather than neurons; and the knockout is constitutive. But the finding that the non-hallucinogenic agonist lisuride also mobilizes lysosomal calcium, while having low overall calcium efficacy, is a useful spoiler for anyone hoping this maps neatly onto the psychedelic-versus-non-psychedelic split. What it does offer is a new node — a lysosomal channel downstream of a receptor whose own ligands are hard to make selective — and that is a more tractable medicinal chemistry problem than biasing 5-HT-2A itself.

Staying on the same receptor and on a tool compound that just played a starring role in that paper. A group working in C57 black six mice asked what 5-HT-2A antagonism does to fear extinction, comparing glemanserin, which hits 5-HT-2A and 5-HT-2C, against volinanserin, which is the selective one. Acutely, both increased freezing to the first conditioned stimulus on the first extinction day, in males. Repeated glemanserin went further and genuinely impaired extinction in males — more freezing across trials, more trials needed to reach criterion. Volinanserin produced only a transient increase and did not delay extinction acquisition. Neither did anything in females. The obvious explanation is the 5-HT-2C component, so they tested a selective 5-HT-2C antagonist in a separate cohort of males, and it did not reproduce the effect. Which leaves a ligand-specific effect that is not cleanly assignable to either receptor as usually defined — a reminder that "the 5-HT-2A antagonist did X" is a sentence that depends on which one you used, at what dose, and in which sex.

Third, an attempt to get molecular traction on clozapine-induced neutropenia, from a Queensland consortium. The clinical problem is well posed: clozapine is the only antipsychotic with established efficacy in treatment-resistant schizophrenia, fewer than one in four eligible patients ever receive it, and the roughly one percent risk of neutropenia is a large part of why. They recruited six people with treatment-resistant schizophrenia who had stopped clozapine — three because of neutropenia, three for unrelated reasons — collected peripheral blood mononuclear cells, exposed them in vitro to clozapine alone or clozapine plus its two major metabolites, and ran single-nucleus sequencing. Fifty-nine thousand nuclei, twenty-six clusters.

The result with the most signal is an asymmetry. In the donors with a neutropenia history, clozapine triggered a substantial transcriptional response — forty-five genes up, concentrated in naive CD4 and CD8 T cells. In the donors without that history, the response was one gene. Effect-size concordance between the two groups was essentially zero. A co-expression module in inflammatory-response naive CD4 T cells tracked neutropenia status, with sterol regulatory element binding factor 1 as its apparent hub, which is a slightly uncanny echo of Monday's Parkinson's screen landing on the same transcription factor from a completely different direction. The metabolites added little. Now the caveats, and they are large. Three donors per group. An absence of response at n equals three is not evidence of absence. And the preparation is mononuclear cells, which by definition contain no neutrophils — the entire inference about granulocyte fate is made from lymphocytes, bridged by plausible stories about one gene depleting NAD-dependent granulopoiesis and another binding a neutrophil protease. As a hypothesis-generating first map, it's worth having. As a biomarker program, it needs an order of magnitude more donors and the cells that actually die.

Fourth, a short one on where drug metabolism happens. A rat study looked at oral oxycodone and conditioned place preference, and specifically at brain CYP2D, the enzyme that converts oxycodone to oxymorphone. Inhibiting the enzyme centrally — propranolol delivered intracerebroventricularly, before two of six conditioning sessions — blunted place preference, with a trend toward aversion to the oxycodone-paired compartment. Inducing brain CYP2D with twelve days of nicotine did not change preference, but trended toward reducing that aversion. And oral oxymorphone itself produced a place preference that central inhibition did not touch, which is the control that makes the interpretation hold: the manipulation acts on the conversion step, not on the opioid response. The implication is that interindividual variation in a brain enzyme — not the hepatic pool, which dominates systemic metabolism — could contribute to how rewarding oral oxycodone is. Which connects directly to the CYP2D6 genetics we covered last month, where the risk haplotype's expression effect was brain-specific and absent in liver. Two different experiments arriving at the same unobvious place: for this enzyme and these drugs, the organ that matters is not the one doing most of the metabolism.

Fifth, a chronic unpredictable stress study in rats that is really an argument about sex-stratified design. Eighteen males, twenty-two females. The behavioral readout is negative cognitive bias — freezing to an ambiguous context — which is one of the better translational endpoints in this area because it predicts episode onset and severity in humans rather than just looking depression-adjacent. Stress increased freezing to the ambiguous context, and it did so equally in both sexes. Underneath that identical behavior, almost nothing matched. Stress decreased ventral dentate neurogenesis in both sexes, but increased activation of newborn neurons in dorsal dentate in males only; amygdala c-Fos rose in stressed males; global network efficiency across the fifteen regions they mapped fell in males and not in females. And when they modeled freezing from regional activation, the predictive combination — new neuron activity together with ventral CA1 — ran in opposite directions in the two sexes. If you had run this in males only, you would have a clean mechanistic story. If you had run it in both sexes and pooled, you would have gotten the behavior right and the mechanism wrong, because the two sexes partially cancel. That is a more pointed argument for stratification than the usual one.

Sixth, a treatment-prediction study in depression that is honest about its own ceiling, in Translational Psychiatry. Fifty-two patients with major depression and forty-two controls, structural MRI before cognitive behavioral therapy, symptom change on the Beck Depression Inventory. Mean improvement was about eight points. Among clinical predictors, only baseline perceived stress predicted improvement. Among cortical measures, exactly one region survived correction: gray matter volume of the right middle temporal gyrus, with a standardized beta of 0.55 for total symptom reduction. Then the test that actually decides whether a biomarker is useful. They entered trait anxiety, perceived stress and childhood trauma first, and added the imaging measure second. The increase in explained variance was fifteen percent — and did not reach significance after correction, at p equals 0.066. The same slippage shows up elsewhere in the paper: the association with percentage reduction, rather than total reduction, also failed to survive. So the finding is a single region, dependent on how you scale the outcome, that does not demonstrably beat three questionnaires. I say that as a compliment to the authors, who ran the hierarchical test and reported the number rather than stopping at the one surviving region.

Seventh, de novo protein design aimed at migraine, from Imperial College London with collaborators in Tokyo. The target is PACAP-38, which is where the migraine field has gone after the CGRP antibodies, and the strategy is a ligand trap rather than a receptor blocker. They designed a hundred mini-proteins with BindCraft against the ordered N-terminal twenty-seven residues of the peptide, taken out of the cryo-EM structure of the receptor complex — the C-terminal eleven residues are disordered and were deliberately not targeted. Of the top twenty by design score, three bound. Of forty-four shorter designs, thirty-one to eighty residues, which is the size range you would actually want for a therapeutic, zero bound. Overall hit rate, under five percent.

What happens next is the interesting part. The best binder came in at twenty-six nanomolar by surface plasmon resonance, and circular dichroism said it was ninety-three percent helix, essentially as predicted — the fold was right. The interface was less right. They computed alanine-substitution energies for the nine residues at the predicted contact surface, and eight of nine confirmed experimentally. But when they scanned more broadly, two further interface residues gave mutants with higher affinity than the parent, down to sixteen nanomolar. A round of ProteinMPNN redesign then produced a variant at four and a half nanomolar. So the model was a good enough map to make protein engineering worth doing, and not good enough to tell you which residues to leave alone. There is no cell assay and no in vivo work here yet, and a sixty-three-residue mini-protein against a peptide that circulates is a long way from a drug. But as a measurement of what current design tools give you on a flexible peptide target, this is unusually legible.

Eighth, the most conceptually interesting AI paper I've read this week, from Johns Hopkins with co-authors at Microsoft and Apple. The question is where a language model's capacity for scientific discovery comes from, and the answer they argue for is a property of the training data, not of the model. The starting point is a known result that ties hallucination to missing probability mass, estimated by the Good-Turing statistic — essentially the rate of monofacts, statements that appear exactly once in training. Under that framework discovery is impossible, because everything unobserved is treated as equally likely to be false. Their revision is to carve the unobserved space into a candidate frontier, where the data elevates a statement above noise without settling it, and an arbitrary remainder. Discovery lives on the frontier, and it is bounded above and below by the same monofact rate that bounds hallucination.

Then they tested it, which is what lifts this above a position paper. They built training sets with monofact rates swept from zero to one in two domains — materials discovery against a temporally held-out snapshot of the Materials Project, and regulatory genomics against a massively parallel reporter dataset published after both models' knowledge cutoffs — and fine-tuned two open-weight model families. Discovery increased monotonically with monofact rate in both domains, both model families, both training stages, and both decoding regimes. More reasoning effort raised the absolute rate and left the trend untouched. They separated the obvious confound, training-set diversity, by varying epochs rather than data. And the prediction that makes the theory falsifiable held too: in-distribution, raising the monofact rate raised discovery and hallucination together, though not proportionally — discovery moved from twenty-two to thirty-four percent while hallucination moved from sixty-two to sixty-four. The operational reading is uncomfortable for anyone building scientific corpora. Deduplicating your rare facts, which every data pipeline is built to do, is removing exactly the mass from which discoveries are drawn. I'd note the definition of discovery is narrow — exact recovery of a material or sequence that exists and was withheld — and absolute materials rates are a few percent. But it's a real experimental handle on something usually discussed in the abstract.

Ninth, a benchmark that measures the thing most agent benchmarks miss. It evaluates patient-advocacy agents — the administrative work of getting a patient the care a clinician has already recommended, which in the United States is a two-hundred-sixty-five-billion-dollar annual overhead. Forty-seven interactive worlds built from de-identified real cases reviewed by expert advocates, each requiring the agent to find hidden information and coordinate insurers, clinics, suppliers and pharmacies. The distinguishing design choice is that the engine checks every action at the moment it is taken, against three conditions: does the agent have permission, does the fact it is relying on come from a source it actually confirmed, and is the action prohibited.

Fourteen frontier models, three runs each, nearly two thousand runs. Of the nine hundred twenty-one runs that reached every required outcome, two hundred fifty-four — twenty-eight percent — got there by an invalid route. The strongest model reached all outcomes in eighty-two percent of runs and passed all the procedural checks in only fifty-three. Eighty-two percent of the permission failures concerned patient consent, and the single largest category was exchanging patient information without it. Then the diagnostic that tells you what to fix: when two models were handed a verified route, their pass rates went from thirty percent to ninety-two, and from forty-four to ninety-seven. So the bottleneck is finding a lawful route, not executing one. For anyone deploying agents against healthcare administration, that is both encouraging and a warning — the fix is retrieval and planning, and the failure mode in the meantime is an agent that gets the walker approved and releases the records to do it.

To close, something lighter and more cheerful. A group at Purdue built CryoPilot, two language-model agents that carry single-particle cryo-EM from raw micrographs to a validated atomic model. The reconstruction agent inspects intermediate images and diagnostics, chooses and parameterizes jobs in CryoSPARC, tries alternative strategies, and reverts decisions that don't pan out, with a persistent session database that keeps the whole history traceable across multi-day runs. The modeling agent fixes handedness, builds the model, trims density-unsupported regions and optimizes against map fit, geometry and local model-map agreement. Across nine public datasets, eight reconstructions landed between two point six and three point five angstroms, and seven refined models matched reference structures within zero point four to four point nine angstroms at the alpha carbons. That upper end is not a good model, and one difficult dataset needed a human to suggest a different particle-picking strategy before it worked. But the honest framing is that the expert judgment in cryo-EM processing has always been about which branch to take and when to back out of one, and that is exactly the part this system is attempting — with a log of every decision, which is more than most of us keep.
