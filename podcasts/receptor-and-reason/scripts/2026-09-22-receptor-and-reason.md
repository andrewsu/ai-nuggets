## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. I'm your host, and this is your daily pass through CNS pharmacology, psychiatric and neurology drug discovery, and the computational work closing in on both. Twelve items today, and the thread running through them is the gap between the thing you can measure and the thing you actually want. Target engagement is not clinical benefit. A biomarker that moves is not a symptom that moved. Classification accuracy is not understanding. Almost every paper today is interesting at exactly the point where those two things come apart.

We open with the clearest instance, and probably the most consequential preprint of the week. A group at the Providence VA and Brown has run the first-in-human, active-controlled trial of low-intensity focused ultrasound targeting the amygdala in major depressive disorder. Focused ultrasound at non-ablative intensities is the field's best current answer to a real structural problem — transcranial magnetic and electrical stimulation reach cortex, and the targets psychiatry most wants are deep. Ten patients, single-blind randomized crossover, two ten-minute sonications, right amygdala versus left primary somatosensory cortex as an active control rather than sham. That control choice is the part worth pausing on, because it does work sham cannot: it holds the scalp sensation, the acoustic experience, and the ritual constant.

The target engagement evidence is genuinely convergent. Arterial spin labeling showed perfusion change in the targeted right amygdala far exceeding both the control site and, importantly, the adjacent hippocampal head — so this is millimeter-scale, not just deep. BOLD during sonication picked up ventromedial prefrontal, rostral anterior cingulate, and insula. Resting-state connectivity between basolateral amygdala and sensorimotor cortex dropped afterward. And there's a nice internal control: the somatosensory sonication significantly activated auditory cortex, which is the known confound in this modality, while the amygdala sonication did not — so the amygdala effects aren't an auditory artifact riding along.

Now the part that matters. Symptoms improved over time, but not differentially between conditions, and this study was never powered for efficacy. What it found instead is a negative correlation: participants with *smaller* amygdala perfusion change showed *greater* depression improvement at twenty-four hours and at one week. And the single participant who met the protocol's definition of clinical worsening — worsening that triggered the safety plan and stopped further sonication — had the largest perfusion increase of the cohort, four hundred and forty-one percent above baseline. Adverse events were also more frequent after amygdala than control sonication, at a rate ratio of about two and a quarter. The authors draw the correct inference, which is that the relationship between physiological engagement and clinical effect may be non-monotonic, and that there's likely an optimal window rather than a more-is-better dose curve. Seven of ten participants spontaneously reported calm, clarity, or lightness after amygdala sonication and none did after control, which is a real signal and also exactly the kind of signal that will get over-read. Hold this one as proof of principle for deep noninvasive targeting, and as a warning that engagement metrics are not efficacy surrogates.

Staying with neuromodulation and the timescale problem. A Baylor group has published what I think is the most impressive dataset in psychiatric neurotechnology this month: nearly a hundred and twenty thousand patient-hours of chronic intracranial recording from twenty-four patients receiving ventral capsule ventral striatum deep brain stimulation for treatment-resistant OCD. The clinical problem they're attacking is the waiting. Response to DBS in OCD takes months, and during those months nobody — clinician or patient — knows whether the programming is working.

Their biomarker is almost aggressively simple, and that's the point. They take average local field potential power in a roughly nine hertz band, sampled every ten minutes by the implanted device, fit a lag-one linear autoregressive model on two days of data, use it to predict the third day, and call the resulting coefficient of determination "neural predictability." That's it — no deep network, nothing that needs a training corpus. The intuition is that symptomatic states look smooth and strongly circadian, which an autoregressive model predicts easily, while the responding state is punctuated by frequent transient deflections, which it does not. So predictability falls as people get better.

And it does. Responders showed a significant drop in predictability from pre-stimulation to stable response; non-responders did not. Y-BOCS scores across sixty-seven administrations tracked the preceding two weeks of predictability. But the headline is the timing. Responders separated from non-responders by day four after activation, and in every patient with continuous coverage the measure crossed the decision threshold within the first two weeks — while stable clinical response took an average of two hundred and six days. Seven months of clinical uncertainty, collapsed to about a fortnight of physiological evidence. This is not yet a closed-loop controller and the non-responder group is seven patients, so treat the discrimination statistics as promising rather than validated. But as a way to reduce the blind interval in an intervention where blind intervals cost patients half a year, it's the right idea measured the right way.

Third stimulation paper, and the one that best illustrates that a manipulation can work without doing what you assumed. In Translational Psychiatry, a Munich group used transcranial temporal interference stimulation — the technique where two high-frequency fields, six hundred and six hundred six hertz here, interfere to produce a six hertz envelope where they overlap, letting you reach subcortical structures noninvasively — to target the striatum during an effort-based decision task in the scanner. The question is whether the striatum causally controls decisions to spend cognitive effort, which animal work says yes and human work has never been able to test directly.

Theta stimulation increased sensitivity to both reward magnitude and effort cost. Both. It did not shift overall preference toward high-effort options, which is what the naive "striatal activation boosts motivation" account predicts. It sharpened the trade-off in both directions, consistent with dual-pathway models where the direct pathway carries benefits and the indirect pathway carries costs. Two things make me believe it. First, they ran it twice — an out-of-scanner pilot and the scanner study — with converging results. Second, the frequency specificity: an eighty hertz gamma envelope, delivered with the same carrier frequencies, did nothing, which is the cleanest available argument that the effect is the envelope and not the carrier. Neurally, the striatum's effort representation strengthened, and a mediation analysis put striatum-to-anterior-cingulate coupling, rather than striatal activation itself, on the causal path to behavior. Sobel test z of one point seven one, p of point zero four — so a real but not overwhelming mediation. If you're thinking about amotivation as a target, the implication is that sharpening the cost signal and sharpening the benefit signal may be inseparable.

From stimulation to the pharmacological read-out itself. A preprint from Germany asks whether the aperiodic exponent of the resting EEG — the slope of the one-over-f background, increasingly used as a noninvasive proxy for cortical excitation-inhibition balance — actually behaves the way the E to I story says it should. Twenty-five healthy adults, single-blind placebo-controlled crossover, target-controlled infusion of low subanesthetic ketamine, propofol, or placebo. Ketamine flattened the exponent, shifting toward excitation. Propofol steepened it. Both reduced alpha power.

That much is confirmation. The contribution is the confound they went after. Ketamine reliably raises heart rate and blood pressure, and EEG is not immune to systemic physiology, so the entire literature using aperiodic slope as an E to I proxy under ketamine has an unaddressed alternative explanation. They recorded ECG alongside and tested mediation. The cardiovascular response did not mediate the cortical shift — and when they controlled for heart rate, the ketamine effect was numerically *stronger*, which implies the systemic response has been suppressing the estimate rather than creating it. That is a small, well-designed study doing something the field needed: not discovering the effect, but removing the reason to doubt it.

Since we're on excitation-inhibition balance, a Molecular Psychiatry paper takes the same concept down to cell types. STXBP1-related disorder is caused by de novo heterozygous mutations producing haploinsufficiency, and presents with developmental delay, intellectual disability, early-onset seizures, and autistic features. EEG points at E to I imbalance. The puzzle is that STXBP1 is expressed in essentially every neuron studied, so why an imbalance rather than a uniform deficit? This group ran single-cell RNA sequencing across every cell type in somatosensory cortex of a validated mouse model. Cell-type abundances were normal. The transcriptomic hit was concentrated in GABAergic and glutamatergic neurons, particularly Sncg interneurons and deep-layer pyramidal cells, and — this is the interesting bit — in astrocytes, which don't express STXBP1 at all, implying a non-cell-autonomous response. Differentially expressed genes barely overlapped across neuron types but converged on synaptic and translation-related gene ontology terms, backed by a trend toward reduced protein translation on puromycin incorporation. Excitatory neurons showed more synaptic dysregulation than inhibitory ones. And seizure burden correlated negatively with translation-gene expression in both astrocytes and neurons. So the EEG-level readout of imbalance resolves into differential vulnerability and a translational deficit, not a uniform loss of function — which is a different therapeutic proposition entirely.

Two shorter pharmacology items now, both in Neuropharmacology. The first is a mechanistic dissection of ketamine neurotoxicity, which matters more as ketamine clinics proliferate. Using a mouse cognitive-impairment model and HT22 hippocampal cells, a Chinese group reports that ketamine downregulates Samm50, a protein that maintains mitochondrial outer membrane integrity, with the expected collapse in membrane potential and ATP and a rise in reactive oxygen species — and separately upregulates transferrin receptor one with iron accumulation, lipid peroxidation, and loss of GPX4 and SLC7A11, the signature of ferroptosis. The ordering experiment is what makes it worth mentioning. Ferrostatin-1 and deferoxamine rescued cell death and iron overload but failed to restore ATP or Samm50, whereas Samm50 overexpression restored mitochondrial function *and* brought GPX4 and SLC7A11 back up. So the mitochondrial lesion sits upstream and ferroptosis is downstream, which means an anti-ferroptotic agent would rescue the endpoint you measure while leaving the energetic failure in place. I'm reading the abstract only here — Elsevier full text isn't reachable — so take the two-step model as the authors' claim rather than something I've verified against the figures.

The second is a negative result that deserves to be read. A Hungarian group gave the alpha-2A adrenergic agonist guanfacine for eleven days to Long Evans rats and to the Lisket substrain, a triple-hit schizophrenia-like model, across three doses. Alpha-2 agonists are perennially floated for cognitive and negative symptoms. What they got was dose-dependent impairment following an inverted-U, concentrated in the Lisket animals, on exploration and cognition measures in a reward-based task. Also abstract-only. The inverted-U is the standard caveat for this receptor class, and the finding that the model animals are more sensitive to the impairing arm than controls is the kind of thing that should temper enthusiasm about repurposing here.

Which brings us to repurposing done computationally, and today's cleanest example of a strong association whose mechanism is genuinely unresolved. A group working in All of Us evaluated varenicline against nicotine replacement therapy for incident Alzheimer's disease and related dementias in adults fifty and over initiating smoking cessation. Varenicline arrived as a candidate from their own pipeline — a medication-wide association study in which a transformer trained on roughly a million VA records to discriminate future dementia cases from controls was run through an explainability method to surface protective drug exposures. Varenicline ranked second. The cohort study: nine thousand five hundred and eighty-four people, propensity matched to seventeen hundred and fifty-eight per arm, hazard ratio point five two for incident dementia and point six six for the composite with death.

A halving of risk is a large effect, and the authors spend a substantial chunk of the discussion arguing against their own result, which I appreciate. Varenicline is a more effective cessation agent than nicotine replacement — in a recent head-to-head, fifty-six percent continuous abstinence versus forty-three — and smoking cessation independently lowers dementia risk. So the comparison may be measuring cessation efficacy rather than any direct action at alpha-4-beta-2 nicotinic receptors. Post-treatment smoking status wasn't systematically captured, which is the study's central limitation and the authors say so. What you should take from this is that the AI pipeline earned its keep by nominating a plausible, testable, cheap candidate — not that the mechanism has been established. Those are very different claims and the distinction is where most repurposing literature goes wrong.

Now to the computational side proper, where the same gap appears in a different costume. From Bayer, a system called ASCENT, and I'd flag it as the most useful deployment report I've read this quarter. The problem is real-world clinical data analysis for epidemiology: an executable query can return a perfectly plausible answer to the wrong question, because choices about population, denominator, index date, and time window get made implicitly. ASCENT exposes medical coding, cohort building, and question answering through a shared Model Context Protocol tool surface, deliberately decoupling the governed data layer from whichever client, model, and orchestrator you point at it — both for the standardized OMOP common data model and for native vendor schemas.

They also built an evaluation set called EPITRAP, constructed around recognized pharmacoepidemiological errors rather than around whether the SQL runs. Against a fixed mechanical pipeline, capable models in an agent loop gained about twenty-seven points of accuracy on native schemas and twenty on OMOP. A weaker open-weight model gained nothing, staying at baseline — the capability threshold here is sharp. Two findings I'd underline. Reflection loops raised accuracy with one orchestrator and not with others, while adding twenty-seven percent more tool calls and thirteen percent more runtime across the board. And when they withheld the specialized text-to-SQL tooling entirely and made the agent write its own SQL, accuracy was unchanged — tool calls went up by roughly two-fold and runtime actually went *down*. So the scaffolding buys governance, reusable artifacts, and fewer interaction steps, not correctness. There's also deployment data: thirty-six users, thirteen thousand calls over about ten weeks, and a candid list of failure modes observed in real use, led by plausible answers to the wrong question.

The same theme, sharper, from Shanghai AI Lab. They built OmicsBench, eleven hundred and sixty expert-validated questions across DNA regulation, RNA processing, and protein function, and critically it does not score a label — it scores whether the model produced the evidence chain, against instance-specific rubrics decomposed into atomic evidence units like a TATA box at a given position or a BLAST E-value below a threshold. Across seventeen models they find what they call reasoning degeneration: the specialized scientific language models beat general-purpose models on classification accuracy while failing to articulate the biological evidence supporting their own correct answers. Their reading is shortcut learning — high accuracy from sequence statistics with no mechanistic grounding underneath, which is precisely the failure mode that makes a model useless for discovery even while it looks good on a leaderboard. Their fix is tool-augmented on-policy distillation: let a tool-using agent in the Biomni mould produce answers with tool-verifiable evidence, then distill that on-policy into a student that needs no tools at inference. Across five model sizes from under a billion to twenty-seven billion parameters, evidence grounding improved consistently and prediction improved on most tasks, and the gains held at every scale — which is the argument that this is a training-signal problem, not a capacity problem.

Two more in that family, then we're done. A bioRxiv preprint introduces scACORN, which takes the opposite bet from the monolithic single-cell foundation model. Their premise is that single-cell interpretation isn't one task — the right answer depends on tissue, cohort, perturbation, and annotation resolution — so they build specialized small language models per domain and have a fixed orchestrating agent select and compose them at inference, under a natural-language playbook that is itself optimized from textual feedback with no gradient updates to the orchestrator. Across ten Tabula Sapiens tissues, domain-aligned contrastive adaptation lifted transfer macro-F1 from point three six to point six four, specialized experts hit point eight nine exact-match annotation accuracy. But the number I'd keep is the last one: optimizing the playbook cut unsupported gene citations from fourteen and a half percent to three and a half. Same models, same data, prompt-level optimization only — and the thing that improved most was whether the claims were backed by the genes.

We close with the lightest item, which is also a nice lesson in reading your own subgroups. HPOQuest is a training-free framework for rare-disease diagnosis that treats phenotype collection as a sequential decision problem: start from a handful of observed Human Phenotype Ontology terms, maintain a probabilistic disease ranking, and pick the next question to ask the clinician by information value. Headline numbers are striking — up to thirty points of Recall-at-one and forty-five points of Recall-at-five over sparse-input baselines, and on one cohort it beats a full-profile reference system while starting from three seed phenotypes.

The honest part is the stratification. They split patients by whether the true disease appears in the top fifteen candidates from the seed phenotypes alone. For those cases, Recall-at-one goes from zero to seventy-eight percent on one cohort. For the cases where it doesn't, improvements are marginal everywhere. So active questioning is powerful for refining a differential you're already inside, and close to useless for finding a diagnostic region you've missed — which is, I'd note, roughly the opposite of the clinical situation where rare-disease diagnosis actually fails. They also report that no single acquisition policy wins across cohorts, which is the sort of thing a less careful paper would have hidden behind a best-of aggregate. Knowing which regime your tool helps in is worth more than the headline delta, and today that has been true of nearly everything on the list.

That's the show. Twelve items, and if there's one thing to carry out of it: when a paper reports that its readout moved, ask what it would have taken for the readout to move without the thing you care about moving with it. The best papers today asked that themselves. See you tomorrow.
