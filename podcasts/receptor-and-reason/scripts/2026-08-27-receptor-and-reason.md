## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. I'm your host, and this is your daily pass through CNS pharmacology, psychiatric and neurology drug discovery, and the computational machinery closing in on both. Eleven items today, and the lead is one of those rare papers that spends most of its length demolishing its own results, and is far more useful for it.

The question is subtype selectivity prediction. You have two closely related receptors, you have a set of compounds with measured affinity at both, and you want a structure-based method that tells you which compounds will be selective. This is the everyday task in GPCR medicinal chemistry, and there is a standard way to score it: correlate your predicted selectivity against measured selectivity, and judge that correlation against a noise ceiling you get by propagating assay error. A group working on the alpha-2 adrenergic receptors took that whole apparatus apart on a frozen benchmark of 586 compounds with paired alpha-2A and alpha-2C affinities, and the answer is that almost everything about the setup is flattering the method.

Start with the headline. Glide standard precision docking reaches a Spearman correlation of 0.071 with measured selectivity, and the confidence interval includes zero. Convolutional neural network rescoring with GNINA improves that to 0.188. Now the comparison that matters: a Morgan fingerprint random forest that is never shown a receptor at all reaches 0.564 under the strictest cross-validation split they could construct, and 0.741 under the loose one. A model that does not know the receptors exist beats both docking methods by a factor of three to eight. And they checked that this is not a setup failure — blind redocking of five alpha-2 co-crystals put every one of twenty seeds within two angstroms, with median top-ranked deviations between a quarter and three quarters of an angstrom. Pose recovery is fine. The scoring is what fails.

Then the controls, and this is the part worth stealing. First, the ceiling. Ninety percent of the compound pairs in this benchmark have both of their affinity values from the same source document — same lab, same protocol, same radioligand batch. Propagating single-endpoint noise assumes those errors are independent, and they are not; the within-document error correlation is 0.66. Measure the noise directly from cross-document replicates instead of assuming it, and the attainable ceiling rises from 0.704 to 0.897. That widens every gap, and it means the ligand-only null does not saturate the ceiling either — there is real headroom neither chemistry nor static structure is reaching.

Second, a cluster-identity null. They trained a model on nothing but which chemical series a compound belongs to — a one-hot encoding, no chemistry, no structure. On the dopamine D3 versus D2 benchmark, the field's favorite selectivity test case, series identity alone explains half the variance. On alpha-2 it explains nothing and sits at chance. Which means the standard benchmark is substantially solvable by series recognition, and a structure-based method scored on it is being graded on a task it never had to solve.

Third, a nonselective reference. They ran a metadynamics free-energy protocol on D3 versus D2 and asked what it gives for eticlopride, which has essentially no selectivity. Across nine reasonable ways of defining the basins, it assigned between plus 1.4 and plus 4.8 kilocalories per mole of apparent selectivity to a ligand that has none — while a genuinely D3-selective antagonist came back near zero. The protocol inverted the ordering. They then list five defects in their own run, two of them bookkeeping errors, and argue the design requirement survives all five anyway: run the nonselective control before you quote a selectivity free energy, or an effect-sized number will read as a success.

Fourth, a same-receptor floor. Contact-difference analysis between two structures, tested against a permutation null, said alpha-2 selectivity determinants are enriched 1.88-fold at the extracellular end, at a p-value of five in a hundred thousand. Then they ran the identical analysis on two independent structures of the *same* receptor, where subtype divergence is zero by construction. Adenosine A2A against A2A gives 1.77-fold. Serotonin 5-HT2B against itself gives 1.68. The floor is not one. Against that floor, 1.88 is not distinguishable from crystallographic variation, and they withdraw the claim.

And fifth, the one that reorders everything. Molecular weight alone correlates with measured selectivity at 0.56. Five descriptors you can compute from a SMILES string in milliseconds — molecular weight, heavy atom count, rotatable bonds, polar surface area, and calculated logP — reach 0.645, which is 72 percent of the measured ceiling. So a structure-based method reporting 0.3 on a benchmark like this has not demonstrated structural reasoning; it has demonstrated less than molecular weight. Residualize size out properly, fold by fold, and the pose contact pattern collapses to 0.037, indistinguishable from a size-preserving shuffle — while the ligand fingerprint retains 0.258. There is a real non-size chemical signal in these data. Nothing they could derive from a docked pose touches any of it.

The practical read for anyone working alpha-2: programs chasing alpha-2A selectivity, which is where the withdrawal-suppressing action of clonidine and lofexidine lives, against the alpha-2B vascular liability, should not expect static docking scores to rank candidates. The same caution attaches to xylazine and medetomidine as adulterants in the illicit opioid supply, where subtype attribution is a live question and neither has a co-crystal. And the broader point is that these five controls each cost less than the calculation they qualify. That is the whole argument.

Staying with the theme of measuring the thing you actually need, a bioRxiv preprint asks a question about EEG foundation models that the field has essentially skipped. These models are evaluated almost entirely on disease-discrimination accuracy. But a biomarker needs something discrimination does not imply — test-retest reliability, the stability of a repeated measurement on the same person. The authors froze nine representations, six EEG-oriented foundation models across masked, contrastive and predictive pretraining, plus handcrafted spectral features and two general-purpose time-series models with no EEG exposure at all, and put them through one preprocessing pipeline across two healthy retest cohorts and three neurodegenerative ones.

Reliability, as intraclass correlation, ranged from 0.08 to 0.76 across the nine — a coefficient of variation of 53 percent. Discrimination across the same nine varied about a tenth as much. And reliability was not predicted by pretraining paradigm, not by whether the model had ever seen EEG, and not by discrimination. One of the most reliable representations tested had no EEG exposure whatsoever. Two independent analyses then argue the two properties draw on partly different information: band ablation puts alpha first for reliability and theta first for Alzheimer's and frontotemporal discrimination, and subspace geometry finds the subject-stable directions overlapping the disease-discriminative directions at or below a random null. The cleanest single result is architectural — BENDR's reliability collapses from 0.48 at layer zero to 0.13 at its final encoder stage, replicated in a second cohort, while LaBraM's reliability peaks at block eight and its discrimination at block six, so no single layer is optimal for both. The authors are honest that the discrimination half of several contrasts does not survive correction at these sample sizes. The recommendation stands anyway: if you are pulling a frozen EEG embedding for anything longitudinal, measure its reliability, because nothing on the model card will tell you.

Third item, and we stay computational. In Pharmacometrics and Systems Pharmacology, a group has built ProteinDB, a proteomics-based expression database that drops directly into PK-Sim. The motivation is a structural oddity in open-source PBPK: PK-Sim has historically distributed enzyme and transporter capacity across the body using tissue *transcriptomics*, even though protein abundance is what actually governs disposition. They pulled absolute abundances from PaxDb for solid organs and plasma, plus a dedicated erythrocyte dataset, harmonized to micromoles per liter of tissue, covering about eighteen thousand genes.

The cross-platform comparison is the interesting part. Hepatic cytochrome P450 abundances agree reasonably well across ProteinDB, PK-Sim, Simcyp and GastroPlus. Everything else diverges, and the divergence is concentrated in a handful of proteins — CYP3A5 is 36-fold higher in ProteinDB than in PK-Sim, BCRP roughly 170-fold lower than Simcyp and 50-fold higher than GastroPlus. Drop one or two outliers and the geometric mean fold error falls below two almost everywhere. And for sixteen of seventeen discordant proteins, the cross-platform spread sits inside the inter-study variability already present among the source proteomics datasets — so this is not one platform being wrong, it is quantitative proteomics being noisy and each vendor having picked a different corner of it. On performance, single-compound models for midazolam, digoxin, rifampicin and tizanidine held their ground, and the drug-interaction scenarios improved: rifampicin-midazolam went from an AUC ratio geometric mean fold error of 1.90 to 1.66. The honest limitation is that PaxDb reports averages, so there is no age stratification, no disease state, no inter-individual variability — and intestinal proteins have been reported to vary a hundredfold between people. Call it a better baseline, not a solution.

From modeling how drugs distribute to whether people should still be taking them. The RELEASE trial, out on medRxiv, is a pragmatic cluster-randomized trial across 26 Australian general practices testing antidepressant deprescribing. Four hundred eighty-three adults on antidepressants for more than a year, recruited irrespective of whether they intended to stop, with a mean duration of use of fourteen years. The intervention was an invitation to a GP review plus patient-facing resources for shared decision-making and a guide to hyperbolic tapering — dose reductions proportional to the receptor-occupancy curve rather than linear in milligrams.

Cessation at twelve months was 14.9 percent in the intervention arm against 8.6 percent under usual care, an odds ratio of 1.95 with a lower bound just touching one and a p-value of exactly 0.050. The secondary outcomes are firmer: cessation at six months, 11.7 against 4.8 percent; more than a 75 percent dose reduction at twelve months, 19.6 against 9.9 percent. Depression, anxiety and withdrawal symptom scores were the same in both arms, and no adverse events were attributable to the intervention. The absolute numbers are modest and the primary outcome is on the knife edge — but the population is the hard one. These were people fourteen years in with no intention of stopping, and the thing being tested is essentially an invitation plus decent information. That it moves cessation at all, without a relapse signal, is the finding.

Sticking with what patients expect from treatment, Neuropsychopharmacology has a nicely designed negative result on open-label placebo. The premise is that open-label placebo — telling someone the pill is inert and watching it work anyway — is usually explained through expectation. If that is right, boosting the prefrontal machinery of expectation should amplify it. So they combined an established open-label placebo paradigm for emotional distress with anodal transcranial direct current stimulation over left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, active or sham, in a two-by-two design.

Both main effects came out as advertised in isolation. Under sham stimulation, the open-label placebo group rated distressing images as less distressing than controls, a Cohen's d of about 0.4, replicating prior work. Under active stimulation, controls also became less distressed, d about 0.3. But the two did not add. In the open-label placebo group, active stimulation did nothing, and the group-by-stimulation interaction was significant. Neutral images showed no effect of anything, which is the specificity check you want. The straightforward reading is that whatever the stimulation is doing overlaps with, rather than augments, whatever the open-label placebo is doing — and it undercuts the assumption that open-label placebo runs on the same dorsolateral expectancy circuitry as deceptive placebo. Self-reported outcome, healthy volunteers, so hold it loosely, but the design is clean.

Now a psychedelics item with real translational teeth. PSILAUT, from King's College London, is a pseudo-randomized crossover in 67 adults — 37 autistic, 30 non-autistic, matched on age, sex and IQ — given 2 milligrams, 5 milligrams, or placebo psilocybin on separate visits, with resting-state fMRI at peak effects. This was explicitly not an efficacy trial; it is a target-engagement study asked before anyone runs psilocybin in autism.

At baseline under placebo, network connectivity did not differ between groups. That matters, because it means what follows is a difference in *responsivity*, not a difference in starting state. At five milligrams, the non-autistic participants showed decreased within-network connectivity of the frontoparietal and limbic networks. The autistic participants showed the opposite pattern in between-network terms: increased integration between default mode and frontoparietal, and between dorsal and ventral attention — the same pairs that went the other way in non-autistic participants, with significant dose-by-group interactions. And across the whole sample, the size of that between-network increase tracked Autism Quotient score continuously, not diagnostic category. Subjective effects were modest and comparable between groups, so this is not simply a story about who felt more.

The mechanistic candidate the authors offer is worth sitting with: reduced 5-HT2A density and fewer parvalbumin interneurons in the autistic brain, with those interneurons being exactly the population that normally converts 5-HT2A activation into synchronizing GABAergic constraint on pyramidal cells. Take the constraint away and you would expect precisely this — connectivity rising between networks that are usually segregated. The alternative reading is simple sensitivity, that low doses in autism produce what higher doses produce elsewhere. Either way, dose-finding in autism cannot be inherited from existing trials. No plasma psilocin was collected, which is the limitation I would most want closed, since group differences in metabolism could shift where the peak sits relative to the scan.

Two circuit papers next. In Molecular Psychiatry, a group has gone after diminished interest as a construct separable from anhedonia. The distinction is real and clinically underserved — anhedonia is about hedonic impact and reward valuation, diminished interest is about initiating and sustaining exploratory engagement, and antidepressant trials tend to collapse them. They built an automated paradigm scoring object-supported rearing as a readout of exploratory interest, and found it degrades after two weeks of chronic unpredictable mild stress, before the standard depressive-like measures move.

The circuit is ventromedial prefrontal cortex to *posterior* piriform cortex. Chemogenetic activation of that projection increased interest-related exploration and inhibition decreased it, with no effect on tail suspension immobility, sucrose preference, locomotion, novel object recognition, or the buried food pellet test — so basic olfaction is intact and this is not a general arousal effect. The parallel projection to anterior piriform did nothing at all. Downstream, activating the pathway upregulated Gria1 selectively among glutamate receptor transcripts, with GluA1 protein rising in the postsynaptic density fraction, and no movement in canonical olfactory pathway genes. The physiology is specifically about burst firing: exploration recruits bursts in vmPFC-recipient piriform neurons with tonic rates unchanged, brief hundred-hertz terminal stimulation reproduces that selectively, and chronic stress reduces bursting first and tonic rate only later. Then the closing experiment — tag the vmPFC ensemble active during baseline exploration, optogenetically reactivate its piriform terminals four weeks into stress, and exploratory interest comes back while immobility and sucrose preference stay where they were. A stress-vulnerable pathway dedicated to one symptom dimension, and the piriform cortex turns out not to be doing olfaction here at all.

The other mechanism paper is a genuinely satisfying repurposing argument. SNAP25 encephalopathies are developmental and epileptic encephalopathies caused by de novo variants in the presynaptic t-SNARE, and the seizures are famously treatment resistant. The reason may be that these particular variants — V48F, D166Y, and especially L50S — disproportionately increase *spontaneous* glutamate release while leaving evoked release largely alone, and every antiepileptic we have targets evoked release. The authors confirmed that: levetiracetam and topiramate did nothing for the abnormal network activity or the depolarized resting potentials in human induced neurons carrying these variants.

So they went postsynaptic instead. Lithium is a well-characterized synaptic downscaling agent — it drives AMPA receptor internalization, the mirror image of the upscaling that ketamine and several antipsychotics produce. Chronic lithium reduced miniature EPSC amplitudes, restored resting membrane potential, and normalized action potential waveforms in the mutant human neurons. Then they made a CRISPR knock-in mouse carrying the heterozygous L50S allele, and the phenotype is severe — the colony would not breed naturally, nearly 60 percent of pups died by postnatal day thirty, and adults had frequent seizures, elevated anxiety, social avoidance and impaired long-term memory. Sixteen to nineteen days of lithium chow reduced seizure frequency, improved long-term memory, and increased ambulatory exploration. It did not touch social interaction or motor strength. That partial rescue is the honest result, and the framing is what I want to flag: not lithium for its mood indication, but lithium as a tool for engaging homeostatic plasticity in a disorder defined by too much spontaneous release. That is a target class, not a one-off.

A shorter neurochemistry item from the same issue. Using MALDI mass spectrometry imaging to map neurotransmitters, metabolites and lipid species across rat brain regions after supratherapeutic dosing with three anabolic androgenic steroid decanoate prodrugs — nandrolone, testosterone, and trenbolone — the authors report that each produces a distinct signature rather than a shared class effect. Testosterone and nandrolone raised dopamine and 3-methoxytyramine in the tail of the striatum, a region tied to novelty, stimulus intensity and threat avoidance, and raised phosphatidylethanolamine and phosphatidylserine species alongside it. Trenbolone went the other way on lipids while showing higher monoamine oxidase and COMT turnover ratios, consistent with less efficient release and altered metabolism. The lipid-monoamine associations are correlational and they say so. The value here is mostly as a map — if you care about why different steroids produce different behavioral toxicity, this is the regional neurochemical substrate to argue over.

Two AI items to close. First, HealthBench-Psych, from the digital psychiatry group at Beth Israel Deaconess. HealthBench is OpenAI's five-thousand-conversation, physician-rubric-graded health benchmark, and it now shows up in model cards as the core of health testing at multiple labs. The problem is that it carries no specialty metadata — its tags describe conversational behavior, never medical topic — so a strong HealthBench number reads as competence in psychiatry with no way to check. The authors screened the corpus with a transparent rubric, then validated the subset through two rounds of blinded clinician review with concealed known-exclude controls and a pre-specified stopping rule on the control-inclusion rate. That control design is the methodological contribution, and it caught a first round that let through six percent of known negatives and triggered a recall pass. The result is 610 conversations, spanning perinatal mental health, anxiety, psychiatric medication, mood, sleep, ADHD, substance use, and suicidality.

Twenty models, three cross-vendor judges. The top five are statistically tied, scores between 0.610 and 0.627, and no separation survives multiple-comparison correction. Judges rank near-identically, Kendall tau above 0.92, but differ in severity — and once you correct for severity, apparent self-preference collapses to noise. Two models produced hard refusals: Claude Opus 5 on 1.6 percent of conversations, concentrated on psychiatric-medication and clinician-voiced questions, and Claude Fable 5 on three, all neurodegeneration content. Excluding refusals leaves the frontier unchanged. The useful takeaway is not the leaderboard, it is that the leaderboard *is* flat — psychiatric conversational competence is no longer where frontier models differentiate, and the remaining variance is in refusal behavior.

And to finish, a small ablation study from Pacific Northwest National Laboratory with an unfashionable conclusion. The task is verifiable: given a protein sequence, characterize its function by routing across standard tools. They varied the orchestration harness, the model, the prompt, and whether the agent ran monolithically or federated across institutional boundaries. Model choice dominated everything — Opus around 92 to 94 percent, o4-mini at 40 to 50. Federation cost almost nothing, between half a percentage point and seven, and expert prompting recovered most of that. But the finding worth carrying is the reinforcement-learning baseline. A PPO policy hit 88 percent, at zero token cost, fastest latency, and a consistency score of exactly 1.000 — not a single protein where five rollouts disagreed. The frontier LLMs beat it on accuracy by four to six points, at six to ten times the cost per protein and with imperfect consistency. What the PPO policy cannot give you is a reasoning trace. So the recommendation is a division of labor rather than a winner: for routine verifiable routing, a cheap deterministic policy buys you near-frontier accuracy and perfect reproducibility; save the flexible reasoning for the open-ended part, where you actually need to read what the agent thought.
