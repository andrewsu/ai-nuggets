## Script

Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Saturday, October seventeenth. Ten items today, and unusually for this show they are weighted toward preprints rather than journals — bioRxiv had a strong week in molecular pharmacology, and the Nature-family psychedelics papers that would otherwise have led the episode are behind a paywall I could not get through, so they will have to wait.

Here is the thread running through today's set. Over and over, the interesting result is not that a target was hit but that hitting it recruited something else — a second signalling system, a vascular response, a compensatory channel — and that second thing is where the leverage or the confound actually lives. Pharmacology as a story about what the drug does to the rest of the system, not what it does to its receptor.

We start with the item that would change a clinical development plan, from John Wood's group at the Wolfson Institute at University College London. The background is one of the better-known disappointments in analgesic discovery. Humans and mice who lose functional Nav1.7 in utero grow into adults who feel essentially no pain and are otherwise normal, which made Nav1.7 look like the cleanest analgesic target anyone had ever been handed. Then the selective blockers arrived and were not usable — Nav1.7 is broadly expressed in central, sensory and autonomic neurons, and blocking it acutely produces life-threatening autonomic side effects. The field never really resolved why the genetic knockout is benign and the pharmacological block is not.

This paper goes after the compensation. In sensory neurons from embryonic Nav1.7 nulls, Nav1.1 takes over the excitability that Nav1.7 vacated — antisense oligonucleotides against Nav1.1 reduce excitability in those neurons specifically. But the analgesia itself turns out to be mostly opioid. Two mechanisms converge. Enkephalin levels rise, linked to the lowered intracellular sodium in the null neurons. And the proteins that terminate G-protein signalling, RGS4 and RGS8, are downregulated — RGS8 by about four and a half fold — so whatever opioid tone exists is not being switched off on schedule. A smaller, naloxone-resistant residue of the analgesia tracks with upregulation of several potassium channels already implicated in pain control.

The translational move is the part worth your attention. If Nav1.7-null analgesia is really potentiated endogenous opioid signalling plus delayed G-protein shutoff, you do not need a Nav1.7 blocker at all — you need to recreate the downstream state on top of a drug you already have. So they took suzetrigine, the selective allosteric Nav1.8 blocker approved in early twenty twenty-five, and combined it with CCG-50014, a covalent RGS4 inhibitor, given intrathecally, with and without a dose of morphine well below the antinociceptive range in mice. In formalin-evoked and CFA inflammatory pain, suzetrigine alone was relatively weak — expected, because suzetrigine is about a thousand-fold less potent at the mouse channel than the human one, a species difference the authors trace to a handful of residues flanking the binding site in the domain two S3-S4 loop. Blocking RGS activity potentiated it, and adding the sub-analgesic morphine potentiated it further.

Two honest limits. CCG-50014 needs intrathecal delivery, so an orally active congener is the obvious missing piece, and the authors say so. And the von Frey data are instructive: suzetrigine raised mechanical thresholds but neither CCG-50014 nor morphine added anything there, so the synergy is specific to the inflammatory and nocifensive readouts rather than general. But as a strategy — stop trying to block the channel, reconstruct what losing the channel does downstream — this is the most interesting thing I have read in analgesia this year.

Second, and staying with allosteric pharmacology, a large collaboration out of Shanghai Jiao Tong, Fudan and Beijing Normal has made a dietary amino acid into a receptor ligand with a structure to back it up. Branched-chain amino acid metabolism has been circumstantially linked to neurodevelopmental disorders for years, largely through loss-of-function mutations in BCKDK, the kinase that restrains branched-chain ketoacid dehydrogenase. What nobody had shown is that a specific amino acid directly modulates a specific cortical receptor to control a specific behaviour.

They show that. Fifty percent dietary valine restriction disrupts sociability in mice; comparable leucine or isoleucine restriction does not. Deleting Bckdk only in medial prefrontal cortex, which accelerates local branched-chain catabolism, reproduces the social deficit with normal locomotion and normal anxiety-like behaviour, and local valine infusion thirty minutes before testing rescues it. Then the mechanism: limited proteolysis mass spectrometry, surface plasmon resonance showing nanomolar binding, and cryo-EM converge on mGluR5, with a valine-selective allosteric pocket distinct from the glutamate site.

The signalling profile is what makes this more than a curiosity. Valine is biased. In a beta-arrestin recruitment assay it drives output, but it has little standalone efficacy at the Gq arm measured through TRPC3 currents. What it does is potentiate glutamate-evoked mGluR5 signalling — so it behaves as an endogenous positive allosteric modulator whose concentration is set by diet and blood-brain-barrier transport rather than by synaptic release. Downstream, that potentiation recruits endocannabinoid feedback onto CB1 receptors, which gates inhibitory transmission and preserves the excitation-inhibition balance that prefrontal social behaviour depends on. If you have followed the long and mostly unhappy history of mGluR5 modulators in autism and fragile X, the thought that there is a nutritionally-set endogenous modulator sitting at a previously unmapped pocket on that receptor should rearrange some assumptions.

Third item, also receptor pharmacology with a translational edge, and a nice reminder that an antagonist can be the neuroprotective agent. A group working on traumatic brain injury had previously found the 5-HT1A antagonist WAY-100635 in a screen of human stem cell-derived retinal ganglion cells, where it promotes survival through cyclic-AMP-dependent mitochondrial biogenesis. Here they ask whether that extends to brain injury in vivo. Mice got a unilateral controlled cortical impact over left visual cortex, then daily intraperitoneal WAY-100635 at five milligrams per kilogram starting one hour after injury and continuing six weeks.

The injury produces bilateral retinal ganglion cell loss, worse in the eye contralateral to the lesion, which fits the predominantly crossed murine visual projection. Treatment preserved those cells and their function, improved visual acuity and contrast sensitivity on the optomotor response, preserved visually evoked cortical potentials, and on diffusion MRI selectively normalised fractional anisotropy and radial kurtosis in the optic tract ipsilateral to the cortical injury. In perilesional cortex it preserved neurons and synaptophysin immunoreactivity while sharply reducing microglial and astrocytic activation, and there was less of the injury-associated increase in open-arm exploration on the elevated plus maze — the group's proxy for behavioural disinhibition.

The honest result is the one they report rather than bury: cortical cavity volume was not significantly reduced. The drug is not shrinking the lesion. It is protecting the circuitry around and connected to the lesion, which is the thing that actually determines chronic visual and behavioural outcome. Group sizes are small — five to eight per injury group — and it is male mice only, so treat the effect sizes as provisional. But a repurposable, well-characterised 5-HT1A antagonist with a mitochondrial mechanism and a six-week post-injury dosing window is a serious translational proposition.

Fourth, a methodological paper that I think quietly complicates a decade of psychedelic neuroimaging, from Tagliazucchi's group with Carhart-Harris and Timmermann among the authors. The premise is simple and a little uncomfortable. Functional MRI measures blood oxygenation, not neural activity, and we infer one from the other through an assumed hemodynamic response. Serotonergic psychedelics act at receptors — 5-HT2A among them — that are expressed on vasculature as well as neurons. So if these drugs change the hemodynamic response itself, every BOLD result in the literature is reporting a mixture of neural and vascular effects.

They tested it on existing LSD and DMT datasets — seventy-five micrograms of LSD in fifteen subjects, twenty milligrams of DMT, both placebo-controlled crossovers — using blind deconvolution to estimate the hemodynamic response shape voxel by voxel. Both drugs increased the estimated time-to-peak, LSD mainly in primary and extrastriate visual cortex, DMT more widely. The spatial pattern of those delays correlated with receptor density maps — with 5-HT2A under LSD and 5-HT1B under DMT — though the correlations are modest, around zero point two, so take the receptor attribution as suggestive rather than settled.

Now the part that matters for interpretation, and it is more reassuring than the setup implies. When they deconvolved the vascular contribution out and recomputed functional connectivity, the drug-induced connectivity changes got stronger, not weaker. The vascular effect was masking the neural signal rather than manufacturing it. So the published psychedelic connectivity literature is probably not spurious — it is probably conservative. A biophysical whole-brain model attributes the delay to increased flow-dependent autoregulatory feedback and slower dissipation of the vasoactive drive. The practical upshot for anyone designing a psychedelic imaging study: the drug is doing something to your haemodynamics, and not correcting for it costs you sensitivity.

Fifth, and pivoting toward the computational half of the episode, a drug repurposing framework from Bin Chen's group at Michigan State that inverts the usual approach. Most transcriptomic repurposing matches a disease signature against cell-line perturbation data — LINCS and its descendants. The problem is that a cell line cannot tell you what a drug does systemically, and for a lot of modern drugs the systemic response is the mechanism.

So they dosed rodents and generated multi-organ RNA sequencing — thirty-five drug-perturbed tissue signatures — then matched those tissue-by-tissue against a reference atlas of nine hundred seventy-nine human disease signatures spanning three hundred fifty-three diseases and forty-eight tissues, harmonised using an agentic AI pipeline over GEO metadata. The matching statistic is a Z-transformed reversal of gene expression score against an empirical null of size-matched random signatures.

They validated on semaglutide, which is a good choice precisely because its indication expansion already happened in public. The framework recovered the established indications. More interestingly, it deprioritised Alzheimer's disease — the indication where semaglutide actually failed in trials, and which a naive hype-following method would have ranked highly. Top predictions among common diseases were checked against Mount Sinai electronic health records, and the screen reached a hundred and eight diseases including rare conditions where no EHR-based approach would be powered at all. That last point is the real argument for the method: it is a way to get repurposing signal for compounds that have no real-world evidence yet, which is exactly when you need it.

Sixth, on the evaluation side, OmicsBench, out of Michigan and AfterQuery, and I think it is the most useful agentic-bioinformatics benchmark I have seen. The design insight is about shape rather than difficulty. Most bioinformatics benchmarks pose an analysis as one prompt and grade what comes back. That is not how anyone actually uses these systems — you delegate a chunk, review it, ask for the next chunk, and each chunk depends on the last. So OmicsBench structures each of its ninety-seven tasks as a directed acyclic graph of dependent subtasks — six hundred sixty-two in all — and poses each subtask as a separate turn of a single session, graded deterministically, optionally with feedback on a wrong answer and a chance to retry.

The headline is that nobody is close to saturating it. Across twelve models on three harnesses, scores run from zero point four three to zero point five seven without feedback, rising to between zero point five four and zero point six seven with it. The structural finding is the one I would act on: scores decline on later turns and on dependent subtasks. Every combination loses between zero point zero six and zero point one two by the second turn alone. Feedback recovers part of that, but not all. Whatever is going wrong is a context-and-dependency problem, not a knowledge problem, and single-shot benchmarks are structurally blind to it.

They also ran an unsupervised failure-mode discovery pipeline — describe, embed and cluster the failed attempts — which surfaced twelve bioinformatics-specific failure modes. The dominant one across almost every model is set-selection failure: the agent submits far more or far fewer results than the subtask asked for, between a quarter and seventy percent of assigned failures. One model stood apart, with a fifth of its failures being synthetic or fabricated output and another thirteen percent being no answer at all. The distinction matters for how you supervise: most of these agents filter badly, and some of them make things up.

Seventh, staying with reliability but at the chemistry layer, MolLean, from the National University of Singapore. The problem is specific and familiar: chemical chain-of-thought contains hallucinated structural claims — a functional group that is not in the molecule, a bond quietly lost, a property improvement asserted but never established — and these errors coexist happily with correct final answers, so you cannot catch them by grading the output.

The solution borrows from proof assistants. MolLean maintains an explicit molecular state, and a layered Lean-based kernel checks every transition against it. Transitions construct or modify molecules, readouts obtain information without changing them, and assertions test claims against those readouts. A typed witness protocol separates checks the kernel can do natively on the structure from claims that need an external measurement. Rejected actions leave the state unchanged, which is the key property — the model cannot reason forward from a false premise it just asserted.

The results, on eighty-eight hundred matched examples across twelve tasks with a twenty-seven-billion-parameter open model: whole-trace refinement beat direct chain-of-thought on all twelve, by between twelve and forty-eight percentage points. Atom and bond counting improved by around forty-seven points, which tells you how much of the baseline failure was bookkeeping. Retrosynthesis went from three percent to twenty-one — still bad, but a seven-fold improvement. And the forward-reaction result is diagnostic: one-pass trace execution actually underperformed plain chain-of-thought there, twenty-four percent against thirty-five, and only refinement rescued it to sixty-three. Formalising the reasoning is not what helps. Being told the formalisation failed, and getting to revise, is what helps.

Eighth, an agentic system aimed squarely at neuroscience: Braintree, from Ratan Murty's lab at Georgia Tech. The argument is that vision neuroscience now has all the pieces to run an experiment entirely in silico — image-computable encoding models that predict cortical responses, generative methods for controlled stimuli, agents that can reason over the literature — and nobody had assembled them into a closed loop. Braintree does: given a hypothesis, an orchestrator coordinates agents that read the literature, design an experiment, build the stimulus set, run it against encoding models fit to the Natural Scenes Dataset, analyse the responses, and design a stronger follow-up.

The evaluation is staged carefully, and I appreciate that they report the uncomfortable number. First, reproduction: Braintree recovered the reported finding in all ten published studies it was given, using the original stimuli. Second, replication: remove the original stimuli, make Braintree construct new ones testing the same claim, and it replicates eight of ten — with systematically smaller effects, mean zero point three two standardised units reproduced against zero point two eight replicated, in seven of ten studies. That attenuation is the single most interesting result in the paper, because it is the in-silico analogue of the effect-size shrinkage we see in real replication, and it arrives without any of the usual human explanations.

Third, and most useful going forward, discovery. Braintree designed experiments to find stimulus dimensions that drive two regions differently even when their response profiles look similar — the face-selective regions against each other, the scene-selective regions against each other — and then to find stimuli that discriminate between competing models of the same region. Experiments it discovered using only two models exposed disagreements across a much broader model set, which suggests it found genuine axes of model disagreement rather than overfitting to the pair. That is the real use case: not replacing the experimentalist, but generating the stimuli that would actually adjudicate between theories.

Ninth, the same idea with physical hardware in the loop. GENIE, from Lina Carlini's group at Stony Brook, closes the microscopy experiment loop: plain-language goal in, acquisition, analysis, and a statistically tested answer out, with nine specialist agents and — importantly — every action gated and executed in deterministic code rather than by the language model directly. There is a watcher agent that follows the run and a devil's-advocate agent that argues against the plan.

The demonstration is modest and that is to its credit: GENIE measured mitochondrial membrane potential in live cells and found it is spatially coherent, with a region's potential predicting its neighbours' out to a coherence length of twenty to thirty micrometres — about one cell diameter — after which the correlation falls into the null band. The humans then reproduced the analysis independently and got the same number, and the authors checked that the coherence survives removal of the measured temporal trend. A real if small biological result, obtained by a system that chose its own next acquisition. For anyone running a core facility, the architecture here — language model proposes, deterministic code disposes — is the pattern worth copying.

And to close, something lighter but genuinely elegant, from the Max Planck Institute for Software Systems. The problem SCRIBE addresses is that semantic feature norms — the Binder sixty-five-dimensional space and its relatives — are interpretable and brain-predictive but assume word meanings are fixed. They are not. A knife in "he cut himself with a knife" is not the knife in "he chopped vegetables with a knife." Hand-annotating every word in every context is impossible.

So they use an ensemble of four small open-source models to rate each word on the Binder dimensions in context. Two validations. Behaviourally, they collected context-dependent feature judgements from nine hundred twenty-one participants — as far as I know the first dataset of its kind — confirmed that human ratings do shift systematically with context, and found that SCRIBE captures seventy-five to eighty-one percent of the estimated human noise ceiling. Neurally, on fMRI from people listening to naturalistic stories, SCRIBE predicts responses in language-selective regions significantly better than non-contextual semantic representations, and variance partitioning shows it explains unique variance beyond independently defined syntactic features.

What I like here is the restraint. The contribution is not a bigger model; it is using models as an annotation instrument to extend a theory-grounded, interpretable feature space into a regime where human annotation cannot go, and then checking against humans that the instrument is calibrated. That is a good template for a lot of computational neuroscience that currently reaches for an uninterpretable embedding instead.

That is the episode. The common move, if you want one sentence: valine works by potentiating glutamate rather than by acting alone, suzetrigine works better when you stop the opioid system switching itself off, psychedelics change the vasculature as well as the neurons, and the agentic systems get better when something external tells them they were wrong. Context and feedback, all the way down. Back tomorrow.
