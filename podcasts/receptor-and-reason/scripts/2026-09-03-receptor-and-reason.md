## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. I'm your host, and this is your daily pass through CNS pharmacology, psychiatric and neurology drug discovery, and the computational machinery closing in on both. Eleven items today, and one thread runs through most of them: the proxy comes apart from the endpoint. A drug retained in the compartment but not engaging it. A transcriptional signature that reverses while cell function gets worse. A calcium peak that predicted plasticity last week and doesn't this week. If you fit exposure-response relationships for a living, today is a series of reminders about which variable is load-bearing.

We open with the cleanest piece of human pharmacology on the pile, from the Institute of Systems Neuroscience in Hamburg — Christian Büchel's group, which has spent two decades taking placebo analgesia apart with opioid antagonists. This time the target is the motivation-decision model of pain — the twenty-year-old and never directly tested idea that when a noxious stimulus arrives while you're pursuing something biologically important, the organism implicitly arbitrates, and if getting the thing matters more than protecting the tissue, pain gets turned down. Most of the supporting literature tested something adjacent: reward outcomes, distraction during a rewarded task, decision-making under threat of pain. Not the decision itself.

So they built a task that isolates it. Forty-nine participants got a seven-second heat stimulus, rated it, then saw a monetary offer and chose whether to accept — accepting meant enduring a second, physically identical stimulus, sometimes while squeezing a dynamometer at thirty percent of maximum. Declining dropped that second stimulus by thirty points on the visual analog scale. So you get identical fifty-point stimuli delivered after an accept versus after a decline. Everyone did the whole thing twice, once under saline and once under intravenous naloxone — a bolus of point one five milligrams per kilogram then a continuous infusion — with at least forty-eight hours of washout. Preregistered.

Pain after accepting was lower than pain after declining — a difference of five and a half points on the scale under saline, dropping to three points under naloxone, with a significant decision-by-drug interaction. In the imaging, accepting produced larger responses during pain in pregenual and ventral perigenual anterior cingulate, and stronger coupling between pregenual cingulate and periaqueductal gray. Under naloxone, both of those vanished. The descending pain modulatory system was being recruited by the decision, and blocking opioid receptors took the recruitment away.

Three things make this better than the usual version of this study. They ran a separate experiment stripping out the decision and just presenting reward, loss, or neutral cues at one hundred percent outcome probability: reward anticipation alone did nothing to pain, Bayes factor of thirty-eight favoring the no-effect model. So this isn't reward-cue hypoalgesia relabeled. They checked the carry-over confound properly and found nothing. And they're honest that naloxone halved rather than abolished the behavioral effect, naming endocannabinoids as the candidate for the remainder. What I'd flag is the ceiling they hit: acceptance rates were so high they couldn't estimate whether the analgesia scales with offer magnitude — a preregistered hypothesis, and the thing you'd most want to know if you were trying to dose this mechanism.

Staying with mu opioid receptors but moving to the other side of the receptor's ledger, a preprint from the intramural program at NIDA and the University of Maryland goes after the aversiveness of withdrawal — the part of opioid use disorder that treatment mostly fails to touch. Naloxone-precipitated withdrawal in morphine-dependent mice lights up the capsular central amygdala and, just as strongly, a neglected patch of ventral posterior striatum called the interstitial nucleus of the posterior limb of the anterior commissure. Nobody knew what drives either.

The answer for the amygdala is the parabrachial nucleus. Withdrawal induced c-fos messenger RNA specifically in the external lateral subdivision — and they measured messenger RNA rather than protein deliberately, because morphine alone induces c-Fos protein there and would have contaminated the signal. In that subdivision, eighty-one percent of the withdrawal-activated cells co-express mu receptor and calcitonin gene-related peptide. Chemogenetic silencing of glutamatergic parabrachial neurons nearly abolished capsular amygdala activation and left the posterior striatal activation untouched — so the two hubs have separate drivers.

The behavioral dissociation is the useful part. Silencing the broad glutamatergic population strongly reduced jumping, the somatic sign, and only modestly reduced conditioned place avoidance. Silencing the CGRP or mu-receptor subpopulations that project to the amygdala reduced place avoidance without touching jumping at all. And then the result that earns the paper: silencing parabrachial glutamate neurons and the posterior striatal region simultaneously reduced place avoidance far more than either alone. Not additive — cooperative, which implies the aversion is carried redundantly by two parallel routes, and that a drug hitting one of them will look disappointing in the clinic for reasons that have nothing to do with target engagement. Caveat: males only, because place avoidance was more robust in males, and the authors say so up front.

From aversion to the plasticity that encodes it. A short, clean preprint from a lab at the University of Maryland Baltimore County asks a question that should have been asked years ago: does GABA-B receptor signaling control the sign of activity-dependent plasticity, not just the level of basal excitatory drive? At hippocampus-to-nucleus-accumbens synapses, high-frequency stimulation normally gives long-term potentiation. Pre-treat the slice with the GABA-B antagonist S-C-H 50911 and in females that potentiation flips to long-term depression. In males, nothing changes.

They then dissect it. The CB1 antagonist A-M 251 blocks the depression selectively in D1 medium spiny neurons, matching paired-pulse data pointing to a presynaptic locus there. The mGluR5 antagonist M-P-E-P abolishes it in both cell types. And blocking estrogen receptor alpha with M-P-P restores normal potentiation — so what GABA-B receptors hold in check is an estrogen-receptor-alpha and mGluR5-dependent depression mechanism that isn't unmaskable in males. Washing on the antagonist alone changed neither current amplitude nor paired-pulse ratio, so this is gating plasticity, not shifting baseline. The stated limitation is that estrous cycle wasn't tracked, and since the whole mechanism runs through estrogen receptor alpha, that's the next experiment. Still: baclofen and the GABA-B modulators in development for substance use disorder are being evaluated in designs that would not detect a sex-specific reversal in the direction of reward-circuit plasticity.

Plasticity in the other direction now, and from a therapy that's already in clinics. Evelyn Lambe's group at the University of Toronto, with collaborators at CAMH, took the clinical intermittent theta-burst protocol — six hundred stimuli, about three minutes, the pattern that replaced conventional repetitive TMS on the strength of the THREE-D trial — and asked what it actually does to prefrontal synapses, using ex vivo electrical stimulation and widefield neuronal calcium imaging in mouse slices. In group-housed adults, it reliably potentiates the amplitude and spatial spread of test-pulse-evoked calcium signals, and the size of the calcium elevation during induction predicts how much potentiation you get.

In socially isolated mice — a chronic stress model with reasonable face validity for the social withdrawal of depression — potentiation becomes unreliable. And here's the finding: the induction calcium peaks go higher, not lower, and they stop predicting the outcome. More drive, less plasticity, and the variable you were titrating on is no longer informative. So they went the counterintuitive direction: fewer stimuli spaced further apart, ninety stimuli over about ten minutes. That spaced protocol constrains the calcium excursion and produces greater potentiation than the clinical pattern in isolated animals, whether isolation started in adolescence or adulthood. Caveats: this is electrical stimulation in a slice, not a magnetic field through a skull, and the endpoint is synaptic rather than affective. But "more pulses in less time" has been the direction of travel in accelerated protocols for a decade, and this is a mechanistic argument for the opposite.

Now a computational chemistry paper that I think is genuinely useful bench pharmacology, from a group at the University of the Basque Country and the Donostia International Physics Center. Ligand-directed acyl imidazole chemistry lets you covalently tag endogenous AMPA receptors in living neurons without disrupting function — it's the method behind imaging native receptor trafficking. It works, it was optimized empirically, and nobody knew why. They combined quantum mechanical calculations with molecular dynamics of the reagent bound to the GluA2 ligand-binding domain.

The puzzle is that lysine at physiological pH is protonated and therefore a poor nucleophile, so it shouldn't win. It does anyway: the rate-determining barrier for lysine acylation comes out at eleven point seven kilocalories per mole against seventeen point five for hydrolysis of the warhead — matching the experimental picture, where labeling has a two-to-four-hour half-life in living brain while the reagent's hydrolysis half-life is twenty-four hours. The protein microenvironment then lowers the lysine barrier further, to around ten point six, with arginine one seventy-two acting as an oxyanion stabilizer or aspartate one thirty-nine as an auxiliary base. The payoff is a mechanistic explanation for subunit selectivity: GluA1 carries a serine-to-alanine substitution at position one forty, and alanine can't form the double hydrogen bond that holds the reagent in productive geometry. That's why GluA1 doesn't label. They also note that arginine, aspartate and serine sit near the labeled lysine in GABA-A receptors, which is a testable prediction rather than a hand-wave.

Which brings us to delivery, and to the day's sharpest example of exposure not being engagement. A large collaboration led from Karolinska Institutet, with AstraZeneca's nucleic acid therapeutics group, ran a proper side-by-side comparison of four lipid conjugates on an identical antisense oligonucleotide — a fully phosphorothioated locked-nucleic-acid gapmer against the long noncoding RNA Malat1, chosen because it's expressed uniformly enough to compare organs. The lipids were cholesterol, palmitic acid, docosanoic acid, and eicosapentaenoic acid.

Subcutaneously, the fatty acid conjugates didn't redirect activity out of the liver the way the field hoped; they mostly made liver silencing better, ninety-two to ninety-five percent knockdown against eighty-five for the unconjugated parent, with palmitic acid adding real cardiac potency. But the CNS arm is the news, and as far as the authors can tell nobody had done this with lipid-conjugated oligonucleotides before. After intracerebroventricular dosing, the three fatty acid conjugates improved striatal silencing — roughly fifty to fifty-six percent of transcript remaining versus sixty-eight for unconjugated. Cholesterol suppressed functional delivery in every CNS region tested, despite the highest CNS retention of any conjugate. Light-sheet imaging explains it: the cholesterol conjugate coats the ventricular surfaces and the pia mater and never reaches parenchyma. And unconjugated oligonucleotide beat every conjugate in spinal cord — which, for an intrathecally dosed modality, is the compartment that matters most. Their conclusion is the right one: a lipid strategy that clearly helps in the periphery can be counterproductive in the CNS, because the constraint is the physicochemistry of the delivery compartment, not the molecule.

Same dissociation, different level, in a large preprint out of Evotec in Hamburg on Alzheimer's microglia. They integrated single-nucleus transcriptomics across the disease spectrum and pulled out a disease- and lipid-associated microglial population tied to genetic risk, pathology and cognitive decline. Then they needed a model, and rather than the usual inflammatory stimulation or transcription factor overexpression, they found that ferric ammonium citrate — iron loading — reproducibly drives induced-pluripotent-stem-cell-derived human microglia into a state that correlates well with the patient signature, complete with lipid droplets, lysosomal failure, and impaired amyloid-beta phagocytosis.

Then they screened fifty-four compounds across more than fifteen mechanisms for transcriptional reversal, and this is where it gets interesting. The strongest reversal scores belonged to SYK inhibitors, with BTK inhibitors close behind — and both reduced amyloid-beta phagocytosis. The signature reversed and the function got worse. Most compounds that lowered the disease-state score did so by pushing cells into a different disease-associated state. The compound that worked functionally was L-Y 2090314, an ATP-competitive glycogen synthase kinase 3 inhibitor with clinical history in oncology and mood disorders: it increased phagocytosis, reduced lipid droplets, restored lysosomal degradation, and induced what they call an EnLyso state, with vacuolar ATPase subunits and interleukin-ten up and the disease-associated regulators MITF and PPAR-gamma down. Note it did not return cells to the control state. It made a third state that happens to work.

And the authors are unwilling to claim the target. Two other GSK3 inhibitors, bio-acetoxime and tideglusib, failed to reproduce the phenotype. So either the effect needs an additional kinase, or ATP-competitive versus allosteric selectivity is doing something they can't separate yet. Two lessons: transcriptional reversal score is a screening metric, not an efficacy endpoint, and the goal in microglia may be reprogramming toward a competent state rather than reverting to a naive one.

Two computational items to close out the heavier material. First, the initial public release of OpenBind, from a consortium spanning Oxford's Centre for Medicines Discovery, Columbia, Memorial Sloan Kettering and others. It is, they believe, the largest public single-target experimental structure-affinity dataset: nine hundred twenty-five crystallographic binding events from six hundred ninety-nine compounds against enteroviral 2A protease, with affinities for six hundred one of them, spanning a fragment screen and its follow-on chemistry inside one coherent campaign. That coherence is the point — it's the data structure the field has been substituting proxies for. Their own benchmarking found what you'd expect from a real campaign rather than a curated test set: docking depends heavily on which pocket conformation you start from, pose ranking is hard, and structure-based affinity prediction remains poor. The constructive result is that fine-tuning OpenFold 3 on the fragment-screen structures substantially improved pose prediction and virtual screening for the follow-on series — so early crystallography has value as training data for its own campaign, not just as a picture of a hit.

Second, NS-Copilot from Case Western Reserve, an agentic system for neuroscience data analysis: nine pre-trained models spanning EEG and extracellular spike data behind one natural-language interface, with planner, controller and coder agents, evaluated on Alzheimer's and Parkinson's EEG classification and working-memory spike decoding, eight autonomous runs per task. It beats every baseline on the primary metric of all three benchmarks, including Claude Sonnet 4.5 and Codex writing their own analysis code. Fine. What makes it worth your time is the ablation, which is more honest than most papers in this genre. Strip out the pre-trained models and leave only the orchestration, and it scores below single-shot Codex — forty-nine point eight against fifty-one on the Alzheimer's task, sixty-four point five against sixty-five point five on Parkinson's. Orchestration alone buys nothing. The gain is entirely the model library and the per-task selection, and the size of that gain swings from thirteen points on one dataset to a near-tie on another, which is itself the argument for automating the selection. And the controller's forced retry rounds average slightly negative on Alzheimer's and about two and a third points negative on Parkinson's; its whole value is keeping the best round rather than the last. Twenty runs improved, twenty degraded. If you're building one of these, that's the number to internalize: iterative self-refinement is a search you pay for, and the selection rule is the product.

Two shorter items to finish, both about the gap between concentration and effect. A modeling paper from Central University of Haryana builds a mechanistic HPA-axis model that couples hormone kinetics to the functional mass of the corticotroph and adrenal compartments, adds a time-varying glucocorticoid receptor resistance term, and simulates a hundred-and-eighty-day arc of baseline, chronic stress and recovery — then runs virtual ACTH stimulation tests at each phase. During chronic stress, the cortisol response to a high-dose challenge is blunted even though the adrenal is hypertrophied, because endogenous drive is already high and exogenous ACTH adds little. During recovery, responsiveness lags removal of the stressor by weeks, because gland mass and receptor sensitivity regress slowly. Their claim is that the low-dose one-microgram test tracks partial adaptation better than the standard two-hundred-fifty-microgram test, which risks masking dysfunction through supraphysiological drive. Be clear what this is: a plausible simulation with no parameter fitting to human data and no validation cohort, so the dosing recommendation is a hypothesis, not evidence. But for anyone reading cortisol dynamics in depression, where HPA abnormalities have been reported and unreplicated for forty years, the structural argument — that a static provocation test is querying a system with slow-moving state variables — is worth carrying.

And finally, the lightest item, from a group at the Université de Montréal doing something slightly eccentric with real payoff: gabapentinoid pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics in Caenorhabditis elegans. They paired thermal nociception assays with direct measurement of internal drug concentration for gabapentin and pregabalin. Both produce dose- and time-dependent reductions in thermal avoidance with no change in baseline mobility, so this isn't sedation in disguise. At five hundred micromolar the time course is V-shaped — suppression deepens, then partially reverses, later with pregabalin than gabapentin. And internal concentrations stay elevated through three hundred sixty minutes while the behavior recovers. So the recovery limb is not clearance. Something is actively counter-regulating, which fits the transcriptional and proteomic signatures they reported previously. The exposure-response curves are also notably flat, suggesting a saturable pharmacodynamic ceiling — which, for two drugs whose real-world dose escalation routinely outruns their benefit, is a mechanistic statement worth someone's attention. It's a worm, and nobody should over-read it. But the general point survives the model: if internal exposure is flat and effect is recovering, the interesting biology is in the adaptation, and no amount of pharmacokinetic modeling will find it for you.

That's the day. If you carry one thing: nearly every item here has a measurable proxy that behaved well until the system changed state — retention, reversal score, calcium peak, cortisol response, internal concentration, benchmark number — and in every case the fix was to go find the functional endpoint.
