## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. It's Monday, July twenty-seventh, twenty twenty-six — nine items today, and it shakes out as a drugs-and-mechanisms day. We open on two takes on antidepressant action, one brand new and one hiding in a drug we already prescribe. From there we go to the machinery that sets a drug's efficacy in the first place, then to designing molecules and nominating targets computationally. We swing into a stress-resilience circuit, then two honest methods cautions from neuroimaging in ketamine and schizophrenia, and we close on a natural-product compound that helps most exactly when the task is hard.

We open with a genuinely new angle on treating depression, out of Autobahn Therapeutics. The compound is elunetirom, and the idea rests on a receptor we don't usually talk about in mood disorders — thyroid hormone receptor beta. Thyroid hormone signaling in the brain supports neuroplasticity and cellular energetics, and non-selective thyroid hormone has long thrown off antidepressant hints — but you can't give it chronically without hitting the heart, bone, and metabolism. So the trick here is a brain-targeted prodrug: elunetirom is cleaved by fatty acid amide hydrolase to release the active thyroid receptor agonist, called LL-340001, preferentially in the brain. In primary cortical and hippocampal neurons the active compound grew neurites and branched them, and within twenty-four hours it increased synapse number, an effect that persisted out to seventy-two hours, and it protected synapses against an amyloid-beta challenge. It also pushed up nuclear PGC-1-alpha and NRF2, the number of functional mitochondria, and ATP content. The mechanistic tell is the part to remember: the neuronal growth effect was blocked by a TrkB antagonist but not by a 5-HT2A antagonist. So this is a BDNF-TrkB-flavored plasticity program — mechanistically distinct from the 5-HT2A route that psychedelics and psychoplastogens lean on. What to like: a differentiated mechanism with a plausible brain-selectivity trick, and translational heavyweights like Stephen Stahl and Roger McIntyre on the author line signal a serious push. What to temper: this is entirely primary-culture pharmacology — no behavior, no in vivo depression model in this paper — and it's an industry preprint. Still, a new kind of plastogen is exactly what the field keeps asking for.

Stay with antidepressants, but flip to a hidden mechanism inside one we already hand out. Duloxetine, the serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor, turns out to do something nobody was looking for: it broadly disrupts RNA-protein interactions. The starting observation is that many small-molecule drugs accumulate inside cells — our own cells and gut microbes alike — and the molecular consequences of that accumulation are mostly a blank. Using RNA-binding profiling across a range of bacteria and human intestinal Caco-2 cells, they find duloxetine reduces RNA-binding capacity for roughly eighty percent of RNA-binding proteins in both systems. As a worked example, it interferes with PyrB, an enzyme in pyrimidine biosynthesis, and its interactions with the messenger RNAs it regulates. And the structural mechanism is clean and a little startling: duloxetine competes with aspartate, PyrB's natural substrate, and by occupying that site it weakens the enzyme's grip on stem-loop structures in the three-prime untranslated regions of its RNA partners. The upshot is that a widely used drug has an off-target mode of action operating at the level of RNA-protein recognition — with implications for variability in efficacy, for toxicity, and for how these drugs reshape the microbiome. It's a good reminder that "off-target" can mean something far stranger than just a second receptor.

From what drugs do to the machinery that decides how much they do it. A biophysics preprint takes on a question structural biology has mostly sidestepped: why do two agonists at the same receptor produce different signaling efficacy? Cryo-EM gives us beautiful static snapshots of fully activated GPCR-G-protein complexes, but efficacy is about dynamics — how the G protein actually moves. Using fluorine-nineteen quantitative NMR, which is exquisitely sensitive to a nucleus's local environment, on the stimulatory G-alpha-s subunit with the adenosine A2A receptor as the model GPCR, they build a three-state conformational model of G-alpha-s and then measure how different receptor-ligand complexes populate those three states — and, crucially, how fast the protein cycles between them. The finding is that agonists don't just shift the equilibrium among G-protein conformations; they impose distinct cycling kinetics across the substates. So efficacy isn't one number pinned to a single "active" structure — it's a signature of both equilibrium and transition rates. For anyone doing structure-based design of partial or biased agonists, that's the useful reframing: you may need to design for kinetics, not just for a pose.

That's a natural segue into building molecules. A single-author methods preprint out of Stavanger asks a very practical question in generative chemistry: can you steer a pretrained structure-based diffusion model toward the shape of a known active molecule while preserving a specific chemical warhead you care about — say, a covalent handle? Fragment-growth models tend to trap themselves early with autoregressive decisions and stay blind to the final target shape. The paper adds geometric guidance at inference time. Applied naively, in one shot, that shape guidance did increase how much of the target volume got covered — but it mostly produced disconnected fragments, which are chemically useless. The fix was to reformulate the job as smaller, staged scaffold expansions, with target-directed selection, adaptive growth increments, and a beam search over endpoints, plus a soft-scaffold-inpainting step that grades how much the inherited scaffold is allowed to move while the warhead stays fixed. It's incremental, and performance still hinges on how geometrically compatible the two ligands are — but it's a clean recipe for controllable, warhead-preserving molecular growth, which is exactly the kind of constraint real medicinal chemists actually work under.

Next, a computational swing at neurodegeneration. Rather than treat Alzheimer's, Parkinson's, and Huntington's as three separate problems, this preprint hunts for what they share at the transcriptome level. Pooling patient expression profiles across the three, they land on two hundred seventy-four commonly dysregulated genes and, more interestingly, three shared upregulated pathways — two of them converging on NF-kappa-B, the inflammatory signaling hub, and a third involving RUNX3 regulation of BCL2L11 that's been tied to the odd inverse relationship between neurodegeneration and cancer. From the shared hits they nominate five drug targets — the NF-kappa-B cast, NFKBIA, NFKB1, and RELA, plus TRIM4 and SMAD4 — and then pull FDA-approved drugs that modulate those genes for repurposing. The candidate list is reassuringly boring, in a good way: statins, antihypertensives, antidiabetics, some analgesics and diuretics. The value here isn't a shiny new target; it's the cross-disease framing that says a shared neuroinflammatory axis might be druggable with molecules we already trust. It is in-silico only, and the honest caveat is that shared differential expression in end-stage tissue can reflect consequence as much as cause — but as a hypothesis generator for repurposing, it's a sensible starting map.

Now to circuits and stress resilience. Why do some individuals adapt to chronic stress while others lock into a rigid, non-responsive phenotype? This study pairs machine-learning-based deep behavioral phenotyping with whole-brain imaging — longitudinal manganese-enhanced MRI plus cFOS activity mapping — in mice going through chronic social defeat. Two things stand out. First, active adaptation had a latent signature before the stress even began, sitting in baseline vigilance-like and locomotor kinetics — a pre-existing trait, not just a reaction to the challenge. Second, at the network level, the resilient responders showed a tightly integrated functional module co-clustering the periaqueductal gray, the ventral tegmental area, the basolateral amygdala, and the dorsal raphe, whereas the non-responders showed hyper-modular fragmentation — and specifically, functional connectivity along the dorsal-raphe-to-basolateral-amygdala axis was completely lost in the non-responsive animals. Then the causal test: chemogenetically silencing the basolateral-amygdala-projecting dorsal raphe neurons during a social challenge pushed otherwise-adaptive animals toward the non-responsive phenotype — more social avoidance, more anxiety-like behavior. So that serotonergic raphe-to-amygdala projection isn't incidental scenery; it's load-bearing for adaptive coping. It's a satisfying convergence of a network-level readout and a specific, manipulable pathway.

A methods caution now, and it's a good one. Subanaesthetic ketamine famously alters the content of consciousness while leaving you responsive — a favorite probe of psychedelic-like states, given ketamine's role as a non-competitive NMDA receptor antagonist. The question here: can you decode that state from a single eyes-closed EEG epoch, and which features carry the signal — spectral power, or phase-based functional connectivity? Re-analyzing an openly available sixty-two-channel dataset from ten volunteers, awake versus ketamine, under leave-one-subject-out cross-validation, band power decoded the ketamine state at a balanced accuracy around zero-point-seven-one, while the weighted phase-lag index connectivity was at chance, about zero-point-four-seven — and concatenating the two feature sets didn't beat power alone. The interesting part is why. They decomposed each feature's drug effect into a component shared across people and a subject-specific component. The connectivity effect was large within individuals but almost entirely subject-specific — a shared fraction of only about zero-point-zero-five — so it simply doesn't transfer to held-out subjects. The spectral effect was roughly half shared. And a five-channel lateral montage did as well as the full array. The lesson for anyone building connectivity biomarkers: a group-level connectivity difference can be perfectly real and still be useless for prediction, because its form isn't consistent across people. Power is boring, and it generalizes; fancy connectivity is individual, and it doesn't.

That pairs naturally with a resting-state fMRI methods paper in schizophrenia. Most schizophrenia connectivity work warps everyone to a standard volumetric template and a fixed atlas built from neurotypical brains. This group asks what you lose by doing that, comparing four preprocessing-and-parcellation strategies across two independent cohorts — about a hundred sixty and two hundred fifty-five subjects. Individualized surface-based parcellation — fitting the parcellation to each subject's own cortical folding — consistently detected more pronounced dynamics, in the form of quasi-periodic patterns and stronger default-mode-versus-dorsal-attention opposition, and it produced larger, more reproducible patient-control differences. In other words, forcing everyone into a common atlas smears out real individual organization and costs you sensitivity. But — and this is the honest half — the associations between the imaging measures and PANSS symptom severity did not replicate well across the two cohorts. So better preprocessing buys you cleaner case-control contrasts, and yet stable brain-symptom relationships in heterogeneous psychiatric samples stay stubbornly out of reach. Take both halves seriously: the method genuinely matters, and the ceiling on symptom prediction isn't only a method problem.

And we'll close on something lighter — a natural-product cognitive enhancer. FAE-twenty, ferulic acid eicosyl ester, is a constituent of the plant Rhodiola rosea that's turned up before as a booster of simple Pavlovian conditioning in flies, bees, and mice. Here the question was whether it does anything for the harder stuff — cognitive flexibility, working memory, spatial learning — in mice. The answer was selective in a telling way. Subchronic FAE-twenty clearly improved performance on an attentional set-shifting task — genuine cognitive flexibility — in both sexes of young adults. But it did nothing for spatial working memory in a Y-maze or spatial learning in a Barnes maze, with one exception: it rescued learning in a subgroup of aged mice who otherwise flatly failed the task. So the effect tracks task demand and baseline ability rather than lifting everything uniformly. And the mechanism sketch is charmingly sex-specific and not where you'd expect — no change in hippocampal neurogenesis or dendritic spine density, but instead modulation of the ascending arousal system: more activity in orexin neurons of the lateral hypothalamus in males, and reduced cholinergic activity in the laterodorsal tegmentum in females. A compound that helps most when the task is hard and the learner is struggling, apparently by tuning arousal rather than the hippocampus — a nice note to end on.
