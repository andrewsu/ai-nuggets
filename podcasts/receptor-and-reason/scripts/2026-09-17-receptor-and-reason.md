## Script

Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Thursday, September seventeenth. Twelve items today, and the through-line is selectivity — where it comes from, whether we can measure it, and how often the thing we think we are targeting is not the thing we are actually hitting.

We open with a cryo-EM paper that I think is the most useful piece of CNS structural pharmacology to land this week. The synaptic vesicle protein two family — SV2A, SV2B, and SV2C — are the targets of the racetam antiseizure drugs, and SV2A is where levetiracetam and brivaracetam do their work. SV2C is the odd one out: the evolutionarily oldest member, the least abundant, and expressed almost entirely in the dopaminergic basal ganglia, with genome-wide association signal pointing at Parkinson's disease. Deleting it in mice impairs dopamine release. Nobody has had a structure. This group now reports four: apo SV2C, SV2C bound to padsevonil — the high-affinity non-selective SV2 ligand that UCB took into epilepsy trials — SV2C bound to UCB-F, the one genuinely SV2C-selective tool compound in existence, and, separately, SV2A bound to plosaracetam, the SV2A-selective molecule currently in trials as a cognitive enhancer for dementia that conspicuously lacks antiseizure activity.

The mechanistic payoff is geometric. SV2C has a wider luminal opening than SV2A or SV2B, and that extra room is what lets UCB-F reach the primary binding site and pick up interactions that simply cannot form in the narrower pocket of its sisters. Selectivity here is not a different pocket; it is the same pocket with a different mouth. They also nail down why the SV2A allosteric modulator site is unavailable in SV2C — a phenylalanine sits where the modulator would go, and a glycine-to-asparagine substitution closes off one face of the site. Functionally, padsevonil and UCB-F both cut evoked dopamine release in mouse striatal slices, while plosaracetam did not, and dye-uptake experiments suggest the compounds act on vesicular release rather than on loading. The honest caveat, which the authors state plainly, is that the concentrations needed to see the dopamine effect in slices were orders of magnitude above the binding affinities — they attribute that to poor penetration of two-to-three-hour incubations into thick slices, which is plausible but unproven. Take this as a template for rational SV2C ligand design rather than as evidence that an SV2C drug will move dopamine in a living brain.

Staying with targets that are not where you would expect them, a bioRxiv preprint makes a real case for treating depression from the gut. The receptor is TGR5, the G-protein-coupled bile acid receptor, and the finding is that it sits on a GAD1-positive, GABAergic subpopulation of enteric neurons in the ileum. Chronic restraint stress suppresses ileal TGR5 and drives glycolytic reprogramming and mitochondrial distress in both ileum and hypothalamus. Oral agonism with INT-777 — a selective TGR5 agonist that has been around in the metabolic field for years — reverses the hypothalamic changes and the behavioural phenotype, and it does so through enhanced ileal mTOR and HIF-1-alpha signalling. The circuit claim is the part worth your attention: the effect is abolished by subdiaphragmatic vagotomy and by chemogenetic silencing of paraventricular GABAergic neurons, which puts the pathway through the vagus, into the nucleus tractus solitarius, and up to the hypothalamus. Two loss-of-function manipulations is a decent standard of proof for a gut-to-brain claim, which is a literature that badly needs one. The strategic appeal is obvious — a peripherally acting antidepressant that never has to cross the blood-brain barrier — but note we are still in restraint stress and forced-swim territory, and INT-777's own systemic bile-acid pharmacology is not nothing.

Then a clinical result I did not expect to be as clean as it is. A Lübeck group tested whether odour cueing during sleep can augment trauma-focused psychotherapy, using targeted memory reactivation — pairing a cue with a freshly updated memory and then re-presenting the cue during slow-wave sleep. They ran a mechanistic study and a trial. In twenty-eight healthy participants given a single session of imagery rescripting and reprocessing therapy, re-exposure to the therapy-associated odour during sleep raised slow-wave activity and slow oscillations, and — the part that matters — larger increases in slow-oscillation-spindle coupling predicted greater reduction in emotional arousal when the aversive autobiographical memory was recalled. Then thirty-eight patients with PTSD, severely affected, mostly childhood-onset trauma, got twelve weekly sessions of imagery rescripting with an odour presented in session and re-presented at home by diffuser, randomized to the congruent odour or a different one. Read the numbers carefully. Total clinician-rated PTSD severity did not separate significantly — the condition-by-time interaction came in at point zero seven seven. What did separate was the intrusion cluster, at point zero four five, with negative alterations in cognition and mood and hyperarousal at trend. And remission was faster and more complete: eighty-five percent versus fifty percent, with estimated time to remission of four and a half weeks against eight. Thirty-eight patients is small, the mechanism and the clinic were measured in different samples, and no electrophysiology was collected in the trial. But a home diffuser is about as scalable and cheap as an augmentation strategy gets, and the effect landed on exactly the symptom cluster that memory reconsolidation theory says it should.

The next item is another non-pharmacological intervention, and its interest is entirely in how the target was chosen. Nicotine dependence is roughly three times more prevalent in schizophrenia and is a major driver of early mortality, and prefrontal stimulation — which has FDA clearance for smoking cessation in the general population — has repeatedly underperformed in this group. So the question was whether a different circuit target does better. Sixty-three nicotine users, thirty-one with schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder and thirty-two without psychosis, in a randomized crossover comparing default-mode-network-targeted stimulation with dorsolateral prefrontal stimulation, with cue-reactivity imaging before and after. At baseline they recovered a cue-reactive network overlapping the default mode network — posterior cingulate, medial prefrontal, bilateral lateral occipital — and, interestingly, cue reactivity in schizophrenia did not differ from controls. After five sessions, default-mode-targeted stimulation reduced cue reactivity in the schizophrenia group and only in that group; prefrontal stimulation did nothing in either. Higher baseline unprovoked craving predicted a larger drop. The design has a real confound you should hold onto: the default-mode arm used inhibitory continuous theta-burst while the prefrontal arm used excitatory intermittent theta-burst, so target and polarity move together and the paper cannot fully separate them. It is also five sessions and a brain endpoint, not a smoking endpoint.

Sticking with theta-burst, a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover asked whether a ketone monoester drink primes stimulation-induced plasticity in motor cortex. Forty-four healthy young adults, stratified to intermittent or continuous theta-burst, drank ketone monoester or placebo, got stimulated an hour later, and had motor evoked potentials tracked for thirty minutes. The drink did what it should metabolically — beta-hydroxybutyrate up, glucose down, heart rate up, diastolic pressure down. It did nothing to resting motor threshold, baseline motor evoked potential amplitude, or latency, and the factorial analysis found no modification of the post-stimulation time course. The one exploratory signal ran the wrong way: placebo plus continuous theta-burst produced a significant late rise in motor evoked potential amplitude, and ketones blunted it. Serum mature and pro-BDNF did not budge. I am flagging this precisely because it is mostly null. Nutritional ketosis has accumulated a lot of neuroprotective enthusiasm on thin human plasticity data, and a well-powered crossover finding no priming effect — plus a hint of interference with an inhibitory protocol — is more informative than another positive pilot. The concurrent cardiovascular effects do make the dual neurological-cardiovascular framing worth pursuing on its own terms.

That peripheral physiology point generalizes, which brings us to a Molecular Psychiatry systematic review and meta-analysis of peripheral organ systems in first-episode, drug-naive mental illness. This is the design that matters: everything is measured before any psychotropic has been given, which is the confound that has haunted the metabolic-side-effect literature for thirty years. From twenty-six hundred citations, a hundred and thirty-eight studies, a hundred and five meta-analyzable, and the yield in schizophrenia is broad — raised triglycerides, insulin, insulin resistance, two-hour glucose, waist-to-hip ratio, systolic pressure, neutrophils, monocytes, total white cell count, interleukin-4, and reduced albumin. In depression, raised TNF-alpha and interleukin-10. Interleukin-6 and interferon-gamma up in both. The honest reading is two-sided. Multi-organ dysfunction genuinely predates treatment, so antipsychotics are not the whole story. But the evidence base is lopsided — eighty of the hundred and five studies are schizophrenia, twenty-six depression, four bipolar, and none generalized anxiety, and almost everything measured is immune or metabolic, with two liver studies and a single musculoskeletal one. Kidney and lung are effectively unstudied. That is a map of where the field has looked, not of where the biology is.

If that is how we measure the body, the next question is how we choose the dose, and a systems-biology preprint from the Craig lab introduces DiffDose, which reframes dose-regimen design as finite-dimensional optimal control and then just differentiates through it. The insight is that dose amounts and administration times can be treated as differentiable controls inside the same computational graph as the model, the discrete bolus events, the quadrature, and the objective — so you get gradients by automatic differentiation instead of hand-deriving adjoints and Jacobians for every new model, event structure, or cost function. Three demonstrations. It reproduces the published OptiDose dose-amplitude optima on standard PK-PD benchmarks with faster time to solution. It optimizes the timing, not just the size, of filgrastim across seven cycles of CHOP-14 chemotherapy in a state-dependent delay model of neutropenia — a setting where turnkey adjoints are not available at all — and converges on a robust day-eight window. And it scales to a two-hundred-fifty-member virtual population on a thirty-six-state quantitative systems pharmacology model of mosunetuzumab, the CD20-CD3 bispecific T-cell engager, optimizing six doses per patient against a joint objective of day-eighty-four tumour burden and peak interleukin-6 as a cytokine-release-syndrome surrogate. Eighty-three percent of virtual patients improved residual tumour, eighty percent reduced their worst interleukin-6 peak, sixty-six percent improved both, and a hundred and seventy received less total drug. The conceptual shift is the one the authors name: mechanistic models stop being calculators that score regimens you already thought of and become engines that propose them. The caveat is equally clear — the optimum is only as good as the model, and a virtual population is not a trial.

If models are going to propose things, someone has to grade them, and blinded physician review does not scale. A Harvard Medical School group — Rodman and Manrai, with Beth Israel and Stanford collaborators — releases GRAND-ROUNDS, ninety-two hundred scores from eleven physicians across seven studies, and PrecepTron, a thirty-two-billion-parameter model fine-tuned by low-rank adaptation on a small number of physician examples to grade open-ended clinical responses. Two findings. First, off-the-shelf frontier models used as judges are unreliable in a specific way: the best proprietary model matched physician inter-rater agreement on three of five tasks but fell short on two, no single model matched physicians across all five, and agreement on one task ranged from twenty-eight percent to sixty-eight percent depending on which frontier model you asked. The fine-tuned grader reaches physician-level consistency across tasks, and with it they reproduce the headline findings of five influential studies from JAMA, Science, and Nature Medicine without any new human grading. Second, and this is the part that should change how you read medical AI papers, a scalable grader lets you feed cases in piecemeal. Giving models incrementally longer aliquots of case text, a frontier model solved forty-two percent of post-cutoff New England Journal clinicopathological conferences from fifty tokens or fewer — about forty words — when credited a top-ten differential, falling to twelve percent for a top-one diagnosis. On held-out management cases it reached at least eighty percent of the rubric maximum on four of five questions within the first fifty tokens, three of them within ten. The typical case presentation is sixteen hundred tokens. A benchmark that can be solved from the first sentence is measuring something other than clinical reasoning.

That brings us to three items about what our models are actually learning. The first is an EEG foundation model with an unusually clean argument about objectives. MANAS-2 pairs a hybrid autoencoder that reconstructs both raw temporal patches and compact spectral-band targets with what the authors call constrained reconstruction — a physics-motivated regularizer that penalizes differences in root-mean-square energy between adjacent short windows of the reconstructed waveform. It touches only the temporal decoder output, never the spectral branch. Across seven held-out datasets, adding it lifts frozen ridge recovery of six-band spectral power from an R-squared of point eight-six to point nine-one, and recovery of inter-patch band-energy dynamics from point two-eight to point three-five, while waveform information stays highly recoverable. The general lesson is the interesting one: you can strengthen spectral organization in the encoder through a local energy constraint in the time domain, without adding an explicit spectral target. Masked reconstruction on low-signal-to-noise waveforms optimizes the wrong thing, and the fix can live in the constraint rather than the objective.

Second, a bolder claim in the other direction — that cortical organization is a usable design prior. The Platonic brain bridge hypothesis holds that omni models, the multimodal systems that take video, audio, and text jointly, converge on brain-like representations that are usable in both directions. Model-to-brain, brain-likeness across seven omni models is stable across participants, rises with each added input channel, and their encoders top the out-of-distribution Algonauts twenty-twenty-five leaderboard. Brain-to-model is the novel half: they fix the expert partition of a frozen mixture-of-experts base to the seven canonical functional networks of human cortex, train those experts on questions labelled by the network most engaged by the source clip, and get held-out accuracy gains in all fifteen model-benchmark pairs, averaging six point four percentage points, beating capacity-matched random experts in fourteen of fifteen. Crucially, what is injected is an organizational principle, not brain data — the prior reaches the routing gate through a read-only path you can shuffle or switch off, and validation uses public benchmarks unrelated to the recordings. The honest limits: the prior is defined at seven-network resolution, finer parcellations are untested, and the effect on benchmark accuracy localized to brain-aligned features on only one of three base models.

Third, a corrective from a pharmacology and toxicology group at UT Austin, and it is a genuinely useful negative result. Super-weight work in language models has established that a handful of high-gain parameters can be catastrophically important. This paper asks whether structural extremeness is a transferable mechanism, across text and genomic foundation models, with a twenty-two-model frozen causal census and exact computation of the associated bilinear operator's magnitude and spectral concentration — no diagonal approximation. Activation-derived candidates were functionally enriched relative to both random and top-norm same-layer controls. But neither spectral concentration nor operator magnitude predicted causal effect size, and within the homogeneous text-decoder subset the structural associations vanished entirely. A sweep of thirty-six rows across the full activation-ratio range resolved the enrichment into two regimes: below the detector's threshold the ratio carries no positive information about causal damage, and above it the ratio orders rows but does not grade severity as a dose-response. Two further findings should worry anyone running a one-candidate-per-model audit. A single layer can contain a second, individually catastrophic row that such a census cannot see, and the damage from co-located critical rows is not additive. In one genomic masked encoder they found a robust super-additive pair interaction; in a genomic generative decoder, a sharply position-localized dependence where restoring just the high-gain row's beginning-of-sequence contribution rescued essentially all the damage. Enrichment is general; mechanism is model-specific. Structural prominence is a screening signal, not a measure of importance.

We close on a small patch-clamp paper with an outsized implication for anyone who runs an NMDA-hypofunction model. Dizocilpine — MK-801 — is the workhorse non-competitive NMDA receptor antagonist behind a large fraction of the schizophrenia modelling literature, and the story we tell about it is that it produces a cortical phenotype by disinhibiting parvalbumin-positive fast-spiking interneurons. Those interneurons depend on Kv3.1, the voltage-gated potassium channel that makes their rapid repolarization and high-frequency firing possible, and reduced Kv3.1 has itself been reported in schizophrenia. So this group put MK-801 on Kv3.1 expressed in CHO cells and measured it directly. It blocks the channel, with a half-maximal inhibitory concentration of about eleven micromolar and a Hill coefficient of point eight-nine. The block is stronger at depolarized potentials, it is use-dependent — progressive inhibition with repeated stimulation at one and two hertz, consistent with delayed recovery from inactivation — and it shifts steady-state inactivation in the hyperpolarizing direction. So concentration-, voltage-, and state-dependent, which is a real pharmacological block and not a nonspecific effect. I should say this one is still abstract-only on bioRxiv, so I have not read the figures. But the inference is direct enough to state: if your tool compound blocks the very channel that sustains fast-spiking interneuron output, then in the exact cell type your mechanistic story runs through, you have two mechanisms, not one — and the concentration at which you dose in vivo decides how much of each you are getting.

That is Receptor and Reason for today. A structure that explains selectivity by the shape of a doorway, an antidepressant target in the ileum, an odour that makes psychotherapy stick, a ketone drink that did not do what it was supposed to, and a workhorse antagonist with a second address.
