## Script

Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Monday, October nineteenth. Eleven items, and for once both preprint servers and the Nature family were readable on the same morning, so today's episode has the shape of the literature rather than the shape of my access.

The thread is addresses. Over and over the result that matters is not how much of something you give, but where, when, and against which partner it lands — a seven-day window, one protein interaction out of six, one and a half percent of the spikes, a drug metabolite with no affinity for the target it is prescribed against.

We start with the item most likely to change how someone thinks about treatment timing, in Nature Neuroscience today. The lateral habenula is the structure everyone reaches for when they want a cellular story about depression, and the standard version is static: chronic stress raises habenula activity, antidepressants lower it. This group asked when instead. They gave mice corticosterone in the drinking water and sampled the same structure at baseline and after one, seven, fourteen and twenty-one days using three independent methods — spatial transcriptomics, longitudinal two-photon calcium imaging through an implanted lens, and behavioural phenotyping.

All three converge on day seven. The transcriptional remodeling is not uniform; it concentrates in the lateral territory, where differentially expressed genes run roughly threefold what they do centrally, while the medial domain barely moves — and the glucocorticoid receptor transcript is itself enriched laterally. The imaging shows the same geography and the same clock: following the same neurons across sessions, the cells that change are specifically those unresponsive or inhibited by an aversive airpuff at baseline, and the change is largest laterally with onset at day seven. And the behaviour — threat escape, active coping, grooming, combined into a single phenotypic state — is indistinguishable from controls at day five, diverges at day six, is significant at day seven, and reproduces in a restraint-stress model.

The pharmacology lands on that same date. A single dose of ketamine at ten milligrams per kilogram given at that day-seven onset normalised the population airpuff response back to baseline, and the effect was carried by the shrinkage of one specific functional cluster in the lateral domain, with its neighbours untouched.

What I take from this is not that the habenula matters — we knew that. It is that susceptibility has an onset you can date, that three independent readouts agree on the date, and that the date is a treatment window rather than a descriptive fact. Caveats are real: corticosterone in the water is a proxy for stress and not stress, the authors did not establish what drives the lateral excitation, and a seven-day rodent clock does not port to a clinic running on a seven-week one.

Second, a Nature paper that found a whole class of regulatory elements by asking why they were invisible. Microexons — exons of three to twenty-seven nucleotides — are a neuronal splicing programme already implicated in autism, and the assumption has been that they act as frame-preserving switches toggling protein-interaction surfaces. Running de novo detection across roughly forty-eight hundred bulk RNA-seq samples and forty-seven thousand single nuclei, this group turned up fifteen hundred human microexons nobody had annotated.

Why they were missed is the interesting part. The new ones disproportionately introduce premature termination codons — they are nonsense-mediated-decay switches tuning expression level, not splice switches tuning protein function. The transcripts carrying them were being destroyed before anyone sequenced them. Block translation with cycloheximide, or inhibit the decay pathway, and they appear.

On top of the catalogue they built a code model — gradient-boosted trees sitting on the outputs of existing splicing and RNA-binding-protein models — to discriminate real neural microexons from matched decoys. Two results from it matter. In-silico mutagenesis impact correlates with evolutionary conservation at about zero point nine two, a strong sign the model learned real regulatory grammar. And it independently rediscovers QKI as a repressor acting through motifs twenty to forty nucleotides upstream — a position that would normally be dismissed as branch-point sequence — confirmed by knockdown and minigene mutagenesis.

There are two cautions. The autism genetics is the weakest part, not the strongest: rare variants do show larger predicted effects than common ones across discordant sibling pairs, but the headline variant set sits in about two and a half percent of affected individuals and is prediction-driven rather than functionally confirmed. And a detail for anyone building models: only about twelve percent of these new neural microexons have one-to-one mouse orthologues. The class is substantially primate-specific, so the rodent models the field relies on cannot contain most of it.

Third, a molecular glue, and a nice demonstration that the lever can be kinetic. NOS1AP is a scaffolding adaptor for neuronal nitric oxide synthase, implicated in Alzheimer's, in schizophrenia and depression, and — because this matters for safety — in QT prolongation. It has no enzymatic pocket, and every previous attempt to drug it has tried to break its protein interactions. This group screened a one-point-four-billion-member DNA-encoded library for a compound that stabilises one instead.

The optimised compound, NOSTRA, binds NOS1AP at submicromolar affinity. Across six NOS1AP partner interactions it enhanced exactly one, the interaction with Scribble, with a cooperativity factor of about thirty — and the mechanism is almost entirely residence time. A fourteen-fold slowing of dissociation against less than a two-fold change in association. The binary complex affinity goes from around twenty-six micromolar to about one micromolar in the compound's presence, native mass spectrometry resolves the actual ternary species, and both that and the TR-FRET readout show a proper hook effect at higher concentrations.

Two details make this more than tidy biophysics. The first is a control that should have worked and didn't: Dexras1 binds the same PTB domain as Scribble and is completely unaffected — so the compound is not simply making the adaptor stickier, it needs three-way complementarity. The second is in the patient-derived neurons. At three micromolar, amyloid-beta forty-two falls about forty percent while amyloid-beta forty stays flat; phospho-tau two-seventeen falls about twenty-nine percent while total tau doesn't move. A forty-two-selective effect with forty unchanged is the signature of gamma-secretase modulation, from a compound with no designed relationship to gamma-secretase at all — either a very interesting piece of biology or an off-target effect wearing its best suit, and the paper cannot yet tell you which.

The honest framing: this is a first-in-class chemical probe, not a drug. Three donor lines, everything in a dish, no in vivo work, and the binding site is a computational nomination with no structure behind it. The authors also concede there is no evidence that loss of this complex is part of Alzheimer's pathogenesis in the first place.

Fourth, staying with selectivity, and moving to ion channels. The G-protein-gated inward rectifiers have been an attractive epilepsy target for years and a frustrating one, because the neuronal GIRK1/2 heterotetramer and the cardiac GIRK1/4 channel are similar enough that activators hit both. That is the reason ML297, the field's reference compound, never advanced. This group characterises GAT1508, a bromo-thiophene analogue of it.

In oocytes GAT1508 activates GIRK1/2 with an EC50 around seventy-five nanomolar and produces no significant current at cardiac GIRK1/4; ML297 hits both. A two point seven angstrom cryo-EM structure explains it at single-residue resolution: the bromothiophene tail anchors the ligand so it cannot flip, which re-seats the urea amides onto a different aspartate than ML297 uses. A residue swap, not raw affinity.

Then the combination result, which is the part I find most interesting. In hippocampal slices, five micromolar cannabidiol plus five micromolar GAT1508 suppressed high-potassium spontaneous activity by about ninety-seven percent, against a Bliss-independence prediction of sixty-three percent — an interaction term of roughly thirty-four percentage points with a bootstrapped confidence interval nowhere near zero. In vivo, cannabidiol alone protected none of the animals, GAT1508 alone twenty percent, and the combination seventy-five percent. Cannabidiol's antiseizure mechanism remains famously underdetermined, and convergence on GIRK is a specific, testable claim about part of it.

Two things to hold against it. In their hands ML297 itself did nothing in the seizure model, which is a striking failure of the field's reference compound and makes the comparator arm harder to read; the third urea in the series killed half its cohort from respiratory depression. And the in-vivo combination arm actually received slightly more cannabidiol than the monotherapy arm, which undercuts the dose-sparing framing. No pharmacokinetics, no brain exposure, no chronic epilepsy model.

Fifth, to the clinic, and a fluid biomarker that reproduces an anatomical gradient. Yesterday I covered a PPMI proteomics paper that only yielded biomarkers after the motor scale was recalibrated. This is a different question on overlapping data and it is cleaner. Starting from a hundred and twenty-nine cerebrospinal-fluid proteins in about nineteen hundred PPMI participants, the protein that best tracks dopamine-transporter SPECT is dopa decarboxylase, and it is not close — it ranks first, at an FDR that runs to thirty-three decimal places. It then replicates in a hundred and fifty-four real-world patients in Perugia on a fully automated clinical immunoassay, discriminating positive from negative scans at an area under the curve of about zero point eight one in PPMI and zero point eight seven in the replication.

Two things lift this above a correlation. First, the signal is putamen-specific — much stronger against putamen binding than caudate — so a secreted enzyme measured in lumbar fluid reproduces the rostro-caudal topography of nigrostriatal loss, which it had no obligation to do. Second, levodopa does not break it. Peripheral dopa decarboxylase is deliberately inhibited by carbidopa, which is why blood measurements of this enzyme are useless; the spinal-fluid measure was essentially unchanged when treated patients were excluded. And in prodromal participants, baseline levels predicted motor conversion at least as well as the scan did.

The caveat that should bother this audience most is one the authors raise themselves: dopa decarboxylase is also the serotonin-synthesising enzyme, and psychoactive co-medication was not assessed at all. Four co-authors work for the company whose assay ran the replication.

Sixth, a pharmacogenetics result that arrives through the back door. Cocaine use disorder is roughly fifty percent heritable and conventional genome-wide association has found almost nothing. This group restricted to about forty-nine thousand known expression-QTL variants and, instead of testing single markers, built allele-correlation networks — multilocus haplotypes, including sets spanning different chromosomes. In about seven thousand eight hundred people with opioid use disorder, standard association found nothing for cocaine-use frequency; the network approach found twelve significant haplotypes.

The one worth your time is at CYP2D6, and the interpretation inverts itself twice. The risk haplotype is an expression-QTL associated with increased CYP2D6 expression — but only in brain, with no effect in liver, where baseline expression is highest. Resolving the region against long-read phased human assemblies showed that the haplotype actually rides on structurally complex chromosomes carrying non-functional copies of the gene, in linkage with the classic star-four splice-disrupting allele. So the apparent expression gain is loss of function. CYP2D6 is a minor alternative route to dopamine and to serotonin, which gives you a mechanism for why a hepatic drug-metabolism gene shows up in a brain phenotype at all. The architecture is overdominant — heterozygotes depleted among cases — which single-marker additive models are structurally blind to.

Then the discipline arrives. The African-ancestry replication effectively failed: in an independent cohort the testable loci were either different haplotypes or the same ones pointing the opposite way, and nine of the twelve primary hits do not replicate directionally. So the defensible claim is not twelve new cocaine loci. It is one mechanistic arc, plus a methodological argument that additive single-marker scans leave non-additive structure on the table.

Seventh, a Nature paper that is nominally about brown fat and is, for this audience, about something else. The authors took a hundred thousand PET-CT scans from a cancer centre, identified the roughly nine percent with detectable brown adipose tissue, matched them on age, sex, body-mass index and ambient temperature, and then swept all five hundred and eleven diagnostic code groups without a hypothesis. Ninety-eight associated with brown fat, ninety-two of them at lower prevalence — diabetes, ischaemic heart disease, the expected metabolic set.

Three psychiatric codes fell out of that sweep: depressive episode at an odds ratio around zero point six eight, sleep disorders zero point seven seven, anxiety zero point seven eight. They then ran the same analysis over a hundred and twenty-five home medications, and the SSRIs are negatively associated with brown fat — fluoxetine most strongly, then sertraline and escitalopram — surviving adjustment for the psychiatric diagnoses themselves. The signal tracks the drug, not the depression. Beta-blockers behave the same way, which at least makes adrenergic sense.

The mechanistic half delivers a pharmacology lesson worth the detour. The strongest positive drug association was losartan, and in brown adipocytes the effect ran through PPAR-gamma rather than the angiotensin receptor: it survived knockdown of the receptor, was abolished by a PPAR-gamma antagonist, and — the clean bit — was reproduced by the losartan metabolite with no angiotensin-receptor affinity while the metabolite that is a potent blocker did nothing.

The caveats are structural. Every patient is a cancer-centre patient scanned for oncologic reasons, the exposure is glucose uptake standing in for thermogenesis, everything is retrospective, and the in vitro work runs far above human plasma concentrations. There is no mechanistic follow-up on the psychiatric associations at all. But an SSRI association that holds after adjusting for the indication either reflects a real thermogenic liability of serotonergic treatment or a confound nobody has named, and both are worth somebody's time.

Eighth, something sharper about where a signal lives. In drug-resistant focal epilepsy, interictal spikes on intraoperative electrocorticography are the standard localisation tool and they are not specific. This two-centre study sampled at ten kilohertz and looked for very high-frequency activity, five hundred to two thousand hertz, riding on those spikes. Out of a hundred and thirty-two thousand spikes, one thousand nine hundred and twenty-five carried it — about one and a half percent.

That fraction does all the work. Among seizure-free patients, each additional such spike per minute raised the odds that a contact sat inside the resected region by a factor of about twelve. For spikes in general the same figure is one point zero nine. And they validated across centres in both directions rather than by leaving one patient out.

The counterintuitive part is where it sits. The high-frequency component was less likely on the leading spike of a propagating group than on the lagging ones, backwards from the propagation-onset intuition; the authors read that as the generator lying between electrodes. And it was more common in patients with no lesion on MRI — most informative exactly where imaging fails — while more antiseizure medication meant fewer such spikes, which matters for intraoperative drug state. Being fair to it: the association with postoperative recurrence did not survive correction for multiple comparisons, and the cross-centre validation intervals touch chance.

Ninth, to the computational side, and a paper that is squarely in the Centaur lineage. The problem with Psych-101, the dataset behind that line of work, is that each participant appears in only one task, so it cannot support individual-level modelling across tasks. This group collected one where they do: about five hundred and ninety participants, a quarter of a million decisions, each person completing several of twelve tasks spanning bandits, risk and discounting, structure learning, and social games. Then they fine-tuned an eight-billion-parameter model to predict a person's choices on a target task given that same person's transcript from a different task.

The gains over the task-general baseline are real and small — hundredths of a nat. The important results are elsewhere. First, the signal is genuinely person-specific: substituting a different participant's transcript, matched for format and length, degrades prediction. Second, it transfers within bandits and tradeoffs and fails for social preference, where the cross-task model is slightly worse than baseline on public goods and ultimatum. Whatever signature is transferring, it is not a general trait.

Third, and this is the one to carry: at the participant level, predicted and observed bandit regret correlate at about zero point eight four, but the model's spread is much narrower than the humans'. It gets the rank order of who is impulsive right and compresses everyone toward the typical participant. If you were planning to use a fine-tuned model as a synthetic participant pool for individual-differences work, that is your warning — you recover your correlation and lose your variance, which is the quantity such studies are about. One oddity worth a sentence: giving the base model explicit numerical summaries of a participant's prior tendencies made predictions substantially worse, not better.

Tenth, an agent harness for computational drug discovery, which I want to use to make a point about reading these papers. The system wraps about eighty operations — docking, cheminformatics, database lookups, ADMET prediction — in a persistent object store, and adds a loop that mines its own execution traces into reusable skills with stated applicability conditions, verified by a review agent before admission. On the benchmark it reports about ninety-two percent on affinity comparison against eighty-one for the prior system.

Now look at the ablations, which the authors publish honestly. Swapping the backbone model while holding the harness fixed drops that same number from ninety-two to sixty-two. The harness buys about eleven points; changing the model underneath costs thirty. The skill-evolution loop, the paper's actual novelty, recovers most of the backbone deficit retrospectively on the same benchmark it was mined from — but it makes virtual screening worse, dropping top-three hit rate while improving coverage, and the learned skills still underperform hand-written ones. And on the prospective design campaign, the figure caption states outright that the endpoints shown were selected post hoc, and that on hERG and drug-likeness the system lost to its comparator.

I am being hard on this because the candour deserves rewarding rather than burying. The lesson generalises: when an agentic paper reports a headline number, the question is almost always how it compares to simply changing the model underneath — and that comparison is usually sitting in the appendix.

Finally, something light but better designed than it sounds. A proof-of-concept randomised trial in Barcelona asked whether daily music listening helps chronic non-cancer pain in patients on long-term opioid therapy. The design point is the comparator: not a waiting list, but personalised audiobooks, matched for auditory stimulation, sustained attention, daily routine and therapist contact — everything except the music. Fifty-six patients, four weeks of thirty to sixty minutes a day at home.

Adjusting for baseline pain, the between-group difference at end of treatment was one point two six points on the numerical rating scale favouring music, effect size around zero point seven, persisting at exploratory follow-up. Six of twenty-eight in the music arm hit a thirty-percent reduction; none of twenty-eight did on audiobooks. That these patients are on stable long-term opioids, where you would expect reward circuitry to be blunted, makes that more interesting than it first looks.

But the caveat belongs in the same breath. The unadjusted comparison was null, the primary cumulative pain-relief measure was null, and mood and quality-of-life differences between arms were null. The headline exists in the adjusted model, in an open-label trial, with baseline imbalances that the adjustment covariates are correcting for. A twenty-one percent responder rate against a literal zero in twenty-eight people is also a suspiciously clean split. So: a well-controlled hint that the specific ingredient in music does something beyond structured listening, and a trial that needs doing again at size.

That is Receptor and Reason for today. When an intervention works inconsistently, the first question is usually not the dose.
