## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. It's Wednesday, July 29th, and we've got ten items today — a fundamental Alzheimer's mechanism to open, a run through Parkinson's physiology and the neuromodulators that shape learning and stress, a couple of computational tools worth your attention, and a cautionary note about a drug that's probably in your medicine cabinet to close us out. Let's get into it.

We open with a preprint out of a human iPSC-neuron study that reframes what APOE4 actually does inside a neuron. The title claims APOE4 disrupts the central dogma, and that's not quite as hyperbolic as it sounds. Working with isogenic APOE3 and APOE4 neurons, the group layered transcriptomics, ribosome profiling — the translatomic readout — and proteomics on top of each other, and the striking result is how little the transcriptome moves. The action is downstream. APOE4 changes ribosome occupancy, uncouples protein abundance from transcript level, and, most importantly, globally extends protein half-lives. Neurons stop renewing their proteome. Long-lived proteins pile up, the proteasome and lysosome both lose ground — and they show APOE itself binding more tightly to the neuronal proteasome — and this accumulation shows up during maturation, before the cells slide into a senescence-like stress state. Why this matters: most of the APOE4 story has been told through astrocytes and microglia, and most of the drug attention has been on amyloid. This says the neuron-intrinsic problem is a proteostasis failure that precedes overt pathology, which points at proteome renewal itself as a target for building neuronal resilience — a complementary axis to the anti-amyloid antibodies, which, as the authors note, carry real ARIA risk precisely in APOE4 carriers.

From a molecular mechanism of vulnerability to a very different level of the nervous system — the read-out we actually record from patients. There's a nice study combining OFF-medication subthalamic local field potentials with quantitative MRI markers of nigral and brainstem pathology in thirty-three people with Parkinson's. The subthalamic LFP is increasingly treated as a physiomarker for adaptive deep brain stimulation — beta activity goes up, you turn stimulation up. This work complicates that picture in a useful way. Distinct pathologies mapped onto distinct electrophysiological components. Substantia nigra susceptibility, an iron proxy, tracked low-beta burst occupancy and duration; nigral free water tracked the aperiodic offset and slope; and pedunculopontine — a brainstem cholinergic node — tracked high-frequency aperiodic activity, with that relationship strengthening as nigral pathology worsened. The takeaway is that the subthalamic signal is not a unitary readout of dopamine loss. It's an integrated fingerprint of pathology spread across several interconnected systems, and if you're building closed-loop stimulation on top of it, that heterogeneity is something you'll want to model rather than average away.

Staying in the basal ganglia, but on the therapy side — levodopa-induced dyskinesia. Using activity-dependent ensemble tagging in the six-hydroxydopamine mouse model — that's the classic dopamine-lesion model of Parkinson's — a group asked which upstream inputs recruit versus restrain the striatal ensembles that drive dyskinesia. They found two afferents with opposing jobs: secondary motor cortex drives dyskinesia, and the parafascicular thalamus brakes it. Chronic levodopa remodels these connections asymmetrically — the cortical input biases toward NMDA-receptor-mediated excitation onto ensemble neurons, while the thalamic input recruits stronger polysynaptic inhibition. And when they knocked down the obligatory NMDA subunit GluN1 selectively in those ensemble neurons, peak dyskinesia dropped and the cortically driven component weakened. So dyskinesia gets framed as a targetable imbalance between cortical recruitment and a thalamostriatal brake — and it hands you a circuit-specific rationale for why NMDA-receptor tone in a particular striatal population matters, which is more actionable than "glutamate is involved."

That NMDA-and-dopamine theme is a good bridge to a computational item — a spiking neural network paper on dopamine-modulated spike-timing-dependent plasticity. The standard model of reward learning has dopamine simply scaling the magnitude of STDP through an eligibility trace, and the authors point out a problem: that model keeps learning even at absurdly high dopamine concentrations, because scaling never breaks. But the biology says otherwise — dopamine nonlinearly reshapes the STDP window, actually flipping depression into potentiation at high levels. So they built a rule where rising dopamine progressively biases plasticity toward potentiation until receptor saturation caps the effect. In networks of Izhikevich model neurons — a compact, biologically flavored spiking unit — robust conditioning only emerged inside a realistic dopamine concentration range, and successful learning self-organized into a feedforward backbone embedded in recurrent circuitry that burst selectively to reward-predicting stimuli. It's a modest paper, but it's the kind of correction that matters if you use these models to reason about dopaminergic drugs — a learning rule that works at any dose isn't telling you anything about dose.

From dopamine to noradrenaline, and a rare causal handle in a primate. A group used a pharmacogenetic approach in rhesus macaques to selectively and reversibly silence the locus coeruleus — inhibitory DREADDs expressed in noradrenergic neurons, switched on by a DREADD actuator drug, with a control animal getting the actuator alone. The monkeys ran a hole-board foraging task in two versions: a transparent condition where rewards were visible, needing almost no cognitive effort, and an opaque condition where they had to hold visited locations in working memory. Silencing the locus coeruleus did nothing in the easy condition and impaired performance specifically in the effortful one. That's a clean dissociation, and it directly tests a hypothesis that's mostly been supported by correlational arousal data — that the locus coeruleus mobilizes resources not just for physical effort but for cognitive effort too. For anyone thinking about noradrenergic modulation in attention and effort-based disorders, a reversible primate loss-of-function is about as strong as this evidence gets.

Sticking with noradrenaline but moving to stress physiology — a fiber-photometry study monitoring noradrenaline release in the hypothalamic paraventricular nucleus, the hub where stress signals converge on CRH neurons and the HPA axis. Using a genetically encoded fluorescent noradrenaline sensor in freely moving mice, they could finally watch release dynamics continuously, and the headline is that the temporal signature is stressor-specific. An acute nociceptive stressor produced pulsatile, seconds-scale release. Sustained physical stressors like restraint produced prolonged release that tracked the whole duration of the challenge. So the paraventricular nucleus isn't getting a generic "stress on" signal — the noradrenergic input encodes something about the kind and time-course of the stressor, which is exactly the sort of temporal structure you'd want to know before assuming a drug that blunts noradrenergic tone will act uniformly across stress states.

Now to computational psychiatry, and thought disorder in schizophrenia. This is a resting-state MEG study, twenty-five patients and matched controls, and the methodological move is the interesting part. Conventional beta-band power showed no group difference after correction. But when they stopped averaging and looked at transient beta bursts — their rate, duration, and inter-regional synchrony — dissociable anterior-versus-posterior patterns emerged. Within the default mode network, patients had shorter parietal bursts and increased prefrontal burst synchrony, and those burst features predicted computationally derived measures of how disorganized the patients' speech was. The framing is that beta bursts index the stabilization and reinstatement of internal predictive models, and that thought disorder is what you see when that stabilization fails. The lesson that generalizes beyond schizophrenia: time-averaged spectral power can be flat while the burst-level dynamics underneath it carry the clinical signal. If you're building EEG or MEG biomarkers, the statistic you choose is doing a lot of the work.

And that's a natural handoff to a tool built for exactly this kind of analysis. There's an arXiv preprint called CogEEGAgent, an LLM agent for cognitive EEG analysis grounded in MNE-Python, the standard analysis toolkit. The problem it targets is one we should all be nervous about as agents start doing our stats: a fluent, confident report doesn't prove the agent actually ran the analysis you asked for, or that it didn't quietly try a dozen contrasts and report the one that worked. Their design separates what they call semantic authority from scientific authority. The language model interprets your intent and proposes a registered analysis — but deterministic components validate typed contracts, hold the confirmation data out of the model's reach, force the agent to commit to an analysis before that confirmation data is opened, and only then release evidence-bound results. In effect they're engineering out the researcher-degrees-of-freedom problem — agentic confirmation bias — by design rather than by trust. On a routing benchmark it mapped language to the right analysis better than a matched deterministic router, and the held-out confirmation demonstrably curbed the false positives you get from uncorrected adaptive search. For a field rushing to hand analysis to autonomous agents, this fail-closed framing is the right instinct.

Our second computational item is a drug-repurposing pipeline, and its value is in a comparison rather than a new algorithm. The tool, called CDRPipe, matches disease transcriptomic signatures against drug-perturbation signatures, and the interesting bit is that it harmonizes two very different perturbation resources: the old microarray-based Connectivity Map, with about two thousand quality-filtered experiments, and pseudo-bulk profiles derived from a large single-cell perturbation atlas — the Tahoe collection — with tens of thousands of experiments. Tested against known drug-disease associations, the single-cell-derived signatures recovered far more annotated therapeutics than the microarray ones. But the real finding is that the two resources barely overlapped — only a few percent of recovered drugs in common. They're complementary, not redundant. The practical message for anyone doing connectivity-based repurposing is that leaning on a single perturbation database is quietly narrowing your therapeutic search space, and the fix is cheap — run both and take the union.

And to close, something lighter but genuinely useful — a reminder that a common over-the-counter drug may be doing the opposite of what people take it for. A telemetry study in the 5XFAD amyloid mouse model and wild-type littermates looked at chronic diphenhydramine — the first-generation antihistamine most people know as the sedating ingredient in over-the-counter sleep aids, and an anticholinergic. A month of daily dosing didn't rescue the sleep deficits in the Alzheimer's-model mice; it made things worse. It fragmented the deeper non-REM sleep in both genotypes and pushed more drowsiness into the active phase — the mouse analogue of daytime grogginess. It's a small study and it's a mouse, but it lands squarely on a real clinical question, because chronic anticholinergic use is itself associated with dementia risk, and here the drug degrades exactly the sleep architecture you'd hope to protect in an aging or at-risk brain. Not a mechanism paper — just a clean, cautionary data point about a drug we treat as harmless.
