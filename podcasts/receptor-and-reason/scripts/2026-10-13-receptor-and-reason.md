## Script

Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Tuesday, October thirteenth. Ten items today, and a sourcing note first, because today was the mirror image of yesterday. Both preprint servers rate-limited this machine from the first request — bioRxiv never opened at all, and medRxiv only let anything through once I spaced requests more than a minute apart. What carried the run instead was the Nature family, wide open after being behind a bot check yesterday, and arXiv. So you'll hear more peer-reviewed journal work than usual.

There's a thread worth naming before we start. In almost every paper today, the real finding is a statement about the measuring instrument as much as about the thing being measured. Which alcohol response you chose to record decides which theory of addiction you confirm. Whether you model choice value or choice noise decides whether Parkinson's looks like a change in generosity. Whether your genome scan looks at single bases or at structural variants decides how much heritability you find. And in the computational papers, what the evaluation is allowed to see decides who wins. The best work today knows this about itself. Some of it doesn't.

We open with the item most likely to change a conversation you're already having. A group has published a living systematic review and meta-analysis of MDMA-assisted therapy for PTSD, and — more durably — released the underlying effect-size database as a public resource on Metapsy, so it can be updated as trials report rather than frozen at publication. That infrastructure choice is arguably the bigger contribution, because this is a field where the evidence base turns over fast and where regulatory decisions have already outrun the literature.

The headline numbers are solid and unspectacular in a healthy way. From fifteen hundred eighty-six records screened down to six randomized trials and two hundred forty-two participants in the primary model: a standardized mean difference on PTSD symptoms of about minus zero point seven one, highly significant, with essentially no detectable between-study heterogeneity — though with six studies that estimate carries very wide uncertainty. Response rate ratio one point three five, remission rate ratio two point two five. Dropout averaged under ten percent, against the twenty to thirty percent trauma-focused psychotherapy typically sees. Depression, as a secondary outcome, showed no significant effect.

Two meta-regressions found that more dosing sessions and higher cumulative dose both predicted larger between-group differences. The authors immediately defuse their own dose-response finding by noting that sessions and cumulative dose correlate at zero point nine three — these are not two independent pieces of evidence, they're one, and with six studies it is barely that.

The part that should actually travel is the certainty rating. The authors applied GRADE and came out at low certainty, and they got there by downgrading two full levels for indirectness — not for risk of bias, not for inconsistency, not for imprecision, all of which they explicitly declined to downgrade. The indirectness has three sources. Functional unblinding, which in a trial of a drug that produces unmistakable subjective effects against an inert placebo is close to unavoidable. A mean of thirty-nine percent of participants having prior MDMA experience, which shapes expectancy. And the fact that all six trials ran under a single sponsor's therapy protocol. So the effect size is real and the design quality is good; what is uncertain is whether that number survives contact with a different protocol, a different sponsor, and patients who have never taken the drug before. That is a much more specific and more useful criticism than the usual hand-waving about psychedelic trial blinding, and it's the kind of thing a living review can actually resolve as independent trials report.

From a synthesis of trials to a pipeline that runs the other direction — from genetics all the way to prescription records. A large collaboration published an Alzheimer's drug-target compendium built by chaining together three methods that each cover a different one's blind spot, and the chaining is the point.

They started with Mendelian randomization across one thousand two hundred twenty-nine druggable proteins, using instruments from twelve protein and expression quantitative trait loci datasets, against seven Alzheimer's genome-wide association datasets totaling about two hundred seventy-five thousand cases and one and a half million controls, in both European and African American ancestry samples. They collapsed that into a consensus score, prioritized seventy-three targets, then narrowed to nineteen that also had a druggable pocket and a mechanism pointing the same direction the genetics did.

The sanity check is that this recovers targets we already believe in — BACE2 and the gamma-secretase subunit APH1B on the amyloid side, galantamine's nicotinic receptor subunit and the GABA-B receptor on the neuromodulation side, and phosphodiesterase 5, which is currently in a phase three Alzheimer's trial. A method that rediscovers the existing pipeline is a method you can provisionally trust on the targets it adds.

The one they take furthest is EPHX2, which encodes soluble epoxide hydrolase — the enzyme that breaks down protective epoxy fatty acids derived from arachidonic and linoleic acid, converting them into pro-inflammatory diols. Higher soluble epoxide hydrolase protein, higher Alzheimer's risk. They then do something unusually careful with the locus, because EPHX2 sits next to CLU, a long-established Alzheimer's gene, and the obvious objection is that they have rediscovered CLU wearing a different hat. CRISPR interference on the lead CLU risk variant knocked down both CLU and EPHX2 expression — ambiguous. CRISPR interference on a second, independent EPHX2 variant knocked down EPHX2 only, leaving CLU untouched. That's the experiment that separates them.

Then the functional chain: a coding variant that lowers the enzyme's protein level is protective against Alzheimer's, and when they prime-edit that exact base change into patient-derived induced pluripotent stem cell neurons, phospho-tau drops, in neurons and again in cerebral organoids, with the transcriptional signature pointing at PI3K-Akt activation, which inhibits GSK-3-beta, a principal tau kinase. And then EC5026 — a picomolar soluble epoxide hydrolase inhibitor that already has human safety data — reduced amyloid load, reduced microglial density, and improved performance on novel object recognition, fear conditioning and the Morris water maze in 5xFAD mice.

Finally the third leg: a drug-wide association study over two hundred ten commonly prescribed medications in real-world patient records with one-to-one propensity score matching, which independently flagged baclofen, a GABA-B agonist, as associated with reduced Alzheimer's incidence in people with mild cognitive impairment — a target the genetics had already nominated. Each method here has a well-known failure mode. Mendelian randomization has pleiotropy; organoids have ceiling problems; real-world data has confounding by indication that propensity matching only partly fixes. The argument isn't that any one leg is strong. It's that their failure modes don't overlap.

Now to the paper that most cleanly illustrates today's thread. A four-year prospective study asked which laboratory alcohol response predicts who stays sick, and in doing so ran a head-to-head between two long-competing theories.

One hundred one young adults meeting criteria for alcohol use disorder at enrollment were given alcohol and placebo challenges in the lab, then followed for four years. The retention is remarkable and worth saying out loud: four hundred ninety-eight of a possible five hundred two follow-up assessments completed, ninety-nine point two percent. In a four-year alcohol study that is close to unheard of, and it matters, because differential dropout is exactly how this literature usually goes wrong.

Symptoms declined on average, but the aggregate hides the structure. Sixty percent met criteria at every single follow-up. Only eleven percent remitted entirely. A trajectory model found three groups — high, medium and low — and those with severe disorder at baseline were least likely to remit.

Here's the result. Greater alcohol-induced stimulation, liking, and wanting at the baseline lab session all predicted higher symptom counts across four years, and survived adjustment for baseline drinking and participant characteristics. Sedation did not predict. Heart rate did not predict. Cortisol did not predict. The same split appeared in the trajectory analysis: the high-trajectory group showed greater reward responses, and no difference at all on sedation or either physiological measure.

That's a direct hit for incentive-sensitization — the reward and stimulant properties of alcohol are what track persistence — and a miss for the low-level-of-response account, which would have predicted that blunted sedation does the work. I'd note the honest limitation: this cohort was recruited already meeting criteria, so this is about who stays sick rather than who gets sick, and those may have different predictors. But the design point stands. Had this study measured only sedation and heart rate, as plenty of older work did, it would have reported a null and concluded that lab alcohol response doesn't predict course. The measurement selected the theory.

Which is also exactly what happens in the next one, in a very different setting. A Düsseldorf group looked at social decision-making in Parkinson's disease — twenty-two patients with subthalamic deep brain stimulation, tested both on and off stimulation, against twenty healthy controls, using a social discounting task that measures generosity as a function of social distance.

The surface finding is the one that would have been the headline twenty years ago: people with Parkinson's made generous choices less often than controls. The interesting work is what they did next — fit three computational models that differ in how choice value maps onto an observed decision, and ask which parameter actually moved.

The discount rate — how steeply people devalue benefits to socially distant others, which is the parameter that would mean an actual change in prosocial preference — did not differ between groups in any model. What differed was noise, in three separate forms. The inverse decision temperature was lower in patients, meaning value differences translated less deterministically into choices. In the drift-diffusion version, the coefficient scaling value difference onto drift rate was reduced, meaning prosocial evidence accumulated less reliably. And a model with an explicit attentional-lapse state found that patients were less likely to recover from a lapse once in it, with strong Bayesian evidence off stimulation.

So: generosity intact, policy noisy. People with Parkinson's in this task value others the way controls do and then fail to act on that valuation consistently. That is a materially different clinical story — it reframes reports of selfishness or social difficulty in Parkinson's as an impulsivity and attention problem rather than a change in what patients care about, which is a very different thing to say to a spouse. And the deep brain stimulation result underlines it: stimulation reliably sped up decisions, shortening non-decision time, but had no effect whatsoever on the social choices themselves. Caveats are the obvious ones — modest sample, one task, and the inference is only as good as the model space they searched. But they searched it, which is the part most studies skip.

Staying with the question of what your instrument can see, a Hong Kong-led group asked how much Alzheimer's heritability we've been missing by genotyping the wrong kind of variant. They built a structural variant reference set from long-read sequencing of eleven Chinese genomes, filtered it with a random forest classifier down to forty-four thousand high-confidence variants, and then genotyped those in a Hong Kong cohort of about twenty-three hundred people using short-read data — with a strict concordance filter, because the raw short-read genotypes correlated with long-read truth at only about zero point six, which is a trap worth knowing about if you're planning this design.

The polygenic score result is the one to carry away. A score built purely from common structural variants predicted Alzheimer's better than a score built from single-nucleotide polymorphisms — r-squared of zero point zero five six versus zero point zero four six — and critically, the two scores were almost entirely uncorrelated with each other. Combined, they roughly doubled the variance explained relative to either alone, and a conditional analysis showed that gain wasn't just linkage disequilibrium between the two variant classes. These are genuinely different slices of risk.

The specific hit is a CCG repeat polymorphism in the promoter of FAM193B, reaching genome-wide significance, with alleles ranging from four to over thirty repeats. Longer repeats increase transcription factor binding at the promoter and raise FAM193B expression; higher expression associates with neuronal death pathways and reduced gray matter volume; and overexpressing the gene in cultured neurons increases DNA damage signaling. A repeat expansion of this kind is close to invisible to a standard genotyping array, which is the whole point — this is heritability that was always there and that our instrument was structurally unable to detect.

A shorter item, but a clean mechanistic one. A study in C9ORF72 ALS asked whether astrocytes control axonal mitochondrial transport in motor neurons, using patient-derived stem cell lines and their CRISPR gene-corrected isogenic counterparts — three astrocyte line pairs — in co-culture.

Mutant astrocytes impaired both the proportion and the velocity of motile mitochondria in motor neurons, non-cell-autonomously — the astrocytes alone were sufficient. Two controls make this convincing. Endosomal trafficking was unaffected, so this is cargo-specific rather than a general transport failure, consistent with signaling endosomes running on local glycolytic ATP while mitochondrial motility depends on membrane potential and calcium-regulated adaptors. And RNA sequencing found no genotype-driven astrocyte reactivity, which argues against a gain-of-toxic-function story and for a bioenergetic deficit. Mutant astrocytes showed reduced glycolytic capacity and respiration, dragged down neuronal respiration in co-culture, and intraneuronal lactate didn't change — pointing away from the lactate shuttle as the mediator.

The payoff: overexpressing PGC-1-alpha in the astrocytes alone — boosting astrocytic respiration, not touching the neurons — rescued neuronal mitochondrial motility. That's a causal link from astrocyte metabolism to neuronal transport and a therapeutic handle that doesn't require reaching the motor neuron. The limitation is real and the authors state it: astrocytes came from three patient line pairs, but all the motor neurons came from a single pair.

Now to the computational side, where the same question — what is the comparison allowed to see — keeps deciding outcomes.

The most technically interesting is a chemistry agent built on a masked diffusion language model rather than an autoregressive one. The motivation is genuine rather than fashionable: autoregressive generation is left-to-right and causally masked, a poor fit for molecular representations that need bidirectional context and for tool calls that depend on what comes later in a trajectory. Reframing the whole interleaved reasoning-and-tool-use trajectory as iterative denoising means a tool-use decision can be made with the full trajectory in view. They post-train in two stages and build a six-task chemistry benchmark spanning single and multi-turn tool use.

Under matched model scale, training data, tool budget and inference constraints, the diffusion model beats its autoregressive counterpart by about nineteen to twenty points overall — that's the comparison that carries real weight, because everything else is held fixed. They also report that their eight-billion-parameter model reaches ninety-one point eight percent overall, beating frontier models like GPT-5.5 and Claude Opus 4.7 running ReAct, and beating dedicated chemistry frameworks. Take the second claim much less seriously than the first: the benchmark is theirs, their model was post-trained on this task distribution, and the frontier models were not. The matched-budget ablation against an identically trained autoregressive model is the result; the frontier comparison is marketing.

Second, a medical diagnosis agent with an idea that generalizes beyond medicine. Rather than letting a model generate free-form queries, it constrains evidence acquisition to a finite, clinically grounded action space arranged by cost, and learns a policy over belief-state transitions where each step's utility is diagnostic improvement minus acquisition cost. Cheap channels first; expensive modalities only when the remaining uncertainty justifies them. Straightforward cases terminate early, ambiguous ones escalate.

The gains are substantial — on a New England Journal case set, GPT-4o-mini accuracy went from eighteen point six to thirty-one point two percent — and the authors are explicit about where the gain lives: the underlying diagnostic model is frozen and unchanged. What improved was the decision about what evidence to look at. Learned selection also beat exhaustive access to every modality, which is the counterintuitive and more interesting result: giving the model everything was worse than giving it the right things. They're also candid that naive active acquisition is brittle and that the benefit depends heavily on model capacity.

Third, and this one is instructive mostly as a lesson in evaluation design. A GraphRAG system for clinical suicide risk assessment, grounding retrieval in an ontology rather than a flat vector index, evaluated on fifteen hundred queries across fifteen clinical categories and one hundred patients. Under a paired comparative judging protocol, the graph system wins seventy-six point four percent of criterion-level comparisons against a vector RAG baseline.

Read the breakdown, though, because it tells a more honest story than the headline. Clinical relevance: ninety-four percent wins. Completeness: eighty-four percent. Evidence grounding: seventy-nine percent. Factual accuracy: forty-nine percent wins, forty-six percent ties — essentially a draw. So the ontology buys you coverage, structure, and traceable grounding. It does not make the system more factually correct. And the categories where flat RAG stays competitive are exactly the ones where the clinical information already lives in a small number of structured record fields and needs no relational reasoning to find.

I'd also flag that the evidence-grounding result reversed direction when the authors switched from an absolute judge to a comparative one. They argue, plausibly, that the absolute judge was penalizing an attribution artifact. But a result that flips sign when you change the referee is a result to hold loosely, and to their credit they report both.

And we'll close with the lightest item, which is also the one most likely to irritate you. Three epileptologists ran a deliberately small experiment in what they call full-stack AI academic publishing. Each picked a real published Epilepsia paper and produced a fully AI-fabricated counterpart — using OpenEvidence, Codex, and Claude — then used three different models to perform peer review and make editorial decisions on the resulting six manuscripts, with minimal human supervision.

The epileptologists correctly identified the fabricated paper in four of six pairs. The AI editorial process rejected exactly one of three fabricated manuscripts — and here is the detail that makes the study worth your time: the one that got rejected was the one proposing chicken soup as an intervention for epilepsy. It was caught for being implausible, not for being fabricated. The detection signal was topic absurdity, which is a property the authors supplied by accident and which a bad actor would simply not supply.

Their observations on AI-generated review are sharper than the detection result. The reviews were comprehensive, took one to ten minutes, and covered every section evenly rather than triaging the way human reviewers do. But they were six-plus pages against a typical one to three, heavily formatted, and — the genuinely useful catch — every one of them stated an unequivocal editorial recommendation in the author-facing comments, which violates the standing recommendation that such recommendations go only to the editor. That's a concrete, checkable tell, and it's the kind of thing you'd want in a journal's AI policy rather than a vague gesture at integrity.

The sample is three fabricated manuscripts and three reviewers. You cannot get a detection rate out of that, and the authors don't pretend otherwise — they call it brief and illustrative. Their conclusion is the reasonable one: AI assistance across the publishing pipeline is already good enough to be useful and already good enough to be abused, and the policy question is not whether to permit it but how to require supervision at each step.
