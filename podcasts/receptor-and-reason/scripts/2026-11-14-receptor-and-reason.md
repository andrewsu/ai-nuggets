## Script

Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Saturday, November fourteenth. Today's thread is the apparatus. In every paper on the list, something that looks like a fact about the biology — this drug works, this gene causes that phenotype, this model can see — turns out to depend on a choice the experimenter made about where to measure. The species of the tissue. Which arm of the task. Which end of the regulatory element. Which letters you call the amino acids. Change the apparatus and the finding moves, sometimes all the way to zero. That is not a complaint about sloppy work. In the best of these papers the authors deliberately varied the apparatus to find out how much of their answer was coming from it.

The lead comes out of Charité in Berlin, and it is the sharpest version of this I have seen in a while. Earlier this week we talked about AMPA receptor auxiliary subunits and how they set the potency of blockers at the receptor. This group went and asked the translational version of the same question, in tissue. They recorded seizure-like events — field potential deflections lasting at least ten seconds with superimposed ripple discharges — in cortical slices from healthy mice and from human neocortex resected during epilepsy surgery, twenty-four patients across three neurosurgical centers. Then they ran a panel of AMPA receptor antagonists against those events.

Three of the four drugs behaved exactly as the textbook says. NBQX, the competitive antagonist, blocked seizure-like events in mouse tissue completely and in human tissue near-completely. GYKI-53655, a non-competitive blocker that spares kainate receptors, blocked both. Perampanel, the only AMPA antagonist approved as antiseizure medication, blocked both, though at roughly ten times the concentration you would find in a treated patient's plasma. So far, so unremarkable — a mouse slice is a decent stand-in.

Then they tested LY-3130481, an antagonist that only hits AMPA receptors complexed with the auxiliary protein TARP gamma-eight, and which has carried the names CRC-611 and ES-481 as it has moved through development. The logic of that drug is anatomical: gamma-eight is dense in forebrain and essentially absent from cerebellum, so blocking only the gamma-eight-associated receptors should spare the cerebellar circuits that give perampanel its ataxia and dysarthria. In mouse tissue, that drug barely worked. Fifteen minutes of application did nothing. Forty minutes reduced seizure-like events in thirty percent of slices. Pushing the concentration to thirty micromolar got a responder rate of forty percent. In human tissue, the same thirty micromolar nearly abolished seizure-like events, with a hundred percent responder rate.

Same drug, same concentration, same readout, opposite conclusion. If you had only the mouse data you would call this compound a failure and shelve it. The authors are honest that they cannot say why: they did not quantify gamma-eight protein in either tissue, and the one published comparison of radioligand binding density actually goes the other way, with lower density in human brain. Their best guess is that pharmacoresistant human epileptic cortex is enriched for gamma-eight-associated, slow-gating AMPA signaling in a way that a healthy three-week-old mouse is not — which, if true, means the disease state is part of the target. There is a supporting hint in the data: twenty nanomolar NBQX, a dose low enough to hit fast AMPA currents while sparing the slow ones, did not stop human seizure-like events from starting, but it left them running longer than controls. Starting a seizure and sustaining one may be pharmacologically separable.

Two caveats. These are acute chemically-provoked events, not spontaneous seizures, so this models acute symptomatic seizure rather than epilepsy; and the human data come from five patients' tissue. But the direction of the species difference is the point, and it runs against the direction everyone assumes — we usually worry that the mouse will flatter the drug.

Second, a genome-wide association study of pain in more than one point two million people, out in Nature Neuroscience. Three biobanks, and three genuinely different definitions of the phenotype: self-reported pain intensity over time in the Million Veteran Program, number of painful body sites in UK Biobank, and diagnostic codes in FinnGen. Rather than pretend those are the same trait, the authors meta-analyze them as what they call multidimensional pain and then spend a lot of the paper probing how much the combination is holding together. Genetic correlations across the cohorts are point six-six to point seven-nine — high, and each significantly different from one. A common-factor model says shared risk accounts for fifty-nine to eighty-one percent of trait variance. So the cohorts are measuring mostly the same thing, and the residual is the measurement instrument.

The yield is three hundred forty-three independent loci, ninety-two of them new for pain, and a multi-omics prioritization converging on four hundred ninety genes enriched in synaptic function and axon development. The gene I would watch is WDR-ninety, which came through fine-mapping with overlapping transcriptomic, methylomic and proteomic support — a rare triple.

The part I find more useful is the polygenic overlap analysis, because it separates two things that routinely get conflated. Genetic correlation with neuroticism is modest, but the causal-variant overlap is ninety-three percent — nearly every variant that matters for one matters for the other. What differs is the concordance of effect direction, only sixty-four percent. Compare opioid medication use: ninety-one percent overlap, but ninety-six percent of those shared variants point the same way. A low genetic correlation can mean "mostly separate biology" or it can mean "same biology, inconsistent sign", and those have opposite implications for whether a shared target is tractable. Mendelian randomization then puts pain upstream of substance use disorders across alcohol, cannabis, opioids and tobacco, and bidirectional with depression and post-traumatic stress symptom clusters. Treat the sex-stratified results with caution — the authors note themselves that the sex-differentiated signals may reflect cohort structure more than biology, with male sample size roughly two and a half times female.

Third, also in Nature Neuroscience, a paper that makes the choice-of-control-point explicit. The problem with trying to treat a haploinsufficiency is dosage: you need more of the gene product, but these are genes where overexpression is itself damaging. The standard CRISPR activation move is to park a transcriptional activator at the promoter, which is a blunt instrument — you get a big increase, uncoupled from whatever the cell was going to do developmentally. This group targeted enhancers instead, in human cortical organoids carrying heterozygous mutations in two high-confidence autism risk genes, CHD-eight and SCN-two-A.

With a single enhancer-targeted guide and a dead Cas-nine fused to the p-three-hundred acetyltransferase, they got moderate increases in expression and rescue of the mutant phenotypes: organoid overgrowth and progenitor overproliferation normalized for CHD-eight, upper-layer neuron markers and neuronal firing output rescued for SCN-two-A. The telling control is what happened in wild-type lines. Promoter-targeted guides drove robust increases in both mutant and wild-type cells. The enhancer-targeted constructs produced no activation in wild-type lines at all — the regulatory machinery was already doing its job there, and the enhancer only has leverage where the dosage is short. That is the whole argument for enhancer targeting in a single comparison. The honest limitations: the virus infects only a subset of cells in an organoid, and the effect decays over time.

Fourth, from Madrid, a study where changing which cells you knock the gene out of changes the entire disease. Aralar, the mitochondrial aspartate-glutamate carrier encoded by SLC-twenty-five-A-twelve, is the regulatory piece of the malate-aspartate shuttle, and it has been linked to autism for two decades. Global knockout mice die around postnatal day twenty, which makes the phenotype hard to read. These authors deleted it only in forebrain neurons, postnatally. Those mice grow normally, survive normally, and have normal brain structure and myelination — so most of the lethality in the global knockout was never about neurons.

What they do have is hyperactivity and a marked sociability deficit, with memory, reversal learning and episodic memory all intact. That dissociation is itself interesting, because the textbook reason to care about this shuttle is lactate utilization as a fuel for memory formation, and the memory is fine. The authors' reading, which tracks recent work questioning neuronal lactate oxidation in the presence of glucose, is that lactate's role in plasticity is signaling rather than fuel — and signaling is the part Aralar loss does not touch. Striatal dopamine content is reduced with an elevated DOPAC-to-dopamine ratio, pointing at increased degradation rather than reduced synthesis, and the VTA dopamine neurons themselves show minimal recombination, so the dopamine problem arrives non-cell-autonomously. Elevated lipid peroxidation, induced glutathione pathways, astrocyte activation. And then systemic beta-hydroxybutyrate restores sociability, normalizes the striatal metabolic and neurochemical abnormalities, and reduces the oxidative stress. The transcriptomic coda is the part to sit with: Aralar deficiency on its own recapitulates a substantial fraction of the fasting and ketone-body transcriptional program, which makes the ketone rescue less obviously a simple fuel substitution than it first appears.

Fifth, from Bristol, and this one is directly about assay choice as a confound. Motivation loss is transdiagnostic and badly served by existing drugs, and essentially the entire preclinical literature measures it as extrinsic motivation — work for food. But a lot of what motivation loss costs a person is intrinsic: doing the thing because doing it is the point. These authors built a dual effort choice arena: a sheltered home area with free food and water and two tubes leading out, one to a nesting-material foraging box with apertures of adjustable difficulty, one to a pellet dispenser on a fixed-ratio schedule. Both motivations, one environment, same session.

First they showed the two arms are independently modulable: pre-feeding and food restriction moved pellet trials and left foraging untouched; shrinking the foraging aperture moved foraging and left trials untouched. Crucially, suppressing one did not enhance the other, so this is not a zero-sum allocation task. Then the pharmacology. Escitalopram, venlafaxine and sertraline all impaired foraging — the SSRI-induced indifference effect, reproduced — but only escitalopram moved trial number. Methylphenidate dose-dependently increased foraging; pramipexole, a D-two-D-three agonist, reduced it; neither moved trials. Amphetamine impaired foraging, but only in a highly motivated subpopulation. Reducing dopamine signaling hit both arms about equally, but even that was mechanism-dependent: haloperidol impaired both, while tetrabenazine, inhibiting vesicular packaging rather than blocking receptors, did nothing to either.

So the divergence is not an artifact of running two different studies in two different labs — it survives within one task environment, in the same animals. If your assay only has the food lever, a compound that selectively flattens intrinsic motivation looks clean. The authors are appropriately modest: systemic drugging cannot tell you whether these are separate neural processes or one process read out two ways, and that is the next experiment.

Sixth, a structural pharmacology paper that relocates selectivity out of the binding pocket. Beta-one-selective antagonists are everywhere in cardiology, and the standing puzzle is that the orthosteric sites of the beta-one and beta-two adrenoceptors are nearly identical, so where does cardioselectivity come from? This group solved cryo-electron microscopy structures of three highly selective antagonists — CGP-twenty-seven-twelve-A, a classic tool compound, plus two newer compounds — bound to an inactive-state receptor. Two of the three bind non-canonically, and they pry open a cleft between transmembrane helices four and five next to the orthosteric site, which nobody had seen.

Mutating the orthosteric residues that actually differ between the subtypes only partially explained the selectivity. The kinetics explained the rest, and the numbers are striking. CGP-twenty-seven-twelve-A associates sixty-seven times faster with beta-one than beta-two and dissociates twenty-six times more slowly, for a seventeen-hundred-fold kinetic affinity difference. One of the new compounds gets an eight-hundred-fold difference almost entirely from a three-hundred-thirty-seven-fold faster on-rate, with the off-rate barely moving. Reciprocal chimeras localize the on-rate effect to the extracellular vestibule: swapping the beta-two outer region onto a beta-one core slowed association by sixty-one-fold for one ligand and three hundred sixty-seven-fold for another. Molecular dynamics then shows the helix four-five cleft opening is energetically accessible in beta-one and not in beta-two. The lesson generalizes past beta blockers: if you screen on equilibrium affinity at a conserved pocket, you are measuring the one property least likely to discriminate, and you are blind to the vestibule and the dynamics, which here are doing most of the work.

Seventh, a mechanism paper on inhibitory plasticity. Inhibitory long-term potentiation — moderate NMDA receptor activation strengthening GABAergic synapses — has a well-described early phase of trafficking existing receptors and gephyrin, and a later phase, forty-five to ninety minutes out, that requires new protein synthesis. What has been missing is the excitation-transcription coupling in between. This group shows it runs through calcineurin anchored to AKAP-seventy-nine-slash-one-fifty, which dephosphorylates the transcription factor NFAT-c-three, which translocates to the nucleus at about three minutes and sits there until roughly thirteen. Knocking down NFAT-c-three abolishes the potentiation of miniature inhibitory current frequency, the increases in gephyrin and GABA-A receptor clustering, and the rise in gephyrin and receptor subunit protein — all of it rescued by a human construct resistant to the rodent short hairpin.

Two things make this clean. Basal inhibitory transmission, basal clustering and total messenger RNA levels are all untouched, so this is a plasticity-specific pathway rather than a general handicap. And the mechanism is derepression: the transcription factor represses two microRNAs that otherwise block translation of the receptor subunit and gephyrin messages. The neuron is not transcribing more receptor; it is releasing a brake on translating the message it already has. A knock-in mouse with the calcineurin-binding region of the anchoring protein mutated places the anchoring upstream of all of it.

Eighth and ninth, a psychedelics pair that cuts in opposite directions. The first, from a group working with experienced practitioners, tracked sixty-four-channel EEG and minute-resolution subjective ratings across a naturalistic high-ventilation breathwork session in twenty-five people. The neural changes track what a serotonergic psychedelic does: broadband cortical desynchronization, increased signal complexity, reduced alpha power, and a flattening of the cortical processing hierarchy. Those changes correlated with reported depth of the altered state minute by minute. The controls are what elevate this: they analyzed only resting epochs interleaved through the session, so the measurements are not contaminated by the breathing itself or the music; and separate music-listening and progressive-muscle-relaxation conditions, run with the same facilitator in the same room with the same music, produced neither the subjective scores nor the neural changes. So the cortical state is reachable without a drug — which, if you take the receptor pharmacology seriously, is a genuinely awkward result and a useful one.

The second is the deflationary half. A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of an ayahuasca-inspired DMT-plus-harmine formulation versus placebo across a three-day meditation retreat, thirty-nine people, resting-state scans before and after. The analysis estimates control energy — the modeled input cost of driving the brain from one functional state to another over a structural connectome. Acutely, psychedelics are thought to flatten that landscape. Does it persist into the afterglow? Globally, no: no systematic shift in the distribution of transition costs. Mean control energy rose after the retreat in both arms under one modeling regime, which reads as a retreat effect rather than a drug effect. What survives correction is narrow and regime-dependent — a central executive network increase in the verum group under the long-timescale lens, a visual network decrease under the short one.

And that regime-dependence is the finding. Two choices of stabilization timescale, applied to the same scans, surface different networks, and the networks that show up under each lens line up with their positions on the known hierarchy of intrinsic cortical timescales. A single arbitrary choice of that parameter would have given you one answer and hidden the other. The authors call it exploratory and small, which it is, with nineteen in the active arm.

Tenth, the cleanest diagnostic on the list. Protein language models produce accurate contact maps from single sequences, and the standard interpretation is that they have internalized coevolutionary statistics — the same signal a Potts model extracts from an alignment. Two researchers tested that claim with a global bijective relabeling of the amino acid alphabet: rename every residue consistently across every sequence in a family. Column entropies, pairwise edit distances, every alignment-level statistic is unchanged. A pseudolikelihood Potts model is provably invariant, and in practice returns a bit-identical score matrix. The language models collapse — ESM-two at six hundred fifty million parameters keeps two of its own thirty-eight predicted contacts, an overlap of five percent against a chance rate of two and a half.

That holds across architectures and from eight million to three billion parameters. Scaling makes the models more responsive to biochemical class, but does not restore invariance. The conclusion follows by contrapositive and does not depend on anyone's accuracy number: a model whose contacts change under relabeling is not computing them from column statistics alone. These models are anchored on the literal letters — which means the contact map is not the evidence of coevolutionary learning it is routinely cited as.

To close, the same failure, in a setting where it would kill someone. An audit of five open medical vision-language models on six thousand forty-eight chest radiograph questions, balanced by sex, age, finding and label. Two of the five — HuatuoGPT-Vision and Qwen-two-point-five-VL — detect zero of four hundred thirty-two pneumothorax-positive films. Not few. Zero. They answer no to all eight hundred sixty-four pneumothorax cases, balanced accuracy exactly at chance, while two other models on the identical images detect thirty-eight and sixty-seven percent. It is not prompting, not resolution, not label noise, not scale: four prompt formats, native resolution, single-label cases, a tenfold scale increase that gets you to four percent, and a replication on an independent dataset with radiologist-drawn pixel masks where the failing models miss every confirmed case, including all one hundred forty-four of the largest lesions.

Two details make it worse and more interesting. The models are not hedging. They produce fluent, anatomically specific text naming the pleural line of a collapsed lung, and then deny it, with high confidence and no abstention — a clinician reading that output has no signal that anything is wrong. And a linear probe recovers pneumothorax from the hidden states where recall is zero, strongest in early layers and fading by the decision layer. The finding is represented inside the model and suppressed on the way out. Which is almost hopeful, since it suggests the fix is at the output rather than requiring better perception.

That is the thread, and it has run through every item today. The mouse slice, the food lever, the orthosteric pocket, the promoter, the global knockout, the single timescale, the canonical alphabet, the aggregate accuracy — each of those is a measuring instrument that someone mistook for the thing being measured. The papers worth reading are the ones that went and changed the instrument to see what moved.
