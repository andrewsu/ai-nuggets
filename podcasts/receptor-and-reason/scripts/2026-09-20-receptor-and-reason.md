## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. I'm your host, and this is your daily pass through CNS pharmacology, psychiatric and neurology drug discovery, and the computational work closing in on both. Ten items today, and the thread is decomposition — the question of when breaking a thing into its parts buys you explanatory power, and when it just breaks the thing. Two of today's papers are literally about that trade-off. Most of the rest turn on it without saying so.

The lead is a negative result, and it is the most informative thing that happened this week. On Monday the fourteenth, Contineum Therapeutics reported that the Phase 2 MOONLIGHT-1 trial of the compound they call PIPE three-oh-seven — developed by Johnson and Johnson under a global license — did not achieve its primary endpoint in major depressive disorder. The compound is a selective inhibitor of the M1 muscarinic receptor. The primary endpoint was change from baseline in the MADRS total score at day five, in about a hundred and seven adults with recurrent MDD, dosed as monotherapy, with no more than two prior treatments in the current episode. It was well tolerated with no new safety signals. J and J says it is still evaluating the prespecified exploratory endpoints.

Here is why this matters more than a typical Phase 2 miss. For roughly two decades the most tantalizing loose thread in rapid-acting antidepressant research has been scopolamine. Intravenous scopolamine produced fast, substantial antidepressant effects in crossover trials at the NIMH, and the field's working hypothesis was that the active ingredient in that non-selective muscarinic blockade is M1. If that were true, a clean selective M1 antagonist should reproduce the effect without the rest of scopolamine's baggage. MOONLIGHT-1 is the first properly powered, placebo-controlled, monotherapy test of exactly that proposition — and the design was built around the hypothesis rather than hedged. A day-five primary with a day-two secondary is a bet on rapid onset. They were not looking for a slow monoaminergic signal and missing it by accident.

So the decomposition failed. Pulling M1 out of scopolamine's receptor profile and dosing it alone did not carry the antidepressant effect with it. There are three readings and they are not equally comfortable. One is that the scopolamine effect needs more than M1 — M2, M4, some combination, or the peripheral and subjective effects that make blinding in scopolamine trials notoriously leaky. Two is that the scopolamine literature was always thinner than its reputation, built on small crossover samples, and this is what regression to the mean looks like. Three is a dose or exposure problem, and here I'd note that Contineum ran a carbon-eleven PIPE three-oh-seven PET occupancy study back in 2021, so receptor occupancy in humans is a question they can actually answer — which makes the exploratory dataset worth waiting for rather than dismissing.

The irony worth sitting with: the muscarinic system just produced the first genuinely new antipsychotic mechanism in fifty years with xanomeline, an M1 and M4 agonist. Agonism at the same receptor family, opposite indication, and that one worked. Direction of travel at a receptor is not a detail.

Staying with novel antidepressant targets, a paper in Molecular Psychiatry takes the nociceptin system across three species in one study, which is rarer than it should be. The background is that chronic stress upregulates nociceptin receptors, and NOP antagonism has shown antidepressant and anti-anhedonic signals. What has been missing is the reward mechanism. So they ran three studies. In mice, early-life adversity reduced sucrose preference, produced enduring and sex-dependent changes in effort-related reward behavior, and raised prepronociceptin expression in the ventral tegmental area — and in females, but not males, in dorsal striatum as well. In rats, a nociceptin receptor antagonist enhanced reward learning on a touchscreen probabilistic reward task. And in depressed humans, eight weeks of the same antagonist modulated choice consistency on the effort expenditure for rewards task relative to placebo.

The architecture is the point: the same construct, reward responsiveness, probed with translationally matched tasks at each level. That is how you build a mechanism rather than a correlation. Two honest caveats, though. In the rat study only the top dose, thirty milligrams per kilogram, enhanced reward learning — lower doses did nothing, and a single effective dose at the ceiling of a range is a thin dose-response. And the human result is described as modulating choice consistency, which is a softer and more ambiguous outcome than "improved motivation." A cross-species story is only as strong as its weakest species, and here that is the humans.

Third, and still on reward circuitry in depression, a proof-of-principle randomized controlled trial in Molecular Psychiatry asked whether people can learn to self-modulate their own dopaminergic midbrain. Sixty-two unmedicated participants, both depressed and healthy controls, randomized to active or sham seven-Tesla functional MRI biofeedback, with the ventral tegmental area as the target region — read out in real time and fed back to the participant, who updates cognitive strategy on the fly. High field here is not decoration; the VTA is small and awkward, and the signal-to-noise gain is what makes the region tractable at all.

They used a factor analysis across their clinical measures rather than picking one scale, which is a defensible move in a heterogeneous disorder, and it yielded a single latent improvement factor. That factor was significantly better in active depressed participants than sham immediately post-training and at twenty-four hours. It was diminished and no longer significant at seven days and at thirty days. Depressed mood and negative affect improved; positive affect did not.

Now the part that decides how much to believe. VTA activation did not change over time in the active group relative to sham at the group level. The link between VTA regulation and clinical improvement rests on an across-subject correlation of about zero-point-two-seven, and the authors report — in their own discussion, unprompted — that an exploratory mediation test was non-significant and the association did not survive a within-subject change-versus-change analysis. That is unusually candid reporting, and it should be read as such rather than as a failure. But it means the mechanism claim is not yet supported by this trial. What the trial does show is that a sham-controlled neurofeedback protocol can move mood for about a day. Whether it moved it through the VTA is open.

Fourth, a short one, and a receptor most people are not tracking for psychiatry. Also in Molecular Psychiatry, a group reports that the histamine H2 receptor on parvalbumin-positive neurons of the substantia nigra pars reticulata bidirectionally regulates risk-taking behavior in mice. Losing H2 receptor function increases risky exploration, and the effect routes specifically through the SNr parvalbumin cells that project to superior colliculus. The translational hook is the rescue: pharmacological activation of the H2 receptor within the SNr normalized excessive risky exploration in dopamine transporter heterozygous mice, a well-established ADHD-like model. Histamine H2 ligands are old, cheap, and peripherally familiar — the central pharmacology has been badly under-explored, and a specific cell type and projection with a behavioral rescue is a real lead rather than a receptor fishing expedition.

Fifth, the decomposition theme becomes explicit. A paper in Molecular Psychiatry takes S-ketamine-induced hallucination-like states in mice and refuses to treat them as one phenomenon. They combined an auditory discrimination task with pose analysis to separate false auditory threat responses from general behavioral disorganization, and they did the control work properly — sucrose preference, loss of righting reflex, and prepulse inhibition to rule out anhedonia, anesthesia, and basic auditory deficits as explanations.

What they find is two dissociable pathways converging on the caudal striatum. The basolateral amygdala projection supports salience-weighted perceptual decisions, and when S-ketamine aberrantly recruits it, the animal makes auditory false alarms — it reacts to a threat that is not there. The prefrontal projection instead shapes behavioral expression, and when that one is dysregulated you get disorganized action patterns. Same drug, same terminal region, two different symptoms, and bidirectional chemogenetic manipulation in drug-naive animals reproduces the split. At the network level the caudal striatum shifts from a sparse, high-contrast state to a high-frequency, low-amplitude, diffusely coupled one — less integrative precision, which is a satisfying formal description of what a hallucination-like state might be. Auditory cortex inputs were not broadly suppressed, so this is pathway-specific rather than global NMDA receptor blockade washing everything out. And dexmedetomidine co-administration restored the pathway-level temporal dynamics without simply flattening activity, which is a directly actionable finding for anyone giving ketamine clinically.

Sixth, from bioRxiv this week, and my pick of the preprints. A group in Turin profiled the prefrontal cortex transcriptome of mice at ninety minutes and twenty-four hours after a single dose of LSD, and argue the resulting program looks like hormesis — adaptive stress — rather than damage. At ninety minutes they see the expected immediate-early gene burst, Fos and Egr and Nr4a and Arc and BDNF. But the dominant features are not neuronal. They are metabolic and nutrient-sensing genes, core circadian clock genes, a striking myelin and oligodendrocyte precursor signature, and endothelial and vascular homeostasis genes. By twenty-four hours the immediate-early genes have subsided and what remains is circadian, glucocorticoid-responsive, and TGF-beta modulatory. Ninety-nine genes are regulated at both time points — a persistent core defined by circadian regulation, corticosteroid sensitivity, carbohydrate metabolism, and TGF-beta signaling.

That is a genuinely different picture from the plasticity-centric account of psychedelics, and the glial and vascular component is the part I'd want followed up. But I want to be precise about what the hormesis claim rests on, because the authors are more careful than their title. They compared their signature against nineteen external datasets split into hormetic stressors — fasting, caloric restriction, exercise, ischemic preconditioning — and chronic stress paradigms like social defeat and chronic restraint. The overlap was highly significant with both. Their bootstrap meta-analysis and their threshold-free curves found no significant difference in how strongly LSD resembled one class versus the other. In fact hormetic and chronic stress datasets substantially overlap each other, which they show — there is a generic stress core that makes the two hard to tell apart at all. The hormesis conclusion survives only in the direction-resolved analysis, where genes LSD turns up match genes adaptive stressors turn up. That is a real result and a reasonable inference, but it is one analysis carrying the headline, and the dose here is five hundred micrograms per kilogram, which is high. Bulk sequencing also means the cell-type attribution is inferred, not observed.

Seventh, and this follows directly, because it decomposes the psychedelic effect in behavior the way Turin decomposed it in transcription. In Progress in Neuro-Psychopharmacology, a Krakow group recorded ultrasonic vocalizations from male rats given the 5-HT2A agonist DOI, LSD, lisuride — a 5-HT2A agonist that is not hallucinogenic — and amphetamine. Nearly forty-eight thousand calls, detected with DeepSqueak and clustered without supervision using variational autoencoder embeddings into four categories: twenty-two kilohertz alarm calls, and three fifty-kilohertz types.

Head-twitch response, the standard rodent proxy for hallucinogenic activity, appeared only after DOI and high-dose LSD. Lisuride and amphetamine produced none, as expected. But the vocal repertoire told a different story. The compounds did not merely call more — they shifted which calls. And crucially, high-dose LSD, lisuride, and amphetamine all increased occupancy of the frequency-modulated clusters associated with positive affect. Lisuride is the one to stare at. A non-hallucinogenic 5-HT2A agonist produced the positive-affect vocal shift without producing head-twitch. The authors are appropriately restrained — they say vocalization should be a complementary affective readout, not a substitute marker of psychedelic-like activity. But for the non-hallucinogenic analog programs, which are betting that therapeutic and perceptual effects can be pulled apart, that dissociation is precisely the experiment, and the dissociation held.

Eighth, over to the computational side, staying in psychiatry. A team from Georgia Tech, UT Austin and Northwell Health built CliniCIRCA, a multi-stage LLM framework for reconstructing longitudinal mental-health patient journeys from discharge summaries. The problem is real and specific: a discharge summary contains a patient's trajectory, but not as a timeline — events are repeated, narrated out of order, and anchored only by relative expressions like "three weeks prior to admission." There are no event-level timestamps to extract.

From fourteen thousand eight hundred and eighty-two mental health admissions in MIMIC-three, they built a benchmark of fifty-two discharge summaries on which the system produced nearly sixteen thousand temporally tagged events. A clinician-in-the-loop pass corrected six hundred and twenty-nine errors to make gold-standard labels, and the corrected timelines then drive a summarization stage that compresses each source about one-and-a-half times into a date-grouped chronological record. They scaled to a thousand silver-standard timelines and showed that instruction tuning on them improves five open-weight models over zero-shot and few-shot prompting.

What I like is that the clinician correction is reported as a number rather than buried — six hundred and twenty-nine errors across sixteen thousand events tells you the unsupervised output was decent but nowhere near deployable, which is the honest framing. The caveat is scale: fifty-two verified summaries is a small gold set to hang a benchmark on, and MIMIC-three is old intensive-care data, so how this transfers to contemporary outpatient psychiatric records is untested.

Ninth, the paper that makes today's thread explicit, and my favorite piece of AI work this week. It is called "Rethinking Multi-Agent Collaboration: When More Is Less," from Nanjing University, and it asks when decomposing a task across multiple agents actually helps now that single-agent harnesses have become very good. Their formal move is to model a task trajectory as a dependency graph and note that decomposition is only cheap where the graph has bridge edges — sparse dependencies. Where steps are tightly coupled, splitting the task cuts the dependency chain and you pay coordination overhead for nothing.

The numbers back it cleanly. On Terminal Bench two-point-one, which has dense step-to-step dependencies, a single agent resolves about seventy-seven percent of tasks, and three published multi-agent frameworks come in at sixty-seven to seventy-one. Decomposition actively hurt. On the sparsely-dependent benchmarks it flips — on one of them the single agent scores about fifty-four and the best multi-agent system reaches about seventy-two. Their own system, SAIGE, grows the collaboration graph incrementally from execution feedback rather than committing to a topology up front, and it is the only method that avoids the penalty on coupled tasks while capturing the gains on decomposable ones. Their ablation is the part to remember: neither a bigger agent pool nor deeper recursion consistently improved anything.

For anyone building agentic pipelines for biology — and that is most of this audience — the operational lesson is that the right question is not how many agents but whether your workflow has sparse dependencies. A literature-triage or broad-screen task probably does. A multi-step analysis where each stage conditions on the last almost certainly does not, and a single well-equipped agent will beat your orchestration.

And to close, the lightest item, which is the same lesson in hardware. A collaboration between Tokyo, RIKEN and NYU built WetRobo, a deliberately unglamorous robot kit for biology labs: one arm, an incubator, a reagent bottle with a cap, a Petri dish, some existing motion code, recorded teleoperation demonstrations, and a skill file. The premise is that an experimentalist should be able to give a natural-language task without collecting local training data or training a network — a coding agent observes the local setup, writes a program, and runs it.

They demonstrate three tasks: lifting a Petri dish lid, removing a bottle cap, opening the incubator door. The result that makes the paper is the transfer test. The coding agent completed the cap task in both of two different laboratories. A vision-language-action policy fine-tuned on demonstrations from the first lab succeeded there and failed to transfer to the second. It is three tasks in two labs, so calibrate accordingly — this is a proof of concept, not a platform. But the argument generalizes past robotics: rather than train a policy per environment, ship a kit and let an agent adapt it on arrival. Adaptation at deployment beat specialization at training, which is a version of the same trade-off SAIGE found, and a decent note to end on.
