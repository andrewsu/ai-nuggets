## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. I'm your host, and this is your daily pass through CNS pharmacology, psychiatric and neurology drug discovery, and the computational machinery closing in on both. Eleven items today, and the thread is the other site — the pocket you weren't looking at, the subunit that isn't supposed to be there, the sign that flips when you add sex to the model, the part of the algorithm that fails while the part you were watching looks fine.

The best paper of the week is a preprint that de-orphanizes a brain GPCR and then uses it to extend working memory, in mice and in macaques. The receptor is G-P-R twelve, a thalamic orphan the same group previously pulled out of an unbiased genetic mapping study as a potent enhancer of working-memory maintenance. Prior attempts to de-orphanize it produced conflicting stories, including a model in which the receptor activates itself — extracellular loop two folding into the orthosteric pocket as its own ligand, the way G-P-R fifty-two and G-P-R twenty-one do.

The structure says otherwise. In their two-point-four-angstrom cryo-EM map of the human receptor bound to a mini-G-protein heterotrimer, extracellular loop two is not sitting in the pocket — it's cradling a detergent molecule that slid in during purification. What the structure does reveal is a second, membrane-embedded pocket at the interface of transmembrane helices three and five, the same class of lipid-facing cleft that turns out to be regulatory on the free-fatty-acid receptor G-P-R forty and, recently, on CB1. The mutagenesis makes the case. Point mutations in the orthosteric pocket, including residues in direct contact with the bound detergent, did essentially nothing. Mutations in the side pocket moved activity hard in both directions — one serine-to-phenylalanine substitution nearly abolished signalling, two others roughly doubled it. The switch is not in the orthosteric site.

To find the ligand, they went to the tissue: an epitope-tagged knock-in mouse, receptor immunoprecipitated out of mediodorsal thalamus with twenty animals pooled per condition, lipidomics on whatever came down with it. A clade of twenty-carbon, four-double-bond eicosanoids came out enriched, and screening those gave exactly one hit — anandamide, which roughly tripled receptor activity over basal. Arachidonic acid, lipoxin A-four, and two-arachidonoylglycerol did nothing. Neither did anandamide phosphate, nor synaptamide, which is the same ethanolamide headgroup on a twenty-two-carbon, six-double-bond chain. The receptor wants an ethanolamine head and a twenty-four acyl tail and is unforgiving about both. Thermophoresis confirmed direct binding, and cannabidiol turned out to be an inhibitor.

Two caveats before the in vivo work. The EC-fifty came in around a hundred and seventy micromolar, which the authors flag as probably an underestimate given how badly a lipid partitions in an aqueous assay, but it's still a number to hold lightly. And there's an unresolved coupling question: the structure was solved on a stimulatory G-protein and the cell assay reads out cAMP accumulation, while the in vivo model the paper proposes is Gi-over-Go signalling producing cAMP suppression. Both can be true of a promiscuous receptor, but the paper doesn't reconcile them.

The in vivo data are the reason to care. Using a genetically encoded cAMP sensor and fibre photometry in mediodorsal thalamus while mice ran a delayed non-match-to-place task, they found that receptor activation produces a slow downward ramp in cAMP that persists for up to a minute and tracks, trial by trial, the duration of memory maintenance. That is the thing the neuromodulation field has predicted for thirty years and not observed: a molecular state whose timescale matches the behaviour. Intraperitoneal anandamide extended both the cAMP suppression and memory retention, specifically at long delays, and both effects were absent in knockout mice — which learn the task fine, fall to chance at sixty seconds, and are un-rescued by drug.

Two things lift this above a nice mouse paper. Perfusing anandamide directly into thalamus while imaging prefrontal cortex through a gradient-index lens, they saw no change in overall event rate, no change in the fraction of neurons tuned to each task epoch, and no change in cue-period activity. What changed was the mean activity of neurons tuned to the second half of the delay, and only them. Thalamic inhibitory signalling isn't turning cortex up; it's selectively sustaining the representations that have to survive longest. And then primates: fourteen rhesus macaques on a visuospatial working-memory task with delays out to a minute, given the FAAH inhibitor U-R-B five-nine-seven to raise endogenous anandamide, with a full dose-response in each animal. Robust improvement, on the classic inverted-U.

The framing matters. Cannabinoids are the textbook example of a drug class that degrades working memory, and that reputation is built on THC and on two-A-G at CB1 in hippocampus. Anandamide is a comparatively weak CB1 agonist, and this receptor is thalamic — so what you have is a lipid signalling axis in a different structure, engaged by a different endocannabinoid, running the other way. And the druggable handle is that membrane-embedded side pocket, which shares little sequence with the closest family members and therefore looks like a genuinely selective allosteric target. If you work on cognitive enhancement, this is the most interesting new target proposal of the year.

Staying with receptors doing something they're not supposed to. A preprint from the De Biasi and Dani groups at Penn reports that chronic alcohol recruits GluN-three-A-containing NMDA receptors into adult ventral tegmental dopamine neurons. Eight weeks of intermittent two-bottle choice on twenty percent ethanol, then whole-cell recordings. NMDA-evoked currents went down, more so in withdrawal, and outward rectification went down with them — a biophysical signature rather than just a magnitude change, and that's what sent them looking at GluN-three-A. The proportion of dopamine neurons with functional GluN-three-A-containing receptors rose after chronic exposure, and in withdrawal every neuron they tested had them. Glycine alone depolarized these cells and drove firing, meaning the recruited receptors behave as excitatory glycine-gated channels, and all of it was gone in GluN-three-A null mice. GluN-three-A is a developmental subunit largely switched off in adulthood, whose job is to make NMDA receptors weaker and less calcium-permeable. Finding it reinstated in adult VTA dopamine neurons by drinking reframes the long-standing observation that chronic alcohol blunts NMDA currents — that blunting may not be loss of canonical receptors so much as dilution by a non-canonical subunit that brings its own excitatory conductance. One caveat: this preprint went up yesterday and the full text hasn't rendered, so I'm on the abstract, and I'd want the pharmacology before treating the every-neuron-in-withdrawal number as firm.

From subunits to circuits, and a paper in Molecular Psychiatry on which cells in the bed nucleus of the stria terminalis actually drive arousal. The BNST is usually discussed as an anxiety structure, and it's overwhelmingly GABAergic, which made it an awkward candidate for wake promotion. This group went subpopulation by subpopulation with channelrhodopsin in CRH, CCK, and somatostatin driver lines, and only the CRH neurons did anything. Thirty-hertz stimulation during non-REM sleep produced wake transitions at two-and-a-half seconds latency with a hundred percent transition probability, against sixty-seven seconds at five hertz, and an hour of intermittent stimulation roughly tripled time awake. Photometry lines up: CRH neurons run high during wake and REM, low in non-REM, and their activity turns before the state transition rather than after.

What makes this pharmacologically interesting is the EEG phenotype and the stress arm. Chemogenetic activation raised waking alpha and suppressed high delta — the spectral signature of hyperarousal, the same profile reported in human insomnia and in mice with hypocretin receptor two deleted from dopamine neurons. Chemogenetic inhibition shortened non-REM latency and cut wake time after forced swim, cage change, and predator odour. So this is a genetically defined population downstream of the paraventricular thalamus that you could in principle target for stress-induced insomnia — a far more common problem than primary insomnia, and one badly served by hypnotics that add sedation on top of an active arousal drive. Limitations, which the authors name: mechanistic work almost entirely in males, thirty hertz likely exceeds physiological firing rate, and chemogenetics is too slow to separate blunting the initial stress response from shortening the arousal that follows.

Now a large, careful, mostly negative result that is more useful than it looks. Neuropsychopharmacology published an epigenome-wide association study of psychotherapy response in obsessive-compulsive disorder — eight hundred and eighty-nine adults going through the Bergen four-day concentrated exposure and response prevention protocol, plus three hundred and eighty-four controls, with saliva methylation at baseline, post-treatment, and three-month follow-up. Symptom scores fell from about twenty-six to eleven, so the treatment worked. This is by a wide margin the largest study of its kind, designed to get past the candidate-gene era and its decade of irreproducible findings.

Not a single CpG site reached epigenome-wide significance in any analysis — not at baseline, not across time, not in the change scores. What survived were region-level signals: ten differentially methylated regions at baseline predicting later response, twenty-three stable across time points, and three where within-person change tracked improvement. And then the finding that should govern how you read the rest — nine of the ten baseline regions and twenty-two of the twenty-three stable regions had local methylation quantitative trait loci. Those regions are under genetic control; they are, to a first approximation, a slow and noisy genotype assay. Consistent with that, the variants themselves were not associated with response after correction, so even the genetic signal doesn't hold up as a predictor. Cross-tissue saliva-to-brain enrichment was significant for four regions and absent for most. And smoking and age explained substantially more variance in cell composition than treatment response did.

The right reading is not that psychotherapy has an epigenetic signature. It's that at eight hundred and eighty-nine patients, with genome-wide coverage and a real longitudinal design, the effect of an intensive and effective psychological intervention on peripheral DNA methylation is small enough that it clears multiple-testing correction at no single site, and what does emerge is mostly inherited. Every candidate-gene paper in this literature was powered for something much bigger than what's there. The three dynamic regions — including one at the imprinted PEG-three locus — are worth following, because they're the only signals not obviously explained by genotype, and also the ones most sensitive to medication and comorbidity confounding.

Pairing with that, a gene-by-environment study out of Groningen worth a minute for one result. Serotonin-transporter heterozygous knockout rats as a model of the human short allele, with cumulative early-life stress layered on — dams stressed before, during, and after pregnancy, plus maternal separation in the pups. Two things stand out. The stressed dams showed more arched-back licking and grooming, not less, a reminder that maternal stress and maternal care are not the same variable. And there was a three-way interaction between genotype, stress, and sex on anxiety-like behaviour in which the sign flipped: heterozygous stressed females were more anxious than both their unstressed and wild-type counterparts, and heterozygous stressed males were less anxious. Run this in males only, as most of this literature did, and you conclude reduced transporter expression is protective; run it in females only and you get the standard diathesis-stress story. The main effect is an artefact of whichever sex you used. Also abstract-only today; the body hasn't rendered.

One more preclinical item, a clean surprise. A preprint from the Musiek and Karch labs at Washington University crossed a germline complement C-four knockout onto the five-times-FAD amyloidosis model. C-four is the most upregulated complement gene in astrocytes in aging and amyloid pathology, and the prior from the C-one-q and C-three literature is that complement drives microglial plaque clearance — deleting C-three makes plaques worse. Deleting C-four made plaque burden go down substantially, across multiple regions. Neuroinflammation went down, peri-plaque glial clustering went down, and the remaining peri-plaque microglia shifted toward a more reactive disease-associated phenotype. Less clustering but more DAM character is the interesting combination, and it suggests C-four is doing something outside the classical cascade — setting inflammatory tone rather than acting as an opsonin. Given that C-four is the locus behind the schizophrenia complement story, a second, opposite-direction role in amyloid pathology is worth watching. Same caveat: abstract-only today.

Turning to the computational side, and a paper that is better than its own headline number. ADMET-EvO is a self-evolving agent for ADMET prediction built around a five-stage cycle: formalize the endpoint, profile what's actually limiting inference for it — data scarcity, distribution shift, assay noise — branch into competing hypotheses, test interventions along data, feature, and model axes, then carry supported, rejected, and inconclusive outcomes into the next cycle. On the twenty-two-task Therapeutics Data Commons ADMET benchmark it takes the top task-normalized score at ninety-six point seven-seven.

Which sounds decisive until you look at second place: MapLight plus a graph network at ninety-six point six-four. That's thirteen hundredths of a point over tuned gradient boosting on molecular descriptors, which is inside the seed noise. So I wouldn't call this a modelling win. The two results I would call real are elsewhere. Evidence-guided recipe selection cut cumulative fitting time from two-and-a-quarter hours to thirty-seven minutes, with twenty of twenty-two endpoints inside a pre-specified non-inferiority margin — that's an agent learning which endpoints don't deserve expensive treatment, which is genuinely worth automating. And they defined a post-hoc familiarity score, each test molecule's maximum fingerprint similarity to the training set, and showed it tracks error in twenty-one of twenty-two endpoints, with the most-familiar quartile running about fifteen percent below full-test error. That's a label-free applicability-domain signal you can use for abstention, and for a platform that will be asked about compounds nobody has measured, it's worth more than a leaderboard position.

A second agentic-chemistry paper, from Shanghai AI Lab, takes a different architecture: a general reasoning model, a domain-specialized computational-chemistry model they trained themselves, and a structured registry of quantum-chemistry tools in a hypothesis-compute-revise loop. Three case studies. On a published asymmetric organocatalytic reaction it recovered the literature enantio-determining transition states plus three the original paper hadn't reported, with a difference in activation free energies of one-point-nine kilocalories per mole against one-point-six inferred from the experimental eighty-eight percent enantiomeric excess. On an unpublished alpha-iodoboronate carbon-iodine cleavage under blue light, it ruled out thermal pathways needing more than forty-six kilocalories per mole, ruled out two homolysis routes needing a hundred-and-three and seventy-six against the sixty-three-point-six available from a four-hundred-and-fifty-nanometre photon, and landed on excitation of a caesium phosphide species at sixty-four — a tight match. On the third, nickel-catalysed migratory cross-coupling, it distilled an interpretable selectivity descriptor.

The benchmark table is the part worth arguing about. Their domain model beats GPT-five-point-five on computational route generation, sixty-six percent to forty-five, and loses to it on general chemistry knowledge, eighty-three percent. That split is the honest summary of where domain specialization pays: in knowing which calculation to set up, not in knowing chemistry. And the standing caution applies — every one of these validations is adjudicated by density functional theory, so what's demonstrated is that the agent can drive a quantum-chemistry stack competently, not that its mechanistic conclusions are right. Agreement to a third of a kilocalorie per mole on a free-energy difference is partly error cancellation.

Next, my favourite methods result of the week, because it's a failure diagnosis rather than a system. The setup is standard: train a policy to decide which external tools a frozen reasoning model gets, optimize with group-relative policy optimization. The authors point out that in specialist scientific domains the tool library is small — they use four genomic tools, so sixteen possible subsets — and the expectation over actions is therefore exactly computable. G-R-P-O estimates it by sampling anyway. And the failure mode is lovely: the approximation gets worse as training succeeds. As the policy concentrates on its preferred subsets it resamples the same ones, the sampled rewards collide, and the group-normalized advantage goes to zero. They measure it — the fraction of training questions producing no learning signal at all rises from zero-point-two percent under a uniform reference policy to twenty-point-eight percent after G-R-P-O training. One question in five is dead, and the loss curve won't tell you.

Their fix is to score every subset, optimize the exact expectation, and precompute the reward of every question-subset pair into a table so the frozen reasoner never gets called inside the training loop. It wins in all fifteen combinations of five reasoners and three genomic benchmarks, by six-point-seven-five points on average and up to fourteen, while needing forty percent fewer reward evaluations, and on one benchmark it cuts tools invoked per question from two-point-three-six to one-point-four. This only works because sixteen is enumerable and the reward table is finite. But the diagnostic generalizes to anyone doing reinforcement learning over tool choice: if your action space is coarse and your policy is converging, go measure your zero-advantage rate. Note also the headroom that motivates the exercise — the frozen reasoner gets thirty-nine percent with no tools, forty-nine percent with all tools, and seventy-seven percent under a per-question oracle. Giving an agent every tool is barely better than giving it none.

Two to close. The first is an evaluation from SecureBio called ABLE, which asks how well language-model agents can drive biological AI models — structure prediction and inverse folding — through a dual-use protein design workflow on a human-infecting virus. The pathogen, the property being enhanced, and the scoring function are all deliberately redacted. Fifteen frontier models, eight scored execution steps, plus human expert baseliners.

Three findings. Seven of the fifteen models refused every task, which is itself a datum, and one the uplift literature tends not to report. Among the rest, exactly one achieved what they call workflow success, a perfect score at least once on all eight steps. But the per-task rates are the real story: even the best models sat around thirty percent on critical-residue identification and on design-strategy generation, precisely the two steps requiring you to integrate structural biology knowledge with the tool call rather than just make the call. And the human comparison inverts instructively — expert baseliners beat the models handily on hands-on tool execution and lost badly on planning and on interpreting structure-prediction outputs. That is not the profile people assume when they argue about uplift. The models are good at the parts that look like knowledge work and bad at the parts that look like judgment, so the barrier they lower is the literature-and-protocol barrier, not the do-the-science barrier.

And last, lightest, a statistics paper with a cautionary tale inside it. The method is clean: a Bayesian hierarchical model for mutation-drug response that combines a Gaussian Markov random field prior borrowing strength across pathway-connected genes with a horseshoe prior for sparsity, in a conjugate Gibbs sampler with no Metropolis-Hastings steps. Applied to the G-D-S-C two panel — nine hundred and fifty-one cell lines, two hundred and nineteen driver genes, two hundred and ninety-five drugs — it flags a hundred and twenty-six of sixty-four thousand possible pairs, and both prior layers earn their keep on held-out predictive likelihood.

The problem is what it found. The hits concentrate in two genes: EZH2, associated with sensitivity to forty-five of the two hundred and ninety-five drugs, all in the same direction, and KMT2D, thirty-six drugs, also all sensitivity. A mutation that makes a cell line more sensitive to a sixth of every compound you throw at it is not a pharmacogenomic marker. It's a fitness phenotype — chromatin-modifier loss making cells sicker and easier to kill — and it replicates beautifully, thirty-six out of thirty-six directionally in an independent screen. Directional replication of a nonspecific effect just replicates the nonspecificity. The tell is in the tissue-stratified extension, where the real pharmacology finally surfaces: EGFR-inhibitor sensitivity specific to lung, BRAF-dabrafenib sensitivity specific to skin. Those are the known-true positives, and they only appear once you condition on tissue. The general lesson for anyone fitting sparse models to cell-line screens — before interpreting a marker, check how many drugs it likes, because a gene that is good news against everything is telling you about the cells, not the compounds.

The reinforcement-learning failure was in the estimator, not the policy. And a marker that predicted sensitivity to everything predicted nothing. Look at the other site. That's today.
