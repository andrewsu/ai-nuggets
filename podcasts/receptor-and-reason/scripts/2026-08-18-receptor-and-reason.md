## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. I'm your host, and this is your daily tour through CNS pharmacology, psychiatric and neurology drug discovery, and the computational tools closing in on both. Good news after yesterday: bioRxiv's API is back, so we have the preprint side of the net again, and it delivered. Today's episode has a spine to it — a run of papers circling the same uncomfortable question, which is what you do when the receptor you're aiming at doesn't behave the way the simple model says it should. Eleven items.

We open with a preprint out of the University of Wisconsin at Madison that is, to my eye, the most clinically actionable thing on the table this week. It's about drug-resistant epilepsy, and it's a repurposing story. The background is that about a third of people with epilepsy keep having seizures despite medication, and — this is the brutal part — failing your first anti-seizure medication strongly predicts failing the next one, even when the next one has a completely different mechanism. Only ten to fifteen percent of first-line failures respond to the second drug. That pattern says the resistance isn't about the target; it's about the disease state. This group has been arguing for years that part of that state is inflammatory — specifically recurrent JAK-STAT3 activation in chronic epilepsy — and they'd previously shown that transient treatment with tofacitinib, the JAK inhibitor approved for rheumatoid arthritis, durably suppresses seizures in mice. What's new is that they built a preclinical version of the actual clinical decision. They made chronically epileptic mice with repeated low-dose systemic kainic acid, treated them with carbamazepine, and screened for response using the clinical benchmark — a fifty percent or greater reduction in seizure frequency. Thirty-eight percent responded; sixty-two percent did not. Then, in the mice that had already failed carbamazepine, they added tofacitinib on top and kept the carbamazepine going. In that resistant cohort, dual therapy produced a fourteen-fold reduction in median seizure frequency and an eighteen-fold reduction in time spent seizing. Seventy percent of the resistant animals responded, and among the responders the effect was not incremental — it was a hundred-fold drop, with five of seven mice having no observed behavioral seizures at all during the recording window. Two things elevate this above a typical add-on paper. First, the pharmacokinetics are anchored rather than assumed: they measured tofacitinib brain exposure directly, getting a brain-to-plasma ratio of about zero point one two — low, but the resulting brain concentration still exceeds most reported JAK1 potency values. Second, and more interesting to me, the cognitive result. Epilepsy-associated memory impairment is arguably a bigger quality-of-life problem than the seizures, and it's often made worse by the drugs. Tofacitinib restored both spatial working memory and short-term memory to the level of non-epileptic controls — and it did so in the animals whose seizures did not respond. There's no correlation between seizure reduction and memory rescue. That decouples two things the field routinely bundles together: fix the inflammation and you get the cognition back whether or not you get the seizures back. One more detail — the non-responders had their STAT3 phosphorylation suppressed just as thoroughly as the responders, so non-response isn't a target-engagement failure. This is a mouse preprint with small groups and video-scored rather than EEG-scored seizures, and tofacitinib's immunosuppression is not a trivial thing to hand to a pediatric population. But the drug exists, the dose is known, and the trial design writes itself.

Staying with inflammation as the thing standing between you and a working drug, there's a large and, I think, genuinely important preprint from Johns Hopkins on remyelination in multiple sclerosis. The problem it attacks is this. In MS lesions, oligodendrocyte precursor cells are present — they're right there — but they don't mature into myelinating oligodendrocytes. The field has spent a decade screening for compounds that push precursors to differentiate, found several that work beautifully in a dish, then watched them underperform in patients. The hypothesis here is that the screens were run in the wrong environment, testing differentiation in the absence of the inflammation that is the actual obstacle. So the group asked what inflammation does metabolically. Interferon gamma, which is everywhere in MS lesions, flips precursor lipid metabolism from synthesis toward utilization — the cells burn fatty acids instead of making them, and intracellular fatty acid levels drop. That matters because myelin is mostly lipid; a cell asked to build myelin while its lipid pool drains is a cell that can't finish the job. These starved precursors also adopt an immune-like phenotype with antigen-presenting behavior. The same signature shows up in cells from human surgical specimens, mouse demyelination models, and human MS lesion tissue, which is what makes this more than a culture artifact. Then the causal test: simply giving the cells oleic acid, or blocking fatty acid oxidation, restored differentiation and suppressed the immune-like behavior. Metabolism isn't downstream scenery — it's the mechanism. The therapeutic move is to hit the master regulator, and they used GW3965, a liver X receptor agonist, which rebalanced lipid handling and overcame the interferon-gamma differentiation block in both mouse and human-derived precursors. In vivo they used an adoptive-transfer-plus-cuprizone model where inflammation directly blocks repair, and deliberately delayed dosing three days so as not to simply suppress the peripheral immune response and take credit for it. That worked — the immune infiltrate in the corpus callosum was essentially unchanged — and they still got more mature oligodendrocytes and more myelin. So the claim is specific: LXR activation lets remyelination proceed despite an unfavorable immune environment, which the authors say has not been shown for any prior approach. It's also a target with human genetic support, since a loss-of-function variant in the LXR-alpha gene tracks with rapidly progressive MS, and several LXR agonists have already been through clinical development. The honest caveat is that systemic LXR agonism has a long-standing reputation for driving hepatic lipogenesis, so the translational question is less "does the biology work" and more "can you get CNS engagement without the liver."

From inflammation to a receptor behaving badly. There's a paper in the British Journal of Pharmacology on radiprodil that I'd file under "results that should change how you design a dose-escalation." Radiprodil is a GluN2B-selective negative allosteric modulator of the NMDA receptor, currently in clinical development as a pediatric anti-epileptic. The group did whole-cell and outside-out patch clamp on substantia nigra dopaminergic neurons in acute midbrain slices from neonatal rats, and what they found is a concentration-response curve that does not go the way you'd draw it. Radiprodil produces partial inhibition — only about forty percent maximum — reaching that maximum somewhere between thirty and a hundred nanomolar. Then, at higher concentrations, the inhibition gets weaker. It's a bell-shaped curve. To account for it they fit a two-site model: the expected GluN2B site that mediates inhibition, plus a second, lower-affinity site whose occupancy partially reverses it. They also see a small reduction in single-channel current, so part of the effect is on gating, not binding alone. Be careful — this is one neuron type, neonatal, male, and slices are not people. But if it generalizes even partly, the implication is sharp: pushing the dose up in a patient who isn't responding could actively cost you efficacy, and the shape of that curve would depend on which GluN2 subunits the target neurons happen to express. Allosteric modulators are supposed to be the subtle, well-behaved alternative to orthosteric blockers. This is a reminder that "subtle" and "predictable" are different words.

That NMDA thread continues into another British Journal of Pharmacology paper, and this one takes on a clinical puzzle I've always found irritating. Clozapine improves negative symptoms in treatment-resistant schizophrenia. D-cycloserine, a partial agonist at the glycine co-agonist site of the NMDA receptor, also improves negative symptoms. Give them together and negative symptoms get worse. That shouldn't happen if more NMDA potentiation is simply better. The group modeled this in rats with chronic low-dose MK-801, which produces anhedonia measured as reduced sucrose preference, along with reduced GluN2A and GluN2B expression, reduced NMDA-evoked release, and — importantly — elevated baseline extracellular glutamate and D-serine in the orbitofrontal cortex. Clozapine alone or D-cycloserine alone each restored sucrose preference and the evoked release, without touching the elevated baseline levels. Combined, they overshot: sucrose preference got worse, evoked release and receptor expression went up further, and baseline extracellular levels rose further still. And here's the diagnostic experiment — adding memantine, which preferentially blocks the extrasynaptic, tonically activated NMDA receptor pool, reversed the whole thing. So the story is that the therapeutic effect lives in phasic synaptic NMDA transmission, while the harm lives in tonic extrasynaptic overactivation, and combining two potentiators pushes you across from one into the other. For anyone developing glutamatergic agents for negative symptoms, this reframes the goal: you're not trying to maximize NMDA tone, you're trying to steepen the ratio of synaptic to extrasynaptic signaling, and those are very different medicinal-chemistry problems.

Sticking with negative symptoms and cognition in schizophrenia, but coming at them through ion channels rather than glutamate, there's a paper in the Journal of Psychopharmacology on AUT00206. This is a selective positive modulator of Kv3.1 and Kv3.2 potassium channels, and the rationale is tidy: those channels sit predominantly on parvalbumin interneurons, where they enable the fast repolarization that makes fast-spiking possible. If your model of schizophrenia cognition runs through parvalbumin-cell dysfunction and disordered gamma synchrony — and yesterday we covered a review arguing that a whole set of unrelated-looking vulnerabilities converge on exactly that cell type — then restoring the channel that lets those cells spike quickly is a mechanistically direct intervention rather than a downstream one. They tested acute AUT00206 in female Lister Hooded rats after sub-chronic phencyclidine with a washout, which is a reasonably well-validated model for the cognitive and negative-symptom domains rather than the positive ones. At ten and thirty milligrams per kilogram it significantly attenuated deficits across three tasks: novel object recognition for short-term recognition memory, reversal learning for cognitive flexibility, and social interaction as a proxy for the negative-symptom domain. What makes it worth attention beyond the rat data is that AUT00206 is a real clinical-stage compound with human exposure behind it, so the road is already partly built. Caveats are the usual ones and they're real: acute dosing, one model, one sex, behavioral proxies for constructs that don't translate cleanly.

Now to the circuit level, and a paper in Neuropsychopharmacology from Joshua Gordon's group at NIMH on SETD1A, one of the highest-confidence loss-of-function risk genes for schizophrenia. The question is which circuit its haploinsufficiency actually breaks. The dominant expectation, inherited from work on the 22q11.2 deletion model, is that spatial working memory deficits should show up as reduced hippocampal-prefrontal synchrony. So they recorded simultaneously across prefrontal cortex, hippocampus, and the nucleus reuniens — the midline thalamic nucleus that's the main relay between the other two — while mice did a delayed non-match-to-sample task. Behaviorally, the mutants learned the task normally and were fine when delay durations varied unpredictably; they only underperformed when the delay was held constant across a session, which reads as a failure to settle into an optimal strategy rather than a raw memory deficit. Physiologically, hippocampal-prefrontal theta synchrony was preserved. Intact. The abnormality was instead in the reuniens-to-prefrontal circuit: reduced beta synchrony during the delay, and blunted bidirectional modulation of beta and gamma synchrony across task epochs. In wild-type mice, how much a given trial's beta-gamma synchrony shifted from the sample epoch into the delay predicted whether that trial was correct. In the mutants, that relationship was gone. So two schizophrenia risk variants that both impair working memory do it through different circuits — a caution for anyone hoping a single synchrony biomarker indexes genetic risk generically, and an argument for putting the thalamic reuniens on the list of nodes worth stimulating or drugging.

Circuits that generate percepts from expectations bring us to a preprint from Yale on people who use psychedelics. The clinical issue is persisting perceptual abnormalities — the visual disturbances that outlast the acute experience, the phenomenon underlying the HPPD diagnosis — the main psychotic-like side effect of a drug class marching toward approval. They surveyed a hundred and eighty-six naturalistic users about their history and current symptoms, ran them through a visual conditioned hallucinations task — pairing a tone with a faint stimulus until people see the stimulus when it isn't there — and fit a computational model to the behavior. Users with past or current perceptual abnormalities had started younger, used higher doses, had lower visual thresholds, hallucinated more readily and with more confidence, and had worse sensory discrimination. The interesting part is the model. In psychosis research, conditioned hallucinations are usually explained by overweighting of priors relative to sensory evidence. Psychedelics, under the prevailing framework, are supposed to do the opposite — relax priors. So which is it? Their answer is neither, exactly: the parameter that tracked both past and current symptoms, and that statistically mediated the relationship between dose and symptoms, was reduced decision precision. Prior weighting rose only equivocally, in the way you'd expect when priors and sensory evidence both get more precise together. Their reading is that these are noisy visual systems biased toward detection, in which priors act as templates converting noise into an expected percept. It's cross-sectional, self-reported, and naturalistic, so no causality — heavier users may differ in ways that predate the drug. But it's a testable computational account of a side effect the field currently handles with a shrug, and if it holds, screening on something like decision precision before dosing becomes a concrete idea rather than a wish.

Turning to computational pharmacology proper, there's a preprint from SandboxAQ on finding the first selective ligands for SV2C. Quick anchor: SV2C is a synaptic vesicle glycoprotein enriched in basal ganglia dopaminergic neurons, where it shapes dopamine storage and release, with solid genetic and pathological links to Parkinson's. Its sibling SV2A is the levetiracetam and brivaracetam target — so the family is drugged, but nobody has a selective SV2C probe, which means nobody can really test what SV2C does pharmacologically. There's no full-length SV2C structure either, so the campaign started by building a homology model on SV2A cryo-EM templates and mapping its conformational landscape with molecular dynamics, plain and Gaussian-accelerated, apo and bound to known SV2 ligands. They validated a convolutional-neural-network scoring function retrospectively against a curated SV2A benchmark — correlation about zero point seven two against experimental potencies — then ran nearly six million in-stock commercial compounds down to ninety-four candidates. Seventy-one were tested with a thermal shift assay plus a radioligand competition assay, yielding twenty-two actives, a thirty-one percent hit rate, which is very good for a screen this size. The best compound has a binding affinity around three micromolar at SV2C with better than tenfold selectivity over SV2A and about twelvefold over SV2B. Micromolar is a starting point, not a drug, and these are binders that haven't been functionally characterized yet. But the docking predicts a common binding mode anchored by a conserved tryptophan cage, and that prediction was independently confirmed against an unpublished cryo-EM structure to within about eight-tenths of an angstrom in the binding site — which is the kind of external check that makes me take the rest of the pipeline seriously.

Which brings us to today's agentic-AI item, a paper on arXiv from Zhejiang University and Tencent called PertMind, and I like it because it solves a supervision problem rather than an architecture problem. If you want a language model that reasons well about biology, the current recipe is to pay experts to write reasoning traces — expensive, and worse, it bakes in a human's assumptions about which intermediate steps count as good reasoning. Their observation is that a cellular perturbation atlas already is a reinforcement-learning environment. You apply a compound to a cell line, a latent cascade unfolds, and the assay reports whether a given gene went up, down, or didn't reliably move. That terminal endpoint is a computable reward no annotator had to write. So they took the Tahoe-100M atlas — roughly a hundred million single-cell profiles with precomputed differential expression — seeded a four-billion-parameter open model with a small set of filtered trajectories, then trained with rewards at the gene level, the pathway level, and on output format. The claim that makes it interesting is transfer. Trained only on forward perturbation-response prediction, the model then handles, with no task-specific training, reverse perturbation identification — inferring which perturbation produced an observed cell-state change, where it approaches a purpose-built specialist and holds up better across the harder perturbations — plus double-perturbation reasoning it never saw, and phenotypic-screen hit prioritization, where feeding its generated biology brief to three different frontier models improved all three. General language ability barely degraded. The honest limitation, which the authors state, is that the reward only ever sees the endpoint. Nothing checks whether the mechanistic narrative in the middle is true. So you get a model that is more often right, with no guarantee that it's right for the right reasons — which is a familiar tension, but a sharper one when the output is a mechanistic story a human is going to act on.

Two shorter ones to finish. First, a preprint from UT Dallas that settles a long-running argument about where cannabinoid analgesia actually comes from. The strategy of peripherally restricted cannabinoid agonists — keeping the drug out of the brain to avoid intoxication and tolerance — assumes CB1 receptors on peripheral sensory neurons are sufficient for analgesia. The group built the clean test: a CB1 knockout mouse engineered so the receptor can be switched back on in specific cell types, crossed so it's restored only in Nav1.8-expressing nociceptors. Systemic WIN55,212-2, the workhorse non-selective cannabinoid agonist, produced robust tail-flick analgesia in wild-types and nothing in either the global knockouts or the nociceptor-rescue animals. So nociceptor CB1 alone is not sufficient for acute analgesia — a real negative result for the peripheral-restriction strategy, at least in acute pain. Inflammatory pain showed no difference either way. But after nerve injury, the global knockouts developed mechanical and thermal hypersensitivity earlier than either the rescue mice or wild-types, meaning nociceptor CB1 does restrain the development of neuropathic pain. Different job in a different pain state, and the tool they built is going to be useful well beyond this paper.

And we'll close on a nice three-step piece of pharmacovigilance in the British Journal of Pharmacology, which starts with a mortality database and ends at an ion channel. Across deaths where synthetic cannabinoid receptor agonists were detected, the most commonly co-detected medication with long-QT liability was methadone. Then the signal that motivates everything else: in deaths involving both, the median fatal blood methadone concentration was significantly lower than in deaths attributed to methadone alone. Something is lowering the toxicity threshold. They took it to isolated perfused guinea pig hearts, where methadone alone prolonged the QT interval, the synthetic cannabinoid 5F-ADB alone did not, and the two together prolonged it significantly more than methadone by itself. And then to the channel, where modeling and patch clamp show 5F-ADB is a low-affinity hERG blocker — not enough to do damage alone, but enough to stack. The translation is immediate and unglamorous: people on opioid agonist therapy who also use synthetic cannabinoids are running a lower margin on methadone than their dose implies, and nobody is screening for it.

That's the show — from a JAK inhibitor rescuing drug-resistant seizures to a bell-shaped allosteric curve to a language model that learns biology from what the assay actually measured. Back tomorrow with Receptor and Reason.
