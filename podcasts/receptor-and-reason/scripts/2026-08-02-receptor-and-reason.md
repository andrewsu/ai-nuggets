## Script

Welcome to Receptor & Reason for Sunday, August second. Today leans computational up front — three papers that reframe how the brain assigns credit, holds a memory, and generates its own dynamics — and then works through plasticity, sensory gain, a neuroprotective loop, a genuine new drug target for hydrocephalus, and a sleep-and-depression study to close. Ten items. Let's get into it.

We open with the cleanest statement I've seen in a while of the idea that the brain does gradient descent. This is from Reza Shadmehr's group, working in marmosets trained to make saccades to targets that carried either high or low reward. The problem they're chasing is credit assignment — the same problem deep learning solves with backpropagation. A Purkinje cell in the cerebellum is many synapses away from the eye muscles, so when a saccade lands in the wrong place, how does that individual cell learn its specific share of the blame? The classical answer is that climbing fibers are the teacher, delivering an error signal to Purkinje cells through complex spikes. What's new here is the precise claim about what that teaching signal actually is. They found the climbing fibers use a multiplicative code — they scale the direction of the movement error by the reward context, folding in a reward prediction error, so the same spatial error teaches more when more is at stake. Then, using spike-triggered suppression to measure each Purkinje cell's "potent vector" — the direction the eye actually moves when that one cell changes its firing — they showed the climbing fiber signal was, on average, proportional to the dot product of that reward-weighted error vector onto the cell's potent vector. In plain terms: the teacher isn't broadcasting the raw error, it's handing each cell the gradient of a loss function with respect to that cell's own output. That is exactly the quantity gradient descent needs. It's an interpretive leap built on a specific alignment between error, reward, and a cell's motor influence, and I'd want to see it hold outside the oculomotor system — but as a bridge between machine learning and cerebellar physiology, it's about as concrete as this argument has ever gotten.

Staying in theory but moving to memory: a paper from Larry Abbott's group asks what happens when you stop pretending neurons and synapses live on separate timescales. Standard network models freeze the synapses while the neurons compute, or freeze the neurons while the synapses learn. Here the synapses keep changing under an ongoing Hebbian rule, fluctuating around random baseline weights on roughly the same timescale as the neural activity itself. The result is a new flavor of working memory. Drive the network with an oscillating input, remove the input, and the network keeps oscillating — persistent activity with no explicit storage step, no retrieval step, and no prior knowledge of what the input would be. They trace it analytically to the coupling creating outlier eigenvalues in the connectivity matrix, and then exploit that understanding to build oscillations with dynamics you specify in advance — a dynamic analog of a Hopfield network. The appeal for this audience is that it's a memory mechanism that doesn't need a hand-designed attractor landscape; the plasticity and the dynamics are the same process. It's a model, not a measurement, but it's the kind of model that tells you what to look for in a recording.

The third computational piece tackles a puzzle that's bugged cortical theorists for decades: why does a sheet of cortex with almost uniform six-layer architecture produce such wildly different dynamics from region to region? The proposal is a single organizing variable — each local network's distance to criticality, meaning how close it sits to the tipping point between order and chaos. Fitting resting-state fMRI across all three hundred sixty cortical areas with a simple recurrent network model, they show that one parameter, distance to criticality, reproduces both the local power spectra and the functional connectivity, and that the rank order of regions along this axis is strikingly conserved across people. There's even a universal shape-collapse of the power spectrum that depends only on that distance. The caveat is real — this is fit to slow BOLD fluctuations, which are a coarse readout — but the idea that a conserved criticality gradient is the generator behind cortical dynamical diversity is a clean, testable organizing principle.

Now into molecular plasticity, and a paper that puts astrocytes in the driver's seat of neuronal protein synthesis — from Eric Klann's group. Translation is the rate-limiting machinery for lasting synaptic plasticity and long-term memory, and it's usually studied cell-autonomously in neurons. Here they show astrocyte-secreted proteins set the neuron's translational tone, and crucially that it depends on the astrocyte's state. Conditioned medium from healthy astrocytes raised neuronal translation, and stimulating the astrocytes with BDNF raised it further; but medium from neurotoxic reactive astrocytes — the inflammatory state linked to Alzheimer's — suppressed it. Neuronal mTORC1 activity tracked translational output across the board, and they pinned a specific culprit for the suppression: astrocyte-secreted APOE and its lipid cargo acted as a negative regulator of neuronal translation. The whole effect required neuronal endocytosis of the astrocyte signal and drove glutamatergic synaptic remodeling. Given that APOE is the strongest common genetic risk factor for late-onset Alzheimer's, a mechanism where astrocyte-derived APOE clamps down on the protein synthesis that memory depends on is a provocative thread to pull — and they showed you can rescue the inflammatory suppression at several points in the pathway, which is what makes it interesting pharmacologically rather than just descriptively.

Next, a satisfying dogma correction on how critical periods close. For over twenty years the standard story has been that perineuronal nets — the dense extracellular matrix wrapping parvalbumin interneurons — are what slam the window shut on juvenile plasticity, based largely on the classic result that digesting them with chondroitinase reopens ocular dominance plasticity in adults. This group tested that directly by genetically deleting aggrecan, the essential net component. Delete aggrecan in inhibitory neurons and you eliminate the perineuronal nets entirely — but the critical period still closes on schedule. Delete it in excitatory neurons, or in all neurons, and adult plasticity persists. So the brake isn't the nets around the interneurons at all; it's aggrecan sitting diffusely in the neuropil, secreted by excitatory neurons. That reassigns a century-old structure and, more usefully, tells you the chondroitinase experiments were probably working through the diffuse matrix, not the visible nets. For anyone thinking about reopening plasticity therapeutically — the framing behind a lot of the psychedelic and ketamine excitement — it says you may be aiming at the wrong molecular target.

From plasticity to sensory gain, and a nice piece of state-dependent circuit physiology. We know satiety dulls the sense of smell and fasting sharpens it, in mice and humans alike. This work localizes that gain control to the very first synapse in the olfactory bulb. The mediators are periglomerular cells that co-express insulin receptors and the low-voltage potassium channel Kv1.3. After a glucose load, insulin inhibits the Kv1.3 current in these cells, which makes them more excitable, and their heightened activity drives presynaptic inhibition of the incoming olfactory receptor neuron terminals — turning down the sensory signal before it ever reaches the mitral cells. It's a clean, complete circuit from a metabolic hormone to a specific channel to a behavioral change in food-finding. And Kv1.3 is a druggable channel already of interest in immunometabolism, so a mechanism where it gates sensory input by metabolic state is worth filing away.

Sticking with internal-state control of coding, another group asks whether circadian time reshapes not just how excitable neurons are, but how they actually transmit information. Recording wirelessly from the visual thalamus — the dLGN, the lateral geniculate relay between retina and cortex — in awake mice, they found spontaneous activity is under clear circadian control, peaking in the late subjective day. The interesting part is that the neurons' basic tuning stayed stable across the cycle, but the efficiency of information transfer did not: it depended on the statistics of the visual scene, with a more efficient coding strategy at night under high-contrast conditions, verified by optogenetically probing the retina-to-thalamus synapse. So the clock isn't just dialing gain up and down; it's reconfiguring the code to match the expected visual world at that time of day. It's a reminder that "time of day" is a real variable in any sensory or circuit measurement, not just noise to average over.

Now a therapeutics-facing paper that upends the "microglia are the bad guys" reflex after brain injury. The group identifies gp130 — the shared signal-transducing receptor of the IL-6 cytokine family — as the switch for a neuroprotective conversation between microglia and neurons. Activating gp130 on CNS microglia makes them secrete leukemia inhibitory factor, a neurotrophic cytokine; that factor pushes neurons to secrete IL-6; and the neuronal IL-6 loops back onto microglial gp130, closing a self-reinforcing protective circuit. They show acute gp130 activation improves outcomes across traumatic brain injury, stroke, and spinal cord injury, and that you can drive the loop deliberately with engineered "designer" cytokines. The counterintuitive bit is that IL-6 and gp130 signaling are usually cast as pro-inflammatory villains, and here the same axis, engaged acutely in the right cells, is protective. Whether a systemic designer cytokine can hit microglia selectively enough to be safe is the open question, but as a druggable handle on the secondary injury cascade — where we have essentially nothing — it's a real lead.

Our ninth item is the most straightforwardly pharmacological of the day: a candidate drug target for hydrocephalus, which today is treated almost entirely by surgically shunting cerebrospinal fluid, with all the revisions and infections that brings. Working in rats, this group shows that the kinase SPAK — the STE20 proline-alanine-rich kinase — is a central controller of fluid secretion at the choroid plexus, acting through the NKCC1 cotransporter, the same transporter bumetanide targets, plus the sodium-potassium pump. Systemic dosing with the SPAK inhibitor ZT-1a after a simulated brain hemorrhage measurably reduced post-hemorrhagic fluid buildup within twenty-four hours. They also show that activating the choroid plexus TRPV4 channel drives fluid over-secretion specifically through NKCC1, with SPAK as the required molecular link — so the TRPV4-to-SPAK axis is the proposed lever. It's early and it's an acute twenty-four-hour readout in a rodent model, but a specific, systemically deliverable pharmacological alternative to a shunt is exactly the kind of thing the field has lacked.

And to close, something lighter but psychiatrically pointed. Positive self-evaluation — viewing your own traits favorably — is protective against depression, and this study asks whether you can strengthen it during sleep. Using targeted memory reactivation, they cued positive personality traits with sounds during non-REM sleep and found it boosted people's positive self-evaluation afterward. Two things make it more than a curiosity. First, the cueing evoked trait-specific neural representations in the sleep spindle sigma band, roughly eleven to sixteen hertz, and those sleeping representations aligned with each person's waking self-evaluation structure. Second, both the behavioral benefit and that neural alignment shrank as depressive symptoms rose — identifying a maladaptive self-evaluative process that spans sleep and wakefulness. It's correlational on the depression axis and a long way from an intervention, but the notion that self-worth is partly consolidated during sleep, and that the consolidation is specifically weakened in depression, is a genuinely new place to look.

That's ten for today — gradient descent in the cerebellum, memory in coupled synapses, criticality as the cortical generator, astrocytes and APOE tuning translation, aggrecan reassigned as the critical-period brake, insulin gating smell, the clock reconfiguring the visual code, a gp130 neuroprotective loop, a SPAK inhibitor for hydrocephalus, and sleep-based self-evaluation in depression.
