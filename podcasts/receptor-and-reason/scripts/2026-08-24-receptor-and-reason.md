## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. I'm your host, and this is your daily pass through CNS pharmacology, psychiatric and neurology drug discovery, and the computational machinery closing in on both. Eleven items today. The lead is the most satisfying pharmacology I've read this week, because it gets you a biased agonist without designing one.

Start with the problem it solves. Morphine analgesic tolerance in mice requires three things: the mu receptor, beta-arrestin two, and the tyrosine kinase c-Src. The beta-arrestin leg has been solid since Laura Bohn's knockouts a quarter century ago. The c-Src leg has been a loose end, because the evidence came from dasatinib and from PP2 — promiscuous Src-family inhibitors that hit plenty else. Nobody could say the effect was c-Src, or where in the cascade it sat.

Tim Hales's group at Dundee closed it with a better tool. The compound is a conformationally selective c-Src inhibitor, known in oncology development as N-X-P nine hundred, and given orally thirty minutes before daily morphine it essentially abolishes the tolerance. In vehicle-treated wild types, morphine's antinociceptive E-D-fifty climbed from about one and a half milligrams per kilogram on day one to nine point seven by day ten. At the high dose of the Src inhibitor, it finished at two point six, not statistically distinguishable from day one. In mu-receptor heterozygous mice, where tolerance arrives faster because there are half as many receptors, daily Src inhibition kept morphine working while vehicle animals lost the response by day four.

Now the mechanism, which is where this earns its keep. In beta-arrestin two recruitment assays, the Src inhibitor cut the E-max of DAMGO roughly in half — a hundred percent down to fifty-six — and cut morphine's from thirty-three to fourteen. Potencies did not move. And in the same cells, inhibition of cyclic A-M-P accumulation was untouched: same E-max, same potency, and still untouched when they stripped out receptor reserve with beta-funaltrexamine, the irreversible mu antagonist, to expose any hidden efficacy loss. A cereblon-based PROTAC that degrades c-Src reproduced the arrestin effect, and the size of the effect tracked how much c-Src was left. So this is not a bias-inducing accident of one chemotype. Removing c-Src selectively removes arrestin efficacy.

Two details I'd hold onto. Across six mu agonists — DAMGO, fentanyl, morphine, oxycodone, buprenorphine, oliceridine — the degree to which each phosphorylates c-Src at tyrosine four sixteen correlates with its beta-arrestin efficacy at r equals point nine zero. That puts c-Src inside the arrestin arm rather than merely modulating it from outside. And there's a temporal dissociation the authors don't oversell: Src phosphorylation is gone in under an hour, but arrestin recruitment doesn't budge until eight hours of exposure, four for the degrader. Something slow sits in between, and it isn't total beta-arrestin two or surface receptor number, because neither changed. The honest limits are small mouse groups, six per arm, and the fact that established tolerance was only transiently reversed — one day of restored analgesia, gone by the next. This is prophylaxis, not rescue. But as a strategy it's lovely: rather than spend a decade engineering a G-protein-biased agonist and arguing about whether bias is real, take the agonist you already have and delete the kinase that feeds its arrestin arm.

Second, and staying with opioids but moving into the clinic, a secondary analysis of the IGNITE ADOPT-PGx acute pain trial in Clinical Pharmacology and Therapeutics. The question is whether adding tramadol to oxycodone or hydrocodone after elective surgery spares opioid. The pharmacologic rationale is appealing — tramadol is a weak mu agonist plus a serotonin and noradrenaline reuptake inhibitor, so in principle you recruit descending inhibition and need less of the strong agonist. The data say the opposite. Adjusted cumulative morphine milligram equivalents over ten days were one hundred twenty-two on oxycodone alone versus two hundred five on oxycodone plus tramadol. For hydrocodone, seventy-nine versus two hundred six. Composite pain scores were indistinguishable in both comparisons. And mobility was significantly worse on the combination, thirty-five versus twenty-eight on the oxycodone side.

What makes this hard to dismiss is the sensitivity analysis. Restricting to CYP2D6 normal metabolizers — the patients in whom tramadol's active metabolite actually forms — changed nothing. Restricting to sites that prescribed both strategies, to kill the confound that some hospitals just prescribe differently, changed nothing. Same in the total knee arthroplasty subgroup. The groups are small on the combination side, ninety-six and forty-two, and they were not randomized to tramadol, so residual confounding by indication is live — sicker or more pain-prone patients may get more drugs. But the direction and the magnitude are not subtle. Doubling opioid burden for no analgesic gain and worse mobility is not what an opioid-sparing adjunct looks like.

Third, the same journal, a companion result on the other favorite adjunct. This is a target trial emulation in ten years of US commercial claims, asking what happens when you start gabapentin with opioids in the first month after breast cancer surgery. Eighty-six thousand eligible patients, about seventeen hundred on the combination. The safety news is reassuring: respiratory complications, major adverse cardiovascular events and arrhythmia were all comparable, with risk ratios near one and confidence intervals wide enough that you should read this as no signal rather than as proof of safety. The 2019 FDA warning on gabapentinoid respiratory depression rested on case reports and volunteer studies of eight to twelve people, so a properly confounder-adjusted emulation is a real upgrade. The other finding is the deflating one. Adjunctive gabapentin did not reduce subsequent high-dose opioid use — risk ratio one point two three at the fifty morphine-equivalent threshold, one point zero nine at ninety. Put the two papers side by side and you get a consistent, unglamorous message about perioperative multimodal analgesia: two of its most-prescribed adjuncts are not sparing opioid in the real world, and one of them is adding to it.

Fourth, a modeling preprint that I think is quietly important for anyone who cares about intrinsic excitability as a drug target. The observation it explains is that midbrain dopamine neurons pacemake with wildly different regularity depending on projection target, and the net current driving the interspike ramp is on the order of a picoamp — small enough that single-channel stochasticity should shred the rhythm. So why doesn't it?

The answer is a phase-space geometry, not a balance of conductances. In their model of the regularly firing population projecting to dorsomedial striatum, the after-hyperpolarization is deep enough — because S-K channels make it deep — to remove inactivation from K-V-four. That recruits an A-type current whose slow inactivation, plotted against membrane potential, confines the trajectory to a narrow corridor between two nullclines. Inside that corridor the membrane potential behaves like a slowly moving resting potential, and any noise that knocks it out is damped within milliseconds by fast K-V-four activation. In the model of the irregular population projecting to the medial shell of accumbens, the after-hyperpolarization is too shallow to remove that inactivation, the corridor is never entered, and regularity collapses under the same noise.

Why this matters beyond dopamine. Slow single-spike pacemaking with a tiny net current is the signature of essentially every neuromodulatory nucleus — dorsal raphe serotonin at one to two hertz, locus coeruleus noradrenaline, tuberomammillary histamine — and the paper walks through published waveforms from each showing the same after-hyperpolarization-then-ramp structure. If the mechanism generalizes, then an S-K blocker like apamin isn't a firing-rate drug, it's a regularity drug, and it will do something in one projection-defined subpopulation and nothing in its neighbor. Their simulated apamin does exactly that. The obvious caveat is that this is a conductance-based model fit to in-vitro traces, not a new experiment, and the prediction that the same manipulation dissociates regularity from rate across subpopulations is still open.

Fifth, from Ken Kosik's lab at Santa Barbara with the Goard lab, cerebral organoids carrying the two reciprocal copy number variants of the same twenty-five to twenty-seven gene stretch of chromosome seven — hemizygous deletion gives Williams syndrome, duplication gives Dup-seven — imaged longitudinally from day thirty to day one hundred fifty. The value here is that almost all prior work on this locus is single-gene: knock out G-T-F-two-I, knock out B-A-Z-one-B, infer the syndrome. Dosage syndromes don't decompose that way.

Structurally the two directions diverge early and oppositely. Williams organoids accelerate rosette morphogenesis, more rosettes and thicker layers prematurely; Dup-seven organoids have persistently fewer and thinner ones. Later, Dup-seven has higher spine density than Williams. Functionally, control organoids establish and hold synchronized network activity; in Williams it's delayed and transient, and in Dup-seven it never appears at any timepoint. The gabazine test is the interesting one — as controls mature, blocking G-A-B-A-A stops desynchronizing them, the normal excitatory-to-inhibitory switch; in Williams that desynchronization persists, and Dup-seven, having no synchrony to disrupt, marginally increases it. The pharmacological lead is maraviroc: C-C-R-five antagonism raised spine density in control and Williams organoids and did nothing in Dup-seven. That's a genotype-dependent response to a licensed drug, and worth someone's follow-up. The authors are appropriately careful that "accelerated" versus "delayed" maturation is too simple a frame for what they see, and organoids at day one fifty have very few inhibitory neurons, which limits how much of the synchrony story you should believe.

Sixth, Paul Slesinger's group at Mount Sinai with a delivery tool I hadn't seen used in vivo. Neuropeptide pharmacology has a chronic methods problem: optogenetics or chemogenetics of peptide-releasing neurons drags along co-released transmitters, and cannula infusion has no temporal resolution. Their answer is azosomes — liposomes built with azobenzene-containing lipids that photoisomerize, dumping their cargo on a light pulse. Delivered through an optofluidic cannula that both illuminates and reads photometry, in awake behaving mice.

They characterized release with a dye first — power dependence, pulse-duration dependence, minimal leak before stimulation, near-complete release available within about a two-hour window after infusion. Then oxytocin, verified against a genetically encoded oxytocin sensor and blocked by the oxytocin receptor antagonist ornithine vasotocin analog. Photoreleasing oxytocin into hippocampal C-A-two before a social interaction test reduced excitatory transient frequency and amplitude and shortened latency to initiate contact.

The detail I want to flag isn't their result, it's their control. The oxytocin sensor has an in-vitro E-C-fifty around twenty nanomolar, but in vivo they needed micromolar exogenous peptide to move it at all — a hundred-fold-plus discrepancy they attribute to rapid dilution at the injection site. If you are reading fiber-photometry peptide-sensor traces as concentration reports, that gap should bother you. Compared with caged peptides, which need prolonged ultraviolet illumination to uncage an extracellular pool, this releases within seconds of a brief pulse, which is closer to what endogenous peptide signaling actually looks like.

Seventh, staying on peptides and going wide. A Hungarian group did brain-wide fluorescent in situ hybridization for the glucagon gene in mice, and the count of proglucagon-expressing neuron populations goes from three to nine. Olfactory bulb, claustrum, piriform cortex, basolateral amygdala, posterior hippocampus, posterior hypothalamic nucleus, periaqueductal gray and dorsal raphe, and the dorsal nucleus of the lateral lemniscus, on top of the well-known medullary population. All nine express both prohormone convertases, so all nine can in principle make G-L-P-one, and G-L-P-one immunoreactivity shows up in nearly all of them. The reason they were missed is prosaic: expression is far lower than in the medulla, and the medullary signal is so bright it sets everyone's detection threshold.

The finding worth arguing about is the posterior hypothalamic population, which after the medulla is the largest, roughly half to three-quarters the medullary count. Thirty hours of fasting markedly increased its glucagon-gene message and its proglucagon and G-L-P-one protein. That is backwards. It is regulated like an orexigenic A-G-R-P neuron while making an anorexigenic peptide, and it is regulated in the opposite direction from medullary G-L-P-one neurons, which are activated by feeding. Its projections go to lateral septum, preoptic region, lateral hypothalamus and zona incerta, contacting both receptor-positive and receptor-negative cells. Also a practical warning: the Cre driver lines don't agree. The B-A-C line labels this population plus periaqueductal gray and olfactory bulb but barely touches piriform cortex and amygdala, while other lines do the reverse. Some fraction of the published central G-L-P-one circuit literature is a statement about a driver line.

Eighth, a tutorial in Pharmacometrics and Systems Pharmacology that introduces single-cell physiologically based pharmacokinetics. The premise is that standard P-B-P-K treats an organ's intracellular compartment as one well-mixed pool, while single-cell transcriptomics has been telling us for a decade that the transporters and enzymes in that pool are distributed, not uniform. So they split the intracellular compartment into individual cells, weight each cell's expression-dependent process by a negative binomial drawn over a range of means and dispersions, and conserve the total process across cells. Two worked cases: adavosertib in patients with brain tumors, where blood-brain barrier uptake plus P-glycoprotein and A-B-C-G-two efflux are the expression-dependent steps, and midazolam hepatic metabolism.

The useful output is the negative result. For midazolam, high membrane permeability washes out the cell-to-cell differences almost entirely — so the criterion they derive is that single-cell P-B-P-K only earns its complexity when the expression-dependent process is on the same order as the other kinetic steps. Otherwise you've bought a much bigger O-D-E system and learned nothing. They also state plainly that nobody can currently measure single-cell drug concentrations at scale, so these predictions are unvalidated by construction. Code is on GitHub. I'd read this as a well-posed framework looking for the case where it matters, and the honest guidance about when not to build one is the most valuable page in it.

Ninth, a benchmark result in Nature Biotechnology that I want everyone doing computational design to sit with. The AIntibody challenge is the first prospective, blinded evaluation of computational antibody design where every submission was synthesized as a full immunoglobulin and tested under uniform protocols — high-throughput surface plasmon resonance, orthogonal KinExA, and a five-assay developability panel. Three tasks: affinity maturation within a library, hit expansion and ranking inside heavy-chain C-D-R-three clusters, and out-of-library design. The target was the SARS-CoV-2 receptor binding domain, deliberately, because it is the most characterized protein ever, and the organizers say outright that their results should be read as an upper bound on current capability rather than general performance.

The affinity maturation task is the good news. About thirteen percent of submissions achieved a twenty-fold or better improvement over the parental antibody, and the winner produced a ninety-five picomolar developable antibody statistically tied with the best experimental one at a hundred thirteen. That would replace two to three weeks of combinatorial library work with an in-silico step. But eight participants could not design a single developable binder, and third place by KinExA was a consensus-sequence method using statistics and no machine learning at all.

The hit expansion task is the bad news, and it's worse than "modest." Models found binders at better than ninety-six percent, which mostly reflects a good library. But at improving on the standard practice of picking the most abundant clone, only nine to fourteen percent of submissions succeeded — against thirty-nine percent for picking clones at random. With one exception, the algorithms were worse than random, and even that exception failed on one of the three clusters. In out-of-library design, the nominal winner had a two point nine picomolar antibody with developability flaws that would have killed it as a candidate, and forty-six percent of all submissions were either nonbinders or nondevelopable. The conclusion the organizers draw is against one-model-for-everything and for a portfolio, applied per task and per data regime. The conclusion I draw is that retrospective benchmarks in this field have been telling us almost nothing.

Tenth, and a small companion to that, a workshop paper from the generative-and-agentic-biology track at this year's ICML. It asks whether a language model can write the ranking policy that shortlists designed protein binders — not generate binders, just combine precomputed structural-confidence and interface-quality proxy scores into a multi-metric ranking so you spend your limited wet-lab slots well. On a ten-target held-out split, averaged over five sampled policies, GPT-4o reaches point five eight nine recall at ten. The strongest single fixed feature — one of the interface confidence scores you already have — reaches point five seven one. That is a real but very thin margin, and the authors present it as such. The honest reading is that the interpretability is the product: you get a legible, auditable ranking rule instead of a number, at roughly the performance of the number.

Which brings me to the last item, back in psychiatry. A group at Sword Health with a Yale collaborator asked not whether language models help people, but what they actually do in a therapy conversation. They built an ontology of ten therapeutic moves grounded in the MULTI-sixty inventory, validated it with five licensed psychologists, and scaled it with a model judge matched to human agreement. Then they coded real counseling transcripts and model-led sessions against it.

The differences are systematic rather than random. Anchored in a human transcript prefix, models over-inquire — asking questions at up to three times the human clinician rate — and carry forward whatever strategy the human started, while rarely initiating one themselves. Remove the human prefix and let the model run the session, and the distribution swings: about half the inquiry mass disperses, interpretation becomes the third most common move at around three times the human rate, and skill building essentially disappears. So the failure isn't a generic blandness; it's a specific profile, heavy on probing and interpretation, light on teaching and practice. Exposing the ten moves to the model as tools — no fine-tuning — roughly halves the deviation from the human distribution and improves turn-level agreement by seven to nine points. Narrows, doesn't close.

The authors' own framing is the part I'd underline. Inter-annotator agreement was only moderate, the patient in their model-led condition is another model, and matching a human move distribution certifies nothing about competence or safety. Which is exactly right. But as an instrument, this is what the field has been missing — a shared, auditable vocabulary for saying what a model is doing in a clinical conversation, instead of arguing about outcome scores on instruments that were never designed to tell you why.

That's eleven. The thread, if you want one: three of today's papers are about a therapeutic strategy that looked obviously right on mechanism and doesn't survive contact with measurement. Tramadol should spare opioid. Gabapentin should spare opioid. A model that has read every antibody should beat picking a clone at random. Good pharmacology is mostly the discipline of checking.
