## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. I'm your host, and this is your daily pass through CNS pharmacology, psychiatric and neurology drug discovery, and the computational machinery closing in on both. Eleven items today, and if there's a thread it's relocation — several of these papers move the mechanism away from where the field had it parked. The site of action turns out to be peripheral, not central. The risk gene maps to motor cortex, not prefrontal. The druggable step isn't the receptor, it's the machinery that eats the receptor. And in the lead item, the thing you have to fix isn't the biology at all — it's the instrument.

Start with that one, because it's the most consequential paper of the week for anyone who does pharmacological imaging in rodents. Nature Neuroscience published SORDINO, a new MRI pulse sequence out of a group that has been chipping away at zero-echo-time functional imaging for several years. The problem it solves is embarrassing when you say it plainly: the gold-standard sequence for functional MRI, gradient-echo echo-planar imaging, is deafeningly loud. In a mouse that means either you anesthetize the animal — and now every drug effect you measure sits on top of an anesthetic — or you spend weeks habituating an animal that is being screamed at by a magnet.

SORDINO's move is to stop switching the gradients. Existing zero-echo-time methods acquire data while the gradient sits still, then pay for a ramp and a settle between spokes. This sequence acquires only during the ramp, holding total gradient amplitude nearly constant and rotating the direction continuously — the authors' analogy is a sweep second hand versus a ticking one. The result is a mean slew rate of about two-tenths of a tesla per metre per second. Conventional echo-planar imaging at matched resolution runs above twelve hundred. That is four orders of magnitude, and acoustically it means the sequence is indistinguishable from an idle scanner.

The physiology follows. Mice going through five days of head-fixation habituation with SORDINO had serum cortisol back to baseline by day five and held their body weight; mice getting echo-planar imaging had cortisol elevated throughout and lost weight. And because the gradients aren't thrashing, the electromagnetic interference collapses too, which is the part that should interest this audience most. They ran spike recordings with a conventional online sorter during active scanning. They ran fast-scan cyclic voltammetry during active scanning, with no interleaving. They put a miniscope on the animal's head and imaged a hundred and five prelimbic neurons at cellular resolution while acquiring brain-wide functional images. None of that is possible with echo-planar imaging.

The contrast mechanism is not BOLD, and they're careful about proving it. Imaging fresh arterial and venous blood, SORDINO gives a higher signal from deoxygenated blood — the inverse of BOLD. What it's actually reading is a combination of inflow and tissue oxygen acting through T-one shortening, validated against fibre photometry and voltammetry ground truth during gas challenges. Practically: contrast-to-noise comparable to echo-planar imaging at nine-point-four tesla, higher temporal signal-to-noise, no ghosting, no distortion, and measurable regional homogeneity in the amygdala, where echo-planar imaging is essentially blind from susceptibility dropout. They then map resting-state networks in twenty-five awake mice, recover the canonical triple-network organization, and image two brains at once during social interaction.

Why I'd call this the item of the week: if you want awake-mouse pharmacological MRI with simultaneous dopamine measurement — the drug on board, the animal behaving, the neurochemistry and the whole-brain signal on the same clock — the blocker has been the instrument, not the biology. This removes the blocker, and they're releasing the sequence and the database. The honest caveat is that it's one nine-point-four-tesla system in one lab, and a sequence whose functional contrast is oxygen-and-inflow rather than BOLD will need its own interpretive literature before people can port intuitions across from human work.

Which is a decent segue to what human pharmacological imaging currently delivers, because a preprint this week is a useful cold shower. Nineteen healthy young adults, double-blind placebo-controlled crossover, seven-tesla resting-state imaging before and after five milligrams of donepezil, second scan at three hours to catch peak plasma. The hypothesis was the standard one: cholinergic potentiation should reorganize the attention networks and decouple them from the default mode network. What they found was one cluster. Two hundred and ninety-six voxels in left inferior parietal cortex, within the dorsal attention network, family-wise-error corrected p of point-zero-zero-nine. Nothing in the ventral attention, frontoparietal, or default mode networks. No network-level changes in global connectivity strength. The dorsal-attention-to-default-mode decoupling everyone expects showed up in exploratory analysis and did not survive correction. And on the Attention Network Task, donepezil did not improve attentional orienting at the group level.

I like this paper because it reports the primary endpoint honestly instead of promoting the exploratory one. The authors' own read is the right one — healthy young adults probably sit near optimal cholinergic tone, so there's little headroom, which is exactly why acetylcholinesterase inhibitors do something in Alzheimer's disease and almost nothing in undergraduates. One methodological wrinkle worth flagging: participants watched nature documentaries during the scans rather than fixating, for tolerability. That's naturalistic viewing analyzed with a resting-state pipeline, and naturalistic viewing itself drives attentional networks. Given nineteen subjects and one surviving cluster, I'd treat the left parietal finding as a lead, not a result.

Now a genuinely surprising piece of genetics. A medRxiv preprint integrates the latest large depression genome-wide association data with an adult human brain single-cell discovery atlas plus five independent replication atlases spanning regions, developmental stages, species, and disease states — north of five million cells and nuclei. Across several different polygenic mapping frameworks, the common-variant signal for major depressive disorder concentrates in neurons rather than glia, which is unremarkable, and then consistently prioritizes primary motor cortex as the anatomical context, which is not. The field's priors are prefrontal, limbic, reward. Motor cortex is not on the list.

At subtype resolution it lands on parvalbumin-positive inhibitory neurons and a layer two-to-four excitatory population, and integrative gene prioritization nominates C-N-N-M-two as the leading parvalbumin-linked candidate, with N-E-G-R-one on the excitatory side. Then they go and do the experiment. In chronic stress models, C-N-N-M-two protein falls in motor-cortical parvalbumin and somatostatin interneurons. Optogenetic activation of motor cortex parvalbumin neurons rescues stress-induced depression-like behaviour. Tracing identifies a projection from motor cortex to lateral and ventrolateral periaqueductal grey, and rotarod exercise recruits that pathway with antidepressant-like effects.

The reason to take this seriously rather than filing it as an atlas artifact is the convergence: statistical prioritization, replication across six atlases, protein-level change under stress, and a causal optogenetic manipulation all pointing at the same cell type in the same region. The reason to hold it loosely is that psychomotor features of depression are real and motor cortex has always been a plausible substrate that nobody prioritized, so there's a mild risk of a satisfying post-hoc story. And the exercise arm, while charming, is the weakest link — rotarod is not a clean model of an antidepressant intervention.

Staying with excitation-inhibition balance, and moving to a preprint that went up today with what I think is a legitimately clever therapeutic proposal for schizophrenia. The starting observation is the field's oldest failure: NMDA receptor hypofunction is the central story, and every attempt to potentiate the receptor directly — glycine-site agonists, D-serine, positive allosteric modulators — has produced modest or inconsistent clinical results. The authors' hypothesis is that potentiating a receptor doesn't help if the problem is that the receptor is being removed from the surface and degraded.

So they went after the degradation machinery. The endosomal sorting complexes required for transport are what commit an internalized membrane protein to the lysosome, and T-S-G one-oh-one is the ESCRT-one component that recognizes ubiquitinated cargo and starts that commitment. In primary neurons given sub-chronic phencyclidine followed by washout — a model designed to capture persistent adaptation rather than acute blockade — knocking down T-S-G one-oh-one raised surface expression of both the GluN-one and GluN-two-A NMDA receptor subunits and, crucially, of the GABA-A gamma-two and GABA-B receptor subunits as well.

That dual effect is the whole point. Boosting excitatory drive alone in a disinhibited network is the thing everyone worries about; raising both arms restores the ratio. And the pharmacology is untouched — the GABA concentration-response curves gave half-maximal concentrations of fifty-six versus fifty-nine nanomolar with and without knockdown, so this is purely a trafficking effect on how many functional receptors sit at the membrane, not a change in what they do once there. Downstream they get recovery of PSD-ninety-five, BDNF, ERK signalling, and activity-dependent nuclear translocation of Fos.

The caveats are large and I want to be clear about them. This is dissociated culture, not circuit and not animal. It's genetic knockdown, not a molecule — and T-S-G one-oh-one is a tumour suppressor handling a very wide cargo set, so a systemically delivered ESCRT-one inhibitor is not an obvious drug. What the paper actually establishes is that the trafficking bottleneck is real and manipulable, which reframes what a target in this space should look like. That's worth something even without a compound.

An adjacent idea, and one that comes with a marketed drug attached. Molecular Psychiatry has a paper tracing how a high-calorie diet impairs cognition without going through amyloid or tau. The chain starts at a peroxisome-proliferator-activated-receptor response element in the promoter of PQBP1, a transcription-splicing coupling factor. PPAR-gamma transactivates PQBP1; high-fat feeding activates MEK-ERK signalling, which phosphorylates PPAR-gamma at serine two-seventy-three and suppresses that transactivation; PQBP1 falls in neurons; alternative splicing of synaptic genes goes wrong; and both excitatory and inhibitory synapse markers drop, along with spatial and aversive memory.

The rescue arm is what elevates it. Pioglitazone — an ordinary thiazolidinedione, which is known to block that serine two-seventy-three phosphorylation — restored PQBP1, restored excitatory and inhibitory synapse markers, and restored performance on water maze, Y-maze, and fear conditioning, at both three and six weeks of dosing. Viral PQBP1 delivery did the same thing independently. And the epistasis is clean: pioglitazone fails in PQBP1 conditional knockouts. You rarely get a mechanistic chain, two independent rescues, and a negative control that places the drug's effect through the proposed node. It's a mouse paper about diet-induced cognitive impairment, so temper expectations about translating to clinical dementia — but as a demonstration that a metabolic drug can act on a neuronal splicing program, it's tidy.

Two chemistry items now, both about what a druggable target is allowed to look like. The first is a fragment-based campaign on Rho GTPase signalling. GTPases are famously hard to inhibit competitively because the nucleotides they bind do so with affinities down into the picomolar range — you cannot out-compete that. This group's approach is to stop treating the GTPase as the target and treat the complex as the target. Using crystallographic fragment screening and in silico screening, then fragment merging, they built covalent compounds that bind Rac1 at cysteine eighteen in the nucleotide pocket, and that preferentially engage the complex Rac1 forms with Kalirin, its guanine nucleotide exchange factor.

The mechanism is a molecular glue, and it's counterintuitive in a nice way: the compounds make no direct contact with Kalirin at all. The proposal is that they reshape the switch-one region of Rac1 so that it grips Kalirin harder, trapping the exchange factor in a ternary complex that cannot turn over. So you inhibit nucleotide exchange twice — competitively at the pocket, and by sequestering the catalyst.

The selectivity argument is the part worth carrying away. Exchange factors are catalytic and therefore present at far lower abundance than their GTPase substrates. If your glue only forms a stable inhibited species in the presence of the exchange factor, then in a cell you'd sequester the small catalytic pool and leave the large, pleiotropic GTPase pool free to do its other jobs. That is a route to exchange-factor-selective pharmacology without ever finding a binding site on the exchange factor. Kalirin is a neuronal Rho exchange factor with long-standing links to schizophrenia and Alzheimer's disease, so the target choice is not incidental. These are early-stage covalent fragments and the cellular selectivity profiling is preliminary, but the concept is the news.

The second chemistry item is a generative model, and it's asking the same question from the design side. ShEPhERD-two proposes that the right specification for a small molecule isn't the molecule and isn't the protein pocket — it's an interaction profile: shape, electrostatics, and directional pharmacophores. Condition the generative model on that profile and you decouple the structural hypothesis from the chemotype entirely. From one model with no task-specific retraining, they demonstrate bioisosteric fragment merging, dual-target design, selectivity engineering, and modality hopping, with better strain and better fidelity to the target profile than their previous version.

The argument they make against the prevailing approach is a good one. Most three-dimensional generative models learn all-atom geometry end to end and therefore have to rediscover, implicitly and from data, that an amide N-H sitting two-point-nine angstroms from a carbonyl oxygen is a hydrogen bond. Making the interaction explicit means the model can be told what to satisfy rather than having to infer it. For anyone doing structure-based design on CNS targets, where you often have a clear pharmacophore hypothesis and a very constrained property window, being able to hold the interaction fixed and hop the scaffold is exactly the operation you want.

Back to the clinic, and a result about dopamine agonists that inverts the standard story. Impulse control disorders — compulsive gambling most notoriously — are a well-known complication of dopamine replacement therapy in Parkinson's disease, and much more so with agonists than with levodopa. The going explanation borrows from the rodent literature, where risky choice in the presence of reward-paired audiovisual cues depends on D3 receptor activity and is worsened by ropinirole. Slot machines are, after all, reward-paired-cue delivery devices.

Sixty-two patients with Parkinson's disease — thirty-four on levodopa alone, twenty-seven on levodopa plus an agonist — did a risky decision task in cued and uncued versions, both on and off medication, against thirty-six age-matched controls. Everybody chose more riskily when cues were present; that replicates. Levodopa alone did nothing to risky choice. But the agonist group's effect appeared specifically in the uncued version, raising risky choice until performance was indistinguishable from the cued condition.

Read that carefully, because it's the opposite of the expected finding. The drug isn't amplifying cue reactivity. It's making the cue unnecessary — abolishing the normal difference between a context that pushes you toward risk and one that doesn't. Clinically that's a worse picture, not a milder one: it predicts risky reward-seeking decoupled from the situational triggers that patients and families are usually told to watch for. It also complicates the rodent-to-human translation this field has been leaning on. Sixty-two patients is a real but not large sample, and the design is cross-sectional on existing prescriptions rather than randomized, so channeling by indication is in play.

Now a human oxytocin study that I think is more interesting for its site-of-action claim than for its behavioural result. Two hundred and fifty adult men, pre-registered, randomized, double-blind, five arms: placebo plus placebo; placebo plus twenty-four international units of oxytocin; placebo plus forty-eight units; atosiban plus placebo; and atosiban plus twenty-four units. Atosiban is an oxytocin receptor antagonist that does not meaningfully cross the blood-brain barrier, and everything here was given oromucosally, on the tongue — not intranasally. The readout was an autism-sensitive eye-tracking paradigm pitting dynamic social stimuli against geometric patterns.

Forty-eight units increased preferential viewing of social stimuli; twenty-four did not. The effect was mediated by the rise in plasma oxytocin and was larger in participants with lower autism-quotient scores. And atosiban on its own reduced social preference below placebo, and blocked the twenty-four-unit dose.

That last result is the one that matters. An antagonist that stays peripheral moved social attention downward from baseline, which implies endogenous peripheral oxytocin receptor signalling is contributing to social attention under normal conditions — and that exogenous oxytocin can work through the same peripheral route, with the authors pointing at Gq-coupled receptors on vagal afferents. A decade of intranasal oxytocin trials has been built on the premise that the interesting action is central and the nose is a delivery shortcut to the brain. If a substantial part of the effect is peripheral and vagal, the dosing logic, the route, and the pharmacokinetic target all change. Caveats: men only, a single attentional readout that is a long way from clinical benefit, and a dose-response with exactly two doses, one of which did nothing.

Speaking of routes that need better safety data, an analysis of the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System from two thousand to twenty twenty-four asks a question the psychedelic renaissance has mostly not asked: arrhythmia liability. Against a background of more than eighteen million unique cases, about sixty-two thousand mentioned one of the drugs of interest, with dofetilide and naltrexone used as positive and negative controls. Restricting to ventricular arrhythmia proper, ibogaine came back with a proportional reporting ratio of a hundred and forty-two — roughly six times dofetilide's twenty-four — with a corresponding QT-prolongation signal of a hundred and twenty-one. Mitragynine, the kratom alkaloid available over the counter in much of the country, came in at four-point-four for ventricular arrhythmia and about sixteen for QT prolongation, in the same neighbourhood as methadone. MDMA showed a QT signal but no ventricular arrhythmia signal; LSD and mescaline showed neither.

None of this is new pharmacology — ibogaine's hERG blockade and the case reports of sudden death in Africa and in overseas treatment centres are well documented. What the analysis adds is calibration against known proarrhythmics in a common framework, at a moment when a federal executive order is directing agencies to accelerate psychedelic development and expedite review. I'd be careful with the magnitude, though: the ibogaine estimate rests on five ventricular arrhythmia reports out of twenty-five total, and disproportionality analysis on numbers that small is badly behaved. It's also notoriety-prone in exactly the way spontaneous reporting always is. The signal direction is credible and the regulatory point stands; the point estimate should not be quoted.

Finishing with a pharmacogenomics study whose most valuable output is a number nobody asked for. Three hundred and nine Swedish children and adolescents starting ADHD medication, genotyped, with symptom scales at baseline and three months. Polygenic score for ADHD was associated with higher symptom scores overall but not with change on treatment. Polygenic score for educational attainment was associated with lower scores overall and with smaller reductions at follow-up, though that one didn't survive false-discovery correction. So: no usable pharmacogenomic predictor, consistent with this group's earlier finding that nothing biological, clinical, or cognitive predicted outcome in the same cohort.

Then they built fourteen machine learning models across forty-nine features, and report plainly that the models could not generalize. What they did instead is the interesting move: they estimated information-theoretic upper bounds on achievable classification accuracy given the data. Seventy-two percent from polygenic scores alone, eighty-four percent from clinical and demographic variables, ninety-two percent combined. That's a ceiling, not a performance claim, and ceilings derived from three hundred subjects should be read with suspicion. But publishing the bound alongside the failure is the right instinct. Half the machine-learning-in-psychiatry literature would be improved if authors asked how much signal is in principle available before reporting how much their model extracted.

That's the episode. A silent scanner that finally makes awake pharmacological imaging in mice a real experiment, a cholinesterase inhibitor that does much less to the attention networks than the field assumes, depression risk genes pointing at motor cortex interneurons, a trafficking-based rather than agonist-based attack on NMDA receptor hypofunction, a splicing factor that connects diet to synapse loss and hands you a thiazolidinedione, a molecular glue that inhibits a GTPase by trapping its exchange factor, a generative model that designs to interactions rather than to molecules, dopamine agonists that decouple risk-taking from its cues, an oxytocin antagonist that argues the receptor you want may be in the vagus, an arrhythmia signal for ibogaine and kratom, and a pharmacogenomic ceiling published next to a pharmacogenomic failure.
