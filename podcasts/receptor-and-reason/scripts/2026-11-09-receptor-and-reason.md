## Script

Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Monday, November ninth. Eleven items today, and the thread running through most of them is the gradient that isn't there — the dose-response, the accumulating exposure, the mechanism that should scale with the effect, and what you actually find when you go and measure it.

We start with a result that lands directly on the synaptic plasticity story for psychedelics. A Karolinska group has published a pre-registered PET sub-study of a randomized psilocybin trial in major depression, using carbon-eleven UCB-J, the radioligand for SV2A, which is the standard in-vivo proxy for synaptic density. Thirty-five participants with moderate-to-severe recurrent depression, randomized one-to-one to a single twenty-five-milligram dose of psilocybin or a hundred milligrams of niacin as active placebo, both with psychotherapeutic support. Scans two to seven days before dosing and about fifteen days after. The clinical effect was real: MADRS fell from twenty-six to thirteen in the psilocybin arm and barely moved in the niacin arm, from twenty-six to twenty-four. And SV2A binding did not go up. No treatment effect in frontal cortex, no treatment effect in hippocampus, in either the total distribution volume or the binding potential — the hippocampal estimate is essentially zero with a confidence interval spanning plus or minus one-and-a-half units. The authors are careful that those intervals are wide and small effects can't be excluded, but the direction of the field matters here. Psychedelics induce dendritic remodeling in rodents within twenty-four hours, there's a pig study showing increased SV2A binding after psilocybin, and the simple story everyone has been telling is that the drug restores synapses that stress took away. In humans with depression, two weeks out, at a dose that produced substantial and sustained improvement, that increase is not detectable.

What did show up was in the secondary analyses, and it points somewhere different. Higher *baseline* SV2A binding predicted greater subsequent improvement in the psilocybin group, surviving correction for multiple comparisons at two of the three pre-registered timepoints, day eight and day fifteen, in both regions. The change-score associations looked impressive unadjusted — correlations around zero-point-seven at day forty-two — but once you adjust for baseline binding and baseline symptom severity, which you must, because the baseline is mathematically inside the change score, they attenuate and none survive correction. So the honest reading is: the thing that predicts response is the synaptic capital you start with, not the synaptic change you undergo. The authors offer synaptic reserve as the interpretation, with the appropriate caveat that the treatment interaction didn't survive correction either, so this could be a general prognostic marker rather than anything psilocybin-specific. And they raise, without flinching, the alternative that the antidepressant effect here runs through expectancy and unblinding. Put this next to the five-lab Bay Area mouse replication we covered last Tuesday, where the acute effects were rock solid and every persistent therapeutic effect failed to replicate, and you have two completely independent methodologies — preclinical behavior and human molecular imaging — arriving in the same week at the same uncomfortable place. The acute pharmacology is not in doubt. The durable mechanism is.

Staying with the question of what a drug's targets actually do, a drug-target Mendelian randomization study on topiramate and eating disorders. Topiramate is an antiseizure drug used in epilepsy and migraine, and it is very widely prescribed off-label for appetite suppression and weight loss, which puts it in regular contact with patients who have eating disorders. Nobody really knows whether its pharmacology pushes that risk up or down. The approach here is to use cis-expression quantitative trait loci for the genes encoding topiramate's molecular targets as instruments, which gives you a lifelong genetic proxy for inhibiting each target individually. They validated five instruments consistent with the antiepileptic action — carbonic anhydrase four and the calcium channel subunit CACNB3 in brain, and carbonic anhydrase four plus the kainate receptor subunits GRIK1 and GRIK3 in blood — and ran them against anorexia nervosa, twenty-four thousand cases, and binge-eating behavior, thirty-nine thousand cases, plus eight other psychiatric outcomes exploratorily. One signal survived correction: genetically predicted inhibition of carbonic anhydrase four expression in blood was associated with reduced binge-eating risk, odds ratio zero-point-eight-seven, holding up in the multi-SNP sensitivity analysis. Colocalization support was moderate rather than strong — a posterior probability of fifty-eight percent for a shared causal variant, which is suggestive and not conclusive. Nothing for anorexia, nothing for the eight exploratory disorders. What I like about this is that it decomposes a dirty drug into its targets and finds the appetite-relevant signal sitting on the carbonic anhydrase arm rather than the glutamatergic or calcium-channel arms — which is a testable hypothesis about why the weight effect happens, rather than a correlation with the drug as a whole.

Two clinical observational items next, both of which are about distinguishing a real exposure gradient from the appearance of one. First, nicotine and cannabis co-use in early psychosis, from the Early Psychosis Intervention Network — seven hundred thirty-eight individuals, mean age twenty-two, across multiple sites. Fifty-nine percent used neither substance, seventeen percent nicotine only, nine percent cannabis only, sixteen percent both. Symptom burden on the Modified Colorado Symptom Index showed a significant effect of use category, and it was driven specifically by the co-use group: the people using both had greater symptom burden than those using neither, than nicotine alone, and than cannabis alone. More frequent use tracked with higher scores. The design is cross-sectional, so direction of causation is genuinely open — more symptomatic patients may self-medicate more, with both — but the specificity of the co-use effect over either single substance is the part that is hard to explain away as simple severity, and it matters clinically because nicotine use in psychosis is often treated as the benign one of the two.

Second, and more pointed methodologically: sleep-medication use and cognitive decline, in the US Health and Retirement Study and its European counterpart, SHARE. Baseline analyses reproduce the familiar worrying finding — sleep medication use associated with faster decline in global cognition and memory, small effects, around six one-thousandths of a standard deviation per year in the US cohort. But then they do the thing the field keeps not doing. They run a pre-specified landmark analysis classifying people by how many of two consecutive survey waves they reported sleep-medication use, and ask whether the decline scales with cumulative reporting. It does not. Reporting at both visits was not associated with accelerated decline in either cohort. What *was* associated with steeper decline was reporting at exactly one of the two visits — the intermittent pattern — and specifically in Western Europe and among the more educated strata. Extend the window to three waves and no contrast survives familywise correction, and sustained three-visit reporting is statistically equivalent to persistent non-use, within a pre-specified margin of five one-hundredths of a standard deviation per year, with equivalence p-values at a thousandth or below. That's a formal equivalence test, not an underpowered null. The interpretation the authors land on is that the signal is not the drug accumulating in the brain; the signal is the transition — the period in which someone is starting, stopping, or cycling on and off sleep medication, which is also the period in which sleep and mood are unstable. The exposure that looked like a dose is actually a marker of turbulence.

Now to mechanism, and a paper from here at Scripps — the Kelly lab, with Lashuel at EPFL and collaborators in Oviedo and at Harvard. The compound is AA-20, a triazine-core autophagy activator they previously pulled out of a high-throughput screen for lipid droplet clearance, and whose selling point is that it induces autophagy without inhibiting mTORC1, sidestepping the immunosuppressive and metabolic liabilities of rapamycin-class approaches. What's new here is neuronal context and target identification. In hippocampal HT22 cells AA-20 increases autophagic flux, raises cathepsin D activity, and — unlike Torin 1, which induces comparable numbers of degradative compartments — specifically increases lipid-droplet-containing ones, with a one-and-a-half to two-fold rise in lysosomal acid lipase activity. So this is lipophagy, selective lipid droplet degradation, not generic autophagy. In human iPSC cerebral organoids carrying the Swedish APP mutation, two weeks of dosing reduced triglycerides, diacylglycerols, free fatty acids and cholesteryl esters toward the isogenic wild-type profile, with free cholesterol going up as you'd expect if cholesteryl esters are being hydrolyzed — and all of it blocked by a lysosomal acid lipase inhibitor, which is the control that makes the mechanism claim stick. It also corrected the electrical hyperexcitability those organoids develop on multielectrode arrays, a phenotype that maps onto early Alzheimer's.

The target turns out to be CDK5, and the pharmacology there is more interesting than a clean inhibition story. They show direct binding by three orthogonal methods — a NanoBRET tracer displacement in live cells, a thermal shift that moves CDK5's melting temperature from thirty-nine to forty-six-and-a-half degrees, and surface plasmon resonance against recombinant CDK5 with its activator p35 giving a dissociation constant of eighty nanomolar. But the functional consequence is *increased* phosphorylation of ULK1 at serine-seven-seven-seven and serine-six-two-two, and a CDK5-CDK2 inhibitor blocks both the phosphorylation increase and AA-20's effect on autophagic flux. So a compound binds the ATP pocket of a kinase and the downstream substrate gets more phosphorylated, not less — the authors propose a substrate-preference model rather than inhibition. And in a primary neuron alpha-synuclein fibril seeding model, AA-20 cut phosphorylated serine-one-twenty-nine alpha-synuclein by around sixty percent at two micromolar, more in neurites than cell bodies, with knockout controls showing it isn't changing fibril uptake. Caveats are honest ones: the cellular potency sits in the low micromolar range while the biochemical affinity is nanomolar, and the whole package is cells and organoids, no animal. But a lipophagy inducer with a defined neuronal target that moves both a lipid phenotype and a proteinopathy phenotype is a real tool compound.

A conceptually adjacent paper on timing rather than target. Using inducible, reversible ATF4 gain-of-function in Drosophila, a group asked whether transient activation of the integrated stress response early in life changes ageing outcomes. It does, and the specificity is the finding. ATF4 induction confined to early adulthood cut mortality risk by roughly fifty percent across all later ages and improved neuromuscular function in old flies. The acute transcriptional response was transient as expected, but it was replaced by a distinct secondary programme of metabolic remodeling that persisted. Restricting induction to neurons was sufficient to extend lifespan, slow locomotor decline and increase starvation resistance in old age — and it avoided the body weight and fecundity costs that ubiquitous induction imposed. The lasting imprint shows up non-autonomously in the fat body: heterochromatin gene induction, reduced transposable element expression, higher mitochondrial respiratory capacity. A brain-to-periphery axis set by a pulse of stress signaling that has long since switched off. For a field where ISR modulators are being pushed as chronic therapies for cognition, the message that duration and tissue are the determining variables — not the amount of pathway activation — is one worth sitting with.

On the translational side, the statistical design of the Path to Prevention platform trial in early-stage neuronal alpha-synuclein disease, from Berry Consultants with the Michael J. Fox Foundation and academic collaborators. This is a phase 2A, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled perpetual platform embedded inside the PPMI observational cohort, which solves the enrollment problem that makes prodromal Parkinson's trials so painful. Each regimen gets a hundred twenty-five participants on active therapy plus a variable number on placebo, follow-up ninety-six to a hundred forty-four weeks. Two co-primary endpoints: putamen specific binding ratio on dopamine transporter SPECT, and the motor section of the standard Parkinson's rating scale, each analyzed with its own longitudinal Bayesian mixed-effects model. The design decisions worth flagging are the two forms of borrowing. Controls from other regimens in the platform are dynamically discounted rather than pooled at full weight, and placebo estimates are additionally supplemented with non-randomized PPMI registry participants. Simulations off two hundred seventy-five PPMI-eligible participants say each regimen is well powered for a forty-percent slowing on both endpoints — and, notably, the registry borrowing buys up to four percent power. That is a small return for a meaningful amount of inferential exposure to non-randomized data, and the fact that they report it that plainly is to their credit.

Shifting to sensory neuroimmunology, a nice piece of circuit pharmacology on Langerhans cells. These are the epidermal antigen-presenting cells, and the finding is that they act as amplifiers of cutaneous sensory signaling rather than as participants in it. Anatomically and transcriptomically they associate preferentially with non-peptidergic sensory neurons. Activating them increases neuronal and behavioral responses to thermal, mechanical and pruritic stimuli — but without directly exciting the sensory neurons, which is the distinction that makes this a gain-control story. Inhibiting or ablating them selectively reduces itch evoked by pruritogens. The mediator they nominate is semaphorin 7A: it sensitizes cultured sensory neurons to chloroquine and histamine, neutralizing it abolishes the extra scratching that Langerhans cell activation produces, and its receptor PlexinC1 sits on most MrgprA3-positive pruriceptors, with PlexinC1 blockade reducing the neuronal response. For chronic itch, where the therapeutic options remain thin, a ligand-receptor pair that sets pruriceptor gain without being the excitatory drive itself is an attractive place to intervene.

Three computational items to close. First, a benchmark from the Fraser lab at UCSF that is unusually blunt about what current models can't do. They take deep mutational scanning of the MET kinase domain across eleven different inhibitor conditions — so you have, experimentally, eleven genuinely different mutational fitness landscapes for the same protein, and the shifts don't even track inhibitor class cleanly — and ask whether AI models reproduce that ligand dependence. Largely, they do not. ESM2 hits a Spearman correlation around zero-point-five on the untreated control and then drops an average of fifty-three percent across the inhibitor conditions. ProteinMPNN, fed inhibitor-specific predicted structures, drops fifty-four percent, which tells you that handing a model a different conformation is not the same as telling it about the ligand. Protein language models with structural or evolutionary augmentation improve things by less than zero-point-zero-five in correlation. The ligand-aware geometric models, LigandMPNN and LASErMPNN, do partially recover experimentally validated resistance hotspots, which is the constructive half of the result. And affinity predictors systematically underestimate how much mutations change binding, which they attribute to mutation-invariant structural priors inherited from co-folding frameworks plus the absence of mutation-conditioned supervision in the training data. Their conclusion — that the bottleneck is data, not architecture — is the one that should worry anyone planning resistance prediction into a program.

Second, an audit presented at Machine Learning for Health that should be read by anyone fine-tuning a model on biomedical text. Twenty-five biomedical domain fine-tuned models paired against eleven base checkpoints, evaluated not on value-centric refusal — the usual safety framing — but on knowledge-centric abstention: does the model decline to answer when the question is genuinely unanswerable or the evidence is incomplete. Base models were already bad at this, with a median appropriate abstention rate of eight-point-three percent on the MedAbstain benchmark. After fine-tuning, the median fell to four-point-two percent, decreasing in twenty-one of twenty-five pairs, a median within-pair relative drop of forty-five percent, surviving both false-discovery correction and base-clustered inference. The obvious benign explanation would be that fine-tuning made the models better at telling answerable from unanswerable questions, so they abstain less because they need to less. The authors test exactly that and find no support — informedness, if anything, trended down. The second benchmark moved in the same direction but didn't survive correction, so they restrict the robust claim to one dataset, which is appropriate. The practical point stands: the domain-tuned medical model you downloaded because it scores better on medical question-answering is also, with high probability, more willing to answer a question it cannot answer.

And to close, Glyph, from the Stanford AI in Medicine group with Mayo. The premise is that automated clinical discovery is capped by its feature vocabulary — phenome-wide association studies can only find associations among phenotypes somebody already defined and coded, and agentic systems querying those same databases inherit the same ceiling. Glyph instead takes foundation model embeddings of three modalities — abdominal CT volumes, radiology report text, lab results — decomposes them with sparse autoencoders into interpretable features, names those features as clinical concepts, and tests them against outcomes. Applied across forty-one thousand six hundred patients, with a discovery site of fourteen thousand and three held-out sites, they tested six hundred four thousand concept-outcome associations against the hundred forty-one outcomes most predictable from the embeddings, yielding three hundred ninety-seven predictive concepts and four-and-a-half thousand Bonferroni-significant associations. Established relationships come back out — hepatic, renal, cardiovascular, oncologic — which is the sanity check. The interesting claims are the next two: because the embedding is separated into concepts, you can adjust for general illness severity and watch nonspecific associations attenuate while disease-specific ones survive; and compared against disease markers harvested by automated literature search, the top ten Glyph concepts reproduced more often in the held-out cohorts with significantly larger odds ratios. That last comparison is the one to hold at arm's length — "our features beat what a literature search found" depends heavily on how good the literature search was, and these are prognostic associations in a retrospective imaging cohort, not mechanisms. But the framing deserves attention: interpretability work has mostly been aimed at explaining what a network is doing, and this is an argument for pointing the same tools outward, at what the network noticed that we haven't named yet.

That's the show. Five labs and one PET camera agree that the acute pharmacology of psilocybin is not the problem; a drug decomposed into its targets puts the appetite signal on carbonic anhydrase; two consecutive waves of sleep-medication reporting turn out to be safer than one; a Scripps compound binds a kinase and increases its substrate's phosphorylation; and a pulse of stress signaling in fly neurons, switched off long ago, is still remodeling the fat body in old age.
