## Script

Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Wednesday, August fifth. A quick note on today's mix before we start: the freshest, juiciest pharmacology preprints from the last day or two — a schizophrenia rescue with an LSD1 inhibitor, a focused-ultrasound ketamine delivery system, a new striatal signaling molecule — all posted in the last twenty-four hours and their full text hasn't rendered yet, so they're held for a coming episode rather than summarized off an abstract. What that leaves is still a good ten items: we open on the spatial physics of parkinsonian beta, move through two Alzheimer's mechanism papers and a lifespan map of the human EEG, then spend the back half on a genuinely useful reckoning happening in computational drug discovery, and we close with an LLM made to speak like a patient with Alzheimer's. Let's get into it.

We open in the subthalamic nucleus. Elevated beta-band activity, roughly thirteen to thirty-five hertz, in the STN is the canonical pathological signature of Parkinson's — it tracks bradykinesia and rigidity, high-frequency stimulation suppresses it, and the degree of suppression tracks symptom relief. But essentially all of that work treats beta as a spectral and temporal phenomenon; nobody had asked how it's organized in space inside the nucleus. This group took multi-contact local field potential recordings from Parkinson's patients' DBS electrodes and asked whether beta propagates as a traveling wave — a spatially moving oscillation, the kind already described for theta in hippocampus and beta in sensorimotor cortex. Fitting pairwise delays between contacts to a simple spherical wave model, and guarding hard against overfitting, they found evidence for traveling beta waves in eleven of twenty hemispheres, present on average about sixteen percent of the recording. The sources sat in the dorsal motor territory of the STN, and — the clinically pointed bit — propagation speed correlated positively with residual motor impairment. So parkinsonian beta isn't a spatially static blob; at least intermittently it's a mesoscopic wave sweeping out of the motor STN. If that holds up, it matters for the next generation of sensing-guided, adaptive DBS, because a sensor that only reads amplitude at one contact is blind to a feature — wave direction and speed — that may carry independent information about the pathological state.

Staying with disease-driven excitability but moving to Alzheimer's, there's a nice causal story about an early driver of hippocampal hyperactivity. Network hyperexcitability is one of the earliest detectable abnormalities in AD, it predicts and accelerates decline, and it depends on voltage-gated sodium channels — but the field has focused on the Nav subtypes expressed in the mature brain. This group asked about Nav1.3, a subtype normally confined to early development and largely switched off in adults. In the 5xFAD mouse, a common amyloid model, they found Nav1.3 aberrantly re-expressed in the dentate-gyrus-to-CA3 circuit, and localized it by immuno-electron microscopy specifically to presynaptic mossy fiber terminals. The timing is the argument: three-month-old mice already showed elevated CA3 population activity by fiber photometry, before oligomeric amyloid-beta had meaningfully accumulated — that rose later, by seven months. So the excitability comes first, and it coincides with the ectopic Nav1.3. Then they closed the loop: knocking down Nav1.3 in CA3 with a lentiviral short-hairpin normalized the network activity. A developmentally-restricted channel getting switched back on at a specific synapse, driving early circuit dysfunction, and being druggable in principle — that's a much more actionable target than "amyloid causes hyperactivity somehow," and it's a subtype-selective sodium channel story worth watching.

The second Alzheimer's paper zooms out from a single channel to a whole-brain proteome, and it's about one of the strongest genetic risk genes we have, ABCA7. Despite being right up there with APOE in risk association, how ABCA7 loss actually contributes to disease has stayed murky. The group ran label-free quantitative proteomics on whole brains from four genotypes — wild-type, Abca7 knockout, an amyloid model, and the double — across four ages from fifty to two hundred days, then did the full systems workup: differential abundance, gene-ontology enrichment, interaction networks, and cross-comparison to human AD proteomic datasets. The recurring convergence is the useful part. Across genotypes, the altered proteins kept landing on insulin-receptor and PI3K-AKT signaling, MAPK signaling, immune and complement pathways, vesicle trafficking and acidification, and the ubiquitin-proteasome system, with a bias toward membrane-associated proteins — consistent with ABCA7's known lipid-transport role. This is associative, not a mechanism nailed down, and whole-brain proteomics blurs cell types. But it reframes ABCA7 deficiency as sitting at the intersection of brain insulin signaling and neuroinflammation rather than acting purely through lipid handling, and it hands you a ranked list of pathways — insulin signaling especially — to interrogate more directly.

To round out the electrophysiology thread before we turn computational, a normative resource that's quietly valuable. This group analyzed resting-state EEG from 1,763 healthy people aged five to eighty-five and mapped how spectral, complexity, and waveform-morphology features move across the whole lifespan. The headline is that maturation and aging are emphatically non-linear — most age-sensitive features change fast in childhood and adolescence then stabilize, while a subset has a distinct turning point in midlife. And the trajectories aren't spatially uniform: the same feature can follow different arcs over different scalp regions, and transition ages recur in orderly regional sequences, hinting at a shared reorganization chronology. Why care? Because most EEG aging studies used narrow age windows and small samples and consequently baked in a linear assumption that's just wrong. A properly non-linear, spatially-resolved normative model is exactly what you need to say whether a given patient's EEG is atypical for their age — the same logic as brain-age gap models, but for electrophysiology, and directly relevant to spotting deviations in neurological and psychiatric conditions.

Now we turn to computational drug discovery, and there's a real reckoning underway about whether machine-learning docking actually works, so let me take three papers in a row that speak to it. The first is a sober benchmark on protein-ligand pose prediction. Deep-learning structure predictors like AlphaFold2 give you one static conformation per target, which is a poor stand-in for the bound state whenever recognition depends on receptor flexibility — cryptic pockets, induced fit, water-mediated binding. This group built a category-stratified benchmark across twenty-nine protein-ligand systems and compared poses derived from receptor conformational ensembles against AlphaFold2 as a matched static baseline. Taking the single best pose each method could produce, the ensemble approach beat AlphaFold on twenty-one of twenty-nine targets, mean RMSD three-point-four angstroms versus five-point-six — and, crucially, the advantage concentrated exactly where theory predicts, in the induced-fit and water-mediated classes. That's the honest, strong version of the result. But they were equally honest about the catch, and this is the sentence to internalize: when you have to pick the right pose blind, with no access to the answer, their own autonomous scorer's success rate actually matched-or-modestly-trailed AlphaFold's top-ranked poses under the strictest accuracy-plus-validity criteria. So ensembles clearly contain better poses; reliably selecting them without the crystal structure is the unsolved problem, and they name that scoring bottleneck as the real rate-limiter. It's a company preprint with a proprietary scorer, so weight it accordingly, but I respect a benchmark that reports the unflattering number without spin.

The second paper is the sharpest of the three, and it goes right at why machine-learning scoring functions look so good on paper. Structure-based scoring functions built on ML have been posting superior virtual-screening numbers over classical methods, but this group asked how much of that is understanding binding versus exploiting artifacts. They identify what they call "pocket bias" — a spatial coordinate-frame leak that comes from how binding pockets are statically extracted, which quietly inflates benchmark performance — and then they benchmark two graph-neural-network scorers against two deliberately minimalist ML models and classical AutoDock Vina under four progressively stricter regimes: remove pocket bias, then reduce structural data leakage, then finally test on out-of-distribution protein families. The result is bracing. Once you strip pocket bias and leakage, every ML model's performance drops substantially, and on out-of-distribution targets classical AutoDock Vina — a physics-based docking program from the twenty-tens — outperformed the ML models in five of seven screening tasks and dominated the docking-power evaluation. The take-home isn't "ML docking is fake"; it's that leaderboard rank is being driven by evaluation design and dataset artifacts to a degree that can completely mask whether an algorithm generalizes. If you're choosing a virtual-screening tool for a novel target, this is the paper to make you demand out-of-distribution numbers before you believe anyone's benchmark.

The third computational-binding paper is more granular but in the same spirit of "the physics you're skipping matters." The binding affinity of a ligand depends on the protonation and tautomeric states of both partners, and most pipelines assign one fixed charge and one tautomer and move on. This group used Multi-Conformation Continuum Electrostatics — a physics method that computes the Boltzmann-weighted distribution of protonation and tautomer states rather than picking one — on nine kinase domains bound to eighteen FDA-approved inhibitors. Two findings stand out. The protein's overall charge stayed remarkably stable even when binding a charged drug, but the individual inhibitor's charge was dynamic, frequently gaining or losing a proton upon binding a specific target. And for tautomers, they found cases with three-to-six kilocalorie-per-mole differences in binding free energy between states, where a minority tautomer in solution becomes the dominant species in the bound pocket. If your docking or free-energy calculation locked in the solution-phase tautomer, you got the energetics wrong by an amount that swamps the precision you were chasing. It's a concrete argument for treating protonation and tautomer states as an ensemble in any serious affinity prediction on kinase-like systems.

From binding to allostery, there's an ambitious attempt to give allosteric prediction a transferable, sequence-level prior — which the field mostly lacks, because dynamic descriptors of information flow are computed one structure at a time with no reusable memory. The idea is to build what the authors literally call an alphabet of allostery: a dictionary of local "contact words," short sequence windows anchored by small spatial cliques of residues, each carrying a distribution of transfer-entropy scores — transfer entropy here being a directional measure of how fluctuations at one residue drive another, computed over a Gaussian network model, which is a cheap elastic-network approximation of protein motion. They mined over a hundred and thirty million unique words from the whole non-redundant Protein Data Bank, so any new protein can be projected onto the dictionary to get a per-residue allosteric track with no system-specific fitting. Validated against the Allosteric Database, annotated allosteric sites behave as transfer-entropy sinks — information receivers — and directionally, source residues predicted where the largest apo-to-holo conformational rewiring happens while sinks marked the stable positions. Be clear-eyed: the site-discrimination effect is real but small, a ROC area of about zero-point-five-four, so this is a weak prior, not a detector. The interesting move is conceptual — turning allostery into a reusable vocabulary you can look up rather than recompute — and they spin the most context-variable words into a proposed design toolkit for engineering allosteric switches, which is where I'd expect this to earn its keep.

The last two items are about large language models doing biology, and the first is a genuinely clever reframing of how to use them. The usual pattern compresses heterogeneous evidence — genomic features, homology, database annotations — into numbers and fuses them before prediction, which buries the contribution of any single piece of evidence. This group keeps the evidence uncompressed: they build structured, named, individually-perturbable evidence records and let a general-purpose LLM reason directly over those profiles, with no task-specific training. Their test case, phage-host prediction — which bacterium a given bacteriophage infects — is a nice stressor because host specificity depends on integrating conflicting genomic and functional clues. The system, PHI-Reason, hit species-level top-one accuracies in the fifties-to-sixties percent across benchmarks, beating established numerical methods, and generalized to eukaryotic virus-host prediction. But the part relevant to this audience is the interpretability discipline: because each evidence record is a separate object, they could systematically knock out individual pieces and measure how each supported, complemented, or misled the inference, and they used a Jacobian readout on the model's internals to quantify when the generated rationale actually departed from the supplied evidence. That last check — measuring the gap between the model's stated reasoning and the evidence it was given — is the kind of hygiene that separates an LLM you can trust in a discovery loop from one that just sounds confident.

And we'll build down with the item that's the most fun to think about, a computational-psychiatry piece that runs the logic backwards. We know changes in spontaneous speech are an early signal of Alzheimer's, and that LLMs can detect them from transcripts. But detection alone doesn't tell you whether the model's internal representations of "AD-like language" are functionally doing anything. So this group did an intervention: in an eight-billion-parameter open model, they identified feed-forward neurons that fire more for Alzheimer's transcripts than for controls, then scaled those neurons' output contributions up or down during generation, and put the edited models through the same twelve-turn neuropsychological battery a patient would take, scored by blinded human raters. Amplifying the AD-associated neurons produced graded impairments across story recall, verbal fluency, working memory, and discourse, and — the striking parallel — it reduced lexical surprisal, idea density, and syntactic complexity in the same directions reported for actual human AD speech. Turning those neurons down largely preserved, and sometimes slightly improved, performance. Now, the obvious caution: this is a language model, not a brain, and inducing a linguistic phenotype is not modeling the disease. But as a tool it's provocative — a controllable, dissectable system where you can causally manipulate a "phenotype" and read out exactly which computations shift, which is something you cannot ethically or practically do in a patient. Whether that buys real mechanistic insight or just a compelling mimicry is the argument worth having, and it's a fitting place to end.

That's the show for today — traveling beta waves reframing parkinsonian STN activity, an ectopic developmental sodium channel driving early Alzheimer's hyperexcitability, an ABCA7 proteome pointing at brain insulin signaling, a non-linear lifespan atlas of the EEG, three hard looks at whether computational docking and scoring actually generalize, a transferable alphabet for allostery, an evidence-grounded LLM for host prediction, and an LLM taught to speak like Alzheimer's.
