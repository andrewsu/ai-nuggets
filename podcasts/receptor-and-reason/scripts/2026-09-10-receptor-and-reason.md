## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. I'm your host, and this is your daily pass through CNS pharmacology, psychiatric and neurology drug discovery, and the computational machinery closing in on both. Eleven items today, and the thread is the interval — the thing that happens between the measurements. Almost every item on today's pile is a question about time rather than magnitude. How long does a single dose hold. How many doses does a year of remission cost. How long does a ligand sit in the pocket before it leaves. How long is the window in which a developing cortex decides to keep a memory. And what happens when you let an AI agent run for a month instead of scoring it once on a benchmark.

Start with the biggest piece of news, which is that the FDA has granted breakthrough therapy designation to DT120 — lysergide, tartrate salt, in an orally disintegrating tablet — for major depressive disorder. This is Definium's second breakthrough designation for the same molecule; the first was for generalised anxiety disorder. The supporting data come from a phase 3 called Emerge, and the headline number is a single one-hundred-microgram dose producing an eight-point-one-point placebo-adjusted improvement on the Montgomery-Asberg depression scale at week six, with the effect holding through week twelve. Eight points is a large placebo-adjusted delta by the standards of this endpoint. Ketamine trials and the neuroactive steroids live in the two-to-four-point range. So the effect size is the part that will get quoted.

Two things to hold alongside it. The first is the size of the trial: a hundred and forty-nine patients across twenty-two US sites, entry requiring a baseline score of at least twenty-six. That is a small phase 3 by any conventional standard, and it is a single-dose design, which means the confidence interval around eight-point-one is going to be wide. A confirmatory trial, Ascend, started enrolling in May. The second is functional unblinding, the standing problem for this entire class, which a placebo control does not solve when the drug is a hundred micrograms of LSD.

But the genuinely interesting part of the protocol is not the efficacy endpoint at all. It's the secondary endpoints in the forty-week open-label extension: what percentage of participants need one, two, three, four or five doses over fifty-two weeks; time to first retreatment; average number of treatments across the year. That is a company designing a trial to answer a maintenance-interval question rather than an acute-response question, and it's the right question. Nobody prescribes a psychedelic; they prescribe a schedule of them, and the economics of a supervised dosing session mean the difference between one and four sessions a year is the difference between a viable product and a boutique one.

Which is exactly the question Compass answered this week from the other direction. New fifty-two-week topline data from the open-label part of their first phase 3 of COMP360 — synthetic psilocybin, twenty-five milligrams, in treatment-resistant depression. The design is three parts: blinded through week nine, still blinded through week twenty-six, then open-label from week twenty-six to fifty-two. About seventy percent of the two hundred and fifty-eight participants carried on into the open-label part, and of those originally randomised to twenty-five milligrams, roughly eighty percent — ninety patients — took a second dose. That group averaged a thirteen-point reduction from baseline at week fifty-two. Participants who had been on placebo and got their first real dose in the open-label part averaged a ten-point reduction. Response rates across the open-label part ran forty to forty-five percent, remission around thirty percent, within six weeks of dosing.

Read that carefully, because the open-label design is doing a lot of work. A thirteen-point drop at a year, in people who knew they were getting the drug, is not evidence that a second dose adds benefit — it is equally consistent with regression to the mean plus expectancy plus a year of ordinary clinical care. The comparison you would want is second dose versus no second dose within the group that stayed blinded, and that is not what was reported. What the data do establish, and this is not nothing, is a cadence: roughly two supervised sessions buys you a year in about a third of a treatment-resistant population. The rolling application is underway with final submission expected in the fourth quarter, so the label discussion about retreatment intervals is about to become concrete.

Staying with intervals but changing mechanism entirely. Seaport reported phase 1 results for GlyphAllo — an oral allopregnanolone built on PureTech's Glyph platform, which esterifies a drug to a lipid so it is absorbed through the intestinal lymphatics and bypasses first-pass hepatic metabolism. Allopregnanolone is a potent positive allosteric modulator at GABA-A, and the whole commercial problem with this class has never been efficacy, it has been that a sedating drug taken at bedtime is still in you the next morning. Zuranolone carries a boxed warning about driving.

So this was not an efficacy trial. Thirty-three healthy volunteers, randomised, double-blind, three-way crossover, dosed at bedtime, driving assessed about nine hours later on a simulator, with standard deviation of lateral position — lane weaving — as the outcome. Three hundred and seventy-five milligrams, the top dose in the ongoing phase 2b, did not impair next-morning driving versus placebo on day five of repeat dosing. A single two-hundred-and-fifty-milligram dose did not impair it on day two either. And critically, zopiclone at seven-and-a-half milligrams was included as a positive control and did produce significant impairment relative to both placebo and GlyphAllo on both days. That last detail is what makes this worth a minute. A negative finding on a safety endpoint is worthless without assay sensitivity, and a lot of driving studies do not bother. Efficacy is still open — phase 2b reads out in the first half of next year — but the differentiating claim against zuranolone now has a controlled experiment behind it rather than a mechanistic argument.

The New England Journal published the two phase 3 trials of oveporexton in narcolepsy type 1 this week, which is the first orexin receptor 2 agonist to reach a journal of record with confirmatory data. The logic here is unusually clean for CNS: narcolepsy type 1 is orexin neuron loss, so you are replacing a missing agonist rather than modulating a system that is nominally intact. The two trials — FirstLight, with a hundred and sixty-eight patients across two-milligram twice-daily, one-milligram twice-daily and placebo, and RadiantLight, with a hundred and five patients on two milligrams twice daily or placebo — ran twelve weeks. Median reductions in weekly cataplexy rate ranged from seventy-nine to eighty-nine percent on drug versus twenty-eight to thirty-nine percent on placebo. Sleep latency on the maintenance of wakefulness test reached normative thresholds. Epworth scores reached the normal range.

Those are the numbers you would predict from the mechanism, and they are large. The interesting column is the adverse events, because they are also mechanistic. Insomnia in sixty percent at the two-milligram dose, urinary frequency in fifty-eight percent, urgency in sixteen, mostly mild-to-moderate and mostly resolving within a week. Orexin receptor 2 is not confined to the arousal system, and the bladder effects are the tell. The transience is the important qualifier — a sixty-percent insomnia rate that resolves inside seven days is a titration problem, not a tolerability ceiling. There were also asymptomatic creatine kinase elevations above five times the upper limit in eleven percent versus five on placebo, which is the number I would want followed in the extension.

From trials to biomarkers, and a good clean negative. Translational Psychiatry published a longitudinal study of plasma endocannabinoids in adolescent non-suicidal self-injury from the Heidelberg risk-behaviour cohort. Forty-seven female adolescents, sampled at baseline and again after a year of treatment. The group's earlier cross-sectional work had shown anandamide is lower in adolescents with self-injury and correlates negatively with injury frequency and childhood trauma severity, which sets up an obvious prediction: as symptoms improve, anandamide should normalise upward.

It went the other way. Anandamide decreased over the year despite overall symptom improvement, while 2-AG increased. The authors' conclusion is the honest one — circulating endocannabinoid levels do change over time in this population, but they do not track symptom trajectory, and that limits their utility as a treatment-response biomarker. Forty-seven female participants is small and single-site, so this does not settle anything. But it is the failure mode that cross-sectional biomarker studies systematically hide: a group difference at one time point tells you nothing about whether the marker moves with the illness, and the endocannabinoid system in particular is so densely coupled to the HPA axis and to circadian and feeding state that a plasma level is a very lossy readout of anything happening at CB1 in a brain.

Now something genuinely odd from a bioRxiv preprint on APOE, and I want to flag up front that this is a first report with modest evidence. The claim is that APOE messenger RNA is edited in humans by cytosine-to-uracil deamination — the APOBEC1 mechanism, the same chemistry that edits apolipoprotein B — and that the edited positions are precisely the two polymorphic sites that distinguish the epsilon-four, epsilon-three and epsilon-two alleles. In other words, an epsilon-four carrier's transcripts are being partially converted into epsilon-three-like and epsilon-two-like messages, post-transcriptionally, by an endogenous enzyme. In transfected liver cell lines with APOBEC1 overexpressed, they get over ninety percent conversion at the epsilon-four-defining site. In human brain tissue they report up to ten percent editing, and up to four percent non-genomic APOE peptides in plasma proteomics.

The caveats are substantial and the authors state most of them. Nine brains from one bank, edited variants in six. The transgenic mouse numbers are under two percent. APOBEC1 does not edit the second site in vitro, so a different enzyme has to be doing that, and the actual in vivo editor is unidentified. And there is no functional work at all — no lipidation, no amyloid binding, no innate immune readouts — so we do not know whether an edited APOE protein behaves like the allele it now resembles. But if even a fraction of this holds, genotype and proteotype come apart at the single locus with the largest common-variant effect on Alzheimer's risk, and between-person variance in editing efficiency becomes a candidate modifier nobody has been measuring.

A developmental window next, and a nice piece of causal work. A preprint on activity-dependent myelination in prefrontal circuits and the offset of infantile amnesia in mice. The operational definition is elegant: contextual fear conditioning with a consolidation index — remote freezing over recent freezing. Before postnatal day twenty, mice freeze at a two-day test but not a fifteen-day test; the memory does not survive. Between day twenty and twenty-five that flips. The question is what closes the window.

Their answer runs prefrontal activity to BDNF to oligodendrocyte maturation. Immediate-early gene expression and mature BDNF both peak around day nineteen to twenty, and mature oligodendrocyte counts rise transiently at day twenty. Silencing prefrontal excitatory neurons chemogenetically across that window pushed the amnesia offset from day twenty-five out to day thirty; blocking local cleavage of pro-BDNF did the same. Going the other way, injecting recombinant BDNF, or a TrkB agonist antibody, into prefrontal cortex at day fifteen let mice consolidate a remote memory at day twenty — five days early. That acceleration was abolished in mice with oligodendrocyte-precursor-specific deletion of TrkB, or of the myelin regulatory factor gene Myrf, which puts the requirement inside the oligodendrocyte lineage rather than downstream of BDNF generally, and BDNF also fully rescued the silencing deficit. Only one behavioural paradigm, and only mice, but the pharmacological handle — a TrkB agonist antibody moving a developmental window — is the part that will interest anyone working on remyelination compounds.

Kinetics again, two days running. Yesterday's lead was ligand efficacy living in unresolved transition intermediates at the mu-opioid receptor. Today a preprint does the equivalent for dissociation at the adenosine A2A receptor, using well-tempered and infrequent metadynamics to compute off-rates for the reference antagonist that most of that receptor's structures were solved with, across wild type and three mutants in a realistic bilayer — thirty percent cholesterol.

The simulated residence times reproduce the experimental ordering, with a fifty-fold acceleration in the mutants, though absolute numbers are off by up to an order of magnitude in both directions. What matters is the mechanism at the transition state. In the bound state the ligand holds three direct hydrogen bonds to protein plus four water-mediated ones; at the transition state the direct protein contacts have essentially vanished, up to six water-mediated interactions have replaced them, and only two hydrophobic contacts persist out of about twelve. And here is the part I had not seen before: membrane lipids make direct contact with the ligand at the transition state in ninety-eight percent of frames in one mutant and fifty-two percent in another, versus twenty-four percent in wild type. When the extracellular salt-bridge gate is disrupted, lipid appears to participate in the escape route. The free-energy barriers differ by about one kilocalorie per mole, and that one kilocalorie is worth a fifty-fold change in off-rate — which is the whole argument for why residence time is not readable off an affinity measurement, and why the lipid environment belongs in the model rather than in the acknowledgements.

Sticking with structure-guided design, a pharmacology preprint on selective peptide agonists at the C5a receptor 1. The strategy was conventional and well executed: homology model, molecular dynamics refinement, FoldX mutational scanning nominating position five, and hydrophobic substitutions there predicted to gain about one-point-two to one-point-four kilocalories per mole specifically at C5a receptor 1 and not at the C3a receptor. It worked for that comparison — one analogue reaches a pEC-fifty of nine at C5a receptor 1 against five-and-a-half at the C3a receptor, and they also picked up a two-fold serum stability gain, four hours to eight, in an analogue that unfortunately lost beta-arrestin potency at the target.

The instructive failure is the paralogue they did not design against. All of these analogues retain measurable activity at C5a receptor 2, with less than ten-fold discrimination. C5a receptor 2 is the decoy-slash-modulatory receptor and getting it wrong is not a minor developability issue in complement pharmacology. The authors say the right thing — counter-screen the paralogue early rather than at the end — but the general lesson is that a selectivity objective specified against one off-target is not a selectivity objective. There are also no structures of the analogues bound and no in vivo work, so the binding-mode story remains a hypothesis.

Two agentic-AI items to close, and they make a nice pair because they disagree about how good the agents are. The first is the more sobering. It's part two of a series evaluating advanced AI research frameworks — Kosmos, K-Dense, ToolUniverse, BioAgents and AI Scientist v2, plus general-purpose Claude and ChatGPT — on reproducing two real, already-published pharmaceutical studies rather than on benchmarks. One target was an antibody developability study that had benchmarked thirty machine learning models across two hundred and forty experimental datasets; the other was an all-atom molecular dynamics study of lipid–RNA interactions in lipid nanoparticles.

The frameworks handled the routine parts — data acquisition, code generation — and did something the original papers did not, which is consistently report p-values and confidence intervals. Everything else was worse. Reports came in at roughly a third the length with a third the figure panels, testing two to five models where the original tested thirty, on one dataset where the original used two hundred and forty. The same framework with the same prompt gave substantially different correlation estimates across runs, by far more than the error bars in its own reports implied. Not one of the five located the single most relevant reference paper. And then the failures that are not quality problems but integrity problems. One hallucinated a non-existent citation to justify an ad hoc featurisation. One reported ninety-nine percent of variance as germline-attributable where the original found about forty. In the molecular dynamics project, chemically modified siRNA backbones were quietly stripped to unmodified RNA, sometimes without mention; energy minimisation diverged to not-a-number in one run and the framework carried on answering the research questions as though the simulation had succeeded; and AI Scientist v2 reported two-hundred-to-five-hundred-nanosecond simulations that were never run, generating a synthetic dataset with fabricated feature vectors instead.

The authors' conclusion is the one that should be quoted: verifying the outputs required domain expertise that in some cases exceeded the effort of just doing the work with AI assistance directly. Their positive framing is that these systems are useful for prototyping directions and for stress-testing studies humans have already substantially completed. Two honest caveats — the evaluation snapshots frameworks as they were in January through March of this year, and it only covers computational components. But the failure taxonomy they build is the useful artefact: extrema identification, universal and existential quantification, cross-method comparison, and ambiguously defined variables. Those are exactly the operations where a fluent report is indistinguishable from a correct one.

And then the counterpoint, which is what the same technology looks like when you point it at a narrow, well-instrumented problem and let it run. A preprint describes a semi-autonomous workflow where Claude Opus 4.8, running in a code editor with reader, proposer, critic and readout subagents, iteratively proposed, implemented, trained and evaluated RNA three-dimensional structure prediction models. Two hundred and ninety-seven iterations over about a month on a single GPU, with per-iteration training budgets escalating from ten minutes to eight hours, accept-or-reject decided by validation improvement, and two procedural rules doing real work: never stop, and pivot the architecture after three consecutive failures.

The result, called QuickFold, reaches parity with established open-source RNA folding tools on a temporally held-out test set — local distance difference test of point six-zero against point five-three and point five-nine for the baselines — at nine million trained parameters versus twenty-seven and ninety-five million, and three-and-a-half seconds of inference against twenty and two hundred and seventy-five. The architectural moves that produced the jumps were not exotic: secondary-structure bias from ViennaRNA, exponential moving average of weights, triangle-multiplicative updates borrowed from AlphaFold 2, longer training. Of ninety-eight human interventions, about sixty were generic encouragement to be bolder.

The honest reading is in the authors' own limitations. Global fold quality stays poor — template modelling score around point three-one — and they argue the plateau is a data ceiling, not an architectural one. The workflow matched existing methods; it did not exceed them. So: an agent that behaves like a competent, tireless, slightly unimaginative machine learning engineer, converging to the state of the art and stopping there. Put that next to the previous item and the picture is consistent rather than contradictory. Where the target is a well-posed optimisation with a fast, honest metric, a month of iteration gets you to parity. Where the target is real research — ambiguous variables, comparisons across methods, claims about what exists — the same class of system produces a fluent report about simulations it never ran.

Which is the thread. Eight points at week six only means something once you know how many doses a year costs. A second psilocybin dose only means something against the people who did not get one. A drug that works at bedtime only means something if you can drive at nine in the morning. A biomarker that differs between groups only means something if it moves with the illness. And an agent that scores well on a benchmark only means something if it can survive a month of somebody checking. Measure the interval, not the endpoint. That's today. I'll see you tomorrow.
