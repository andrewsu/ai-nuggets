## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. It's Tuesday, August twelfth, and today is almost entirely a bioRxiv day — the arXiv listing API was hard rate-limited from our host, and the Nature psychiatry journals were behind their usual login wall, so the neuroscience preprint firehose carried the episode. It carried it well. We've got a new molecular brake on glutamate release, a psychedelic doing something surprising to microglia, some sharp computational drug-design work, and, to close, an antipsychotic moonlighting as an antiviral. Nine items. Let's go.

We open with the cleanest new-target story of the week, out of St. Jude. The authors went looking in the dark proteome — proteins that are deeply conserved from worms to humans, enriched in the brain, and completely uncharacterized — and pulled out one they've named RABIN, spelled R-A-B-I-N, for Rab inhibitor. Its gene is LRRC57. It's a small, twenty-seven-kilodalton horseshoe-shaped protein that sits on synaptic vesicles in glutamatergic terminals. And here's the mechanism, which is the interesting part. Rab GTPases are the master switches for vesicle trafficking; when they're loaded with GTP they recruit effector proteins to drive vesicle docking and release. We've long known how Rabs get switched off — the GAPs, the GDIs, the usual machinery. What we didn't have was a mechanism that restrains the active, GTP-bound Rab from ever reaching its effector. RABIN is that mechanism. It binds multiple active synaptic Rabs and competitively occupies the effector-binding surface — a decoy. Delete it from forebrain excitatory neurons and glutamate release climbs, the recycling vesicle pool expands, turnover speeds up, and the circuit tips into hyperexcitability with epileptiform activity. Those mice have a lowered threshold for kainic-acid seizures, and — the detail that matters therapeutically — the phenotype is rescued by levetiracetam, the antiseizure drug that works through the vesicle protein SV2A. Run it the other way, overexpress RABIN, and you suppress release and protect against induced seizures. The authors are careful to say RABIN isn't a disease gene — no human mutations have ever been found, which makes sense given the conservation. Instead they frame it as an epistatic modifier with therapeutic upside, and they reach for the PCSK9 analogy: a target you'd never treat by fixing a mutation, but one you might drug to dial a whole system down. A presynaptic-release rheostat is exactly that kind of target.

Staying at the excitatory synapse, a nice companion piece on Homer1. A group reports a brand-new de novo human variant, an arginine-to-tryptophan swap at position 297, sitting in the coiled-coil domain that lets Homer1 tetramerize. Homer1b/c is the scaffolding protein that physically clusters metabotropic glutamate receptor five together with the calcium machinery — the IP3 receptor, STIM proteins, the store-operated calcium entry apparatus. The variant behaves as a dominant negative. In sensory neurons it blunts growth-cone turning toward BDNF gradients, a guidance process that depends on store-operated calcium. In hippocampal neurons it lowers spine density, keeps the endoplasmic reticulum from infiltrating spines, drops metabotropic glutamate receptor five expression, and flattens the calcium response to group-one receptor activation. Super-resolution imaging shows the receptors and their partners literally declustering. It's a clean mechanistic account of how one point mutation in a scaffolding hub can propagate into the kind of connectivity defects we associate with epilepsy and autism — and a reminder that the druggable surface in these disorders is often the scaffold, not the receptor. It also lands next to the RABIN story in a way I find useful: both papers are about restraint at the synapse — one presynaptic, holding release in check, the other postsynaptic, organizing the calcium-signaling response — and in both, losing the brake tips the circuit toward hyperexcitability. Different proteins, same failure mode, and the same therapeutic logic of restoring a set point rather than blocking a receptor outright.

Third, the psychedelic item, and it's not about the trip. N,N-dimethyltryptamine — DMT, the endogenous tryptamine — has been racking up preclinical wins in ischemic stroke: smaller infarcts, less neuroinflammation, better recovery. Nobody really knew why. This group went to primary microglia and asked whether DMT acts on them directly. It does. After an inflammatory challenge, DMT pushed microglia back toward a homeostatic morphology and dialed down phagocytosis, and the proteomics showed it wasn't a blanket shutdown — it selectively suppressed cytokine, chemokine, and oxidative-stress pathways while sparing the arachidonic-acid–prostaglandin axis. On receptors, DMT bound microglial sigma-1 receptors at micromolar affinity, and the pharmacology dissociated cleanly: the morphological reprogramming needed both sigma-1 and serotonergic signaling, but the drop in phagocytosis was largely independent of either. In brain slices under mild oxygen-glucose deprivation, DMT cut microglial activation, slowed spreading depolarizations, and reduced neuronal injury — and that tissue-level protection rode on serotonergic signaling. The takeaway is that DMT is a targeted immunomodulator of microglia with context-dependent receptor logic, which is a much more actionable story for stroke translation than a vague anti-inflammatory hand-wave. And the sigma-1 angle is the part I'd watch: sigma-1 is a chaperone at the ER-mitochondria interface that keeps coming up in neuroprotection, and if a chunk of DMT's benefit runs through it rather than through the classic 5-HT2A trip receptor, that opens the door to capturing the neuroprotection without the psychedelic experience — the same non-hallucinogenic-derivative logic that's driving a lot of the current psychedelic medicinal chemistry. One caveat worth stating plainly: this preprint went up yesterday and the full text hasn't rendered yet, so I'm working from a detailed abstract — I'll revisit if the body changes the picture.

From there to the reward system. A multidimensional profiling study of the lateral habenula — the little epithalamic hub that gates reward and aversion by projecting onto the midbrain monoamine centers. We know the habenula is heterogeneous, but it's been hard to tie a neuron's genetic identity to what it actually does in a behaving animal. Using existing transgenic lines to tag specific subpopulations, the authors line up spatial gene expression, intrinsic electrophysiology, and projection targets, and then show that two genetically defined subtypes respond differently during flexible reward-guided behavior — one tracking value and prediction error, another tracking directional movement. The point is parallel channels: distinct, genetically addressable habenular populations feeding the serotonergic and dopaminergic systems separately. And this matters because the habenula has become one of the more interesting antidepressant targets going — it's where the rapid antidepressant action of ketamine has been tied to a specific burst-firing pattern, and it's an emerging deep-brain-stimulation target in treatment-resistant depression. The problem with hitting it therapeutically is that "the lateral habenula" isn't one thing. If value-coding and movement-coding cells are separable populations projecting to different monoamine systems, then the blunt approaches — lesion it, stimulate it, silence it wholesale — are almost certainly hitting channels you didn't mean to. This kind of subtype map is the prerequisite for doing anything precise there.

Sticking with serotonin and dopamine, but taking a hard left into weird and wonderful. Arc is the neuronal gene that evolved from a retrotransposon and, remarkably, still forms virus-like capsids that package RNA and ferry it between cells. It's been a mechanism in search of a clear behavioral job. This study, in Drosophila, gives it one: setting sleep depth. Knock out the fly Arc genes and sleep gets deeper and more consolidated — higher arousal threshold to a physical nudge — without disturbing the day-night pattern. The requirement maps specifically to serotonergic neurons, and to a lesser degree the dopaminergic PAM cluster, and — this is the key control — both the phenotype and its rescue depend on the capsid actually forming. So this is capsid-mediated intercellular signaling between two modulatory neuron populations tuning an ongoing homeostatic behavior. It's early and it's a fly, but the idea that a domesticated viral protein is a bona fide signaling channel in the monoaminergic sleep circuitry is the kind of mechanism that rewrites a textbook paragraph.

Now three computational items. First, a structure-based drug-discovery pipeline aimed at Sanfilippo syndrome type C — a lysosomal storage disorder, formally mucopolysaccharidosis three-C, where a broken enzyme lets heparan sulfate pile up and drives progressive neurodegeneration. Enzyme replacement barely crosses the blood-brain barrier, so the strategy here is substrate reduction: instead of clearing heparan sulfate faster, make less of it by inhibiting a biosynthetic enzyme upstream, NDST1. The team docked roughly four million drug-like compounds plus the FDA-approved set against the enzyme's sulfotransferase domain, filtered on predicted pharmacokinetics, then ran microsecond molecular-dynamics simulations and binding free-energy calculations on the survivors — and in parallel used diffusion-based protein design to generate peptide binders for the same site. They come out with four distinct small-molecule scaffolds and three peptides, the lead compound at a predicted binding free energy around minus thirteen kilocalories per mole. It's all in silico — no wet validation yet, and a predicted binding free energy with an error bar nearly half its size tells you how much these numbers should be trusted — but it's a well-constructed argument that NDST1 substrate reduction is worth chasing for a disease with almost no options. Substrate reduction has a real precedent in this space — it's how miglustat and related drugs work in other lysosomal disorders — so the concept isn't speculative, only the target and the molecules are. And targeting a biosynthetic enzyme upstream is an appealing way around the blood-brain-barrier problem that has hobbled enzyme replacement for the neurological forms of these diseases. Treat this as a hypothesis-generator with a nicely stacked computational argument behind it, not a candidate.

Second computational piece, and it's a method the pose-prediction crowd should note. Protein-ligand co-folding models — the AlphaFold-style systems that fold a protein and its small-molecule partner together — are great at the protein and often wrong about where the ligand actually sits. This paper, Boltz-Perturb, makes a sharp diagnosis: using true-coordinate injection, they show the model's learned energy landscape already contains the correct binding-mode basins. So the failure isn't knowledge, it's sampling. The fix is entirely at inference time — perturb the model's conditioning signals to force it to explore alternative poses — no retraining. Their perturbation strategy improves top-twenty binding-mode success rates by roughly three- to eight-fold, and beats the brute-force approach of just cranking the diffusion temperature while using over seventy-five percent less compute. Why care? Because co-folding is quietly becoming the default first pass for structure-based design — it's cheaper than docking into an experimental structure you may not have, and it handles induced fit that rigid docking misses. But if the pose is wrong, everything downstream — the binding free energies, the SAR you read off the model, the analog you decide to make — inherits that error. A training-free trick that recovers the right pose most of the time, for a quarter of the compute, is the kind of unglamorous engineering that actually moves a discovery pipeline. And the deeper lesson generalizes past this one architecture: a lot of the headroom in generative structure models is locked up in how we sample them, not in the weights.

The third and lightest of the computational trio pairs naturally with that. A de novo binder-design study makes the case that a good global structure score is not the same as a good binding pocket. Designing protein binders for three chemically very different natural products — flexible capsaicin, compact limonene, rigid quercetin — they show that many backbones with excellent overall confidence scores fail to form a sensible local pocket, and only a subset preserve the pocket geometry and ligand contacts after you actually design the sequence and re-fold it. The practical message is the same as Boltz-Perturb's from the other direction: evaluate the local ligand environment explicitly, because the global metrics will lie to you about the part you care about.

And to close, a genuinely fun one for a pharmacology audience. A screen of nearly fifteen hundred FDA-approved drugs for compounds that block hepatitis B transcription pulled out two familiar names: vorapaxar, the PAR-1 antagonist antiplatelet drug, and aripiprazole, the antipsychotic. Both suppress viral replication, and they do it through host signaling, not the virus directly — converging on STAT3, but by different routes. Aripiprazole knocks down the transcription factor HNF4 through an ERK/JNK pathway; vorapaxar works through PAR-1, SRC, and EGFR. Both held up in human liver chimeric mice at clinically relevant exposures. Now — this is a liver-and-virus story, not a brain story, and nobody's suggesting you treat hepatitis B with an antipsychotic. But the reason it belongs on this show is the reminder it carries: the drugs we know as CNS agents have rich, promiscuous host-target pharmacology, and a dopamine-receptor drug turning up as a STAT3-dependent antiviral is exactly the kind of off-target biology that repurposing screens keep surfacing. Worth filing away.
