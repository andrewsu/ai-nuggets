## Script

Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Sunday, October eleventh. Ten items today, and one sourcing note up front: bioRxiv rate-limited this machine for most of the run, so only two preprints from that server made it through with readable full text. medRxiv was wide open, journals were fine, arXiv was fine. The imbalance you'll hear toward clinical and computational work is a network artifact, not a judgment about the week.

There's a thread running through almost everything today, and it's worth naming up front. Every result here is a comparison, and in nearly every case the thing being compared against was chosen rather than measured. Pick the comparator and you have largely picked the conclusion. Some of today's papers understand that about themselves; some don't.

We open with the one where the comparator makes the claim stronger rather than weaker. A group has used de novo protein design to build a selective inhibitor of human ASIC one-a — the acid-sensing ion channel that opens when tissue pH drops and that has been implicated for two decades in the neuronal death that follows ischemic stroke. The target has always been attractive and always been hard. The best tool the field has is psalmotoxin, a tarantula peptide that works beautifully in a dish and does not cross the blood-brain barrier, and small molecules have had selectivity problems. So the authors went computational: they targeted the extracellular acidic pocket, where a particular phenylalanine kept showing up as a hydrophobic hotspot, and ran the now-standard pipeline — backbone generation with RFdiffusion, sequence design with ProteinMPNN, structural filtering with AlphaFold2. Twelve thousand backbones, seventy thousand sequences, seventy-six candidates synthesized and put on automated patch clamp. Two hits. The lead, which they call Denasin one, is forty-four amino acids, with an IC fifty around twenty-three nanomolar and near-complete block.

Two things make this more than another design-pipeline paper. First, selectivity: no inhibition of ASIC one-b, two-a, or three under the conditions tested, which is exactly where small molecules have failed. Second, and more interesting mechanistically — circular dichroism says Denasin one is essentially unstructured in solution, under ten percent helix, and the cryo-EM structure of the bound complex at about two point seven angstroms shows it adopting the designed helix-turn-helix fold only once it's on the channel. That's fold-upon-binding, and it isn't what the pipeline was asked for: AlphaFold2 filtering selected for a predicted folded structure, and what came out was a peptide that only folds on arrival.

And then the in vivo comparison, which is the part I'd hold onto. In transient middle cerebral artery occlusion in mice, intrathecal Denasin one took infarct volume from about nineteen and a half percent down to about eight point six percent. Psalmotoxin, run in parallel, got it to thirteen point one. Beating the field's reference peptide in the same experiment is a much stronger statement than beating vehicle, and it's the kind of comparator choice that costs you if the molecule isn't actually better. Caveats are real and the authors list them: one sex, intrathecal delivery, no pharmacokinetics, no blood-brain-barrier data, no toxicology. This is a platform paper with a genuinely good molecule in it.

Staying with ion channels but moving to the opposite pole of the design spectrum — a framework for CLIFAHDD, an ultra-rare neurodevelopmental disorder caused by de novo gain-of-function variants in NALCN, the sodium leak channel. No approved treatments, so the strategy is repurposing. They built a fluorescence membrane-potential assay in cells expressing the full NALCN channelosome, screened about twelve hundred mostly-approved compounds at ten micromolar in a single pass, and got eleven hits at a sixty-percent-inhibition threshold. The lead is aprepitant — the NK-one receptor antagonist used clinically as an antiemetic — with an IC fifty of two point two micromolar on wild-type NALCN.

Here's why this one belongs in the comparator thread. The paper is framed as AI-enabled, and the AI component is Boltz-2 co-folding predicting binding affinity and pose. But read the sequence of events: the screen found the hits, and Boltz-2 was then run retrospectively across the library, where it placed four of the eleven experimental hits in its top thirty by predicted affinity. That's a real signal and worth reporting. It is not the same thing as the model having found the drug. The lead was picked by a human expert panel weighing pharmacology, brain penetration, safety, and regulatory status. The honest description is a phenotypic screen with a model-based sanity check and expert selection on top.

The number that actually worries me is elsewhere. Aprepitant's maximal inhibition of wild-type NALCN is only about thirty-six percent — and against the two pathogenic variants they tested, potency improves slightly while maximal inhibition falls to roughly fourteen percent. The compound is least efficacious against the thing it's meant to treat. Selectivity is also uncomfortable: aprepitant hits Cav three-point-one with an IC fifty of one point six micromolar and over ninety percent block, more potent and far more complete than its action on the intended target. For an untreatable ultra-rare disease the risk calculus is different and I understand the urgency, but fourteen percent block of the mutant channel is a thin thread.

Staying with drug discovery but moving from a single target to a whole phenotypic landscape — a Molecular Psychiatry paper that knocks out eleven high-confidence autism risk genes in zebrafish and asks whether they sort into pharmacologically meaningful groups. F-zero CRISPR knockouts of SYNGAP1, POGZ, SHANK3, KMT5B, CHD8, GRIN2B, PTEN, ARID1B, FOXP1, ADNP and SLC6A1, paralogs targeted together, then a high-frame-rate social interaction assay whose output is run through an autoencoder, wavelet decomposition and watershed segmentation into eighty behavioral categories. Each mutant gets an eighty-dimensional fingerprint. Those fingerprints are then correlated against a reference drug library, which is where this becomes pharmacology rather than genetics. SYNGAP1 knockouts correlate with serotonergic compound classes at essentially one point zero — SNRIs, 5-HT2 antagonists, 5-HT1 agonists all at zero point nine nine. POGZ and SHANK3 line up with D1 antagonists at around zero point six five. Most of the rest correlate with D2 and D4 antagonists and anti-correlate with mu-one opioid antagonists, which generated a testable prediction: give those mutants naloxonazine, a mu-one-selective opioid antagonist, and their social preference should improve.

They ran it, and the honest scorecard is seven of eleven predictions correct. ARID1B, ADNP, GRIN2B and PTEN were predicted responders and responded; KMT5B, CHD8 and SYNGAP1 were predicted non-responders and didn't respond. FOXP1 and SLC6A1 were predicted to respond and didn't, and POGZ and SHANK3 responded despite failing the correlation threshold. The authors say plainly that eleven genotypes cannot establish predictive accuracy, and that the analysis enriches for rather than determines pharmacological responsiveness. Two things to hold onto. The comparator problem is in the rescue criterion: a "modest rescue" is defined as a treated mutant that is statistically indistinguishable from wild-type while also not significantly different from the untreated mutant — two null results converted into a positive call, which is exactly what underpowering produces. Three of the six rescues rest on that criterion, and three rescue arms ran below the paper's own stated power floor of sixteen per group. And the reference library covers only dopaminergic, serotonergic and opioidergic compounds — so SLC6A1, a GABA transporter mutant, was scored against a library containing no GABAergic drugs at all, then counted as a failed prediction. Controls are uninjected siblings rather than scrambled-guide injections, and wild-type fish never appear in the clustering that defines the subtypes, so "distinct from each other" is never referenced to the normal behavioral range.

Now to the clinical side, and to a result with a genuinely impressive headline number and a genuinely serious comparator problem. A company has built what they call a neuroplasticity score: patient-derived induced pluripotent stem cells differentiated into cortical neurons, exposed to an antidepressant, and scored by how far the treated condition's morphology and synaptic puncta profile moves from the matched vehicle condition. The pipeline was frozen before clinical linkage and never refit, which is the right discipline. Biological validation against BDNF dose-response is clean — Spearman rho zero point eight three. Then seventy-five treatment episodes across forty-four patients in two cohorts. Response rates by score tertile, pooled: sixteen point seven percent in the bottom third, forty percent in the middle, eighty-four point six percent in the top. Area under the curve about zero point eight one in both cohorts independently. And the within-patient result, which is the one that should matter most: among twenty-two patients who contributed both a drug that worked and a drug that didn't, the higher-scoring drug was the one that worked in nineteen of twenty-two.

Here is the problem, and the authors do disclose it. The larger cohort was selected out of STAR*D specifically for patients who had one failed and one successful treatment episode. That manufactures the roughly fifty percent base rate the headline is measured against, and worse, it makes response almost collinear with treatment level — five percent response at level one, eighty-nine percent at level two, one hundred percent at level three — while the score itself also rises with treatment level. Put treatment level into the model and the odds ratio stays around nine but the significance evaporates, p equals zero point zero seven three. So in the cohort that provides sixteen of the nineteen within-patient wins, the biomarker cannot be separated from line of therapy. The second cohort is genuinely independent but contributes three of three patients to that headline, and twelve of its thirty-five outcomes are the treating clinician's unblinded judgment rather than a rating scale. The score is also plate- and cohort-relative, so the tertile cutoffs don't travel. I want this to work — functional patient-derived prediction of antidepressant response is one of the real prizes in this field — and the within-patient design is the right instinct. It needs a prospective cohort that wasn't assembled to contain one success and one failure.

Next, a randomized experiment that I think everyone prescribing or deploying clinical AI should read, with a comparator caveat that is not cosmetic. Two hundred twenty-five physicians from forty-two countries, median ten years of intensive care experience, each worked four anonymized neurocritical care cases with an AI assistant at four sequential decision points. Same model, same weights, same interface in both arms — the only difference was a concealed case-specific system instruction making the model push a predefined harmful option. The adversarial model advocated that harmful target at ninety-four percent of targeted decision points. Physicians' final decisions were harmful forty-nine point four percent of the time with the adversarial assistant versus two percent with the aligned one, an adjusted difference of forty-seven percentage points. Eighty-four percent of physicians made at least one harmful decision. Sixty-four physicians revised a correct draft into a harmful final answer. The gap widened over the course of the conversation rather than narrowing. And detection essentially failed: about a third of physicians said something felt off, but only two of two hundred twenty-five named systematic steering, and eighty-seven percent of harmful decisions came from sessions where nothing was flagged. Trust tracked agreement with the AI, not correctness.

That is alarming and the direction is right. But note what the comparison is. There is no unassisted arm, and buried in the results, the physicians' own first drafts — written before any AI message — were correct about eighty-five percent of the time in both arms. So the real contrast is adversarial AI against an unusually ideal assistant, one advocating correctly at ninety-nine percent of points, not against a clinician working alone. That forty-seven-point gap measures the spread between best-case and worst-case assistance, which is the right quantity for a safety argument about model integrity and the wrong one if you read it as "AI assistance makes physicians forty-seven points worse." Also worth flagging: harm was defined by the authors' own expert panels, each target proposed by one panelist and accepted on two concurrences, and what physicians actually decided was classified by language-model judges with human adjudication on a sample. No data, code, conversations, or adversarial prompts are being released.

Which brings us to benchmarking itself, and a paper that deserves credit for undermining its own thesis in public. A group benchmarked LLM agents on black-box optimization across five task families — synthetic functions, hyperparameter tuning, database tuning, chip macro placement, and molecular design — against a proper slate of classical methods: Gaussian-process Bayesian optimization, TuRBO, covariance matrix adaptation, tree-structured Parzen estimators, Sobol, random search. Headline: the agentic configuration beats the best classical optimizer in four of five families, and crushes the molecular design baselines.

Three findings inside matter more than the headline. Giving the agent more optimizer tooling made it worse — adding Gaussian-process tools dropped the mean score from zero point five eight nine to zero point five three eight. Giving it more domain knowledge made it worse: richer priors underperformed plain semantic descriptions, and wrong priors cost more than none. And most tellingly, letting the agent run a short warm-up and then handing the search to plain TuRBO scored zero point five seven zero — indistinguishable from full online agentic control at zero point five six nine, and well above TuRBO alone at zero point four seven five. That's a clean decomposition: the language model contributes a good initialization, not a better search policy.

And the comparator is literally built into the ruler. The scoring function is constructed so that the matched Gaussian-process baseline's final value equals zero point six by definition, with seventy percent of the weight on anytime progress rather than final solution quality — a metric that rewards fast early improvement, which is exactly where a GP that spends its early budget exploring looks worst. Two runs per task, one in the frontier model comparison, no variance reported. The molecular design margin is largely a budget artifact: a genetic algorithm scoring zero point zero five four under a two-hundred-fifty-evaluation cap is a genetic algorithm that has run for about one generation. The paper also concedes the database-tuning loss outright, where TuRBO beats the agent by twenty-six percent relative.

A companion item from the same week takes the opposite approach to agentic science, and it's the one I'd actually point a pharmacometrician at. The system is called LLM-IDEA, and the idea is to stop asking a language model to guess the next experiment and instead run classical structural identifiability analysis on the model it currently has. A council of language-model agents proposes hypotheses and designs; a deterministic engine with no language model in it computes the rank and condition number of the sensitivity matrix, names each confounded parameter direction as an explicit vector, and issues one of three verdicts — keep searching, run a better experiment of the same kind, or this class of experiment is exhausted, change the kind. The worked pharmacokinetic example is the one that will land for this audience: an oral one-compartment model comes back rank three of four, confounded along the volume-times-bioavailability axis at a cosine of exactly one. Sampling four times denser doesn't fix it; extending to seventy-two hours doesn't fix it. Adding an intravenous arm, where bioavailability is fixed at one, restores full rank — which is, of course, the absolute-bioavailability design pharmacologists have used for decades. That's the point: the engine rediscovers the right answer from the structure alone, and can certify when more of the same data will never help. It also catches the flip-flop ambiguity only once the intravenous arm is added.

Where this gets honest is the agent half. The headline architecture comparison — a five-agent council versus a single generalist — equalized wall-clock time, not compute, and under that budget the council issued between ninety-two and a hundred sixty-nine model calls per run against the single agent's ten to fourteen. Roughly a tenfold advantage. When calls were actually matched in a follow-up, the two arms tied, and the authors state in the paper that by their own registered falsification criterion the claim is not a result. Their own design-ranking criterion also loses to plain A-optimal design across sixty-two systems, and their sweep found sixty of those sixty-two already identifiable at round zero — a modest base rate for the problem the system is built to solve. The deterministic certificate is genuinely valuable; the agentic wrapper, by the paper's own matched-budget test, is not yet carrying weight.

Three shorter ones to close, all on the same theme. First, an EEG study classifying ADHD in children that is unusually honest about its own ceiling. Seventy-seven children, nineteen channels, and a deliberately controlled comparison of three feature strategies: an expert-designed set, the full eleven-hundred-feature space, and a language-model-guided selection. The expert set won for every classifier. The language-model arm is a clean negative — it never beat the expert comparator and showed no consistent gain over using everything. The best configuration hit seventy-one point four percent accuracy under leave-one-subject-out, but across a hundred repetitions of ten-fold cross-validation the mean was sixty-two point eight percent against a no-information rate of fifty-eight point four. And the sharpest number in the paper: when the pre-specified artifact rejection gate was actually applied, accuracy fell about fifteen points to roughly fifty-six percent — below the no-information rate. Epoch rejection rates differed by group, so a meaningful share of the discriminative signal may be movement and artifact burden rather than neural dynamics. The authors decline to adjudicate that, and they lead their abstract with the lower number rather than the higher one, which is rarer than it should be.

Second, speech markers of depression, from two samples at one community mental health center. Cross-sectionally, voice quality features — shimmer and harmonics-to-noise ratio — separate depressed participants from controls robustly, with adjusted betas up to one point nine. Then the longitudinal and predictive arms collapse entirely. Of the features carried forward, exactly one cleared correction for tracking symptom change over time, and not a single speech-by-time interaction was significant anywhere. For prediction of treatment response, cross-validated areas under the curve ran zero point six five to zero point seven three, and no comparison survived correction — nothing beat a model of age, sex, and baseline depression score alone. The structural problem is in the first table: ninety percent of the depressed group was recorded in-person as inpatients, no control was ever recorded inpatient, and two-thirds of controls were recorded virtually. Shimmer and harmonics-to-noise ratio are precisely the acoustic measures most sensitive to recording conditions, and a two-level virtual-versus-in-person covariate cannot absorb that. Add that ninety-three percent of the depressed group and none of the controls were on psychiatric medication, and the cross-sectional effect has at least two explanations competing with depression.

And finally, the lightest item, a purely computational one that I'd treat as hypothesis generation and that says so itself. A network analysis asking which biological pathways connect psychiatric medications to weight gain. The construction is careful — a seventeen-thousand-protein interactome from seven databases, eighty-one percent of retained edges supported by at least two sources, clustered into communities, intersected with adiposity gene sets and seeded with drug-protein interactions for fifty-nine antidepressants and antipsychotics. Monoamine GPCR enrichment is overwhelming; serotonin seven is targeted by thirty-eight of the fifty-nine drugs, D2 by forty-seven. But two things keep this in the hypothesis column. The random walks were initialized at the adiposity-associated vertices, and the neighborhood enrichments that follow are computed on sets where the large majority of members were those starting vertices — two hundred forty-four of two hundred ninety-five in one case. Finding adiposity pathways in neighborhoods seeded with adiposity genes is close to circular. And the adverse events are never actually modeled: the analysis cannot distinguish a drug that causes weight gain from one that causes weight loss. Meanwhile the community sitting closest to the drugs in network space — a GABA-A receptor cluster — showed no adiposity enrichment at all. Proximity to the drugs and enrichment for the phenotype never co-peak, which is itself the most informative result in the paper.

That's the ten. The through-line, one more time: a de novo peptide that beats the field's reference toxin in the same animals, an AI framework whose model was run after the screen, a biomarker that can't be separated from line of therapy, an adversarial-AI experiment with no unassisted arm, and a benchmark whose scale is pinned to one of its competitors. None of those are fatal and none of them are hidden — most of these authors say so themselves. But the comparator is where the argument lives, and it's the first thing worth checking. Back tomorrow.
