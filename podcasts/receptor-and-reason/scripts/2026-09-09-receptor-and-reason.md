## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. I'm your host, and this is your daily pass through CNS pharmacology, psychiatric and neurology drug discovery, and the computational machinery closing in on both. Twelve items today, and the thread is displacement. Over and over on this week's pile, the thing doing the causal work sits one step away from where the naming convention puts it. Ligand efficacy turns out not to live in any structure anyone has solved. A drug memory turns out to run through an NMDA receptor subunit that the atlases call astrocytic. A stress-resilience signal turns out to be strongest in oligodendrocytes rather than neurons. Semaglutide's hypothalamic effects arrive from the brainstem. And the difference between two cocaine self-administration schedules turns out to be a fact about the reinforcer economy rather than a fact about cocaine.

We open with the best piece of structural pharmacology this week, from Marta Filizola's lab at Mount Sinai with Georgios Skiniotis at St. Jude. The question is as old as the efficacy concept itself. Why does a full agonist produce more signal than a partial agonist at the same receptor, when both are bound and both are activating? The field's working answer for the last decade has been conformational selection: the ligand stabilises a different active state. This paper argues that at the mu-opioid receptor the answer is not in the states at all. It is in the transitions between them.

The two ligands are well chosen. Lofentanil, an extremely high-efficacy fentanyl analogue, and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, the kratom-derived weak partial agonist that keeps coming back as a template for low-efficacy analgesia. Time-resolved cryo-EM had already caught GTP-bound receptor-Gi complexes with both ligands at three points along the activation path. What this group added is about a millisecond of aggregate all-atom molecular dynamics — five hundred forty-six microseconds for the full agonist, four hundred ten for the partial — with adaptive sampling used specifically to bridge the unsampled gaps between the known structures, and Markov state models built on top to yield populations, pathways and rates rather than pictures.

Four previously uncharacterised intermediates fall out. And here is what lifts this above a nice free energy landscape: they went back to the cryo-EM particle stack and looked for them. Three-dimensional variability analysis found density clusters matching all four, with map correlation coefficients around point nine two, occupancies between roughly three and nine percent of the analysed particles, and resolutions of six to seven angstroms. That is low resolution, but the point is that these conformations were sitting in the experimental data the whole time, unclassified because nobody knew to look for them.

The ligand difference then lives almost entirely inside those intermediates. The resolved endpoint states look nearly identical for the two compounds. The full agonist sends about ninety-nine percent of its reactive flux down a single sequential pathway, with a mean first passage time of roughly two and a half milliseconds. The partial agonist scatters flux across several alternative routes, the dominant one skipping an intermediate entirely, and stretches that same journey to about fifty-five milliseconds. That is a twenty-fold kinetic penalty that no static structural comparison would ever show you. Structurally it corresponds to the partial agonist holding receptor and G protein in tighter contact for longer, with the alpha-helical domain of the G protein staying more open — trapping, rather than progressing.

The implication I would take seriously is uncomfortable for how the whole biased-agonism enterprise currently screens. Solve a structure, dock into it, read out a signalling ratio at equilibrium: if efficacy is a kinetic property of transient states, all three steps are measuring a shadow of the thing. The authors are appropriately careful — they never observed actual G protein dissociation, Markov state model stationary distributions are equilibrium objects being applied to a fundamentally non-equilibrium process, and intermediate definitions depend on featurisation and clustering choices. Also mouse receptor, human G protein, one membrane composition. But the cryo-EM cross-validation is what keeps this from being unfalsifiable.

Staying with G protein-coupled receptors and moving to the screening side, a preprint out of the virtual screening community makes an argument with the same shape: the useful signal is already being generated and then thrown away. The context is gigascale docking. When a library runs to trillions of compounds, the dominant tractable strategy is synthon-based hierarchical screening, where you dock a minimal enumeration library of fragments first and only expand the top scorers into full molecules. The bottleneck is that hundreds of fragments score comparably well and only a handful can be expanded on a fixed docking budget. People patch this with a general-purpose prescreening model — PharmacoNet, which predicts interaction hotspots from a protein structure and ranks candidates by graph matching against fixed interaction-type weights. Fixed being the problem: the same weights for every pocket.

The insight is that the fragment docking step you already ran tells you which hotspots and which interaction types this particular pocket actually rewards. So they use it twice: once for its intended purpose, and once to steer the pharmacophore density maps and recalibrate the interaction weights for that target. On three structurally distinct receptors — CB2, GPR91 and 5-HT2A — enrichment at the top hundred improves over stock PharmacoNet by roughly nine-fold, seven-fold and one point seven-fold, beating the underlying pipeline and two learned baselines. The fitted per-target weights are also interpretable, which is a second and arguably more useful output: a readout of what the model thinks the pocket cares about. Zero additional experiments, no retraining. The honest caveats are large and stated: three GPCR targets only, licensed commercial library and docking engine so exact reproduction is gated, and everything is docking score against docking score. Nobody made a compound.

From pockets to memory, and a preprint on the cocaine engram lands on a molecular target I would not have guessed. The design is standard: cocaine conditioned place preference, then re-exposure to the paired context to force recall, with the immediate early gene Fos marking the activated ensemble. They sorted medial prefrontal cortex neurons by Fos status and by neuronal identity and ran a thirty-one gene panel chosen for plasticity, excitability and neurotransmission. Twenty-two genes moved with activity, six with context. The single most strongly induced gene in the Fos-positive population was Grin2c, which encodes the GluN2C subunit of the NMDA receptor.

That is a surprise, because in the mouse cortex reference atlases Grin2c is expressed at low levels and predominantly in astrocytes. The authors' reading, which I find plausible, is that the atlases profile animals taken straight from an unstimulating home cage, so an activity-induced neuronal pool is invisible by construction. RNAscope confirmed it: the total number of Grin2c-expressing cells did not change, the cell-type ratio did. Then the causal test — neuron-selective viral knockdown of Grin2c in medial prefrontal cortex reduced place preference on the day fifteen and day twenty-two tests and accelerated extinction, with the day five test in the same direction but short of significance. GluN2C-containing receptors have distinctive biophysics, low magnesium sensitivity and small conductance, and there is real if early pharmacology aimed at them. A drug-memory-selective NMDA receptor subunit would be a genuinely attractive handle, given how badly non-selective NMDA blockade behaves clinically. One paper, one panel of thirty-one genes, and no electrophysiology showing the receptor is actually functional in those neurons.

Same drug, opposite methodological pole, and a result the field should sit with. A Neuropsychopharmacology paper from Matthew Banks's group at Virginia Commonwealth tested whether intermittent access to cocaine sensitises its reinforcing efficacy. The intermittent access model — five minutes on, twenty-five minutes off — is the current favourite for modelling binge patterns, and the literature is fairly consistent that it raises progressive-ratio breakpoints and behavioural-economic demand relative to extended continuous access. Banks's lab instead used cocaine-versus-food choice, which deliberately strips out the rate-altering effects that contaminate single-operant procedures: if a rat presses less because cocaine has impaired it, choice is unaffected, because both levers are available.

Thirty-five rats, both sexes, three supplemental access conditions run against a stable baseline. Neither schedule increased cocaine choice. Both decreased it, extended access more so in the second week. And then the control that makes the paper: they ran the same schedules with liquid food instead of cocaine. Intermittent food access decreased food choice and increased cocaine choice. So the intermittency effect is not about drugs at all — it shifts choice away from whichever commodity you gave freely and toward the alternative. Read that back onto the demand literature and the parsimonious explanation is that supplemental access produced tolerance to cocaine's intake-limiting effects rather than sensitisation of its reinforcing effects, which inflates breakpoints without changing what the animal values. If you are using intermittent access as a face-valid model of escalating addiction to screen medications, this says your dependent variable may be measuring impairment.

Opioid pharmacology of a much less controlled kind next. Xylazine, the veterinary alpha-2 adrenergic agonist, now adulterates a large share of the illicit fentanyl supply, and the preclinical literature on chronic co-exposure is thin. This preprint compared chronic fentanyl, chronic xylazine and the combination in male and female mice, with sleep architecture, respiration and peripheral molecular readouts. Fentanyl alone produces the expected pattern: wake during the light period, then compensatory non-REM rebound in the dark. Adding xylazine blunted that homeostatic rebound in males while preserving the light-period disruption, and shortened sleep bout duration in both sexes. Yohimbine partially reversed the sleep effects, but on different measures in each sex, which is where the authors flag that xylazine also has kappa opioid activity and kappa receptor expression is sexually dimorphic in mice. Respiratory depression was worse with the combination, particularly in males, and interestingly female mice were more resistant to fentanyl-induced respiratory depression overall. No skin lesions, despite that being the signature clinical finding in humans — worth knowing if you were planning to use this model for wound biology. The molecular hook is fibroblast growth factor twenty-one, elevated in combination-exposed animals, mostly females. That hepatokine is brain-penetrant and has been tied to reduced opioid and alcohol preference, which lines up eerily with survey and self-administration data showing that xylazine-adulterated fentanyl is consumed at lower rates. Single subanaesthetic xylazine dose, so the dose-response is entirely open.

Two GLP-1 items, and they disagree productively about where the evidence stands. First, mechanism, from Linda Engström Ruud's group in Gothenburg. Semaglutide's weight loss requires the dorsal vagal complex, but how a brainstem entry point produces hypothalamic effects has been unresolved, and the field had largely settled into local hypothalamic accounts — arcuate and dorsomedial hypothalamic populations that inhibit AgRP neurons. This paper shows the brainstem does it from upstream. A PACAP-expressing population in the nucleus of the solitary tract projects to both regions, and projection-specific optogenetics separates the two pathways cleanly. Both suppress feeding specifically under elevated motivational drive — fasting refeed, palatable food — while sparing ordinary dark-phase chow intake, and both suppress fasting-induced AgRP neuron activation. But the metabolic effects diverge: the arcuate-projecting pathway drives ketogenesis and weight loss independently of food intake, while the dorsomedial hypothalamic pathway lowers energy expenditure. Stimulating semaglutide-responsive projections to the same two targets reproduced the pathway-specific effects. Non-aversive suppression of motivated feeding, dissociable from the energy expenditure arm, is exactly the map you would want if you were trying to engineer a next-generation agonist that keeps the anorexia and drops the malaise.

Second, the clinical claim, and a nicely designed attempt to kill it. There is a large observational literature reporting that GLP-1 receptor agonists reduce dementia incidence, and the standard objection is confounding by things electronic health records do not contain — education, and Alzheimer's genetic risk. A group spanning Harvard, Boston University, Brown and UCSF used All of Us, which uniquely links records, survey and genotype, in an active-comparator new-user design: fourteen thousand three hundred sixty-four adults with type two diabetes, four point three years of follow-up, three hundred sixty-four incident dementia cases. Education strongly predicted who started a GLP-1 agonist — a relative risk of about one point five for college or above versus less than high school. APOE epsilon four carriers were less likely to start one, with a relative risk of point nine four for one allele and point eight four for two. So both candidate confounders are real and measurable. Adjusting for all of them moved the hazard ratio from point eight five to point eight four. Nothing. And the hazard ratio itself sits where the randomised trials sit, not where the optimistic observational literature sits. The authors' inference is the right one: the discrepancy is probably not unmeasured confounding by these variables but differences in cohort composition and effect modification. The APOE finding is the sleeper here — a genotype that determines both the exposure and the outcome, differentially distributed across treatment arms, in every observational study of these drugs that has never genotyped anyone.

There is a shorter glutamate item worth a minute. Forebrain overexpression of the neuronal glutamate transporter EAAT3 has been the standing mouse model tied to SLC1A1, the transporter gene that dominated early obsessive-compulsive disorder genetics and then failed to survive modern genome-wide association studies, even though the associated variants raise expression. This preprint asked what that overexpression does to executive function across three domains. Cognitive flexibility is impaired in both operant extinction and reversal. Working memory fails outright — the animals never acquire the trial-unique delayed nonmatching-to-location task. Impulsivity is elevated, specifically as premature responses in the five-choice serial reaction time task. And attention, accuracy and perseverative responding are all intact. That dissociation is the useful part: a compulsivity model whose cognitive signature is flexibility and acquisition rather than attention, which is a testable prediction about which patients a glutamate-modulating agent would help. Genetic model, no pharmacology in this paper, and the transporter's human genetic credentials remain shaky.

Molecular Psychiatry has a habenula paper that earns its place mostly on design. Chronic social defeat, then classification of a hundred six surviving stressed animals plus thirty naive controls into five behavioural phenotypes by social interaction, sucrose preference and tail suspension, then single-cell transcriptomics on the habenula — nearly ninety-nine thousand cells. Two findings worth carrying. First, the tail suspension test ran backwards: stressed mice were less immobile, not more, with a receiver operating characteristic area under the curve of point two nine. Social defeat appears to promote active coping, which is a real caution for anyone using immobility as a generic despair readout across stress paradigms. Second, the cell-type attribution. The susceptible phenotype's differential expression concentrated in lateral habenula neurons, consistent with two decades of hyperexcitability work. But the resilient phenotype was dominated by glia, with oligodendrocytes carrying the largest gene set — and the high-immobility phenotype mapped specifically onto the HbX subregion, where cholecystokinin signalling has been implicated before. Resilience as an oligodendrocyte program rather than a neuronal one keeps recurring across brain regions, and it is a considerably harder thing to drug. Classification areas under the curve for the other two tests were point six one and point five six, so these phenotypes are statistically real but not crisp, and there is no antidepressant arm.

Two psychedelics items close out the science, and the first one is mechanistic. A preprint gives acute LSD to rats and runs resting-state functional MRI, and the interesting design choice is temporal: fifteen minutes of baseline, then two consecutive fifteen-minute post-injection windows analysed separately, with independent component analysis, dynamic graph metrics and dynamic causal modelling. The claim is that the network response is not a state, it is a trajectory — early on, widespread cortical coupling changes and reduced posterior-to-frontal directed influence; later, a relative shift toward hippocampal and parahippocampal control of the default mode reorganisation. If that holds it reconciles a genuinely annoying inconsistency in the human literature, where within-network coherence goes down and global connectivity goes up depending on when you scanned. Now the caveats, which are heavy. Nine male rats, no vehicle group and no time-matched control, isoflurane anaesthesia at one point five to one point eight percent throughout, and of all the directed connectivity changes exactly one survived false discovery rate correction. The later hippocampal shift, which is the headline, is explicitly descriptive. I would treat this as a well-motivated design that needs an awake replication, not as a result.

Second, who actually gets the drug. A medRxiv analysis puts the first standardised year of Oregon's supervised psilocybin services — just under six thousand encounters in 2025 — next to national survey data on people reporting past-year psilocybin use. The two populations barely overlap. Oregon's clients are fifty-eight percent female, eighty-two percent aged thirty-five or older, with fifty-nine percent earning above ninety-five thousand dollars, and they overwhelmingly cite wellness. Nationally, people using psilocybin are sixty-four percent male, mostly under thirty-five, and carry serious clinical burden: twenty-three percent with a past-year major depressive episode, thirty-seven percent with serious psychological distress, sixty-two percent with a substance use disorder. Racial and ethnic minority representation in the Oregon programme is a fraction of the national figure. Sixty-one percent of clients travelled from out of state, averaging one point three encounters. Sessions cost eight hundred fifty to three thousand dollars and sometimes over five thousand, out of pocket, uninsured. The consequence that matters for anyone reading this programme's safety data: the widely quoted adverse event rate under half a percent comes from a self-selected, higher-income, clinically stable, wellness-motivated, largely tourist population, and it should not be generalised to the people who actually need supervision. Oregon's reporting mandate is aggregate-only with a third of clients opting out of demographics, so this is a descriptive comparison of two differently biased datasets and the authors say so.

We finish light, with plumbing. A preprint introduces bioq, a command-line client backed by a hosted fleet of thirty-eight or so containerised drug-discovery tools spanning structure prediction, de novo design, docking, affinity estimation and ADMET. The observation behind it is one anybody who has tried to chain these has lived: the science is not the obstacle, the CUDA and conda dependency conflicts are, and half the models will not fit on hardware a normal lab owns. So each tool gets one container, execution goes to serverless GPUs billed per job, and the client itself has a single runtime dependency. The genuinely forward-looking design choice is that the interface is self-describing and uniform across the fleet — a describe-and-run contract — which is aimed squarely at coding agents rather than humans. That is the right read of where the bottleneck is moving. An agent can plan a discovery workflow perfectly well and still be defeated by thirty-eight different argument conventions. It is open source under a permissive licence, which is what distinguishes it from the vendor platforms in the same slot. What it is not is a benchmark: there is no evaluation of whether an agent driving this fleet finds better molecules, only that it can drive it.

Which brings the thread back around. Efficacy was in the intermediates, not the endpoints. The cocaine schedule effect was in the economy, not the drug. The GLP-1 discrepancy was not in education or APOE. Resilience was in the oligodendrocytes. Every one of those is a case where the measurement was pointed confidently at the wrong place, and the correction came from someone running the control that the naming convention made look unnecessary. That's today. I'll see you tomorrow.
