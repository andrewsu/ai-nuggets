# Receptor & Reason — 2026-08-13

## Script

Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Thursday, August thirteenth. Today is heavy on psychedelics, but with a genuinely mixed bag underneath — an autocrine opioid mechanism inside the cocaine-relapse circuit, a GLP-1 drug that reaches into the cholinergic system, human assembloids that sort themselves into disease-prone and disease-resistant, and a nice trick for squeezing signal out of sparse drug-discovery data. Let's get into it.

We start with the biggest clinical news of the week. Definium Therapeutics reported positive topline results from Voyage, a phase three trial of DT120 in generalized anxiety disorder. DT120 is an orally disintegrating tablet formulation of lysergide — that is, LSD — given as a single supervised dose. Two hundred fourteen patients, all with a confirmed generalized anxiety diagnosis and at least moderate severity at baseline, were randomized one-to-one to a single hundred-microgram dose or placebo, then followed for twelve weeks. The primary endpoint, change on the Hamilton Anxiety scale, moved by about eleven and a half points on drug versus about six on placebo — a placebo-adjusted difference of five and a half points, with a Cohen's d around 0.8. That is a large effect from one dose. And it was fast: the separation from placebo was already almost eight points by week one, and clinicians rated patients as meaningfully less impaired by day two. Response rates were more than double placebo, and full remission was reached by about one in seven treated patients versus one in twenty-five on placebo. Adverse events were mild to moderate, clustered on dosing day, with no suicidality signal. Here's the honest reading. This is the second positive phase three for this compound, after a depression trial in June, and it would be the first genuinely new mechanism approved for generalized anxiety in nearly two decades — a space that has been stuck on the same serotonergic and benzodiazepine options for a very long time. It's also worth noting the positioning: this isn't psilocybin, and it isn't a microdose. It's a full, standardized, single hundred-microgram exposure to LSD, engineered into a dissolvable tablet with defined pharmacokinetics — the pitch is a reproducible pharmaceutical product rather than an artisanal trip. The obvious worry with any psychedelic trial is functional unblinding — patients know whether they got the drug, which can inflate a subjective anxiety score — and Definium is running a second pivotal trial with a low-dose fifty-microgram active-comparator arm specifically to address that, reading out next month. That readout matters more than this one for interpreting the effect size. The other practical catch is in the numbers they quietly reported: patients needed on average more than six hours of in-clinic monitoring before discharge. A single dose that holds for twelve weeks is compelling, but the delivery model is a supervised clinic day, not a prescription you fill — and that shapes who can actually access it.

Staying with serotonergic psychedelics but dropping down to the bench, there's a nice preclinical paper doing multidimensional phenotyping of TCB-2. TCB-2 is a selective agonist at the serotonin 2A receptor, used as a tool compound to probe the psychedelic mechanism. The usual rodent readout is the head-twitch response, which everyone treats as the proxy for psychedelic-like activity. The argument here is that head-twitch alone is far too thin a description. So they measured, simultaneously, licking during a learned auditory task, pupil size, eye position, blinking, open-field locomotion, and head-twitches scored automatically with pose tracking. TCB-2 constricted the pupil, suppressed licking across the whole task while preserving its cue-locked timing, reduced locomotion, and dose-dependently drove head-twitches. The revealing part is what happened when they pretreated with volinanserin, a selective serotonin 2A antagonist. It only partially blunted the pupil and head-twitch effects, and it did not prevent the drop in licking or the other changes at all. In other words, a supposedly clean serotonin 2A tool compound produces a spread of effects that a serotonin 2A blocker can't fully account for. The takeaway is methodological and worth internalizing: if you characterize these compounds by head-twitch alone, you're compressing a multidimensional pharmacology down to one axis, and you'll misread both the receptor logic and the off-target contributions.

That theme — that psychedelic action is broader than a single neuronal receptor event — is exactly the premise of a hypothesis piece in Molecular Psychiatry on psychedelics and astrocytes. This one is a perspective, not new data, so take it as a framework rather than a result. The authors argue that the field's neuron-centric, serotonin 2A-on-pyramidal-cells story leaves out the astrocyte, which is positioned to integrate neuromodulatory signals, tune synaptic transmission, handle metabolic support, and gate neuroimmune responses. They propose a neuro-glial ensemble as the minimal functional unit that actually translates receptor activation into lasting circuit plasticity and behavioral change — pulling in astrocytic calcium signaling, gliotransmission, and immune gating. It's speculative, but it's a testable kind of speculative — they lay out specific predictions about astrocytic calcium and metabolic coupling you could go check — and it dovetails with the growing evidence that the durable, plasticity-driven effects of these drugs long outlast the few hours of acute receptor occupancy. For anyone building computational models of psychedelic action, the message is that a neuron-only model of the response is probably underspecified.

If astrocytes and immune gating are part of the durable response, one way to look for that peripherally is circulating microRNAs, and that's the next paper — a placebo-controlled study of a single oral dose of psilocybin in sixty-two healthy adults, with blood drawn at baseline, six hours, and seven days. Using small-RNA sequencing and a regularized feature-selection approach, they found exactly two microRNAs significantly changed at six hours and — importantly — nothing surviving at seven days, and nothing in placebo. The two hits mapped onto neuroplasticity signaling and inflammatory pathways, which is the mechanistically satisfying direction. But I'd hold this loosely: it's an acute, transient, small-magnitude signal in healthy volunteers, framed honestly by the authors as proof-of-concept for whether blood microRNAs could ever index a psychedelic response. It's a first data point on a peripheral biomarker, not a biomarker.

Now let's leave the psychedelics. There's a mechanistically clean paper on semaglutide reaching into the brain's arousal and cholinergic systems. GLP-1 drugs are being eyed for everything from addiction to dementia, but which central circuits they actually touch is mostly hand-waving. Using immediate-early gene mapping, the authors show that an acute dose of semaglutide activates orexin neurons in the lateral hypothalamus and basal forebrain cholinergic neurons, in both sexes. Then, with microdialysis in awake animals, they show semaglutide acutely raises acetylcholine release in the ventral hippocampus — and, notably, it does so in aged rats as well as young ones. Given how central hippocampal cholinergic tone is to learning and memory, that's a concrete mechanistic bridge from a GLP-1 agonist to the exact system we target in cognitive decline. The caveat they're upfront about: this is immediate-early gene and microdialysis evidence, so it shows these populations are engaged, not that semaglutide binds GLP-1 receptors directly on orexin neurons — the effect could be indirect.

From arousal circuits to reward circuits — a really elegant piece of synaptic pharmacology on cocaine relapse. The setup: after abstinence from repeated cocaine, the D2 medium spiny neurons of the striatum release less GABA onto the ventral pallidum, which disinhibits the pallidum and drives drug seeking. This paper asks what suppresses that GABA release, and the answer is an autocrine opioid loop. Cocaine abstinence raises enkephalin in those same D2 neurons, and the enkephalin acts back on mu opioid receptors on the neurons that released it, shutting down their own GABA output. They nail it with a conditional knockout of the enkephalin gene specifically in D2 neurons: in those mice, cocaine abstinence no longer suppresses GABA release, and applying enkephalin by hand reinstates the suppression in normal tissue. So a cocaine-driven, endogenous-opioid mechanism is hijacking the striatopallidal brake on reward seeking. It's an ex vivo slice story, but it names a specific, druggable node — enkephalin and mu opioid signaling inside the indirect pathway — for cocaine use disorder, where we have essentially no approved pharmacotherapy.

Two shorter items to bring us down. First, a clever human-model paper on mesocortical assembloids. These are iPSC-derived assembloids fusing forebrain and midbrain organoids to reconstruct the corticodopaminergic pathway — the circuit implicated in Parkinson's, schizophrenia, and autism. The nuisance in organoid work is that genetically identical assembloids under identical conditions behave inconsistently, and everyone treats that as noise to be standardized away. The authors flip it: they interrogate each assembloid nondestructively — high-density electrical recording, a Raman-based readout of secreted tau and neurofilament, and spatial transcriptomics — and find the assembloids spontaneously sort into disease-prone and non-prone states, with distinct oscillatory dynamics, protein-secretion signatures, and junction-specific neuronal hyperactivation with neuron-glial uncoupling. They roll it into a composite risk score. The provocative claim is that this intrinsic variability mirrors the inter-individual variation in disease vulnerability we see in people — meaning the spread is signal, not error. It's in vitro, and disease-prone is a signature rather than a validated disease, but as a live, nondestructive risk-profiling platform it's a genuinely different way to use these models.

Second, briefly, a Molecular Psychiatry review consolidating the case for the NLRP3 inflammasome as a psychiatric drug target — again a review, so no new data. It walks through NLRP3 biology in central neuroinflammation, the evidence for dysregulation in schizophrenia and related disorders, and the NLRP3 inhibitors that have been tried, and it's refreshingly candid that direct clinical evidence is limited and causality isn't established. Worth having on your radar as the neuroinflammation-as-target thesis keeps maturing — and it's a useful counterweight to remember alongside the astrocyte and microglia stories, because the peripheral NLRP3 inhibitors that already exist make this one of the more immediately actionable inflammatory targets if the causal evidence in psychiatry ever firms up.

And to close, one for the computational pharmacology side. A paper called DegradeQuery tackles predicting whether a PROTAC — a proteolysis-targeting chimera, the bifunctional molecules that drag a target protein to an E3 ligase for destruction — will actually degrade its target. The problem is that degradation depends jointly on the molecule and its cellular context, and public databases hold thousands of molecule-target-ligase records but measured degradation for only a small fraction. Their move is a counterfactual pretraining objective: instead of ignoring all the label-missing records, they contrast each recorded tuple against perturbed alternatives, so the model learns that small structural swaps should produce predictably different degradation outcomes. That turns the unlabeled majority into usable pretraining signal, and it beats supervised baselines on both degradation classification and generalization to new compounds. It's not agentic, and the authors flag that cell-line-specific effects and degradation kinetics are still hard — but as a way to exploit the sparse, context-dependent data that plagues this whole modality, it's a smart, transferable idea. And it generalizes well beyond degraders: any time you have a big table of molecule-context relationships with measurements for only a handful of the cells, the counterfactual-contrast framing is a way to stop throwing the empty cells away. That's most of computational pharmacology, honestly.
