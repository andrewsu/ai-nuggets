## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. It's Friday, July twenty-fourth, twenty twenty-six, and it's a strong psychedelics-and-reward week, so we're running ten items. We open on a pair of psilocybin papers that answer very different questions with the same drug — one on how psilocybin acts on cortical dendrites and why the acute effect is gated by brain state, the other a computational-and-electrophysiology account of how psilocybin reshapes decision-making under uncertainty. From there we stay in the motivation-and-reward space — an orexin circuit that links threat to approach behavior through accumbens dopamine, then two addiction papers, one dissecting glutamatergic control of cannabinoid self-administration and one separating dopamine from acetylcholine in nicotine impulsivity. Then two peripheral-pain pharmacology papers that make a nice pair — cannabinoids and terpenes silencing sensory neurons, and a clean electrophysiological explanation for why Nav one-point-seven blockers keep failing in the clinic while a Nav one-point-eight blocker succeeded. We close on the computational side — two AI-driven drug-design frameworks and a computational-psychiatry paper on how learned value overgeneralizes in people with bipolar-spectrum symptoms.

Opening with the item that gives the cleanest new mechanism — a psilocybin dendritic imaging study from Alex Kwan's group, the lab that has done much of the foundational work linking psychedelics to structural plasticity in frontal cortex. The question they're chasing is the central paradox of psychedelic therapeutics — how does a drug that clears the body in hours produce durable changes in mood that last weeks. The leading hypothesis has been that psilocybin drives synaptogenesis in cortical pyramidal neurons, and since most excitatory synapses form on dendrites, the acute action presumably starts there. So they did two-photon calcium imaging of apical dendritic tufts in layer-five pyramidal tract neurons of mouse medial frontal cortex, the same subcerebral projection cells implicated in psilocybin's long-term behavioral effects. Three findings. First, a single dose transiently increases the rate of spontaneous dendritic calcium events, and the time course tracks the drug's pharmacokinetics in the brain. Second — and this is the interesting one — that effect is brain-state-dependent. It happens selectively during quiet wakefulness and not during active movement, and it's abolished by cell-type-specific deletion of the 5-HT2A receptor. Third, and most provocative, is a dissociation. Under normal conditions, the dendritic branches with more calcium activity are the ones that go on to form new spines — calcium predicts plasticity, as decades of work would predict. After psilocybin, that predictive relationship breaks. The acute calcium signal and the subsequent structural remodeling come apart. The honest caveat, which the authors flag, is that in this head-fixed imaging preparation they didn't actually observe psilocybin-induced spinogenesis, unlike their own earlier home-cage work, so the plasticity arm is inferential here. But two takeaways survive that. The state-dependence gives a cellular correlate for the "set and setting" clinical intuition — psilocybin's cortical impact is strongest in a quiescent state, which is exactly how dosing sessions are run. And the decoupling of acute dendritic activity from long-term remodeling suggests the acute subjective response and the therapeutic structural change may be more separable than the field has assumed — which is precisely the premise behind the non-hallucinogenic psychoplastogen programs.

Second, staying on psilocybin but moving from dendrites to decisions, from Bita Moghaddam's group. If the first paper asks how psilocybin acts on a neuron, this one asks how it reshapes behavior and cortical coding during flexible learning. They trained rats on a value-based probabilistic choice task, recorded single units from medial prefrontal cortex, and fit reinforcement-learning models to the behavior. The headline is an asymmetry. Psilocybin improved choice selection when uncertainty was low, and increased exploration when uncertainty was high — an uncertainty-dependent effect rather than a blanket change. And in the reinforcement-learning model it shifted learning rate bidirectionally in a very specific way — it increased the rate of updating from rewarded actions while decreasing updating from unrewarded actions. In other words, under psilocybin the animal weights good news more heavily and discounts bad news. That behavioral signature had a direct neural correlate — prefrontal neurons enhanced their representation of rewarded outcomes and diminished their representation of unrewarded ones. What makes this worth pairing with the Kwan paper is that it operates at a completely different level of analysis but tells a compatible story. Where the dendritic paper gives you the 5-HT2A-gated cellular event, this one gives you the computational consequence — a reweighting of cortical coding that favors learning from positive information. That asymmetric optimism in the update rule is a plausible bridge to the antidepressant mechanism, since negatively-biased belief updating is one of the more robust computational signatures of depression. The limitation, which the authors are upfront about, is that you can't model the psychedelic experience itself in a rat — this is the learning-and-flexibility component isolated from the subjective trip, which is arguably the point.

Third, moving from psychedelics to the arousal-and-motivation circuitry, and a nice piece of circuit dissection on orexin. Orexin — hypocretin — neurons broadcast arousal signals across the forebrain, and one dense but understudied target is the supramammillary region of the posterior hypothalamus. Using tracing, RNAscope, fiber photometry, and optogenetics, this group maps what that projection does. Anatomically, supramammillary neurons express both orexin receptor subtypes, with the type-two receptor nearly twice as abundant as type-one — worth noting because type-two signaling is classically tied to arousal and wakefulness while type-one is tied to reward and dopamine, and here they heavily overlap. Functionally, the photometry is the striking part — these orexin terminals are strongly activated by footshock and by conditioned threat cues, and they're actively suppressed during water consumption. So they encode salience and threat, not hedonic reward. And yet, when you optogenetically drive those same terminals, it's reinforcing — it supports instrumental responding, biases place preference, and triggers rapid dopamine release in the nucleus accumbens. So this is a circuit that's recruited by uncertainty and threat but drives approach and motivated behavior through mesolimbic dopamine. That's a satisfying resolution of something the orexin field has wrestled with — how the same system can be implicated in both stress and reward. It also matters therapeutically because dual orexin receptor antagonists are already approved for insomnia — the "-orexant" drugs — and there's real interest in orexin antagonism for addiction and for stress-related relapse. A circuit that converts threat salience into dopamine-driven approach is exactly the kind of substrate that would explain why orexin antagonists blunt cue-driven drug seeking.

Fourth, into addiction proper, and specifically cannabis use disorder — a question about which glutamatergic inputs to the nucleus accumbens control vulnerability. The accumbens is the classic addiction hub, but it's driven by multiple glutamatergic afferents and their individual contributions to cannabinoid addiction have been unclear. This group used intravenous self-administration of WIN 55,212-2 — a synthetic full cannabinoid agonist used as a tool compound in place of THC because it self-administers reliably — combined with pathway-specific chemogenetic inhibition. They chronically silenced either the dorsal-hippocampus-to-accumbens pathway or the basolateral-amygdala-to-accumbens pathway during the development of addiction-like behavior, using DREADDs driven by continuous minipump delivery of deschloroclozapine. The result is a double dissociation with a shared component. Inhibiting either pathway increased the proportion of mice that developed an addiction-like phenotype, and both specifically increased persistence of drug seeking when the drug was unavailable — so persistence is the common consequence of losing glutamatergic drive to the accumbens. But the two pathways then diverge — silencing the hippocampal input raised motivation, impulsivity, reward sensitivity, and resistance to extinction, while silencing the amygdalar input selectively enhanced cue-induced seeking. The counterintuitive framing worth sitting with is that inhibiting these excitatory inputs increased vulnerability rather than protecting against it — implying these projections normally provide a regulatory brake on the transition to compulsive use. That's a useful corrective to the reflex that more accumbens glutamate always means more addiction.

Fifth, staying in addiction and staying on the theme of pulling apart mechanisms that usually get lumped together — dopamine versus acetylcholine in nicotine behavior. The specific target is impulsive action, which is increasingly seen as a driver of persistent smoking but is hard to isolate behaviorally. They trained rats to self-administer intravenous nicotine in a Go/No-Go task, where nicotine was available during Go periods and withheld during No-Go periods, and measured impulsive action as responding during the No-Go periods. First, a clean baseline result — nicotine self-administration itself increased No-Go responding relative to saline, confirming nicotine increases impulsive action rather than just general activity. Then the pharmacology dissociates two receptor systems. The dopamine D1 antagonist reduced nicotine intake but also reduced inactive-lever pressing, so its apparent effect on impulsivity was confounded by a general drop in operant output — an honest negative they don't paper over. But the D1 agonist reduced nicotine-motivated responding without touching No-Go responding, and the non-selective nicotinic antagonist mecamylamine reduced both intake and impulsive action. So the cleaner reading is that nicotinic receptor signaling carries the impulsive-action component, while D1 signaling carries the motivational component. The translational hook is that a D1 agonist might reduce how hard someone works for nicotine without addressing the impulsivity that drives lapses — which argues that motivation and impulsive action may need to be targeted separately in smoking-cessation pharmacotherapy rather than assumed to move together.

Sixth, shifting to peripheral pain pharmacology, and a cannabinoid paper that bridges nicely from the addiction block since it's the same receptor family in a very different role. The motivation is non-opioid analgesia, and the substrate is the dorsal root ganglion — the peripheral sensory neurons, including nociceptors, that express CB1 and CB2. Using whole-cell current-clamp on small-diameter mouse DRG neurons, they tested cannabidiol, THC, terpene mixtures, individual terpenes, and whole Cannabis leaf extracts on neuronal firing. Both CBD and THC produced concentration- and time-dependent inhibition of action potential firing, with similar half-maximal concentrations around five micromolar. Terpenes — linalool, beta-pinene, myrcene — did the same. And the interaction data are the interesting part — combining CBD with THC, or CBD with terpenes, enhanced the inhibition beyond the individual components, which is the first rigorous electrophysiological support I've seen for the so-called entourage effect, the widely-claimed but poorly-evidenced idea that whole-plant cannabis formulations are more than the sum of their isolated cannabinoids. A CB2-selective inverse agonist also reduced firing, pointing to functional CB2 on these neurons. The caveats are real — five-micromolar potency is not pharmaceutically impressive, some effects were partly receptor-independent, and CBD had modest effects on the hERG cardiac channel, which is a liability flag. But as mechanistic grounding for multi-component cannabinoid analgesics acting directly on peripheral nociceptor excitability, it's a useful data point.

Seventh, staying on peripheral nociceptors and staying on the theme of ion-channel control of firing, but this is the one with the most direct drug-development lesson. Nav one-point-seven voltage-gated sodium channels are heavily expressed in human nociceptors, and humans with loss-of-function mutations in Nav one-point-seven are congenitally unable to feel pain — which made Nav one-point-seven the most-hyped analgesic target of the last two decades. And yet selective Nav one-point-seven blockers have failed repeatedly in the clinic, while a selective Nav one-point-eight blocker, suzetrigine — approved last year as the first genuinely new class of acute-pain drug in a generation — succeeded. This paper asks why, using recordings from actual human dorsal root ganglion neurons at body temperature, comparing a selective Nav one-point-seven inhibitor against published suzetrigine data. The answer is mechanistically clean. Blocking Nav one-point-seven does raise the action-potential threshold substantially — it makes neurons harder to fire in the first place — and prolongs the refractory period. But it has little effect on repetitive firing once a strong stimulus gets the neuron going. Suzetrigine, blocking Nav one-point-eight, does the opposite — it dramatically suppresses repetitive high-frequency firing, which is what actually encodes intense pain. So the two channels do different jobs — Nav one-point-seven sets the threshold, Nav one-point-eight sustains the train — and it's the sustained train that matters for the pain signal. That's about as satisfying a post-hoc explanation for a decade of clinical disappointment as you'll find, and it reframes Nav one-point-seven not as a failed target but as the wrong biophysical lever.

Eighth, moving to the computational side and AI-driven drug design, with a preprint that is a smart, narrow methods advance rather than a grand claim. The context is co-folding models — the successors to AlphaFold that predict a protein and a bound ligand together, like Boltz-2. They're powerful for orthosteric binding, but they have a systematic bias — they've essentially memorized that ligands go in the canonical active site, so even small chemical fragments, which ought to sample many pockets, default to the orthosteric site. That's a real problem if what you want is allosteric or cryptic sites, which is where a lot of the interesting selectivity in modern drug discovery lives. Their method, called CAFE, is a clever inference-time trick that needs no retraining — you place a competitive blocker in the orthosteric site first, which forces the model to fold your fragment into non-canonical pockets instead. Using ADP as the orthosteric blocker for kinases, they show CAFE substantially expands allosteric-site exploration, with predicted binding free energies that match or exceed known crystal-structure poses without any refinement, and it surfaces cryptic pockets that conventional pocket-detection tools miss entirely — some more thermodynamically favorable than the known sites. They generalize it to the RAS-MAPK pathway and to fragment-screening campaigns. The reason this is worth a working scientist's attention is the training-free framing — it's a protocol you can layer onto an existing co-folding model today to redirect it toward allosteric discovery, rather than a new model you'd have to wait for and validate from scratch.

Ninth, and staying with generative AI for drug design but pushing toward systems pharmacology, which is closer to this show's core. The critique motivating the paper is that most generative drug-design AI follows a one-drug-one-target paradigm, which is a poor fit for chronic, systemic, and heterogeneous diseases where the pathology is distributed across cell types. Their framework, GEM-GPT, tries to design molecules that revert a cell-type-specific disease transcriptome back toward a healthy one. Architecturally it fuses a single-cell RNA-sequencing foundation model with a molecular GPT, so the molecule generator is conditioned on cell-type-resolved gene-expression state — it can propose different molecules for different cell types in the same disease. They report it beats prior generative baselines, generalizes to cell types it wasn't trained on, and — the part relevant here — they run a case study on opioid use disorder, where it nominates both novel chemotypes and existing FDA-approved drugs predicted to normalize cell-type-specific disease phenotypes in individual patients. I'd treat the OUD result as illustrative rather than validated — this is entirely computational, there's no experimental confirmation that the generated molecules do anything, and reverting a transcriptomic signature is a long way from a therapeutic effect. But the conceptual move — coupling single-cell state to molecular generation so that "personalized" means cell-type-resolved rather than just patient-labeled — is the right direction for systems pharmacology, and OUD is a well-chosen proving ground given how distributed and cell-type-specific addiction neurobiology is.

Closing on the lightest item, a computational-psychiatry study on how learned value overgeneralizes — relevant to bipolar-spectrum vulnerability. Positive overgeneralization is the tendency to take a specific success and inflate it into broad expectations of future reward, and it's been linked to mania risk — but it's mostly been measured with self-report, which tells you little about the underlying cognition. This group built a behavioral paradigm that quantifies how a learned value spreads to novel, perceptually-similar stimuli, and fit psychometric functions to get an individual's breadth of generalization. In an online transdiagnostic sample of a hundred sixty-three people, broader task-based reward generalization tracked both self-reported positive overgeneralization and subclinical bipolar symptoms. Then they give it a computational account — a reinforcement-learning model in which a self-efficacy term modulates how strongly anticipated future value influences learning. Turning that modulation up reproduces the broader value spread they see behaviorally. It's a modest study — online, subclinical, correlational — and it won't change anyone's practice. But it's a nice example of the strategy the field keeps returning to — take a fuzzy clinical construct like grandiose overoptimism, build a task that isolates it, and give it a mechanistic parameter you could in principle track or target. That's the throughline across a lot of today's episode, actually — psilocybin's asymmetric update rule, the orexin salience-to-approach conversion, and now optimism as a tunable learning-rate term.
