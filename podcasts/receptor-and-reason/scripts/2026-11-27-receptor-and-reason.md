## Script

Good morning. This is Receptor and Reason for Friday, November twenty-seventh. Twelve items today, and we open on a muscarinic result that is interesting precisely because half the animals didn't respond.

A group at the University of South Carolina School of Medicine has tested the M4 positive allosteric modulator VU-oh-four-six-seven-one-five-four — I'll call it V-U-one-fifty-four, the Vanderbilt tool compound that most of the preclinical M4 literature runs on — in SAPAP3 knockout mice, the standard model of pathological repetitive grooming. The behavioral readout is the part worth noting first: rather than scoring grooming by hand, they used depth motion sequencing, which segments spontaneous behavior into its component syllables, so you can see whether a drug suppressed grooming specifically or simply flattened everything the animal does. That distinction is the whole question with a compound acting on striatal cholinergic tone.

At an intermediate dose, V-U-one-fifty-four reduced excessive grooming in male knockouts without significantly reducing other active behaviors. At the top dose it reduced behavioral output across the board, in both sexes and both genotypes — which is sedation wearing a therapeutic costume, and the authors say so. So there is a window. The problem is that in female knockouts the window appears not to exist: little to no grooming reduction at any dose that wasn't simply suppressing movement, despite no baseline sex difference in grooming in that line.

Two things make this more than a curiosity. First, the circuit dissection cuts against the obvious interpretation. Deleting M4 from the dorsomedial striatum increased baseline grooming in males — so those receptors are doing something tonic — but deletion did not block the drug's effect. Whatever population V-U-one-fifty-four is working through to reduce grooming, it isn't dorsomedial striatal M4, which points toward cholinergic interneuron autoreceptors or presynaptic heteroreceptors on corticostriatal terminals. Second, the same sex-dependence has been reported for psilocybin in this exact model, by a different group. Two mechanistically unrelated compounds reducing compulsive grooming in males and not females is a much stronger hint that the circuit generating the behavior differs by sex than either result alone.

Their proposed explanation for the drug part is receptor reserve: females in their hands had more Chrm4 transcript per cell, though the same was true for Chrm1, so this is not M4-specific. If female striatum sits closer to maximal M4 signaling at baseline, a modulator that only potentiates endogenous acetylcholine has less room to work. That is testable, and it matters well beyond this model, because the M4-directed programs now moving through psychiatry are dosed on efficacy data that mostly didn't stratify this way. They also didn't track estrous stage, which they acknowledge.

Staying on receptor pharmacology, but in the negative direction. A screen of an eighteen-hundred-compound approved-drug library against the human mu-opioid receptor, read out as inhibition of forskolin-stimulated cyclic AMP in a reporter cell line, produced what looks at first like a repurposing paper and turns out to be an assay-hygiene paper. Seventeen active wells, two hundred and four compounds, nine hits on deconvolution, six with interpretable concentration-response curves — niclosamide, sanguinarine, hexachlorophene, ataluren, altrenogest and nitazoxanide. Five of the six suppressed cyclic AMP about as completely as DAMGO, the standard peptide agonist.

Then they did the control that most repurposing papers skip. Naloxone shifted DAMGO's potency roughly a thousand-fold. Of the six hits, exactly one — altrenogest, a progestin — showed any statistically significant naloxone-sensitive shift, and a modest one. And altrenogest produced no detectable beta-arrestin-2 recruitment at all, which in a less careful write-up is precisely the result that gets reported as G-protein bias. The honest reading is that five of six compounds were lowering cyclic AMP through something other than the receptor — endogenous receptors, phosphodiesterases, adenylyl cyclase, or simply cell health — and that a bias claim built on a single pathway readout without receptor-dependence controls is not a bias claim. Given how much of the biased-agonism literature has been walked back over the last six years, this is a useful thing to have written down with numbers.

A mechanistic depression paper next, in Neuropharmacology, and this one I read in abstract only — it's an Elsevier item with no body available, so take it as a pointer rather than a verdict. The claim is a chain running from RNA modification to AMPA receptor trafficking. In chronic mild stress mice, the kinase CK1-delta is upregulated and phosphorylates stargazin — the AMPA receptor auxiliary subunit — at threonine three-twenty-one, which disrupts stargazin's interaction with GluA1 and impairs receptor trafficking in hippocampus. Upstream, the transcription factor YY1 drives CK1-delta expression, and NAT10-mediated acetylation of YY1 messenger RNA stabilizes it, amplifying the whole loop. Knockdown or pharmacological inhibition at either end restores the stargazin-GluA1 interaction and relieves depression-like behavior. Whether NAT10 is a drug target or just the most upstream thing they could manipulate is exactly what the abstract can't tell you, which is why it's a pointer.

From mechanism to the clinic, and to a trial whose primary endpoint failed while its symptom scale did something dramatic. The Laureate Institute for Brain Research has posted an interim analysis of a randomized, double-masked mechanistic trial of a multicomponent metabolic formulation in major depression — D-beta-hydroxybutyrate as an alternative oxidative substrate, plus about thirty micronutrients and mitochondrial cofactors, over eight weeks, in patients enriched for low-grade inflammation. The registered primary outcome was nucleus accumbens activation during reward anticipation; the secondary was medial prefrontal activation during self-referential processing.

Neither moved. The accumbens estimate was effectively zero with a credible interval straddling it in both directions. The exposure data are equally sobering: peak post-dose ketone concentrations around zero-point-two millimolar, which is a biochemical signal of compliance, not nutritional ketosis. And yet clinician-rated depression scores favored active treatment by seven and a quarter points on the Montgomery-Åsberg scale, with a credible interval excluding zero. The self-report scale moved by about two points, with an interval spanning zero.

Twenty-two evaluable patients. A seven-point clinician-rated separation with no target engagement, no corresponding self-report movement, and a sponsor with equity in the formulation. I want to be careful here, because the authors themselves are careful — they label it exploratory, they flag that adjusting for participants' guesses about their assignment attenuates the effect, and they note the analysis population is evaluable rather than randomized. But the shape of this result — objective target null, clinician-rated scale strongly positive, patient-rated scale flat — is the classic signature of unblinding and expectancy, and the thirty other ingredients mean that even if the effect is real, attributing it to ketones is unsupported. The value of the paper is that the mechanistic arm was prespecified and reported honestly. Most supplement trials in this space simply report the rating scale.

A useful counterpoint on durability, and another abstract-only item. The International Journal of Neuropsychopharmacology has a post hoc analysis of SUSTAIN-3, the long-term open-label extension of esketamine nasal spray in treatment-resistant depression — over a thousand patients, mean exposure around forty-one months, nearly thirty percent past five years. At the end of the maintenance phase, remission was about fifty percent by clinician rating and about a third by patient self-report. Among those with at least four years of continuous treatment, roughly forty-seven percent had been in remission for three years or more by the clinician scale, versus twenty-nine percent by self-report. Open-label, so the absolute numbers are soft and survivorship is doing work. But note the same clinician-versus-patient gap we just saw, here at a fifteen-point spread, in a setting where nobody is blinded at all. It's worth keeping as a prior whenever a depression trial reports only one of the two.

Now two genetics-to-mechanism papers that both answer the question "which variant actually does something."

The first is alcohol use disorder, from a group doing allele-specific open chromatin mapping in microglia. The standing problem is that almost every alcohol use disorder GWAS hit is non-coding, so you get a locus and no mechanism. They integrated allele-specific chromatin accessibility in microglia with expression QTLs, postmortem human microglial expression, and iPSC-derived microglia, and prioritized a single variant whose minor allele better matches binding motifs for ETS-family transcription factors. That allele associates with higher CD163 expression in postmortem microglia from people with alcohol use disorder and in iPSC-derived microglia after ethanol exposure. Then the validation that makes it worth your time: knocking out the variant, or converting the minor allele to the major allele by editing, abolished the ethanol-induced CD163 response — and changed ethanol-induced phagocytic function. That is a non-coding risk variant walked all the way to a cell-biological phenotype in the relevant cell type, under the relevant exposure. The framework generalizes; the specific gene may or may not.

The second is Parkinson's, and it has direct implications for who should be in LRRK2 inhibitor trials. The pathogenic LRRK2 variants everyone knows sit in the catalytic core — the kinase domain variant raising intrinsic activity, the GTPase-domain variants shifting the conformational equilibrium toward active. This paper characterizes a haplotype from a multigenerational Spanish family carrying two variants in the N-terminal armadillo domain, which is the membrane-recruitment end of the protein. One of the two roughly doubled Rab10 phosphorylation in cells, and the same pathway activation showed up in primary neutrophils and monocytes from carriers — including carriers without Parkinson's — with coordinated increases across six endogenous Rab substrates by targeted mass spectrometry.

The mechanism is the good part. The activating variant sits next to one of the armadillo domain's Rab-binding sites, and a substitution that disrupts that site abolished its activating effect while leaving the GTPase-domain variant's activation intact. They then tested five rare armadillo variants from public genetic datasets and found two more that activate in the same site-dependent way, and three that don't. So this is a third, mechanistically distinct route to LRRK2 pathway activation — not catalysis, not conformation, but recruitment — and it comes with a functional assay that sorts rare armadillo variants into activating and benign. If kinase inhibitors are going to be prescribed on genotype, that sorting is the bottleneck.

Two papers on temporal interference stimulation now, and they fit together unusually well — one human, one biophysical, and the model explains a parameter choice the human study had to make by assumption.

The human one is from Tononi's group at Wisconsin. Thirty-two participants received one-hertz temporal interference stimulation during non-REM sleep, targeting left insula and ventromedial prefrontal cortex, with eleven more receiving carrier-only as control. Using high-density EEG and individualized electric field modeling, they found slow-wave density rose about twenty percent during stimulation and about thirty percent after, across both shallow and deep waves, with essentially no change in wave morphology — about one percent shifts in amplitude against a twenty-to-thirty percent shift in how often waves occurred. Carrier-only showed no density change. Source reconstruction showed each wave recruiting more cortex in both arms, including the control, which the authors read as sleep simply deepening over the night rather than as an envelope effect — a nice piece of self-correction. There is a dose-response relation: participants whose individual field models showed stronger delivered field shifted further toward slow-wave sleep. And the gain faded across the night's successive stimulation blocks, consistent with declining homeostatic sleep pressure, which means averaging across blocks will dilute the effect in any trial designed that way.

The companion, from a computational group in Spain, asks a question the empirical literature has mostly skipped: what sets which beat frequency a given neuron amplifies? They built morphologically detailed conductance-based models of CA1 pyramidal cells and four interneuron classes and swept carrier frequency, envelope frequency, amplitude and orientation. The applied field has no spectral component at the beat frequency at all, so that response has to be manufactured by nonlinear membrane dynamics first — and the gain applied to it afterward is band-pass, suppressed at low frequencies by slow voltage-gated currents and peaking at the compartment's resonant frequency. The consequences are concrete. Modulated stimulation beats an unmodulated carrier only when the envelope matches the target's resonance, near firing threshold. The advantage survives carriers as low as two hundred and fifty hertz, far below the fourteen-hundred-hertz floor that simplified neuron models implied — so carrier frequency is less constrained than the field assumes. Under distributed synaptic drive, the advantage disappears, though frequency selectivity persists, which is a real caveat for an awake brain. And because resonance depends on conductance composition, the optimal envelope frequency differs across cell types: parvalbumin basket cells gain an advantage only when resonant conductances are added. Read together with the Wisconsin study, the implication is that one hertz worked for slow waves, and the right envelope for a different target is an empirical question nobody has answered.

Two items on automating evidence synthesis, which for neuroimaging has quietly become a pharmacology-adjacent problem, since so many receptor and treatment claims now rest on meta-analytic maps.

The first is a benchmark. NeuroMetaBench assembles expert-curated coordinate-based meta-analyses — author-stated eligibility criteria, article inclusion decisions, the specific analyses selected within each paper, and reference maps — covering sixteen hundred article inclusions across seventeen review articles, with analysis-level detail for thirty-five meta-analyses. Benchmarked against it, the established automated tools — Neurosynth, NeuroQuery, NeuroVLM — showed limited to moderate spatial correspondence with expert maps, varying a lot by topic. That is a more honest accounting than the field usually gets, because these tools are routinely used as ground truth in papers that never check them against a curated reference.

The second, from a Texas-and-Oxford collaboration, is the obvious next move and the result is sharper than I expected. AutoNIMA uses language-model agents at every stage: screening articles against the researchers' own stated eligibility criteria, then parsing the heterogeneous coordinate tables to pick which analyses within a paper are actually relevant. It beat a search-only baseline in twenty-six of twenty-seven meta-analyses. But the decomposition is the finding: article-level screening added almost nothing over a well-crafted keyword search. Essentially all the gain came from analysis-level selection — choosing which of the several contrasts reported in one paper belongs in this synthesis. In an emotion-regulation review, existing tools pooled the increase, decrease and maintain contrasts into one blurred map; separating them recovered the expert pattern. The lesson transfers directly to pharmacological meta-analysis: the unit of eligibility is the analysis, not the article, and tools that pool at the article level will keep producing maps that look convergent because they averaged across the distinction you cared about. One sobering aside — manually checking the transcribed eligibility criteria forced revisions in five of nine reviews, because the published criteria were ambiguous or inconsistently applied.

And to close, something small and practical. The group behind Live Mouse Tracker — the system that records positions and annotates social behavior for groups of mice over days to months — reports that fewer than ten percent of its users analyze their own data, because doing so requires writing custom code. Their fix is a Model Context Protocol server that exposes a local tracker database to a consumer language model, so a user can ask questions in natural language instead. They demonstrate it on a comparison of homozygous versus heterozygous Shank3 mutants and check the output against previously published analyses done without AI, and they ship system prompts and guidelines intended to keep the analyses valid and reproducible. As far as they know it's the first MCP server in behavioral science. The interesting number isn't the architecture, it's the ninety percent: a rich longitudinal behavioral dataset where the analysis bottleneck was literally that the biologist couldn't write the query. That is a very fixable failure mode, and it exists in a lot more labs than this one.
