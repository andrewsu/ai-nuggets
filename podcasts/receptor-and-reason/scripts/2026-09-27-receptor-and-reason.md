## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. I'm your host, and this is your daily pass through CNS pharmacology, psychiatric and neurology drug discovery, and the computational work closing in on both. Ten items today. Housekeeping first: bioRxiv's metadata API has now returned empty responses four mornings running, so preprint discovery again came off the per-subject RSS feeds. The medRxiv feeds are back after yesterday's outage, and they carried today's lead.

We open with the largest clozapine utilisation study I am aware of, and the most interesting result in it is a null. An international consortium pulled outpatient prescription and dispensing data from forty-two countries and territories — a little over one point one billion people — and computed, on a single common protocol, how many individuals actually received clozapine per hundred thousand population, every year from twenty fifteen to twenty twenty-four.

The headline spread is hard to take seriously. Among adults aged twenty to sixty-four, Croatia sits at four hundred and fifteen per hundred thousand and Finland at three hundred and two. Oman is at zero point five, Ethiopia at zero point three — a nearly fourteen-hundred-fold gap, and it barely moves when you age- and sex-standardise. The same group's previous study, which was mostly high-income countries, found a three-hundred-fold spread; adding low- and middle-income settings quadrupled it.

Now the null. They correlated utilisation with gross domestic product, the GINI coefficient, urbanisation, population size, total and per-capita health expenditure, mental health as a share of the health budget, psychiatrist density, and — this is the one that matters — a published index of how stringent each country's haematological monitoring requirement is. Not one of those indicators explained the variation.

These are ecological correlations across a few dozen countries, explicitly exploratory, and a null in that design is weak evidence on its own. But it points somewhere useful, because the two stories the field usually tells are both resource stories: poor countries cannot afford the monitoring, and stringent monitoring suppresses prescribing. Neither survives contact with this dataset. Monitoring stringency in particular has been the load-bearing assumption behind the recent American and European relaxations of the blood-count requirement, and the authors note that those relaxations have not so far produced sustained increases in use.

Two more things worth carrying out of it. Utilisation peaks between fifty and fifty-nine years old in most countries, and since the age distribution of schizophrenia peaks a long way earlier, that is a fairly direct readout of delayed initiation — people reaching the one drug that works for treatment-resistant schizophrenia after decades of illness. And their patient-level prevalence estimates disagree substantially with the sales-based defined-daily-dose analyses that have dominated this literature: countries change rank, some trends run in opposite directions, and overall growth is seventeen percent on their measure against thirty-nine percent on sales. If the question is how many people are being treated, count people. Against a conservative treatment-resistance prevalence of about ninety-nine per hundred thousand, seventeen of thirty-one countries fall below the line — so this is not a low-income-country problem.

Staying with antipsychotics, a preprint out of Saudi Arabia runs a disproportionality analysis on the Saudi Food and Drug Authority's spontaneous reporting database for atypical antipsychotics in children and adolescents with autism — seven hundred and one adverse-event reports between twenty twenty-one and twenty twenty-four. I want to use it as a teaching case, because it illustrates a failure mode that recurs constantly in this literature.

Risperidone accounts for ninety-two percent of the reports. Olanzapine is twenty-seven reports, quetiapine twenty-one, aripiprazole five. And the comparator in a disproportionality analysis is the rest of the database, so here each drug is effectively being compared against risperidone. What comes out is risperidone flagged for nervous-system disorders, olanzapine for metabolic and haematological events, quetiapine for cardiac events, aripiprazole for gastrointestinal ones — every one of them what the label already says, which is reassuring about the method and close to uninformative about the drugs. And the precision is brutal. The quetiapine cardiac signal, driven entirely by QT prolongation, carries a reporting odds ratio of three hundred and fifty with a confidence interval running from eighteen to six thousand seven hundred. The aripiprazole gastrointestinal signal rests on five reports.

What is actually worth something here is the age analysis. Adolescents on risperidone had higher adjusted odds than younger children across five separate system organ classes, with no sex difference anywhere. That is a hypothesis about developmental pharmacokinetics or cumulative exposure, and it is the part of the paper that is not reciting the package insert back to you. The general lesson is that a national spontaneous-reporting database with one drug at ninety-two percent share can do signal detection but cannot do within-class comparison, and the two get conflated routinely.

If prescribing variation is not explained by health systems, the next place people look is patient biology. Progress in Neuro-Psychopharmacology has a systematic review this week on cytochrome P four fifty two C nineteen and two D six variation and serotonin-reuptake-inhibitor outcomes specifically in obsessive-compulsive disorder. The motivation is sound: almost all the pharmacogenomic evidence for antidepressants comes from depression or mixed psychiatric cohorts, and OCD is treated with higher doses for longer, which is where metabolic capacity should matter most.

They searched five databases from inception to the end of last October. Three studies met criteria — ninety-one patients, then seventy-four of whom thirty-nine had OCD, then a hundred and eighty-four. That is the entire OCD-specific evidence base. Two D six variation moved antidepressant blood concentrations, which was never in doubt, but did not consistently predict clinical response; one retrospective study linked reduced two D six metabolism to more failed medication trials and to venlafaxine adverse effects; the two C nineteen-sertraline and two D six-fluoxetine response associations were not significant. Overall certainty, very low, on the authors' own assessment.

I am flagging this less for the result than for the calibration. Pharmacogenomic panels are being sold into psychiatry on the strength of pharmacokinetic plausibility. The concentration effects are real; the outcome evidence in this indication is three studies with no independent replication. Those are different claims and they deserve to be priced differently.

Sticking with treatment resistance, a Taipei Veterans General group pooled a hundred and thirty-two participants from two of their own randomised ketamine trials — one saline-controlled, one midazolam-controlled — to ask whether ketamine's antidepressant effect is broad or symptom-specific. Single infusion at zero point five milligrams per kilogram, sixty-six per arm.

At four hours, nine of the ten items on the Montgomery-Asberg depression rating scale cleared false-discovery correction, as did all four literature-derived symptom domains, at four hours and again at day two. Apparent sadness, pessimistic thoughts and suicidal thoughts each moved about one point one scale points relative to control. The effect is broad, and it is early.

Then they did the fashionable part: Gaussian graphical models of the symptom network at baseline versus day two, and a treatment-node network intervention analysis to ask which symptoms ketamine acts on first and which follow. Global network strength rose from three point four to four point five, marginally significant on permutation, with no detectable change in structure. And here is the honest bit, which is why the paper is on the list. That network analysis retained nine edges at a fifty percent bootstrap selection threshold, four at sixty percent, and two at seventy-five; baseline edge stability was, in their word, limited. They conclude that the network associations were sensitive to reporting thresholds and did not establish symptom-specific mechanisms.

That is the correct conclusion, and it is rarer than it should be in this literature, where a figure of a graph with edges drawn on it tends to get read as a causal claim about which symptom drives which. A hundred and thirty-two participants cannot estimate a ten-node network reliably at any threshold. The item-level finding stands. The network part is a null wearing a picture.

One more from the same journal, a nice piece of circuit pharmacology. A Kurume University group asked whether two mice showing the same depression-like behaviour are actually in the same neural state. They induced stress resilience two independent ways — electroconvulsive stimulation, or viral overexpression of delta-FosB in the nucleus accumbens — and confirmed it by preserved social interaction after chronic social defeat.

Then they gave repeated cocaine, and both resilient states broke. Social avoidance returned, and forced-swim immobility rose selectively in the defeated animals. Conditioning to the cocaine context was not required, and there was no generalised locomotor suppression, so it is not a performance artefact. The mechanistic hook is a retrograde chemogenetic experiment: inhibiting tyrosine-hydroxylase-positive accumbens-projecting neurons attenuated the cocaine-associated social avoidance, in a way the authors carefully describe as target-dependent — the same manipulation does not rescue social avoidance arising by other routes.

So you have two animals, indistinguishable on the behavioural readout, differing in whether that readout is sensitive to dopaminergic inhibition. That is an uncomfortable message for anyone using social interaction ratio as a depression endpoint in a screen: it aggregates over circuit states, and the compound you are testing may act on only one of them. Male mice only, group sizes from eight to thirty-three.

To receptor pharmacology now, and a drug-repositioning result from Bahia Blanca with New College of Florida on the serotonin three A receptor — the serotonin-gated cation channel in the pentameric ligand-gated family, the target the setrons hit competitively. There has been sustained interest in negative allosteric modulators there as an alternative to competitive antagonism, and this group found two sitting in plain sight: piperazine, and its derivative hydroxyzine.

Hydroxyzine is a first-generation antihistamine prescribed constantly for anxiety and for pruritus, with a pharmacology that is already promiscuous. Macroscopic current recordings show both compounds inhibit the human receptor allosterically rather than competitively. Computational analysis proposed two distinct sites at subunit interfaces — one in the lower extracellular domain, one in the upper transmembrane domain — and they went after both with mutagenesis at four residues. Some mutations abolished the inhibition. Others enhanced hydroxyzine's effect, which is the more informative outcome, because an enhancing mutation is much harder to explain away as a nonspecific or folding artefact than an abolishing one.

Two reasons this matters past the repositioning pitch. If hydroxyzine is a serotonin-three negative allosteric modulator at attainable plasma concentrations, that is a pharmacology already running unexamined in a very large number of patients, and it is on nobody's interaction list. And an allosteric site that does not compete with serotonin is exactly the modality you would want for the central indications the setrons never reached. I am working from the abstract rather than the full paper, which is behind Elsevier's wall, so the concentrations at which this happens are the number I would want next — and they decide whether this is clinical pharmacology or just pharmacology.

Now the computational centrepiece, from Hananel Hazan and Michael Levin at Tufts, and the framing is better than the usual channelopathy-modelling paper. Their pipeline starts from patch-clamp recordings of a variant channel and searches for an intervention that never touches the mutated channel at all — asking instead which other, pharmacologically accessible conductances you would have to modulate, and by how much, to bring the neuron's firing back to the reference pattern. The output is concrete: cut sodium conductance to zero point seven nine times baseline, raise delayed-rectifier potassium to one point nine four, leave leak about where it is.

They run it on two real clinical variants: an arginine-to-cysteine substitution at position eight fifty-nine in Na-v one point one, from a four-generation family with generalised epilepsy with febrile seizures plus, and an aspartate-to-glycine variant at position two hundred and twelve in K-v seven point two — that is K-C-N-Q-two — causing benign familial neonatal seizures. For the potassium variant, in a multi-compartment pyramidal-neuron model, the compensating prescription recovers about eighty-nine percent of the reference firing pattern.

Three findings are worth your time. The first is degeneracy, and it has therapeutic teeth. Thirty-seven distinct conductance triples fire exactly the same number of action potentials at every one of thirty-five injected-current levels, so the variant's observed behaviour does not identify a unique underlying configuration — and every candidate intervention is required to work across all thirty-seven admissible ground truths rather than against one assumed model. The same redundancy appears on the intervention side: ten thousand seven hundred and eighty triples tie at the best achievable score and produce only six distinct firing patterns between them. That is why this is tractable at all. You are not trying to hit a point in parameter space. You are trying to reach a region.

Second, the sodium variant is not uniformly hypo- or hyperexcitable: less excitable than wild type between a hundred and forty and two hundred and ten picoamps, indistinguishable from two twenty to two fifty, and more excitable from two sixty up. A loss-of-function label on that channel is a statement about one stimulus regime, not about the neuron.

Third, and this is the honest negative result that makes me trust the rest of it. They built a differentiable Hodgkin-Huxley model in PyTorch, and gradient descent on the conductances found the compensating configuration in one of four variant configurations. Direct search over the same parameter box found it in four of four, and two of the four gradient runs ended up worse than administering nothing at all. The three-to-four-orders-of-magnitude speedup they report is entirely a throughput story — batching plus kernel fusion, sixty-four thousand parameter sets per second on a consumer graphics card against roughly twelve per second on a seventy-two-core node — and they say so explicitly: the gain comes from a fast forward model feeding a search, not from the gradient. Differentiability bought them a fast simulator, not a better optimiser.

The dosing-tolerance analysis is the part a pharmacologist should read. Efficacy survives a median joint perturbation of plus or minus eleven percent across all three accessible conductances, but only plus or minus three percent under the worst combination of directions — your therapeutic window, with the typical case and any guarantee you could write differing more than threefold. It is all in silico, on two variants, with no wet validation. But naming the currents and the magnitudes is a far more actionable output than most computational channelopathy work manages.

From a single neuron to a whole discovery stack. Anew Labs — ByteDance's drug-discovery unit — has posted a technical report on what it calls Anew D-D-E, an agentic engine chaining structure prediction, pocket identification, affinity estimation, generative design and an in-house language model into a closed loop with experimental feedback.

The component results are respectable rather than startling — sixty-two percent top-one success on the AlphaFold-three antibody benchmark, seventy-six percent on a molecular-glue set, and an affinity model that returns a predicted free-energy distribution with a self-estimate of its own reliability rather than a bare point value. The result with real experimental weight is the nanobody campaign. The agent designed fifty V-H-H sequences from a humanised scaffold, got seven hits under four hundred nanomolar by surface plasmon resonance, then ran in silico affinity maturation and reached a ten point seven percent success rate at single-digit nanomolar. That is a good hit rate for de novo biologics design. The catch is that the target is masked throughout the paper, so you cannot judge how hard it was.

But the number I would point at is their language model. They post-trained a hundred-billion-parameter model on pharmaceutical research and development, and built PharmBench — two hundred scenario-based questions written by their own in-house medicinal chemists and project leaders, covering hit identification, lead optimisation, ADMET, and advance-or-deprioritise decisions. On that benchmark, designed by their own experts and presumably reflecting their own house reasoning style, their model scored fifty-four point six five percent and ranked third out of seventeen, behind GPT five point six Sol and a couple of tenths of a point behind Kimi K three.

I think that is the most informative number in the paper, and to their credit they published it. A frontier general model with no pharmaceutical post-training beats a purpose-built hundred-billion-parameter domain model on a benchmark the domain model's own company wrote. The case for building in-house then rests on deployment and data control — which they state plainly, since pipeline information is sensitive — and not on capability. Note the absolute scale, too: the best score any model achieves on expert-written drug-development reasoning is in the fifties. Nobody is close.

A much smaller paper makes a sharper version of the same point about where the headroom sits. Cell-Lens, from Shanghai AI Laboratory with Nanjing University, is a training-free change to how you show a cell to a language model.

The current convention is the cell-sentence: take the cell's top genes ranked by expression, write them out as a list, hand that to the model. Cell-Lens instead writes the cell as typed blocks — paired measurements from other modalities, assay-observed zeros, marker-supported programs. Nothing about the model changes. The gain is up to twenty-seven points of accuracy across four public benchmarks and five model families, weaker models gain most so the spread between models narrows, and reasoning-token usage falls twenty-two to thirty-one percent while accuracy rises — the opposite of the usual trade.

The mechanism is the good part. C-D-eight-A, the defining marker of CD8 T cells, appears in only seven to nine percent of those cells' cell-sentences, because ranking pushes it below the cutoff. The paired protein measurement carries the same identity ninety-three to ninety-eight percent of the time. So a model reading only the ranked gene list is guessing CD8 T cells at chance — not because it cannot reason, but because the evidence was deleted during serialisation.

Two controls make it convincing. Swap a cell's added block for another cell's and accuracy falls below baseline, so the gain is not length. And drop the protein magnitudes, keeping only the ranked identities, and nothing changes — the model is using the names, not the numbers. That is a slightly deflating finding about what LLM reasoning over single-cell data currently consists of, and a completely practical one: before anybody fine-tunes another model on this data, check whether the information reached the prompt at all.

Last, and lightest, a small study that uses a language model as a measuring instrument rather than as an agent, asking whether perplexity — how hard a model finds it to predict the next word — picks up formal thought disorder in child and early-adolescent onset psychosis. Twenty-three cases, fifteen controls, which is tiny. The language sample is not: hours of manually transcribed diagnostic interviews per participant, averaging about six and a half thousand words per case.

They computed two quantities: perplexity from LLaMA three point two, which predicts each word from the words that preceded it, and pseudo-perplexity from BERT, which predicts each word from the whole sentence in both directions. Cases showed higher LLaMA perplexity than controls after adjusting for age and transcript length. BERT pseudo-perplexity showed no significant group difference. And the developmental result is the more interesting one: perplexity fell with age in cases and not in controls, and it held when restricted to participants with hallucinations or delusions in the preceding fortnight, so it is not simply that older participants were less symptomatic.

Their reading is that language in childhood-onset psychosis starts more childlike and matures more slowly, converging toward controls who are already adultlike across the range. The divergence between the two model types is the methodologically useful part: bidirectional models are more sensitive to syntactic structure, autoregressive ones to vocabulary choice and parts of speech, so a signal that appears in one and not the other localises the deviation. Thirty-eight participants, cross-sectional, and thirty-nine percent of cases were on an antipsychotic at interview with no analysis of that, so this needs replication before anyone builds on it. But asking which model fails to predict the patient is a real measurement design, not a metaphor.
