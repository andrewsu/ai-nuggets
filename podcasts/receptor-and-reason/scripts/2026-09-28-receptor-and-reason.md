## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. I'm your host, and this is your daily pass through CNS pharmacology, psychiatric and neurology drug discovery, and the computational work closing in on both. Ten items today. One housekeeping note: bioRxiv's metadata API is fully healthy this morning after a rough week — seventeen hundred and thirty-four preprints across the window, day by day, no gaps. The reading side was the bottleneck instead, with the Cloudflare layer throttling full-text fetches hard enough that several good candidates are deferred rather than dropped.

We open with the most complete attempt I have seen at automating the front end of CNS drug discovery, and what makes it worth your time is not the architecture, it's the evaluation.

A group working with the Indiana University School of Medicine and Purdue TREAT-AD Center built an agent they call ARSA, an autonomous research scientist for target nomination in Alzheimer's disease and related dementias. You hand it a research interest in plain language, optionally some molecular data, and it hands back a ranked, evidence-linked shortlist of targets. Under the hood it searches a corpus of about two hundred thousand PubMed-indexed papers, formulates hypotheses, then — and this is the interesting design choice — decomposes each hypothesis into atomic claims and audits them individually as supported, partially supported, unsupported, or refuted. It then plans and runs the molecular analyses itself across fourteen precomputed datasets spanning four of the major brain cohorts, and prioritises candidates against nine knowledge bases. Median runtime, under nine minutes.

Now the evaluation, which is where most agentic-science papers wave their hands. Three things. First, they hard-cut the literature corpus at the start of last year, generated a hundred hypotheses, and asked whether the subsequent literature actually went there. All hundred were topically related; around seventy percent showed specific biological correspondence, with two independent model evaluators agreeing on three-quarters of them. Second, they compared nominations against Agora, the community target registry, and got mean overlap ranging from about thirty-four to about fifty-two percent depending on the dataset — every run producing both registry-listed and unlisted candidates.

Third, and this is the one that matters: fourteen human experts spanning bioinformatics, structural biology, assay development, and medicinal chemistry rated sixty genes, shuffled together into three surveys — retained nominations mixed in with candidates the agent had rejected, and no category labels shown. Retained nominations scored three point three two on a five-point credibility scale against two point one five for rejected comparators, a paired difference of one point one eight points, and retained candidates came out ahead in every single questionnaire. Restrict it to the nominations that are not in Agora and the difference is still one point zero six.

That last comparison is the real result. Recovering known targets is a retrieval problem. Producing targets the community has not nominated, and having domain experts independently judge them credible, is the thing you actually want. Two honest caveats the authors state plainly: there is no wet-lab validation anywhere in this paper — expert credibility is an opinion, not an experiment — and they never measured whether the agent saves anyone any time. That second gap is bigger than it sounds, because "faster than a postdoc" is the entire commercial premise of this category and nobody is measuring it.

So let's take the next step down that pipeline: from a nominated gene to a drug you could actually give someone. Molecular Psychiatry has the largest cohort yet of loss-of-function variants in WDFY3 — thirty-two individuals — and it consolidates this into a recognisable monogenic disorder: autism and ADHD features, mild to moderate developmental delay, and usually macrocephaly.

Then they go after mechanism. Knocking WDFY3 down in SH-SY5Y cells impaired clearance of protein aggregates, increased proliferation, and cut neurite outgrowth. Transcriptomics pointed at WNT/beta-catenin and MAPK/ERK, confirmed at protein level: beta-catenin down, GSK-3 beta up. And once you write "GSK-3 beta up" in a neurodevelopmental disorder, there is an obvious next move, because lithium inhibits GSK-3 beta. They treated with lithium chloride and it did what you would hope — phospho-GSK-3 beta went up within fifteen minutes, and in cortical neurons from haploinsufficient embryos, eight days of treatment increased dendritic arborisation above the untreated heterozygous level, with no effect on wild-type neurons.

I like the logic and I want to be clear about what it is not. The concentration is five millimolar lithium chloride. Therapeutic serum lithium in patients runs roughly zero point six to one point two millimolar, so this is several-fold above the clinical range in a dish, and lithium's therapeutic index is famously narrow. The gene-expression effect is also noisy in a way worth naming — the reported increase in Wdfy3 expression in heterozygous cells is about a hundred-fold with a standard deviation of a hundred and eighty-three, on four replicates. That is a signal you note and go replicate, not one you quote. What the paper genuinely establishes is a pathway rationale for testing a GSK-3 beta inhibitor in this specific genotype. Lithium is the crudest available tool for that job, not the endpoint.

Staying with neurodevelopmental repurposing but with much tighter causal control — a group across Zhejiang University Children's Hospital, Central South University, and Imperial College reports that xenon rescues autism-like behaviour in mice. Xenon is an NMDA receptor antagonist already used clinically as an anaesthetic and studied as a neuroprotectant, and here it is given at twenty-five percent in oxygen, ten minutes a day for ten days.

The behavioural result holds across two genetically distinct models, a Mef2c mutant and a Shank3 knockout, in both sexes, on object recognition, three-chamber social preference, and Y-maze. Improvements were still present ten days after the last exposure, and a single thirty-minute exposure was enough to move all three measures.

The mechanism is where this gets unexpected. You would predict NMDA antagonism. What they find instead is that xenon reduces glutamic acid decarboxylase — both Gad1 and Gad2 — in inhibitory neurons of the paraventricular thalamus, on single-cell sequencing with qPCR confirmation. Less GABA synthesis means disinhibition, and patch-clamp confirms it: paraventricular thalamic glutamatergic neurons sit at a depolarised resting potential, need less current to fire, and see a drop in inhibitory postsynaptic current frequency with amplitude untouched — a presynaptic signature. Then the causal chain, properly done in both directions. Muscimol into the paraventricular thalamus abolishes the benefit. Chemogenetic inhibition of the glutamatergic neurons abolishes it; chemogenetic activation reproduces it without xenon. Optogenetic inhibition of the projection specifically to central amygdala blocks it; activating that projection reverses the phenotype on its own.

That is a genuinely well-constructed circuit paper. One caution on the reporting: in the Shank3 model, several of the rescue claims are framed as the treated knockouts no longer differing significantly from wild-type controls. Absence of a difference is not evidence of equivalence, particularly at these group sizes, and the Mef2c data — where they show positive within-genotype differences — is the stronger half of the paper.

A third angle on drug exposure in an autism model, this one much more modest but pointed at something I had not thought about. A reanalysis of a public prefrontal-cortex RNA-seq dataset from Arid1b haploinsufficient mice asks what fluoxetine does to ADAR editing. Adenosine-to-inosine editing is not a side channel here; it is one of the main ways the neuronal transcriptome gets tuned.

The design matters for how much weight to put on it. Fluoxetine was delivered through the mother's milk from postnatal day three to twenty-one, and cortex was sampled at postnatal day one hundred and twenty — so this is developmental exposure with a very long read-out. Four animals per group, four groups. The findings: fluoxetine shifts the number of edited sites in opposite directions in wild-type versus heterozygous animals; the editing landscape differs across conditions; and among evolutionarily conserved recoding sites, twelve were edited in vehicle-treated animals versus eight in fluoxetine-treated ones — including two sites in Gria2, the GluA2 AMPA receptor subunit whose editing at the Q-to-R site is what makes the channel calcium-impermeable.

Gria2 editing is one of the small set of recoding events with an unambiguous functional consequence, so finding it moves under developmental SSRI exposure is a legitimate flag. But four per group, a secondary analysis, no behaviour, no causal manipulation, and no direct measurement of Q-to-R editing efficiency itself. The paper's closing suggestion that this could contribute to autism pathology runs well ahead of what four mice per arm can support. Treat it as a hypothesis worth powering properly.

From SSRIs in mice to SSRIs in patients. A group at the National Institute of Mental Health and Neurosciences in Bangalore, with collaborators in Dortmund and Melbourne, imaged fifty healthy controls, forty SSRI responders, and forty non-responders with obsessive-compulsive disorder — and defined the groups stringently, at least thirty-five percent Y-BOCS reduction for responders, under twenty-five percent after at least two adequate trials for non-responders, with partial responders excluded entirely.

They ran it hypothesis-free, looking at amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations across the whole brain, and the regions that came out were the right anterior middle temporal gyrus and the right temporal pole — not the cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical loop that dominates the OCD literature. Non-responders had higher amplitude at both, relative to responders and relative to controls. Seeding from those clusters, responders showed lower connectivity to posterior occipital and parietal cortex than non-responders.

Two things to hold onto. The clean part is that non-responders were the deviant group on the regional measure while responders looked like controls — which is the pattern you want if you are hunting a resistance marker rather than a state marker. The awkward part is the connectivity analysis, where responders differed from both other groups and non-responders did not differ from controls at all. Those two results point in different directions and the paper does not really reconcile them. Add a five-minute resting scan, which is short by current standards, plus concomitant clonazepam in twenty patients and antipsychotics in seven, and this is a lead rather than a biomarker.

Since selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors and cognitive behavioural therapy are the two first-line options in both depression and OCD, it is worth looking at what is happening on the psychotherapy side. A Tsinghua-led group has MACBT, a multi-agent system that encodes the five-stage CBT workflow — assessment, Socratic questioning, cognitive restructuring, behavioural experiments, monitoring — as five collaborating agents, sitting on a fourteen-billion-parameter open model fine-tuned with supervised learning and preference optimisation. The genuinely thoughtful component is the memory module, which tracks cognitive-distortion type, frequency, severity, and restructuring efficacy across sessions and produces a pre-session report — the part of therapy that is real clinical labour and that transcript-level systems ignore.

Then you look at the evaluation and the whole thing floats free of the ground. The training corpus is Chinese CBT dialogue generated by having language models play both patient and counsellor. The single-session evaluation uses a hundred virtual patient profiles. The longitudinal evaluation uses thirty simulated three-session units. The scoring is done by a language model acting as judge, on zero-to-three scales. Professionalism two point six two, authenticity two point two five, and the best overall mean in the comparison table is one point eight three out of three.

There is not a human being anywhere in that loop. Synthetic patients, synthetic training data, synthetic evaluator. What the numbers establish is that this system's outputs resemble the same model family's idea of good CBT more than the baselines' outputs do, which is close to tautological. I am not dismissing the memory architecture — it is the right idea, and the ablation showing cross-session persistence beats single-session memory by a wide margin is at least internally consistent. But the paper describes itself as clinician-facing decision support, and no clinician has looked at it.

Which is a decent transition into two benchmark papers that are, in effect, about the same disease: evaluations that flatter the method.

The first is PLABench, from Missouri, on protein-ligand binding affinity prediction. They benchmark nine deep-learning methods — six structure-based, three sequence-based — under leakage-controlled conditions, on blind targets from the most recent CASP round, on temporally held-out ChEMBL data, and on the standard Davis and KIBA sets, standardising every structural input through AlphaFold3 so the comparison is actually fair.

Three findings you should carry. Structure-based models with the largest training sets do come out on top overall, but with severe target-to-target variability that the aggregate metric hides. High regression accuracy repeatedly coexists with an inability to rank congeneric ligands within a narrow, high-affinity range — which is precisely the discrimination that matters in lead optimisation. And the one I would put on a slide: increasing structural fidelity does not monotonically improve affinity prediction. Even given ground-truth experimental crystal structures, several structure-based architectures fail to beat a sequence-only baseline. Whatever the structure branch is learning, it is not reliably learning binding mode. On the target classes, cytochrome P450s and transcription factors degrade badly for every architecture tested — the authors point at missing cofactor support, catalytic heme in particular, which is a concrete and fixable input-side problem rather than a mystery.

The companion is TopU-LBVS, from UT Dallas and the National Institute of Biological Sciences in Beijing, doing the same job for ligand-based virtual screening. They build ninety-three targets across seven protein classes out of ChEMBL, with property-matched, structurally similar decoys at a fixed one-to-forty active-to-decoy ratio — hard negatives, deliberately stripped of the physicochemical and nearest-neighbour shortcuts that make standard decoy sets easy.

The controlled experiment is the elegant part. Seven targets, same training data, same validation, same test actives, same ratio — the only thing that changes is whether the test decoys are random or hard. Mean enrichment factor at one percent falls from twenty-one point two to five point five. A nearly fourfold collapse, caused by nothing but swapping the negatives, and the direction holds in sixty-seven of seventy paired model-target comparisons.

And the ranking under hard negatives should embarrass a few people. Morgan fingerprints plus a random forest is the best method overall, at nine point one eight, beating every graph neural network, every fingerprint hybrid, and a large pretrained molecular transformer. The best pure graph network manages four point four seven. Bolting fingerprints onto the graph models recovers a couple of points and they still land two and a half below the random forest. For this audience the per-class numbers are the sting: GPCRs are the weakest class for the leading methods, with the best of them managing five point two one, and on the hERG channel target, averaged across all ten models, enrichment falls from sixteen point two on random decoys to three point six on hard ones. If your screening cascade includes a CNS target and an off-target cardiac liability, those are the two columns you care about, and they are the two where the shortcut-free evaluation hurts most.

So what happens when you put an agent on top of scores like these? A group across the Beijing Life Science Academy and the Chinese Academy of Sciences built a closed-loop language-model medicinal chemistry agent for hit-to-lead optimisation, combining target and starting-hit context, literature retrieval, property tools, docking, and — crucially — feedback from previously scored analogues. Six kinase targets, six competing methods, six independent runs each, a fixed forty-candidate budget.

On the pre-specified composite objective, the agent wins on four of six targets. Fine. But then they re-scored the identical candidate sets under two other reasonable decision rules — best docking within a drug-likeness-qualified subset, and docking-versus-drug-likeness hypervolume — and the winners rearranged completely. Under gated docking the wins split four ways across the agent, a greedy baseline, a genetic algorithm, and a non-iterative language-model comparator. Same molecules, same campaigns, three different answers to "which method is better."

That is the paper's real contribution and the authors know it — they frame the headline metric as a decision rule that has to match the objective you actually optimised for. The mechanistic explanation is visible in the candidate distributions: the agent's proposals sit in higher drug-likeness regions while still reaching decent docking scores, whereas the genetic algorithm buys stronger docking by pushing a large fraction of candidates below a drug-likeness of zero point five. The ablation is clean too — removing iterative feedback from scored candidates hurts on five of six targets, while removing the pre-retrieved literature context barely registers. Retrieval-augmented generation is decorative here; the closed loop is what is doing the work. The erlotinib case study takes docking from about minus six point eight to about minus eight point six kilocalories per mole with drug-likeness rising from zero point four two to zero point seven three, holding the anilinoquinazoline core. And to their credit, the abstract says outright that these are hypotheses for experimental testing, not validated leads.

To close, something lighter but pointed in the same direction. PRAXIS-VirtualCell, out of Shandong University, is a framework for agentic virtual-cell experiments spanning E. coli, yeast, and human K562 cells. The architecture is mostly plumbing — data packs, models, adapters, execution environments — but one design decision is worth taking away regardless of what you think of virtual cells.

The language model is explicitly denied numerical authority. It can parse a question, plan, route, and write up the interpretation. It cannot create data, alter feature mappings, generate model outputs, issue validation evidence, or override an execution decision. Numbers come only from registered predictors, compiled into a deterministic execution graph. And when the runtime checks data availability, model compatibility, and evidence scope and finds them unmet, it returns a structured abstention rather than letting the model produce a plausible substitute. Failure states are preserved in the output instead of being smoothed over.

I have no idea whether their virtual cell predicts anything useful — the paper is thin on that, and it reads as an architecture proposal rather than a results paper. But the principle generalises well beyond virtual cells, and it is the distinction between the two ends of today's episode. ARSA is convincing partly because every nomination carries its evidence and its decision trail, so an expert can check it. MACBT is unconvincing partly because the whole chain, from training data to judge, is the model talking to itself with nothing anchoring it. Deciding in advance what your language model is not allowed to assert is turning out to be a more reliable safety mechanism than trying to make it assert only true things.
