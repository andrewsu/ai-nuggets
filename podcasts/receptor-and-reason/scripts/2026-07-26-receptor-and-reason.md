## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. It's Sunday, July twenty-sixth, twenty twenty-six — ten items, and it's a psychedelics-and-computation day. We open on a question hanging over the entire microdosing field: does giving people a small dose of psilocybin every day quietly put their heart valves at risk — and a modeling paper that tries to answer it with pharmacology instead of hand-waving. From there we stay with psychedelics for a mouse study on what one of these drugs actually does to the structure of cortical networks. Then we swing into computational drug discovery — a generative model that designs molecules straight from gene-expression signatures, and a sharp cautionary result about trusting language models to reason their way through chemistry. We look at two new therapeutic ideas aimed at Parkinson's — a supramolecular way to degrade alpha-synuclein, and a computational model of deep brain stimulation that fails in an instructive way. Then two takes on where schizophrenia actually begins, a piece on the epigenetics of memory persistence, and we close on a mushroom that arguably shouldn't exist.

We open on a safety question that the psychedelic field has mostly been answering with vibes. Repeated low-dose psilocybin is being developed as a scalable outpatient treatment for mood and anxiety disorders — the idea being you dose people regularly rather than once. But psilocin, the active metabolite, binds the cardiac 5-HT2B receptor, and sustained agonism at 5-HT2B is exactly the mechanism that gave us fen-phen valvulopathy and the valve disease from old ergot dopamine agonists. So chronic dosing raises a real worry. This group argues the field has been asking the wrong pharmacological question — that valvular risk doesn't track binding affinity, it tracks functional efficacy times exposure duration. They built an exposure-response model that converts plasma psilocin into a time-integrated measure of how much extra 5-HT2B G-q signaling you're driving above the body's own serotonin tone, then calibrated it against drugs and conditions with known valvular outcomes. Every exposure that's caused human valve disease scored high on their scale; everything that hasn't scored low, with a clean separation and no misclassifications across seven clinical anchors. A candidate three-milligram daily psilocybin regimen scored two orders of magnitude below the weakest valvulopathic exposure. And the reason is mechanistically satisfying: psilocin is a low-efficacy partial agonist at 5-HT2B — a ceiling around half of serotonin's maximum, versus ninety-six percent for the fen-phen metabolite — and it has a short half-life, about two and a half hours, so daily dosing produces a brief pulse that decays to zero rather than the sustained plateau the fibrotic program actually requires. They back the model with twelve days of continuous psilocin in rats at plasma levels well above the projected human peak, and blinded histopathology found no valve lesions. Why it matters: this reframes the microdosing safety debate from an unanswerable "is there any risk" into a quantified, monitorable risk. The honest caveats are real — twelve days can't rule out fibrosis that takes months to develop, and psilocin is actually a fuller agonist at the beta-arrestin arm of 5-HT2B than at the G-q arm, so whether arrestin signaling is fibrogenic is a genuine open question. But as a framework, this is the right way to think about it, and its practical recommendation — put serial echocardiography into these trials — is one regulators will likely insist on anyway.

Staying with psychedelics but moving from the heart to the cortex. We have good human neuroimaging showing that psychedelics reorganize large-scale brain connectivity — the networks that are normally segregated start talking to each other. What's been missing is the circuit mechanism. This group used widefield calcium imaging across the cortex of awake mice and gave them DOI — a selective 5-HT2A agonist used as a tool psychedelic. DOI acutely decreased the modularity of the cortical network, meaning the normally distinct modules became more correlated, specifically by strengthening the coupling between anterolateral and posteromedial cortical domains. And when they measured functional influence — who's driving whom — DOI increased both thalamo-cortical and long-range cortico-cortical influence onto that anterolateral domain, all of it 5-HT2A-dependent. So this is a mouse correlate of the network desegregation people see in human psychedelic imaging, and it fingers thalamocortical drive as a candidate engine for that reorganization. It's correlative on the mechanism — they're careful to say they speculate the influence changes are causing the coupling — but it's a concrete, manipulable circuit handle on an effect we've mostly described at the level of whole-brain fMRI.

Now into computational drug discovery, with a generative model that starts from biology rather than a target. Most molecular design is target-based or structure-guided — you have a protein pocket and you design for it. But plenty of disease biology isn't captured by a single predefined target. This group's framework, called Tx2Mol, takes a gene-expression signature — a system-level readout of a disease or a perturbation state — and generates candidate molecules while trying to preserve that phenotypic signal all the way through generation, which is the hard part, because it's easy to produce a chemically plausible molecule that's lost any connection to the biology you asked for. Across ten cancer-relevant gene-perturbation benchmarks it beat nine transcriptome-guided baselines, improving similarity to known active ligands by about twenty-four percent on average and over fifty percent on one HDAC1 benchmark, and it generalized to noisy single-cell profiles and to patient-derived disease signatures, steering generation toward approved-drug chemical space. The significance for this audience is the phenotype-first framing — designing molecules for a desired transcriptional state when you don't have a clean target, which is a lot of CNS biology. The caveats: it's a preprint, the headline metric is similarity to molecules we already know, which rewards rediscovery, and the validation is in cancer rather than brain. But the framework itself is target-agnostic, and that's the interesting part.

And a cautionary companion to that, because it's tempting to hand chemistry over to language models. A new preprint makes the case that chemical chain-of-thought reasoning functions as a hallucination-prone molecular scratchpad. The setup is familiar: chain-of-thought prompting improves large-language-model scores on chemistry tasks like property prediction and reaction outcomes. But when these authors actually audit the intermediate reasoning steps, they find those steps are frequently populated with chemically invalid molecules and impossible reactions — even in cases where the final answer comes out right. In other words, the fluent, step-by-step scratchpad that makes the model look like it's reasoning through the chemistry is not a reliable record of valid chemistry; it just looks like one. The takeaway is a useful corrective to the interpretability enthusiasm: visible reasoning is not verified reasoning, and if you're deploying these models for real molecular design or synthesis planning, you need an external chemical validator sitting on top of the scratchpad rather than trusting the narrative. Pair it with the Tx2Mol item and you get the honest state of the field — generative chemistry is getting genuinely good, and the reasoning traces around it are still not to be trusted at face value.

From designing molecules to destroying them, and a fresh angle on Parkinson's. A hallmark of synucleinopathies is misfolded, aggregated alpha-synuclein that dodges the cell's normal clearance machinery. Targeted protein degradation is the obvious therapeutic idea — recruit the cell's own disposal system to a target — but the first-generation degraders are bifunctional chimeras, a target-binding ligand tethered by a linker to a ligand that recruits the degradation machinery, and those have struggled to get into cells, to stay stable, and to generalize. This group takes a completely different structural bet: peptide amphiphiles that self-assemble into high-aspect-ratio supramolecular nanofibers, with the fiber surface presenting peptide epitopes that simultaneously grab alpha-synuclein and recruit the machinery for chaperone-mediated autophagy — routing the target into lysosomal degradation. In cells, these nanofibers internalized on their own and selectively knocked down alpha-synuclein. It's early and it's in vitro, so hold the enthusiasm, but the appeal is modularity — you swap the monomer to change what you target — which they pitch as a platform for degrading aggregation-prone proteins more broadly. For a target class as stubborn as intracellular aggregates, a multivalent macromolecular degrader is a genuinely different tool than a small-molecule PROTAC.

Staying with Parkinson's but on the systems-modeling side, and I want to flag this one precisely because it's a negative result. Deep brain stimulation reliably helps freezing of gait, but nobody actually knows the mechanism. This group built a basal-ganglia model using a dynamic neural field to handle both action selection — which action fires — and action specification — the continuous parameters of how it's executed. The model cleanly separated healthy from dopamine-depleted regimes: under normal dopamine it selected actions stably, and after depletion it fell into the impaired, freeze-prone dynamics you want to see in a Parkinsonian model. Then they modeled DBS the standard way, as a scaled reduction of afferent drive to the subthalamic nucleus and the internal globus pallidus — a formulation that's worked in previous computational studies. And in their framework, it did not consistently restore action selection. That's the whole point: a convenient, widely used account of how DBS works didn't reproduce the clinical benefit inside a more elaborate model, which is a useful signal that "DBS just turns down subthalamic drive" is probably too simple, and it pushes toward alternative formulations of what the stimulation is actually doing. A tidy positive result would have been less informative.

Now two papers on where schizophrenia actually starts, and the first one relocates the origin out of the cortex entirely. The standard story is disrupted postnatal maturation of prefrontal circuits, but that leaves open what triggers it. This group went after the medial pulvinar — a higher-order thalamic nucleus wired to prefrontal cortex — in marmosets. Bilateral medial pulvinar lesions in newborn animals altered the diffusion-imaging trajectory of prefrontal cortex across adolescence and produced working-memory deficits in adulthood — and critically, the same lesion made in adult animals did not cause those deficits, so there's a developmental window. The lesioned animals showed reduced thalamocortical input onto layer-3 parvalbumin interneurons, weaker prefrontal gamma power, less parvalbumin, and fast-spiking interneurons stuck in an immature-like physiology. The reframing is the payoff: some of the cortical inhibitory dysfunction we associate with schizophrenia may not originate in the cortex at all, but in the thalamic input that instructs cortical maturation during a specific early window. That's a different place to look for both mechanism and, eventually, intervention.

The second schizophrenia paper stays molecular and lands on an unusual culprit — a pseudogene. Mitochondrial Complex I dysfunction is a recurring finding in schizophrenia, centered on the NDUFV2 core subunit. These authors report that a pseudogene of that subunit is upregulated in the brain and peripheral cells of patients, and that its level runs inversely to NDUFV2 expression and to mitochondrial respiration. Their in-silico work argues against the obvious explanation that it's acting as a microRNA sponge, and implicates the pseudogene itself. When they overexpressed it in healthy-derived lymphoblast lines, mitochondrial membrane potential, network dynamics, and oxygen consumption all dropped; when they knocked it down in patient-derived lines, those measures recovered toward normal. And in rat cortical neurons, forcing the pseudogene up reproduced the mitochondrial disruption alongside impaired synapse formation and reduced spontaneous firing. It's mostly lymphoblast lines plus a rat-neuron confirmation, and the precise mechanism by which a pseudogene suppresses its parent gene is still unresolved — but it nominates a specific, potentially tractable lever on the bioenergetic deficits in schizophrenia, which is a category we currently have essentially no drugs for.

One more mechanism piece before we close, this one on the epigenetics of making a memory stick. It's been known for decades that long-term memory consolidation needs waves of transcription in the hours after learning, but the epigenetic contribution to those waves has stayed murky. This group focused on HDAC3, a known memory brake, and its relationship to the transcription factor NF-kappa-B, which is one of HDAC3's deacetylation targets. Inhibiting HDAC3 with a selective inhibitor — given either right after training or a full six hours later — enhanced memory persistence in a novel-object-recognition task, while inhibiting NF-kappa-B at those same timepoints impaired memory. And the HDAC3 inhibitor increased the nuclear fraction of NF-kappa-B in hippocampal CA1, which is the mechanistic link — the first in-vivo evidence tying HDAC3 activity to NF-kappa-B nuclear translocation during consolidation. The biphasic part matters for anyone thinking about HDAC inhibitors as cognitive enhancers: there are at least two distinct time windows where the intervention lands, so timing relative to learning isn't a detail, it's part of the mechanism.

And we'll close on a piece of fungal biology that reads like a warning label. The two most famous mushroom chemicals are psilocybin, from magic mushrooms, and the amatoxins, from the deadly poisonous ones — and until now, nobody had found a single species that makes both. This group reports that a mushroom called Galerina indica — which they nickname the dark magic mushroom — produces both psilocybin and amatoxins, confirmed by mass spectrometry and by the presence of both biosynthetic gene sets in its genome. The evolutionary story is the fun part: the phylogenetics suggest Galerina picked up psilocybin biosynthesis by horizontal gene transfer after it was already making amatoxins, and that it acquired psilocybin twice independently — and intriguingly, gaining psilocybin may have coincided with a drop in amatoxin potency. So horizontal gene transfer can weld two potent bioactive systems into one organism and reshape both. It's not a drug-discovery paper, but it's a vivid reminder that the chemical repertoire of living things is a lot more modular and a lot more mixable than tidy phylogenies suggest. That's the show — from a modeling argument that low-dose psilocybin is probably kind to your heart valves, to a mushroom that combined the trip and the poison. Back tomorrow.
