# Receptor & Reason — 2026-08-04

## Script

Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Tuesday, August fourth. Today's lineup runs from a channelopathy that turns two familiar drugs into candidate blockers, through a pair of papers converging on the same addiction phenotype from opposite directions, into a couple of computational drug-discovery pieces worth arguing about, and it closes with a songbird that may help us tell aging apart from Parkinson's by voice alone. Ten items. Let's get into it.

We open with a nice piece of pharmacology-meets-structure on GIRK2, the G-protein-gated inwardly rectifying potassium channel encoded by KCNJ6 that sets neuronal excitability. A patient carrying a rare variant, a glycine-to-cysteine swap at position one-fifty-four, presented with mild features of Keppen-Lubinsky syndrome — which is itself interesting, because the classic selectivity-filter mutations at neighboring residues cause severe disease. The group combined molecular dynamics with patch-clamp electrophysiology and found the variant widens the selectivity filter, so the channel loses its potassium selectivity, starts letting sodium through, and loses inward rectification — a clear gain-of-function, and mechanistically a cousin of the old weaver mouse mutation one residue over. The part that earns this the lead spot is what they did next: an in-silico plus electrophysiological screen of FDA-approved compounds turned up nefazodone, an antidepressant, and eletriptan, a triptan used for migraine, as potent blockers of both wild-type and mutant GIRK2 — and through distinct mechanisms, with eletriptan behaving like a voltage-dependent intracellular pore blocker because it carries a positive charge. So you get a rare-variant channelopathy, a crisp biophysical explanation for why it's pathogenic, and two already-approved drugs as starting points for repurposing. That's the whole arc from mutation to candidate therapeutic in one preprint.

From a single channel to the circuits of addiction, and here I want to pair two independent preprints that landed in the same window and, read together, are stronger than either alone. Both are about punishment-resistant reward-seeking — the willingness to keep pursuing a reward despite a paired aversive consequence, which maps closely onto the diagnostic core of addiction. The first tackles a long-standing observation that this phenotype is less prominent in females than males. Chronic estradiol manipulation made female mice more punishment-resistant, but — and this is the twist — not by boosting dopamine peaks on rewarded actions, which is what a naive reward-learning account would predict. Instead it exaggerated dopamine dips on unrewarded actions. And when they optogenetically mimicked those dips, they accelerated punishment resistance in both sexes. The interpretation is that the dips suppress learning from unrewarded actions, which fits a policy-based view of dopamine rather than a pure reward-prediction-error one. It's a reminder that the sign and timing of dopamine transients carry different computational jobs, and that a hormone can retune one without touching the other.

The companion paper comes at the same phenotype from developmental stress rather than hormones. Chronic unpredictable stress during adolescence — a known lifelong risk factor for addiction — produced a durable increase in punishment-resistant reward-seeking in mice, and they traced it to a specific circuit. A subset of central amygdala neurons projecting to the substantia nigra pars lateralis becomes persistently hyperexcitable, and that disinhibits dopamine release in the tail of the striatum, newly recruiting that region into punishment-resistant behavior. Normalizing the amygdala hyperexcitability, or optogenetically counteracting the excess tail-of-striatum dopamine, prevented the phenotype. So one paper says exaggerated dopamine dips write in punishment resistance, the other says recruiting a normally-latent amygdala-to-nigra-to-striatum dopamine pathway does it — different entry points, both landing on aberrant striatal dopamine as the lever. If you work on addiction vulnerability, these two belong side by side on your desk.

Staying in the striatum but switching neuromodulators, there's a clean computational study on acetylcholine's role in reversal learning. Dopamine gets most of the attention, but striatal acetylcholine is also modulated by reward-predicting cues and outcomes, and its actual computation has stayed murky. Working in dorsomedial striatum, the group used genetic and physiologically-guided optogenetic tools: blocking phasic acetylcholine modulation impaired reversal learning, while enhancing it specifically during non-rewarded trials made reversals faster. Trial-by-trial dynamics were best captured by a reinforcement-learning model in which the animal builds a new latent state when experience is poorly explained by existing states — acetylcholine, in this framing, signals when to spin up a new state rather than keep updating an old one. And they earned real credit by testing a counterintuitive prediction from that model: if acetylcholine's job is to resolve ambiguity about whether a reward was truly omitted, then making the omission explicit should reduce the need for it. They added an auditory omission cue, and it substantially rescued the reversal deficit caused by genetic acetylcholine knockdown. That's a model that predicted a non-obvious behavioral rescue and got it — the strongest kind of evidence for a computational account.

Next, a circuit for avoidance under chronic threat, which is squarely relevant to anxiety and stress disorders. Using predator odor as an innate, persistent stressor, the group homed in on a population in the lateral septum that integrates threat and sets how long an animal waits before exploring. Calcium imaging plus activity-based transcriptomics showed these predator-responsive septal neurons are GABAergic and express neurotensin, and single-nucleus sequencing confirmed neurotensin-enriched inhibitory clusters dominate the responsive population. Chronically activating these neurotensin neurons produced avoidance even with no predator around, and silencing them abolished predator-enhanced avoidance. So neurotensin signaling in the lateral septum acts as a brake on exploratory drive that gets engaged by sustained threat — a concrete, genetically-defined handle on a behavior that's exaggerated across many neuropsychiatric conditions, and a reminder that neurotensin, an underused peptide system, keeps showing up at the intersection of stress and motivation.

From a defined peptidergic population to an orphan receptor, there's a characterization of ADGRD1 — also called GPR133 — one of the adhesion GPCRs. This is a Gs-coupled orphan that raises cAMP, expressed in neural tissue with fetal-brain splice variants, and largely unstudied in the CNS. Knocking it out in mice gave a selective behavioral signature: reduced nest-building and lower exploratory drive, but intact motor coordination and recognition memory. Electrophysiology showed a trend toward impaired long-term potentiation, and — the more striking finding — the knockouts were markedly more susceptible to kainate-induced excitotoxicity. Transcriptomics pointed to coordinated shifts in inhibitory signaling, extracellular matrix, and cytoskeletal genes, consistent with reduced synaptic stabilization and a more vulnerable hippocampus. Adhesion GPCRs are a frontier for CNS drug discovery precisely because so many are orphans with unclear ligands; putting a behavioral and vulnerability phenotype on GPR133 is a useful step toward taking it seriously as a target.

Then a mechanistic neurodevelopmental piece on Rett syndrome that reframes it partly as an astrocyte lipid problem. Rett is caused by loss-of-function mutations in MECP2, and astrocyte dysfunction is increasingly implicated through non-cell-autonomous effects. Astrocytes are the brain's main cholesterol source, supplying neurons via ApoE-containing lipoproteins. In Mecp2 knockout astrocytes, the group found reduced nuclear SREBP2 — the master transcriptional regulator of cholesterol synthesis — along with downregulated cholesterol biosynthesis and transport genes, intracellular accumulation of cholesterol and its precursor desmosterol, lower ABCA1, and defective ApoE lipidation even though ApoE itself was still expressed and secreted. In other words, the astrocytes make and ship ApoE but fail to load it properly with cholesterol, starving neurons of the lipid they need to mature synapses. It's a specific, druggable-looking node — the SREBP2-to-ABCA1-to-ApoE-lipidation axis — in a disorder where cell-autonomous neuronal fixes have been hard, and it argues for looking at the astrocyte compartment.

Now two computational drug-discovery pieces, starting with one that takes a swing at a real credibility problem in the field. Virtual screening should let you triage tens of billions of compounds, yet it's rarely the primary hit-discovery strategy, and the authors argue that's because the methods genuinely underperform: classical docking generalizes but is limited by crude scoring functions, while recent machine-learning docking is expressive but doesn't transfer to novel molecules and pockets, with reported accuracy often inflated by train-test leakage. Their answer is a physics-plus-machine-learning hybrid — DODock and DOScore — meant to stay expressive while generalizing out of distribution. The evidence is prospective, which is what matters here. A blind prediction of a drug candidate binding PCSK9 recovered the later-solved crystal pose to one-point-two angstroms. And they ran live screening campaigns against four structurally distinct targets — the ectoenzyme CD73, the kinase IRAK4, the protease Factor eleven, and the protein-protein interface of IL-seventeen — pulling out chemically novel, biochemically and cellularly active inhibitors, with roughly a hundredfold better hit rate than a recent machine-learning screen in one case. The healthy skepticism to keep: this comes from an industry group with patent filings on the very inhibitors, so independent replication matters. But prospective wins with a solved crystal structure as ground truth are exactly the currency this field has been short on.

The second computational piece is about interpretability in receptor pharmacology, using the angiotensin II type-one receptor as the testbed — not a CNS target, but the method generalizes to any GPCR you care about. Allosteric modulators are attractive for selectivity, but finding them and mapping how they work is hard, and most molecular-dynamics or machine-learning approaches are correlation-driven black boxes. The group built an interpretable probabilistic model — a Bayesian network over residue-level interaction energies extracted from MD ensembles — that identifies local and long-range energetic couplings and traces the communication pathways from the ligand pocket to the G-protein interface. They then prioritized those pathways with whole-receptor alanine scanning plus in-silico deep mutational scanning, which flagged residues whose importance only shows up under specific substitutions and surfaced a cryptic intracellular pocket rich in communication hubs. And they closed the loop: structure-based screening against that predicted pocket yielded a small, fragment-like negative allosteric modulator, dubbed Q-two, that dampens angiotensin-driven Gq signaling. An interpretable allostery map that actually delivers a functional modulator is a template worth borrowing for the receptors this audience cares more about.

We'll build down with the lightest item, though it's a clever one. Aging and early Parkinson's produce similar voice changes in people, which makes differential diagnosis hard, and the brain's contribution to that vocal decline is murky. So this group turned to the zebra finch, whose song is a well-controlled, brain-driven vocal behavior, and applied the same acoustic analysis toolkit used on human speech. Across two open datasets, aging birds showed non-linear trajectories — middle-aged birds actually differing from both younger and older ones in fundamental-frequency measures, with age-related increases in spectral slope and intensity variability but stable timing. In the Parkinson's arm, birds given alpha-synuclein overexpression in the song nucleus Area X showed larger changes in pitch variability, declining frequency modulation, and vocal intensity than controls. The promise is a tractable animal model where you can dissociate the acoustic fingerprints of normal aging from those of synuclein pathology — and, further out, a hint that the right voice features might do some of that separating in humans too.

That's the show for today — a channelopathy repurposing story, two roads into punishment-resistant reward-seeking, acetylcholine as a state-construction signal, a neurotensin brake on exploration, an orphan adhesion GPCR earning a phenotype, an astrocyte-lipid angle on Rett, two computational drug-discovery reality checks, and birdsong as a window onto Parkinson's.
