# Receptor & Reason — 2026-10-27

## Script

Good morning. Today's thread is a quiet one, and once you see it you can't stop seeing it: the number that was never measured. Not a wrong number — an absent one, standing exactly where the load-bearing claim sits. A potency that's quoted but never derived. A dose that lives in a supplement nobody posted. Event counts that appear nowhere in a thirty-page pharmacoepidemiology paper. A variance-explained that's simply not reported. In every case the paper is otherwise careful, the statistics are run, and the sentence everything hangs on is the one with no measurement behind it.

We open with an impressive piece of platform pharmacology out of Copenhagen, with the Luxembourg Institute of Health, and I want to give it its due before taking it apart. They adapted RaPID — random nonstandard peptide integrated discovery, which is messenger-RNA display married to flexizyme codon reprogramming — to a full-length class A GPCR held in a lipid nanodisc. The chemistry is elegant: the start codon is recoded to chloroacetyl-D-phenylalanine, which cyclizes onto an encoded cysteine, so every one of roughly ten trillion library members comes out as a thioether macrocycle. The target was wild-type human CXCR4, and from under a hundred micrograms of receptor in under two weeks they pulled eleven unique binders with no sequence homology to any chemokine or to motixafortide.

The pharmacology is real. The best peptide displaces fluorescent CXCL12 in cells at twenty-six nanomolar, against forty-six for the chemokine itself and fifty-eight for AMD3100 run in the same assay — that's a clean, well-controlled number. And "diverse pharmacology" turns out to mean something interesting: seven of ten peptides are full beta-arrestin agonists at ACKR3, the atypical scavenger receptor, while antagonizing CXCR4. Two are ACKR3-selective agonists with no CXCR4 activity at all. One macrocycle library, one selection, and you get functional switching across a receptor pair.

Now the absences. The paper's translational sentence says treatment at the lead peptide's IC-ninety — fifty-eight nanomolar — should antagonize CXCR4 without off-target function anywhere else. That IC-ninety is never derived. Set it against the paper's own beta-arrestin IC-fifty of fifty-four nanomolar and you need a Hill slope around thirty-one, which no dose-response curve in the paper supports. And the selectivity being certified at fifty-eight nanomolar was measured at five micromolar — about eighty-six-fold higher — in a single-concentration, single-transducer panel with no dose-response for any off-target. Two more absences. Inverse agonism is in a section heading, but it's read off the lower asymptote of an antagonist curve run against ten nanomolar CXCL12 — no unstimulated baseline, no constitutively active mutant, no neutral-antagonist reference. And the binding-mode figure is contact-counting on six single AlphaFold-3 models, from a group that co-authored the benchmark showing AlphaFold-3 peptide-GPCR docking is unreliable. The platform is the finding here. Treat the potency table as a starting point, not a certificate.

Staying on receptors, and on missing numbers in a much more literal sense. A preprint on acute social defeat reports that a single defeat session drives cannabinoid type one receptor-dependent synaptic depression in the projection from anterior ventral subiculum to anterior hypothalamic nucleus, and that this is what produces social avoidance. The design has genuinely nice elements: optogenetic-assisted slice recording with paired-pulse ratio as a presynaptic readout, chemogenetics addressed to the pathway two independent ways, a negative control pathway from posterior ventral subiculum that stays flat, and — best of all — behavior and slice physiology in the same animals. Bath corticosterone reproduces the depression, AM251 blocks it, the CB1 agonist ACEA reproduces it, systemic mifepristone before defeat prevents it, and local AM251 into the hypothalamic target prevents both the synaptic change and the behavior.

That's a coherent glucocorticoid-to-endocannabinoid story. Here's the problem: there is not a single dose, concentration, volume or route anywhere in the document. The Methods are a stub pointing at a supplement that isn't posted, and that supplement also holds every test statistic. So the pharmacological claim — that this is CB1, at a concentration where CB1 is what you're hitting — is currently unauditable, which matters especially for AM251, a compound with a well-known habit of acquiring off-target effects as you climb. Two things to carry regardless. The CB1 dependence is regional, not pathway-specific: a cannula into the anterior hypothalamic nucleus reaches CB1 on every terminal in the neighborhood. And the sufficiency arm is seven cells from four mice, with cells treated as the unit of analysis throughout, which is what's producing those p-values. Males only, no justification, no limitations paragraph, while the paper the authors cite for subicular stress plasticity has "sex-specific" in its title.

Which brings us to a paper where the missing measurement is the entire premise. The Wesson lab at Florida has built the first intranasal opioid self-delivery model in mice — a surgically implanted nasal port through the nasal bone, delivering two microlitres every fifteen seconds onto the turbinates. The motivation is that insufflation is how a very large share of human opioid use actually happens, and that the route should be faster than the subcutaneous and intraperitoneal dosing the entire preclinical literature is built on. Dual-colour photometry in medial nucleus accumbens shell combines a red dopamine sensor with deltaLight, a delta-opioid-receptor-based sensor, and the behavioral package is nice: Straub tail latency of about five hundred and thirteen seconds for intranasal morphine against a hundred and eighteen for low-dose fentanyl, respiratory depression within two minutes of high-dose fentanyl, and sniffing restored eighty-five seconds after intranasal naloxone.

The premise is speed, and there is no pharmacokinetics in the paper. No plasma levels, no brain levels, no time-to-peak, and no within-study intraperitoneal or subcutaneous comparison arm. The route comparison is entirely against numbers cited from other labs — and their own morphine Straub latency, eight and a half minutes, is actually slower than the subcutaneous literature they cite, while the Discussion describes it as under three minutes. Two further things. The opioid sensor's analysis of variance is null on every term — condition, time, interaction — while the abstract states that both drugs increased accumbens opioid dynamics. And one four-hundred-and-five-nanometre control channel is subtracted from both a green and a red sensor, which is not the isosbestic point for the red one, in animals that are hyperlocomoting on fentanyl and recumbent in overdose, with no motion regression and no sensor-null cohort. The model is a real contribution and the respiratory data are the strongest part. The route claim needs the pharmacokinetics that aren't there.

Still on opioids, and now a paper where the missing quantity is the causal arm itself. A group at MIT reports that fentanyl acutely activates paraventricular CRH neurons and raises ACTH — three-ninety-seven versus two-thirty-four picograms per millilitre — and then, over days, suppresses that drive and physically shrinks the adrenal. Four days of fentanyl at zero-point-three milligrams per kilogram costs about a quarter of adrenal volume, and the ACTH stimulation test afterwards is flat: the saline corticosterone-versus-time slope is significantly positive, the fentanyl slope is minus zero-point-zero-zero-two with an R-squared of essentially zero. Transcriptomics puts about three-quarters of the signal in zona fasciculata, with collagen and extracellular-matrix genes up. Peripheral-restricted naloxone fails to rescue the adrenal while brain-penetrant naloxone succeeds, which locates the effect centrally. That's a good package.

Then the other half. They give ACTH for four days, enlarge the adrenal, and fentanyl seeking goes up about a hundred and forty percent under both effort and footshock. The title says adrenal remodeling encodes stress-axis memory to shape seeking. But there is no adrenalectomy, no corticosterone clamp, no glucocorticoid receptor antagonist — nothing that tests the glucocorticoid arm. Systemic ACTH is the manipulation, and melanocortin receptors are not confined to the adrenal; the authors concede ACTH may hit other tissues relevant to motivation. And the fentanyl-atrophy animals were never run on the behavior, so the two halves of the title never meet in a single experiment. The corticosterone headline for the ACTH arm rests on three mice. The behavioral F-statistic has seventy-six denominator degrees of freedom across two groups over six days, which implies roughly seven animals per group with each animal's six days counted as independent — and that pseudoreplication is what produces the p-less-than-point-oh-oh-oh-one.

From opioids to the other addiction pharmacotherapy story of the year. A Bristol and Bath group has the systematic review of GLP-1 receptor agonists for tobacco and alcohol dependence — fourteen randomized trials, eleven hundred and forty-nine participants, liraglutide, semaglutide, exenatide and dulaglutide, all placebo-controlled, preregistered. The honest summary is that almost everything is null. All four smoking estimates cross one: cessation odds ratio one-point-three-nine, cigarettes per day minus zero-point-two-one, Fagerström score minus zero-point-zero-eight. On alcohol, the all-agent estimates for grams per day, AUDIT score, craving, heavy drinking days and drinks per week all include no difference. Exactly one all-agent outcome clears zero, and it's days without alcohol — a count of self-reported days.

The positives are a semaglutide-only cluster, and here's the construct problem: the semaglutide trials are also the alcohol-use-disorder trials, so a drug effect and a population effect are not separable at k equals two or three. Ten of fourteen trials recruited metabolic or obesity populations, and only three of eight smoking trials and three of eleven alcohol trials enrolled anyone actually seeking treatment; restrict to treatment-seekers and the confidence intervals open back up to null. One trial carries about two-thirds of the pooled smoking evidence. And the detail I'd put on a slide: the two outcomes measured with validated instruments — AUDIT and Fagerström — are the two flattest results in the paper. The responsive measures are the unvalidated ones. The authors' own line is the right one: therapeutic potential, but this does not establish a role in addiction treatment, and most analyses did not meet the optimal information size. Thirty trials are ongoing.

Which sets up a paper getting at the same drugs from the other end. AskGLP1, from Arizona and Vanderbilt, screens eight and a half million Reddit posts down to a hundred and sixty thousand, extracts drug–side-effect and drug–purpose relations with a frontier model, normalizes side effects to MedDRA, and builds a quote-grounded knowledge graph with an agent on top. Some of the descriptive output is genuinely useful: nausea in twenty-nine percent of tirzepatide side-effect posts versus thirty-eight percent for semaglutide. But the source comparison is the real contribution — fatigue appears in fourteen percent of Reddit posts and sixteen percent of WebMD reviews but three-point-four percent of FAERS reports, while elevated blood glucose is six percent of FAERS and four-hundredths of a percent of Reddit. Reddit and WebMD rank side effects at a Spearman rho of zero-point-nine-two; either against FAERS is zero-point-two to zero-point-three. Different instruments pointed at different people, and the paper is right to say so.

The headline agent number is where it goes circular. Ninety-one percent no-major-error versus forty-one percent for the keyword baseline — but the reference answers are computed by SQL over the same knowledge graph the agent is querying, while the baseline is scored against a database it cannot see. The authors say plainly that this measures whether answers represent the underlying data, not whether the data are right. The metric that isn't reference-dependent doesn't separate the systems at all: claims backed by cited evidence, eighty-four percent versus seventy-seven, confidence interval minus five to plus seventeen. And the number I'd keep: on the "why do people take it" half of the graph — forty-four thousand edges — precision is zero-point-five-eight on twenty-eight items, and the two human annotators agree with each other at an F1 of zero-point-two-one. That half of the resource rests on a judgment humans essentially cannot replicate.

Now a pharmacoepidemiology paper from Pittsburgh with an unusually clean design and one conspicuous hole. Forty-two thousand autistic new users of antidepressants in a US electronic-health-record network, active-comparator design against SSRIs, overlap-weighted Cox models, covariate balance after weighting inside a standardized mean difference of zero-point-zero-seven-five. The findings: atypicals versus SSRIs, hazard ratio zero-point-seven-nine for suicide-related events; tricyclics zero-point-four-nine; SNRIs flat at zero-point-nine-five. Agent-specific, trazodone is zero-point-six-five and carries the whole atypical class.

The hole is that absolute event counts appear nowhere. No events, no person-time, no incidence rates, no effective sample sizes in the main text. The tricyclic estimate comes from one thousand seven hundred and eighty initiators under as-treated follow-up often measured in weeks, and the authors concede a limited number of events without printing it. You cannot tell whether the headline rests on forty events or four hundred. And the threat the design doesn't address is surveillance bias rather than confounding by indication: the outcome is a billing-code composite dominated by suicidal ideation, which is recorded only if somebody asks. Trazodone is sixty percent of the atypical class and is largely low-dose insomnia prescribing, often outside psychiatric care, where nobody is asking. The paper names the indication version of this problem and never tests the documentation version. Six sensitivity analyses, all varying time windows; no negative-control outcome, no negative-control exposure, no E-value. The age interaction is significant dichotomized at eighteen, not significant linear, and nowhere near significant as a spline.

Related, and with a different missing number. A seven-cohort consortium asks whether polygenic liability for depression, schizophrenia and bipolar disorder predicts treatment outcome — four thousand European-ancestry patients from STAR-D, CATIE, STEP-BD and others. The depression score gives an odds ratio of zero-point-eight-eight per standard deviation for good outcome, which fails false-discovery correction at q equals zero-point-one-one; schizophrenia and bipolar scores are null. No R-squared, no AUC, no variance-explained is reported for any association anywhere in the paper — and from the continuous beta of minus zero-point-zero-eight you can back out roughly six-tenths of one percent of outcome variance. The authors do say the effect is too small to guide an individual patient, which is the right call.

The construct point is sharper than the statistics. There is no drug-specific contrast anywhere — no comparator arm, no score-by-treatment interaction, no within-cohort drug stratification even in CATIE, where randomization actually exists. Treatment is perfectly confounded with cohort. And two of the seven outcomes aren't response at all; they're illness course — percent days well, and a lifetime course code. Drop the course cohorts and the signal weakens, to an odds ratio of zero-point-nine-zero at p equals zero-point-one-three. So the most defensible reading is the one the authors offer in their own mechanistic gloss: a disposition to recurrence that limits the benefit of any treatment. That's prognosis, not pharmacogenomics, and it's worth being precise about which one you're funding.

On to subtyping, where the missing number is a null model. A large Chinese consortium built pediatric cortical-thickness normative charts from sixty-five hundred typically developing children and clustered the deviation maps of five hundred and eighty-seven children with ADHD into two biotypes — two hundred and thirty-eight with widespread thinner-than-expected cortex, three hundred and forty-nine thicker. Effect sizes against controls run to Cohen's d of zero-point-six-four, and the pooled case-control contrast is null, which is a nice demonstration that group averaging hides heterogeneity.

But nothing clinical separates the two groups — not age, not sex, not IQ, not symptoms, not cognition. The separation lives entirely in in-sample partial-least-squares fits. And because each biotype is defined by the sign of a near-global deviation, the partition is close to a split on mean Z across two hundred and nineteen regions — exactly the axis residual global-thickness or site variance would occupy if the hierarchical site term didn't fully absorb it. There is no clustering null anywhere: no gap statistic against a unimodal alternative, no control clustering, and the cross-cohort replication is a fresh de novo clustering in a different dataset yielding a spatial correlation of zero-point-three to zero-point-four-six between two maps that each mostly encode that same offset. The therapeutic claim — methylphenidate versus atomoxetine responding differently by biotype — is a non-randomized sample of a hundred and twenty-eight children split four ways, roughly thirty per cell, interaction p of zero-point-zero-two uncorrected across six tests, inattention outcome null. And the "molecular profiles" are imaging-transcriptomics against six adult post-mortem donors, which can show a spatial correlation with an adult expression gradient and cannot show gene dysregulation in these children.

Two short ones to close, both about evaluating language models, both with the same shape. The first is a lovely little experiment on medical vision-language models. Take sixty image-dependent board-exam questions, then replace the image with a blank white rectangle of identical dimensions, and apply social pressure — a fabricated consensus of three specialists naming a specific wrong option. The model flips to the argued option on ninety-four percent of real-image items and ninety-eight percent of blank-image items, a difference of three-and-a-half points with a confidence interval spanning zero. All hundred and ten first-turn flips land on exactly the option that was argued for. The model notices the image is blank at baseline on thirty-five of sixty items and answers anyway on twenty-nine of those.

The headline reading is that verdict stability doesn't track visual grounding, so you can't use consistency as a grounding detector — and that's right. But the missing number is the ceiling. The primary comparison is restricted to items the model answers correctly without the image, so the "grounded" arm is the set where grounding was demonstrably unnecessary; only four items actually require the image. And with the real-image arm at ninety-four percent, there are at most five-and-a-half points of headroom, against the paper's own turn-zero reproducibility floor of five to eight percent. The mechanical noise exceeds the maximum detectable effect, and everything rests on two discordant items. A strategy of "always flip to whatever was argued" scores ninety-four to ninety-eight here. That's the real finding, and it's a scarier one than the title.

And finally, the closer, which should interest anyone letting a research agent write their background section. A group at NYU defines "claim smoothing" — a conclusion stated more strongly or broadly than the cited evidence supports — and builds an automated pipeline to score it across seven deep-research systems on about fifty-five hundred claims. The engagement numbers alone are worth the paper: one system opens ninety-five of four hundred and ninety-six pages it cites, another fetches thirty-four of fourteen hundred and fifty-eight, and forty-five of sixty-five reports open no cited page at all. Mean smoothing scores run from one-point-seven to three-point-three on a five-point scale, with the worst system materially overstating eighty-five to ninety-three percent of its claims. And the intervention works: handing the model the documents plus calibration instructions drops one system from one-point-nine-five to one-point-five-two.

The missing number is in the judge. It never sees the documents. It scores each claim against an evidence set assembled by a smaller model, half of it gathered by an agent explicitly told to find counter-evidence — and that skeptic's haul scales with how many documents were cited. Across systems, citation count correlates with smoothing at r equals zero-point-five-nine. The worst-performing system cites thirty-one to forty-nine documents per report; the best cites nine to fifteen. The authors report that correlation and don't deflate the headline for it. Validation is against two of the authors, at fifty-six percent exact agreement — which is roughly what the two authors achieve against each other, so the judge is human-level and humans aren't very good at this. And the judge's deviation from the human sign-flips by vendor: it scores one lab's system below the human rating and two others above. There is no limitations section.

So: the potency never derived, the dose never posted, the event count never printed, the variance never reported, the null model never run, the trivial baseline never scored, the judge never shown the evidence. None of these papers is bad; most are better than average. The habit I'd suggest is mechanical. When you hit the sentence a paper is actually for — the one that would change what you do on Monday — find the number in it and ask where it was measured. Often the answer is a different experiment at a different concentration, or nowhere. Thanks for listening, and I'll see you tomorrow.
