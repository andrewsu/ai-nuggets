We open with a target-discovery framework that is honest about exactly how far it has gotten. A small company called Exin Therapeutics has built something they call CircuitATLAS — a knowledge graph assembled from the neuroscience literature, close to four million nodes and seven and a half million edges, most of them extracted by a language model rather than hand-curated. The design choice that matters is what they left out: no direct disease-to-gene or disease-to-protein edges. The idea is that if you let the agent reason straight from a disease label to a gene, it will reproduce whatever the field already believes is the culprit gene, which is the opposite of discovery. Instead the agent has to reason through intermediate layers — phenotype, to circuit, to cell type, to molecular effector — the same chain a systems neuroscientist would walk by hand, just at a scale no one person could cover.

The test case is cortical excitability. The agent nominated A-T-P-one-A-three, a sodium-potassium pump expressed in a specific class of cortical interneuron, as a control point — not because it's mutated in any disease, but because of where it sits in the circuit. They then ran the experiment: deleting that pump specifically in interneurons abolished the beta and gamma frequency response to a focal dose of four-aminopyridine, a drug that blocks potassium channels and is used experimentally to push cortical tissue into a hyperexcitable, almost seizure-like state. The deletion worked, in vivo, and the finding was carried forward into an actual small-molecule chemistry campaign. That is a real validated hit, and it's a nice demonstration of the core idea: you don't have to intervene on what's broken in a disease to fix the circuit — you can intervene on whatever happens to control it.

Here's the honest caveat, and credit to the authors for stating it themselves: this is one case study. The paper says directly that it has not established a success rate, has not been compared against an expert human target-discovery process or a simpler computational baseline, and has not shown it can predict actual therapeutic efficacy. One validated target from one company's internal pipeline is a proof of concept, not evidence the approach generalizes. Worth watching for a prospective, pre-registered test of the same system on a target nobody already suspected.

That same question — does the agent actually know what it's talking about, or is it pattern-matching — is the entire premise of the next paper. A Stanford group built something called DrugTargetWorld, which solves a real problem in this space: you can't benchmark an AI agent's ability to find disease-causing proteins using a real biobank, because in a real biobank you never actually know the ground truth of what's causal versus merely correlated. So they built synthetic biobanks instead — fifty-four thousand simulated patients each, with genotypes, proteins, health records, and for a subset, synthetic brain and body imaging, all generated from a causal model where the experimenters know, by construction, exactly which proteins drive the simulated disease and which ones just look associated with it because of confounding.

They ran nine different AI agents, including ones built on two current frontier models, across five hundred forty separate simulated worlds focused on cardiovascular disease. The best agents recovered about sixty-four percent of the true causal drivers on average — which sounds respectable until you see the other number: on a hundred-point composite score that also credits getting the direction of modulation right and correctly flagging confounded, non-causal proteins, the best agent scored under forty. No agent reliably told apart a protein that causes disease from one that's just a bystander riding along with it. That's the actual bottleneck in automated target discovery, and it's not a solved problem — this benchmark exists specifically because nobody could measure it properly before.

From target discovery to molecule design, same mismatch between reasoning and execution. A group spanning Shanghai Jiao Tong's agriculture and computer science departments built an agent for antimicrobial peptide design that's explicit about a gap most of these systems paper over: language models can read the literature and form a strategy, but they can't directly edit a protein sequence, and the generative models that can edit sequences have no way to incorporate what the literature says. Their system, called EASER, bridges the two with a set of small, trait-specific adapter modules — think of them as dials, one per property — that steer a sequence-generating diffusion model. The agent proposes a hypothesis grounded in retrieved evidence, specifying which part of the peptide to edit and in which direction, tests it, and revises based on what came back.

Tested on designing antimicrobial peptides that have to satisfy three things at once — potency, low toxicity to human cells, and low tendency to rupture red blood cells — the explicit-hypothesis version beat direct sequence generation by a wide margin on a combined quality score, roughly twenty-five to fifty percent higher depending on the comparison generator, averaged across six independent runs. The ablations are the useful part here: strip out the literature retrieval, or the memory of past attempts, or the reflection step, and performance drops each time, which at least tells you the pieces are doing real work rather than window dressing.

Closing out today's agentic-AI cluster with something more modest and more immediately useful. A bioinformatics lab in Corfu built Enrich.AI, a conversational assistant for gene set enrichment analysis — the step where you take a list of genes from your experiment and try to figure out what biological story they tell. The real-world pain point is that there are hundreds of reference gene-set libraries and no good way for a non-expert to know which ones are relevant to their question, while an expert has to manually cross-reference several databases and tens of millions of papers to write up the interpretation. Enrich.AI runs on a reason-and-act loop — it decides at each step whether to query one of two hundred twenty gene-set libraries, search the literature through Europe PMC's forty-million-plus paper index, or pull a gene annotation, and it produces a written interpretation grounded in what it actually retrieved, plus six configurable visualizations. Nothing here is conceptually new, but gluing literature retrieval directly to statistical enrichment output, with the citations attached, is exactly the kind of unglamorous tool that saves real time if it holds up outside its own demo.

Switching gears entirely, now to the question of what's actually broken when we say someone has poor executive function. A review out of a UK and Netherlands collaboration in Molecular Psychiatry makes a case for executive functions — the cognitive machinery for goal-directed control — as a transdiagnostic endophenotype, meaning a heritable, measurable thread running underneath most forms of childhood and adolescent psychopathology rather than a symptom specific to any one diagnosis. The useful move in this review isn't the transdiagnostic framing itself, which others have argued before; it's the reframing of what executive function actually is. The standard view treats it as a capacity — you have more or less of it, like a tank that's full or empty. These authors argue it's better understood as a cost-benefit computation: your brain is constantly deciding whether a given mental effort is worth the expected payoff, and what looks like impaired executive function is often that computation going wrong, not the underlying capacity being damaged. Anhedonia and apathy, on this account, aren't separate problems sitting next to an executive function deficit — they're the mechanism that distorts the cost-benefit math and produces what looks like reduced executive function from the outside. If that's right, it explains something that's bothered the field for a while: why executive-function training programs, which drill the cognitive machinery directly, have mostly failed to move psychiatric symptoms. You'd be training a system that was never actually broken.

That cost-benefit framing pairs naturally with a concrete risk-stratification result, also in Molecular Psychiatry, from two linked Australian cohorts. The question was whether a polygenic risk score for depression adds anything once you already know a kid has elevated internalizing symptoms — anxiety, low mood, somatic complaints — at age fourteen. On its own, landing in the top ten percent of internalizing symptoms at fourteen roughly doubled the odds of meeting criteria for major depression by around age thirty. Landing in the top decile of the polygenic score on its own did a bit better, a bit over three-fold. Combine both — high symptoms and high genetic score — and the odds jumped to roughly eight-and-a-half-fold, with felt prevalence in that group around sixty-five percent actually going on to develop major depression, against a baseline near ten percent in the lowest-risk group. The authors frame this correctly as a clinical-utility argument, not a screening argument: the score isn't being proposed to flag the general population, it's being proposed to refine risk estimates in kids who are already presenting with symptoms. The number worth holding onto with some skepticism is how few people actually landed in that highest-risk cell — only twenty-three individuals out of twelve hundred in the primary cohort were in the top decile on both measures simultaneously, which is the entire basis for the headline eight-fold estimate. The direction is almost certainly right, given it replicates in the second cohort, but that confidence interval is wide for a reason.

Staying with genetics, but turning from risk to a much older and messier question — whether creativity and mental illness actually share a cause, or whether that's folklore dressed up as an association. A Swedish team used their twin registry, nearly fifteen thousand twins with a genotyped subsample, and threw a wide net of creativity measures at the problem: artistic and scientific achievement, creative occupations, the personality trait openness, and direct tests of divergent thinking. The headline finding is a split nobody usually makes explicit: artistic creativity — achievement, artistic occupations, and openness — was consistently associated with worse mental health, including psychosis proneness, depression, and anxiety. Scientific and occupational creativity showed the opposite pattern, flat or even slightly protective. Co-twin control models, which let you separate a shared family or genetic effect from something that's actually acting within one twin and not the other, pointed mostly toward shared genetic factors explaining the artistic-creativity association — meaning it's not that being creative makes you mentally ill, or that mental illness makes you creative, but that some of the same genetic variation nudges both. The one exception, which survived even the strictest longitudinal co-twin design, was a plausible causal path from depressive symptoms toward higher openness over time — the only piece of this whole picture that looks like it might run in a specific direction rather than just reflecting shared genetic background. So: the romantic version of this story is mostly wrong, it's domain-specific, and most of what looks like a creativity-madness link is confounding, not causation.

From genetic confounding to a structural question in addiction, and a finding that argues against treating substance use disorders as interchangeable. A two-site diffusion imaging study compared white matter microstructure across people with cannabis, opioid, or nicotine dependence against matched controls — eighty-seven patients split roughly forty-eight, sixteen, and twenty-three across the three groups. Cannabis and opioid dependence looked remarkably similar to each other: both showed widespread disruption — lower fractional anisotropy, higher diffusivity — across the same fronto-temporo-parietal and limbic pathways, including a tract called the middle longitudinal fasciculus that connects temporal and parietal cortex and is implicated in valuation and cognitive control. Nicotine dependence looked almost nothing like either of them — much more limited changes, confined mostly to anterior and connecting white matter. Only in the cannabis group did the microstructural measure actually predict behavior: more disorganization in that temporo-parietal tract tracked with steeper delay discounting, the tendency to take a smaller reward now over a larger one later, and that relationship was statistically carried by how heavily someone was using, concentrated in daily users. The same brain-behavior link was flatly absent in the opioid group despite similar structural damage. Take the convergence between cannabis and opioid pathways as the more durable result; the opioid arm here is only sixteen people, and this is a single cross-sectional snapshot, so there's no way yet to know whether the white matter changes came before the heavy use or after it.

A shorter item on the imaging-and-computation side before we move to pharmacology proper. Electroencephalogram foundation models — big networks pretrained on raw EEG so they can be reused across different clinical tasks — have mostly chased scale, tens of millions of parameters or more. A University of Illinois and Carle Foundation Hospital team went the other direction: a model with under one and a half million parameters, roughly a fifth the size of the comparison models, trained by having it reconstruct its own masked EEG signal on a large public hospital archive, then frozen and tested cold on two unrelated clinical tasks — flagging abnormal EEGs and recognizing epileptic seizure activity. It held its own against the much larger models on both tasks without any fine-tuning. The result worth remembering isn't the specific accuracy numbers; it's the argument that transferable, clinically useful EEG representations don't require enormous compute, which matters if you ever want this kind of tool running somewhere other than a well-funded academic center.

Three shorter items to close, all clinical pharmacology, all worth thirty seconds each rather than a full segment because each one is a single well-defined question rather than a story with twists.

First, a pharmacogenomics methods comparison for the chemotherapy drug five-fluorouracil, which can cause severe, sometimes fatal toxicity in people with reduced activity of the enzyme that breaks it down, dihydropyrimidine dehydrogenase. In two hundred three patients with full genetic sequencing of the relevant gene, the team compared a direct enzyme-activity assay against a simpler blood-metabolite ratio test for catching people with dangerous variants, and in the process turned up three additional rare variants that current genotyping panels would have missed entirely. The practical message: a phenotypic assay can catch risk that a fixed genotyping panel, by definition, cannot, because it doesn't need to already know which variant to look for.

Second, a modeling paper that puts real numbers on an interaction people have been guessing about. Cannabidiol inhibits several liver enzymes that clear other drugs, and a stepwise pharmacokinetic modeling effort — basic model, then a more mechanistic static model, then a full physiologically based model validated against real patient data — predicted that cannabidiol could raise exposure to sensitive substrates of three major metabolic enzymes by roughly three-and-a-half-fold, four-fold, and just over two-fold respectively. Given how often cannabidiol gets combined with other centrally active drugs without anyone checking for this, having modeled multipliers instead of a vague warning label is useful.

And last, a two-year prospective study of fifty lithium-naive bipolar disorder patients against forty-two healthy controls, tracking inflammatory and metabolic blood proteins before and across lithium treatment. Before anyone had taken a single dose, the bipolar group already showed an elevated baseline signature of several chemokines compared to controls — a reminder that whatever immune dysregulation shows up in bipolar disorder isn't a side effect of the drugs used to treat it, it's there from the start. The cohort is being followed for two years specifically to see whether that signature shifts with treatment and whether it tracks who responds to lithium and who doesn't — that part of the story isn't told yet, but the baseline finding alone is a clean piece of evidence that the field keeps needing restated.
