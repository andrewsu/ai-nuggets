# Receptor & Reason — 2026-08-11

## Script

Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Tuesday, August eleventh. We open with a run of agentic-AI stories — a new way to automate scientific discovery itself, an AI-nominated neuroscience drug target, a medication-safety agent, and a cautionary tale about poisoning peptide generators — before settling into mechanism papers on Alzheimer's, fear memory, and addiction, and closing on psychedelics. Let's get into it.

We start with a paper that isn't about biology at all on its face, but has real teeth for anyone running model-based experiments. A University of British Columbia computer scientist built what he calls the Model Discovery Agent, and the problem it targets is a fundamental one in science: to answer "what would happen if I intervened this way," you need a mechanistic model, not a curve fit, and mechanistic models are only identifiable through experiments, because passive observation can't distinguish between mechanisms that happen to agree on the data you already have. Experiments are expensive, so the real currency is data efficiency. The agent couples a large language model, used purely to propose candidate mechanisms in plain language, with a standard Bayesian toolkit — sequential Monte Carlo to track a posterior over model structure and parameters, simulation-based inference for cases where you can't write down a likelihood, and value-of-information scoring to pick the next experiment to run. When the current best hypothesis fails a predictive check, the language model proposes an expanded hypothesis space, and the value-of-information machinery designs an experiment specifically to pin down the new mechanism — discovery and design reinforcing each other in a loop. The authors validate this on three domains, and the one that matters here is a new benchmark called NeuronBench: six simulated "mystery neurons," each a standard sodium-potassium spiking neuron plus one extra membrane mechanism, deliberately tuned so that textbook stimulation protocols can't tell the mystery neurons apart from a plain one — the agent has to invent its own diagnostic experiments. In a worked example, given only a depolarizing sag and rebound as a clue, the language model proposes four biologically plausible channel candidates; a standard depolarizing pulse leaves all four indistinguishable, but the agent's own designed hyperpolarizing probe nails the correct one in a single shot. Across all three domains, the combined system is substantially more data-efficient than a language model working alone. The obvious caveat: these are simulated neurons with a known ground truth, not real electrophysiology recordings, so this is a benchmark result, not yet a validated lab tool — but as a template for closing the loop between an LLM's prior knowledge and principled experiment design, it's a genuinely useful architecture to watch.

Staying with agentic AI but moving from a benchmark to an actual drug-discovery pipeline, Recursion disclosed that Genentech has advanced the first target from their joint neuroscience collaboration into an early small-molecule discovery program — a real, if early, proof point for AI-nominated neuroscience targets, historically one of the hardest areas to find new biology in. The target came out of a whole-genome CRISPR knockout map built from more than a trillion data points generated from lab-grown, iPSC-derived neurons, the largest such map Recursion has built, with candidates pushed through staged pathway, functional, and disease validation before anything advanced. What's new since earlier updates is that Recursion is now explicit about agentic AI doing real work inside that pipeline: a Target Discovery Agent pairs frontier language-model reasoning with those proprietary multimodal maps to mine them for candidate targets in hours instead of weeks, and sibling drug-design and clinical-strategy agents are already cutting structural-analysis time from four hours to thirty minutes and contributing to faster trial enrollment. The collaboration covers up to forty potential small-molecule programs and has already paid out over two hundred million dollars in upfront and milestone payments. Worth being clear-eyed about the caveat: the target itself is still undisclosed, and this is advancement into early discovery, not a validated clinical candidate — but it is a concrete signal that a computational target-discovery platform can find a starting point in neuroscience that survives experimental scrutiny.

From industry back to a research prototype, but staying on agentic medication safety, a team at Hong Kong University of Science and Technology built a system called ATLAS for a genuinely dangerous gap in LLM-based medication advice: a patient describing one symptom to a chatbot can get a plausible-sounding recommendation that's unsafe once you account for a condition or medication they didn't think to mention — the classic example being someone with unstated kidney disease being told to take an over-the-counter anti-inflammatory. ATLAS asks targeted follow-up questions to build a patient-specific medication-conflict graph from guideline evidence, then runs a risk-first multi-agent policy that screens for contraindications, flags cautions and monitoring needs, and proposes safer alternatives before verifying the final plan. On a benchmark of multimorbid older-adult cases, it beat the strongest proprietary model — Gemini three-point-one Pro — by fifty-four points on a strict joint-correctness metric and about fifteen points on their aggregate safety-reasoning score, with zero unsafe recommendations under automated scoring. A blinded panel of three clinicians reviewing forty cases head-to-head against Gemini rated ATLAS higher on every one of five criteria and flagged an unsafe recommendation in one ATLAS case versus two Gemini cases. Take the clinician numbers with a little salt — forty cases and three reviewers is a modest sample, and their agreement with each other was only moderate — but the structural idea, forcing an agent to actively elicit the information a one-shot query would miss before it gives advice, is the right shape for this problem regardless of the exact margin.

And now the cautionary flip side of agentic biology. A team spanning Michigan Tech, Lehigh, and Kansas State asked whether generative antimicrobial-peptide design models — the kind of tool increasingly used to design new peptide antibiotics — could be deliberately poisoned to hurt one genetic subgroup while looking completely normal to everyone else. The precedent they're drawing on is real: drugs like abacavir cause severe hypersensitivity almost exclusively in carriers of a specific immune-gene variant. They built what they call a Genotypic Trigger, a backdoor that shifts a peptide generator's output toward higher predicted immune-reaction risk specifically for carriers of a chosen HLA allele, while leaving predicted risk for everyone else close to normal. Across several popular peptide-generation models, the attack raised predicted immunogenicity risk for carriers of the targeted allele by more than sevenfold on average, and self-training the poisoned model amplified that further. The unsettling part is what didn't change: antimicrobial potency, general toxicity, and the diversity of generated sequences all stayed close to baseline, meaning the poisoned model sails through the safety screens people actually run. If a model like this ended up on an open-source repository, anyone drawing candidates from it would unknowingly be sampling from a distribution that's quietly loaded against one genetic subgroup — and because that subgroup can be small and underrepresented in general trials, population-averaged safety checks would miss it entirely. It's a proof of concept, not an observed attack in the wild, but it names a blind spot in validation pipelines that's worth taking seriously as these generators get used more.

Staying on that genotype thread but moving from an attack to an honest measurement problem, a benchmarking paper asks how good our computational tools actually are at predicting the effect of pharmacogenomic variants, with a focus on CYP2C9, the enzyme that handles metabolism of a long list of common drugs. Testing a range of methods against variants with either clinical annotations or lab-measured function, the authors find a specific and consistent blind spot: current predictors, including AlphaMissense and recent protein-language-model approaches, are decent at flagging loss-of-function variants that slow drug clearance, but they struggle to distinguish gain-of-function, fast-metabolizer variants from neutral ones — exactly the direction that matters for underdosing risk. A method that folds in protein structure and evolutionary coevolution information, called StructureDCA, came out on top, in some cases agreeing with clinical annotations even better than large-scale lab mutagenesis data does. It's a useful reality check for anyone leaning on off-the-shelf variant-effect predictors for dosing decisions.

Now to a genuinely elegant piece of receptor pharmacology in Alzheimer's disease. Most drug development in AD still chases amyloid, even though Tau pathology tracks cognitive decline more tightly — a recent tally found thirty-two amyloid trials in the clinic against just five for Tau. Carlos Ibáñez's group has been dissecting why by picking apart the p75 neurotrophin receptor, which gets upregulated after neural injury and in Alzheimer's brains and can trigger three separate downstream pathways: NF-kappa-B signaling, a JNK-and-caspase death pathway, and RhoA-ROCK signaling. Generic ROCK inhibitors already reduce Tau phosphorylation, but ROCK does so many other jobs in the body that they're not viable drugs. So instead of blocking the whole pathway, this group engineered mice with surgically precise p75 mutations — one that kills all three downstream arms, one that spares RhoA specifically, and a new mutant they call KKEA that does the opposite: it cuts the receptor's connection to RhoA alone while leaving the other two pathways fully intact. Crossed into a Tau-pathology mouse model, the RhoA-specific-knockout mice improved on every single disease measure the authors tracked — less hyperphosphorylated Tau, less aggregated Tau, less glial activation, less hippocampal shrinkage, less neuron death, and better spatial memory — even with the receptor's other two signaling arms left completely untouched. They backed this up in neuron cultures, where a RhoA activator increased Tau phosphorylation and a RhoA inhibitor, a ROCK inhibitor called fasudil, and a GSK-three-beta inhibitor downstream all reduced it. This is the first genetic, rather than purely pharmacological, demonstration that this one specific arm of p75 signaling is what's driving Tau pathology, which matters because a p75 modulator already reached a phase two trial in Alzheimer's patients with biomarker movement but no significant cognitive benefit — this result gives a much sharper idea of which part of that receptor's signaling a next-generation compound actually needs to hit.

From a receptor mechanism to a receptor-relevant human behavioral trial. A Leuven-based team tested whether sodium butyrate — a short-chain fatty acid that acts as an HDAC inhibitor, opening up chromatin to boost transcription of plasticity genes — can strengthen fear-extinction memory in people. A hundred eighty healthy volunteers went through fear conditioning, learned to extinguish that fear with either a short or a long extinction protocol, and got sodium butyrate or placebo either just before or just after the extinction session. A week later, sodium butyrate reduced the return of fear at retrieval — but only in the group that got the longer extinction training; with the short protocol, the drug did nothing. It also didn't touch reinstatement, the shock-triggered relapse-like return of fear, so this looks like the drug is consolidating what was already adequately learned rather than making someone generally less prone to relapse. There's an interesting wrinkle too: under the long protocol, the drug group actually showed slightly slower physiological extinction learning during the session itself, so whatever sodium butyrate is doing seems to happen after the session, not during it. For exposure-based therapies for anxiety and PTSD, where the whole problem is often that extinction memory doesn't stick, a cheap, already-approved compound that consolidates it is an appealing adjunct — but only if the exposure protocol itself is long enough to give it something to consolidate.

Sticking with a circuit that misfires when things go wrong, but moving to addiction, a team at the Florey Institute in Melbourne went looking for why alcohol use disorder tends to escalate faster in women than men, and found a specific circuit to blame. In mice given binge-like access to alcohol, brain-wide activity mapping showed the basolateral amygdala lighting up specifically in females, confirmed with fiber photometry showing a stronger, more sustained amygdala response right at the onset of drinking in females — a pattern not seen for a sucrose reward control. Globally silencing the amygdala cut both alcohol and sucrose intake in both sexes, a blunt effect you'd expect from disrupting reward circuitry generally. But when they silenced specifically the projection from the amygdala into the core of the nucleus accumbens — the pathway they'd shown was more strongly engaged in females — alcohol intake dropped only in females, with sucrose left alone in either sex. The authors are careful to flag the effect as modest, one contributing circuit among probably several, not the whole explanation for the sex difference. But given that so much of the addiction-circuit literature was mapped in male animals only, a clean, female-specific, alcohol-specific pathway like this is exactly the kind of result that a male-only screen would simply never have surfaced.

And to close, a psychedelics study that complicates a nice, simple story. Psilocybin is often described as boosting empathy, but a Maastricht-led placebo-controlled trial breaks empathy into three separable pieces — correctly reading someone else's emotion, your own emotional arousal in response, and empathic concern, the compassion you feel for them — and found that psilocybin doesn't move them all the same way. Acutely, it actually impaired people's ability to correctly identify positive emotions in others, while increasing both emotional arousal and empathic concern. All of those acute effects were gone within a week. But the one component that mattered turned out to be empathic concern: the size of its acute rise predicted the positive psychological changes people reported later — better mood, better relationships, a better outlook on life — even though empathic concern itself didn't persist. Psilocybin also raised circulating oxytocin, but oxytocin changes didn't track the empathy changes at all, so this isn't simply an oxytocin story. The takeaway is that "psychedelics enhance empathy" is too coarse a claim — it's a dissociation, and the therapeutically relevant thread appears to be compassion specifically, not accuracy.
