# Receptor & Reason — 2026-08-06

## Script

Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Thursday, August sixth. Yesterday I flagged a couple of juicy pharmacology preprints that were held back because their full text hadn't rendered yet — a new striatal signaling molecule and a schizophrenia rescue with an LSD1 inhibitor. Good news: their bodies are up now, so we lead with both today. The rest of the show runs from dopamine circuits through two Alzheimer's drug-discovery stories into a strong run of computational pharmacology, and closes light with a benchmark for language models reading EEG. One item stays on the shelf for next time — a mapping of psilocybin-induced synaptic plasticity, posted yesterday and still abstract-only. Let's get into it.

We open with a paper that quietly picks a fight with sixty years of striatal dogma. The claim is that a small tripeptide called ophthalmate — ophthalmic acid, a close structural analog of glutathione where the cysteine is swapped for aminobutyrate — is a bona fide neuromodulator in the striatum, not just a metabolic byproduct of oxidative stress. The backstory is that this same group had found that levodopa can produce a delayed, sustained motor benefit in Parkinson's mouse models even under conditions where it can't be converted to dopamine, and that benefit tracked an eight-fold rise in striatal ophthalmate — and injecting ophthalmate directly rescued motor deficits. So the obvious question was: does this molecule actually behave like a signaling molecule? Here they show it checks every box. Using tritiated ophthalmate in mouse striatal slices, they find it's taken up by a saturable transporter that glutathione competes for, and it's released on depolarization in a calcium-dependent way — the defining signature of regulated exocytotic release. Then it gets interesting pharmacologically: ophthalmate and dopamine reciprocally regulate each other — ophthalmate boosts evoked dopamine release, while D-two receptor activation suppresses ophthalmate release. It selectively enhances GABA release but not glutamate, and in whole-cell recordings it potentiates AMPA-receptor currents and raises release probability specifically at excitatory synapses onto direct-pathway medium spiny neurons, sparing the indirect pathway. So you have a molecule with uptake, calcium-dependent release, cross-talk with dopamine, and pathway-selective synaptic effects — that's a neuromodulator by any working definition. It's all ex vivo slice pharmacology, so the in vivo circuit role is still inferred, but as a candidate new axis in basal ganglia signaling with direct Parkinson's relevance, this is the kind of thing that reorganizes a textbook figure.

Staying in the striatum and staying with dopamine, but shifting from a new molecule to a new dynamic. There's a nice fiber-photometry study on how dopamine behaves during interval timing — the task where an animal has to estimate several seconds and act on that estimate, which leans on the same executive machinery that fails in Parkinson's, ADHD, and schizophrenia. Using the dLight fluorescent dopamine sensor in dorsomedial striatum, they report something a little counterintuitive. The prior story was that dopamine ramps up toward a timed reward. Here, over the interval itself — before the reward-time increase — dorsomedial dopamine ramped down, and the slope of that downward ramp predicted when the mouse thought the interval had elapsed. So the ramp isn't just anticipatory reward signaling; its trajectory carries the animal's internal estimate of elapsed time. Two more useful details: the ramping differed between male and female mice, and amphetamine reshaped it — which matters because amphetamine is both a dopamine probe and a stand-in for the kind of dopaminergic perturbation you see clinically. It's correlative recording, not causal manipulation, but it sharpens what "dopamine encodes time" actually means at the level of the signal.

From dopamine dynamics to dopamine wiring, there's an elegant circuit-anatomy paper on how substantia nigra dopaminergic neurons are wired by compartment. These neurons have two morphologically distinct dendrite types — ones that stay up in the pars compacta, and long ones that dive down into the GABAergic pars reticulata — and the question is whether inputs respect that geometry. Using spatially targeted optogenetics across several GABAergic and glutamatergic mouse lines, they map it out. Inhibitory reticulata inputs hit the soma and proximal dendrites. On the excitatory side, the split is the headline: pedunculopontine inputs selectively excite the soma and proximal dendrites, while subthalamic nucleus inputs selectively excite the deep reticulata dendrites — mirroring the dendrite-selective inhibition already known from striosomal projections. So the subthalamic nucleus and the pedunculopontine nucleus aren't just two excitatory drivers of dopamine cells; they're wired onto physically separate parts of the neuron, which implies they can be integrated and gated independently. For anyone thinking about the subthalamic nucleus as a deep-brain-stimulation target, the idea that its influence on dopamine neurons is compartmentally segregated from brainstem excitation is a genuinely useful piece of circuit logic.

Now the second held-over item, and it's a strong one for anyone who cares about turning genetics into a drug target. Rare loss-of-function mutations in SETD1A — a histone methyltransferase that writes H3K4 methylation — are among the higher-penetrance risk variants we have for schizophrenia. Most work has pinned SETD1A's effects on the prefrontal cortex, but here they use conditional knockout mice to show the story is regional: the dorsal striatum and the mediodorsal thalamus have their own distinct transcriptomic and neuronal disruptions, enriched for other schizophrenia risk genes, and knocking out SETD1A in either region alone drives selective behavioral deficits. That's the mechanistic half. The pharmacology half is the payoff: reasoning that if you've lost a methyl-writer you might rebalance by inhibiting the opposing erasers, they screened six existing H3K4 demethylase inhibitors and found that TAK-four-eighteen — a selective LSD1 inhibitor, LSD1 also being called KDM1A — restored H3K4 methylation and gene expression and rescued both synaptic and schizophrenia-like behavioral phenotypes in the SETD1A-deficient mice. I like this because it's a clean instance of the epigenetic-rebalancing logic actually working in vivo, with a tool compound that's already been through human safety testing for other indications. The caveat is the usual one for chromatin drugs — LSD1 has broad genomic reach and you're modulating a global regulator to fix a specific circuit — but as a proof of concept that a defined genetic lesion in schizophrenia has a rational, druggable counter-move, it's exactly the kind of result this field needs more of.

Two Alzheimer's drug-discovery papers next, both making the same quietly important point: cognition and plaque can come apart. The first tests a compound called Sob-A-M-2, a brain-penetrant thyromimetic — a thyroid-hormone mimic — that induces the microglial receptor TREM2, which pushes microglia from a pro-inflammatory toward a more restorative, reparative state. In seven-month-old 5xFAD amyloid mice dosed for twelve weeks, Sob-A-M-2 raised brain TREM2 and improved both spatial and associative memory, with a rise in the synaptic markers synaptophysin and PSD-95. The pointed result is what didn't move: amyloid-beta plaque burden was unchanged, and the general microglial activation marker Iba1 was flat — though the phagocytic marker CD68 went up specifically in the hippocampus. So the cognitive benefit here is not coming from clearing plaque; it looks like a shift in microglial phenotype and synaptic support. Given how many anti-amyloid programs deliver plaque reduction with underwhelming cognitive returns, a mechanism that improves memory while leaving plaque alone is a useful counterpoint — and TREM2 activation is already a hot clinical target, so a small-molecule inducer with a thyromimetic backbone is worth tracking.

The second Alzheimer's paper goes after the genetics of risk from the protective end. APOE4 is the strongest common genetic risk factor for late-onset Alzheimer's; the much rarer APOE2 is strongly protective — and the idea here is to borrow that protection therapeutically by dosing recombinant ApoE2 protein into APOE4 brains. The wrinkle is that they produced it in a human cell system so it comes out heavily sialylated, resembling the natural brain form rather than a bacterial protein. In APOE4 neurons, recombinant ApoE2 raised hexokinase-2, lowered endogenous ApoE4, and protected against amyloid-beta oligomer toxicity and oxidative stress. To get it into the brain non-invasively they paired it with a cadherin-derived peptide that transiently loosens the blood-brain barrier, and weekly intravenous dosing in middle-aged and aged APOE4 mice improved learning and memory and boosted synaptosomal glycolytic and exocytotic activity, with the by-now-expected sex differences. This is early and the effect sizes are preliminary, but two things make it notable: it's a protein-replacement framing of a genotype that's usually treated as fixed destiny, and the barrier-opening delivery peptide is a reusable trick for getting biologics into the brain. File it under high-risk, high-interest.

Now a genuinely impressive computational pharmacology paper — the one I'd hand to anyone still assuming virtual screening has to go through a 3D pose. The tool is called Ptarmigan-1, and the premise is a critique: modern screening scores a compound by building an explicit three-dimensional pose, either by docking or by co-folding models, and that presumes a well-defined pocket. But the most interesting unexplored ligandability — cryptic sites, non-orthosteric sites, intrinsically disordered regions — offers no clean pocket to dock into. So they throw out the pose entirely. Ptarmigan-1 is a contrastive model that co-embeds protein residues and small molecules in a shared latent space from sequence and two-dimensional chemistry alone, trained directly on chemoproteomic and bioactivity data. Because there's no structure requirement, it scores a compound in about ten milliseconds instead of the tens of seconds a co-folding model needs, and it resolves each prediction down to the specific residues a compound engages. On well-folded orthosteric targets it's roughly on par with docking and co-folding; on covalent, cryptic, and disordered sites it matches or beats them — including on targets held out of training — and it can screen the entire human proteome against a library of three-point-four billion compounds in under a day. Recasting virtual screening as a nearest-neighbor lookup in a shared protein-ligand latent space, rather than a physics simulation, is a real conceptual move, and the residue-level attributions mean it's not a black box you can't interrogate. The thing I'd want to see is prospective, experimentally-confirmed hits at those cryptic sites — retrospective benchmarks always flatter — but as a fast, structure-free complement aimed exactly where docking is weakest, this is a strong entry.

Sticking with foundation models in molecular recognition but moving from drugs to enzymes, there's a tool called Boltz-2-E-S-I that predicts enzyme-substrate pairings. The bottleneck it targets is systematically mapping which substrates an enzyme actually acts on — a problem where structure-aware methods stumble because they lean on predefined pockets and rigid docking, and catalytic recognition depends on the conformational give that comes with binding. This builds on the Boltz biomolecular foundation model and uses native co-folding, so active-site plasticity falls out of the model rather than being hand-specified, and it layers in evolutionary context and geometric descriptors. It beats sequence-based and rigid-docking baselines and, importantly, discriminates tight sub-family specificities — the fine distinctions that usually defeat annotation — which they demonstrate by prioritizing candidates in the withanolide biosynthetic pathway. For the drug-discovery audience the relevance is pathway de-orphaning and biocatalyst discovery: if you want to reconstruct or engineer a natural-product route, an enzyme-substrate predictor that captures induced fit is a real accelerant. It's a preprint benchmark, so the usual caution about how it does on truly novel enzyme families applies, but it's a clean example of a general foundation model getting specialized into a hard functional-annotation task.

And we'll build down with the lightest item, a benchmark rather than a discovery — but one that's directly about whether language models can be trusted with neural data. It's called BrainBench, and it targets what the authors call comprehensive EEG understanding: not just slapping a label on a recording, but taking a natural-language instruction, doing the signal processing, producing quantitative evidence, and writing a scientifically grounded report. They assemble four subsets — foundational analysis, sleep assessment, neurocognitive assessment, and physiological integration — spanning seventeen datasets and a large pool of real-data instances, and they grade model outputs across numerical, categorical, set, sequence, semantic, and artifact checks, running representative language models through more than a hundred thousand executions. The value here isn't a result so much as a yardstick: as people start wiring LLMs into clinical and research EEG pipelines, we need honest measurement of where they actually deliver a defensible interpretation versus where they produce a confident, wrong report. A benchmark that insists on grounded quantitative evidence and validated artifacts, rather than plausible-sounding prose, is the right kind of pressure to put on these systems — and a fitting place to end, since half of today's show was about not trusting a good-looking number until you've stressed it.

That's the show for today — ophthalmate arriving as a candidate new striatal neuromodulator, dopamine ramping down to keep time in the dorsomedial striatum, compartment-selective wiring of nigral dopamine neurons, an LSD1 inhibitor rescuing a genetic schizophrenia model, two Alzheimer's stories where cognition improves without touching plaque, a structure-free virtual screen that scours the whole proteome, a co-folding model for enzyme-substrate specificity, and a benchmark for language models reading EEG.
