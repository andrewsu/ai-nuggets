## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. I'm your host, and this is your daily pass through CNS pharmacology, psychiatric and neurology drug discovery, and the computational work closing in on both. Eleven items today, and the thread is the comparator. Not the method, not the effect size — the thing you chose to measure against. Several of today's papers are interesting mostly because somebody picked a harder comparator than the field usually picks, and a couple are interesting because somebody didn't.

We start with the clearest example. A group at Illinois, working with a startup called Keiji AI and with authors from AbbVie, has built a multi-agent system for clinical development planning — the work a pharma company does when it decides what a Phase 3 should look like and how likely it is to survive review. That's a crowded space. What makes this one worth your time is not the agent architecture; it's the benchmark. They went to OpenFDA and pulled two hundred ninety-one Complete Response Letters — the letters the agency issues when an application can't be approved as submitted. Two hundred and two of those applications were eventually approved after remediation; eighty-nine never were. The letters say, in the regulator's own words, exactly which design and evidentiary choices were disqualifying. Then they linked each letter back to the corresponding registry record, so you get aligned pairs of regulatory critique and the actual trial design that provoked it, and they held out the trials whose registry entries were updated in 2022 or later so the system is being scored prospectively rather than on material it could have memorized.

That is a genuinely better comparator than what this literature normally uses, which is a binary "did the trial succeed" label scraped off a registry. And the results are more interesting for it. On predicting technical and regulatory success, the system reaches about eighty-five percent balanced accuracy, several points over the best baseline. On proposing protocol-level fixes, it hits an F1 near seventy against the high fifties for the strongest comparator, and it wins on precision and recall simultaneously, which none of the baselines manage. But on the task the whole thing is named for — detecting the deficiencies the FDA actually flagged — it lands at fifty percent F1. Precision fifty, recall fifty. Half of what it flags is not something the agency cared about, and half of what the agency cared about it misses.

The authors say this out loud in their limitations, and they draw the right conclusion: at that level the system is a triage aid that surfaces candidate concerns with the evidence attached, for a human to adjudicate. It is not an autonomous reviewer. I'd go further and say the fifty percent is the most valuable number in the paper, because it is the first time anyone has measured this task against real regulatory judgment rather than a proxy, and now we know how far away we are. The ablation on their memory base is worth noting too — without the distilled store of prior regulatory precedent, the system flags spurious deficiencies, misreading accepted design conventions as flaws. Precedent is doing a lot of the work that looks like reasoning.

Staying with evaluation, but moving to psychiatry. A Johns Hopkins group has built a quality-assurance platform for AI-assisted psychiatric intake — the first structured conversation, where you establish the chief complaint, assess self-harm risk, and characterize symptoms. Their argument is that health systems will be asked to approve these tools and currently have no defensible way to do it, because intake is an adaptive conversation and clinicians legitimately differ in style, so you can't score it against a fixed script. Their answer is a memory-augmented patient simulator built from expert-authored vignettes — three of them here, covering depression, anxiety, and bipolar two — each carrying ten binary evaluation fields and one embedded safety concern.

The pilot puts six clinicians through a twenty-five-minute simulated intake against a matched set of GPT-4o interviewer runs on the same vignettes, and the pattern is the one you would guess but sharper than you'd guess. The model recovered eighty-eight percent of the clinically relevant items embedded in the vignettes; the clinicians recovered thirty-nine percent. The model also made clinical inferences not supported by anything said in the interview at roughly twice the clinician rate, fifty-seven percent versus twenty-eight. And when a safety concern was identified, the model went on to characterize it — asked the follow-up questions that turn a flag into a risk assessment — a third of the time, against two thirds for the clinicians. Hold the absolute numbers loosely, because the denominators here are single digits to low twenties. But the shape of it is coherent and it matches what everyone who has watched these systems work reports: comprehensive information capture, weak discipline about the boundary between what was said and what was assumed, and a tendency to note risk rather than pursue it. The contribution is the harness, not the finding.

Third in this arc, and the strongest methodologically, is a paper in npj Digital Medicine on stigmatizing language and triage. The setup is the comparator I want to flag. Instead of asking whether a model's triage output matches a clinician's, they built Emergency Severity Index-matched pairs from two academic emergency departments where one patient in the pair actually deteriorated within six hours and the other did not. So harm has an objective definition: the model deprioritizing the patient who was about to crash. Then they inserted a single demographic, social, or stigma-related attribute and re-ran it, across eighteen conditions with neutral and stigmatizing phrasings of the same underlying fact, three open-weight models, two hundred twenty-one thousand comparisons.

Race, language, and insurance status produced no consistent harmful shift. What did was the stigmatizing formulation of frequent emergency department use — harmful reprioritization in every model-dataset cell, up to nine point four percent, and exceeding its own neutral counterpart within pairs everywhere, by one to seven points. Psychiatric history moved things too, up to nine and a half percent, though the stigmatizing-versus-neutral contrast only reached significance for one model. The finding that matters for anyone building these pipelines is that same-fact, different-phrasing is the axis of harm. Both versions of the sentence are clinically true. One of them costs the patient. That makes the wording of your input template a safety-critical design decision, not a style choice, and I don't think most deployments treat it that way.

Now to receptor pharmacology, and a translational package on an NMDA receptor modulator called CNS4. It's an anabasine-derived small molecule, and its mechanism is unusual enough to explain: it's not a channel blocker and it's not a straightforward positive or negative modulator. It's agonist-concentration-biased, meaning its effect on the receptor depends on which agonist site you're looking at and how much agonist is around, and the direction flips across GluN2 subtypes — it raises glutamate potency at 2A-containing receptors, raises glycine efficacy at 2C and 2D, and reduces glycine efficacy at 2B-containing receptors. That kind of profile is exactly what people have wanted out of NMDA pharmacology since it became clear that global blockade is a blunt instrument.

The preprint runs it through mice, rats, and — this is the part I haven't seen before — fourteen client-owned dogs undergoing real surgical oncology procedures, eight on drug and six on vehicle. No conditioned place preference in mice, so no obvious reward liability. Dose-dependent reductions in locomotion without rotarod impairment. Body temperature held half a degree to a degree above vehicle in both mice and dogs, which is the opposite of the hypothermia you get with ketamine and is a nice mechanistic fingerprint. Robust reversal of mechanical, pressure, and thermal hypersensitivity in the rat spinal nerve ligation model, holding across three days of repeated dosing without evident tolerance. In the dogs, stable intraoperative pressure and heart rate and about eight percent less propofol for induction.

Here's where I'd push back. The abstract describes CNS4 as having "stress mitigating" effects, based on a sucrose preference experiment after footshock. What the data actually show is that sucrose preference fell with dose — seventy-two percent in vehicle, sixty-one at fifty milligrams per kilogram, fifty-six at a hundred — and the treatment effect did not reach significance. A dose-dependent reduction in sucrose preference is, on its face, the anhedonia direction, not the stress-mitigation direction. The paper's own results section calls it a reduction. Somewhere between the results and the abstract that became a positive. The rest of the package is solid and the dog work is a real translational step; this one line isn't.

Two quick items on how a signal looks when you stop averaging over the thing that matters. First, from Neuropsychopharmacology, real-time serotonin dynamics in the anterior cingulate and the central amygdala during binge drinking, using fiber photometry with the GRAB serotonin sensor. The baseline result is already a useful dissociation: consuming alcohol and other tastants suppresses the serotonin signal in cingulate and increases it in central amygdala, while aversive stimuli raise it in both. So the two structures share an aversion motif and differ on reward. Then binge drinking perturbs this, and the perturbation is non-uniform in three directions at once — by region, by sex, and by stimulus — and shifts across weeks of alcohol experience. The interpretive point is that if you had measured serotonin in one region, in one sex, at one timepoint, you would have gotten a clean answer and it would have been the wrong generalization. They also show that optogenetically driving serotonin terminals in cingulate blunts alcohol drinking while increasing avoidance behavior without being rewarding or aversive on its own, which puts a real behavioral cost next to the therapeutic-looking effect.

Second, a brief communication in the same journal: CRISPR-mediated knockdown of the enzyme that degrades 2-AG, at GABAergic interneurons in the ventral tegmental area, increases operant responding for social interaction and does not increase operant responding for food. Same manipulation, same animals, same reinforcement schedule, two reinforcers, and only one moves. Endocannabinoid tone at that specific population is gating social motivation rather than motivation in general. It's a short paper making one point, but the food condition is the whole paper — without it you'd have a nonspecific arousal result.

From function to structure. A group at the Technical University of Munich and the Leibniz Institute has produced what they call the dynamic pocketome of the trace amine-associated receptors. TAAR1 you know as the CNS target behind ulotaront; TAAR2 through 9 were catalogued as olfactory amine sensors and are now turning up outside the nose. The problem they're addressing is that the recent experimental structures, valuable as they are, are snapshots. So they ran twenty-six experimental structures spanning four human and murine TAAR genes, in apo and ligand-bound states, in triplicate — a hundred fifty-six microseconds of aggregate simulation — and detected cavities across the entire receptor surface throughout every trajectory.

The headline result is that the apo-versus-holo comparison does not resolve into a rule. The orthosteric cavity was usually larger with ligand bound, and removing the ligand often caused it to contract or fragment into smaller pockets — but the flexibility measure showed no uniform trend, so ligand presence is not simply rigidifying the site. Some allosteric pockets are bigger in apo, others bigger in holo. The receptor-specific interplay propagates well beyond the orthosteric site. Their methodological contribution is a global identifier scheme that assigns cavities a spatial identity so they can be compared across independent trajectories, and it earns its place: raw cavity counts substantially overstate the differences between replicates of the same system, because one shared region fragments into different numbers of locally detected pockets. Most of the sampled volume sits in regions reproducibly found in all three replicates. The practical output is a prioritized map of transient, non-orthosteric pockets on a receptor family where almost nobody has looked for allosteric sites, and transient pockets by definition only exist in the simulation.

Which is the right lead-in to two papers about computational drug discovery grading itself honestly. The first defines a task I think should have existed already. Call it specificity optimization: you have a real compound with real properties — potency, pharmacokinetics, maybe clinical use — and an off-target problem. Most structure-based generative work answers this by designing a new selective molecule from scratch, which throws away everything you knew. This work instead asks for the smallest structural edit that widens the gap between intended target and known off-target while preserving the molecule. They built the benchmark out of ChEMBL, taking intended targets from curated mechanism-of-action annotations and off-targets from measured activity. The agent docks the compound against both, compares the poses through residue-aware atom-to-protein contacts, and hands that differential to a language model to propose edits; candidates survive only if they clear similarity, ADMET, and docking-selectivity filters.

Across nine hundred fifteen compounds it improves the target-versus-off-target gap for about eighty-five percent, moving the mean from minus point seven to plus point five kilocalories per mole while holding mean Tanimoto similarity at point seven two to the starting compound. That's docking, so treat the energies as a ranking, not a measurement. The result I'd actually keep is the ablation: replace the residue identities in the contact difference with binary contact indicators — same geometry, same pipeline, just "there is a contact here" instead of "there is a contact with this residue" — and improvement disappears on all twenty-nine ablation compounds. Not degrades. Disappears. Whatever the model is doing, it is doing it with medicinal-chemistry knowledge about specific residues, not with geometric bookkeeping.

The companion piece is from University College London and it does to DrugCLIP what somebody should do to every contrastive screening model. DrugCLIP reframed structure-based virtual screening as retrieval, projecting pockets and ligands into a shared embedding space. Nobody had asked what those embeddings encode. The answer is mixed in a useful way. The pocket embeddings, which were never trained for it, set a new state of the art on pocket similarity search and run more than a hundred times faster than conventional structural descriptors — that's a free tool falling out of a model built for something else. The ligand embeddings encode a pocket-aware notion of chemical similarity that only partly overlaps with fingerprint measures. And under a de-leakage benchmark — the comparator that matters, since this field is riddled with train-test contamination — it genuinely generalizes, recovering the correct bound ligand in the top one percent of fifty thousand candidates for fifty-five to seventy-five percent of unseen targets. Then performance falls as conditions get realistic, and they trace the fall to two specific causes: sidechain reorientation across apo, holo, and AlphaFold structures, and residue mismatch when the pocket itself is predicted rather than known. That second one is the actionable finding. If your screening deployment starts from a predicted pocket, pocket prediction accuracy is your bottleneck, not the retrieval model.

Two to close. A company called Eratos Therapeutics, with Ed Boyden among the authors, applied an in-house engine to publicly available multiplexed expansion-revealing microscopy from the five-x-F-A-D Alzheimer's model and wild-type mouse somatosensory cortex — expansion microscopy physically enlarges the tissue to get nanoscale resolution out of a conventional objective. The engine maps amyloid-beta, the presynaptic active zone protein RIM1, and the AMPA receptor subunit GluA2, and reports nanoscale colocalization of amyloid with GluA2 plus amyloid-bridged three-way assemblies, which they pitch as novel druggable interfaces. I want to like this, because reanalysis of existing high-dimensional imaging is exactly where automated discovery should pay off, and to their credit the caveats are in the text rather than hidden: the amyloid-RIM1 colocalization rate is one point four percent, enriched two and a half fold over a density-matched null but statistically significant in only three of nine regions, and some of the reported centroid distances fall below the registration uncertainty of the method, roughly twenty-five to forty nanometers. So the ternary architecture is a hypothesis generated at the resolution floor of the instrument, by a proprietary engine, on somebody else's dataset. That is a legitimate thing to publish and a premature thing to call a druggable interface.

And we finish with a clean one. A genetics-first repurposing screen for ALS, from an NIH-led group: take genome-wide data on roughly twenty-nine thousand patients and a hundred twenty-three thousand controls, ask which of a thousand approved drugs would push disease-associated gene expression back toward normal, then — and this is the part that makes it more than another signature-reversal exercise — validate the hit in Medicare prescription claims across a hundred fifteen thousand people, simulate the trial on public data, and take it to ex vivo mouse tissue. The compound that comes out is furosemide, which nobody was going to nominate from first principles, and the mechanistic hint from the tissue work is reduced neuronal hyperexcitability. Hyperexcitability is an established feature of ALS motor neurons and the basis of the one approved drug that touches it, so the pharmacology is at least coherent. Three independent comparators — genetic, real-world prescribing, and physiological — pointing the same way is about as much as a computational repurposing paper can do before somebody has to run the trial.

That's the pass. What ties today together is that the interesting numbers were all comparisons somebody chose to make hard: FDA letters instead of success labels, six-hour deterioration instead of clinician agreement, residue identities ablated to binary contacts, de-leaked test sets, density-matched nulls. The papers that look weakest today are the ones where the comparison in the abstract isn't the comparison in the results. Back tomorrow.
