# Receptor & Reason — 2026-08-07

## Script

Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Friday, August seventh. Today's show has a strong serotonin thread running through the front half — a computational model of the psychedelic brain state and a careful dissection of a serotonin 2A agonist — then moves through ketamine, a GLP-1 surprise in the brain, and a run of Alzheimer's and neurodegeneration drug-discovery stories, before closing on a neuro-symbolic system for reading mass spectra and a quiet result about opioid receptors and the seasons. One quick piece of housekeeping first: yesterday I promised the psilocybin synaptic-plasticity map once its full text rendered. It's posted, but the body still isn't up — so it stays on the shelf one more day. Let's get into it.

We open with a computational paper that tries to answer a question the psychedelic field keeps circling: why does the brain on a serotonergic psychedelic look more complex, and what does "more complex" actually mean. The group builds a biophysically grounded mean-field model — a whole-brain simulation where each region is a reduced description of its neurons rather than every cell individually — and they wire in the brain-wide distribution of serotonin 2A receptors taken from imaging. The clever move is how they represent the drug. Rather than hand-waving, they model 2A activation as a specific, electrophysiologically motivated change: a reduction in the leak potassium conductance that sets a neuron's resting excitability. So the whole cascade flows from one molecular knob. And what comes out matches a lot of the human data — the network shifts into an asynchronous, irregular regime with higher firing rates and a drop in alpha-band power, and spontaneous complexity, measured by the Lempel-Ziv index, goes up, just as it does empirically. Here's the part I find genuinely useful: they also reproduce the finding that the perturbational complexity index — the one you get by pinging the brain and measuring the response, the metric used to gauge consciousness under anesthesia — does not rise under the psychedelic. So spontaneous complexity and perturbational complexity come apart. That dissociation is the take-home: these two "complexity" numbers are measuring different physiology, and the field should stop treating them as interchangeable. The obvious caveat is that reducing potassium leak is only one of many things a 2A receptor does, so this is a mechanistic proof-of-principle, not the whole story — but as a bridge from a single receptor-level perturbation all the way up to macroscale signatures, it's a clean piece of work and exactly the kind of thing this show exists for.

Staying with serotonin 2A but coming back down to the animal, there's a nice pharmacology paper doing multidimensional phenotyping of TCB-2 — a selective serotonin 2A agonist used as a tool compound — in mice. The motivation is a real gap: the head-twitch response is the field's workhorse readout for psychedelic-like activity, but a twitch count tells you nothing about physiological state or whether the animal can still perform a learned behavior. So they measured a lot at once — in head-fixed mice running an auditory trace-conditioning task they tracked licking, pupil, eye position, and blinking, and in freely moving mice they took locomotion and an automated, video-based head-twitch count. TCB-2 constricted the pupil without changing eye position or blinking, reduced licking at the highest dose but left the cue-locked timing of the licks intact, reduced locomotion, and drove a dose-dependent head-twitch — and pretreatment with volinanserin, a selective serotonin 2A antagonist, partially blunted the effects, confirming the receptor is doing most but not all of the work. The reason to care is that it pulls apart channels people usually lump together: the head-twitch, the autonomic signature in the pupil, and the integrity of a learned action are dissociable, and if you're using any one of them as a proxy for "psychedelic-like," you're seeing a slice. It pairs nicely with the modeling paper — both are arguments against collapsing a rich state down to a single number.

From classic psychedelics to the other fast-acting antidepressant story, ketamine. There's a neat methods-forward paper on a system these authors call SonoKet — ketamine packaged into acoustically activatable liposomes that stay inert until focused ultrasound uncages them, which in principle lets you deliver ketamine to a millimeter-scale brain region instead of dosing the whole animal and eating the dissociative and cardiovascular side effects. Their earlier work showed the uncaging targets ketamine to the ultrasound spot and produces bigger functional effects than the same dose given free. What's new here is the readout: they used solid-phase microextraction probes coupled to mass spectrometry to sample neurotransmitters in real time — ketamine and its metabolites plus glutamate, GABA, serotonin, and dopamine — in the prefrontal cortex, nucleus accumbens, and retrosplenial cortex of awake rats. Ultrasound alone did nothing to neurotransmitter levels or to where free ketamine went, but ultrasound plus ketamine amplified the neurochemical response. The significance is really about the toolkit: region-targeted delivery of a systemic psychiatric drug, paired with a probe that lets you watch the neurochemistry as it happens rather than inferring it from behavior afterward. It's early and it's rats, but precision neuropharmacology needs exactly this kind of closed loop between delivery and measurement.

Now a GLP-1 result that's worth a minute even though it's not a psychedelic or an antidepressant, because the drug class has quietly become one of the most interesting things in CNS pharmacology. Everyone wants to repurpose semaglutide and its cousins for psychiatric and neurological indications — addiction, mood, neurodegeneration — but the central mechanisms are murky, and that murkiness is a real barrier to rational trials. This group went looking with the activity marker c-Fos after a single dose of semaglutide in rats and found that it switches on two specific systems: the orexin, or hypocretin, neurons, and the basal forebrain cholinergic neurons. Then they followed the anatomy with in vivo microdialysis and showed that semaglutide acutely raises acetylcholine release in the ventral hippocampus — in males and females, and in both young and aged rats. That's a concrete, testable circuit for some of the cognitive and arousal effects people keep reporting anecdotally: an arousal-promoting orexin arm and a memory-relevant cholinergic arm, converging on hippocampal acetylcholine. It's acute and it's a correlational activity map, so it's a starting hypothesis rather than a mechanism nailed down — but it turns "GLP-1 does something in the brain" into a specific pair of neuromodulatory systems you can go after.

That aging-brain thread carries us into two Alzheimer's drug-discovery papers. The first goes after insulin-like growth factor 1 signaling. There's long been a puzzle that IGF-1 is protective in Alzheimer's models, yet the disease seems to involve resistance to it — so this group built a small molecule, which they call A-I-K-3-a-305, that doesn't deliver IGF-1 but sensitizes the brain's response to the IGF-1 it already has, and it crosses the blood-brain barrier. In the APP/PS1 mouse they deliberately waited until twelve months of age, when the pathology is well established and memory is already failing, then dosed daily for three months. Within a month working memory in the Y-maze was back to normal; by three months cognition was fully preserved, anxiety-like behavior was corrected, and brain amyloid-beta was lowered. Sensitizing an endogenous pathway rather than flooding the system with the ligand is a cleaner pharmacological idea — you're restoring responsiveness instead of overriding it — and starting treatment after disease onset is the harder, more honest test. Usual caveats: one model, one lab, preclinical, and IGF-1 signaling touches a lot of biology, so peripheral and long-term effects will matter. But as a mechanism it's a nice departure from the anti-amyloid monoculture.

The second neurodegeneration paper is about a stress pathway inside the cell, and it reaches beyond Alzheimer's. Several subtypes of Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease — the most common inherited neuropathy — come from mutations in the enzymes that charge transfer RNAs, and the authors show that a shared downstream consequence across these and other axonal forms is chronic activation of the integrated stress response, the pathway cells use to throttle protein synthesis when they're in trouble. Their drug, R-T-X-117, is a brain-penetrant small molecule already in Phase 1 that activates the initiation factor eIF2B, the node the stress response clamps down on — and they solved a cryo-EM structure showing exactly how it binds the eIF2B assembly. In a mouse carrying a disease mutation and modeling the CMT type 2 neuropathy, giving the drug after symptoms had already appeared reduced the chronic stress signaling and produced real functional and electrophysiological improvement, with two secreted proteins — GDF-15 and FGF-21 — flagged as potential biomarkers of response. And the reason it lands on this show alongside the Alzheimer's papers is that they went looking more broadly and found the same integrated-stress-response activation across several neurodegeneration models, correlating with progression, notably in Alzheimer's. So this is a clinical-stage molecule with a solved binding mode, pointed at a convergent, druggable node that may matter well beyond the inherited neuropathy it was built for. The Alzheimer's connection there is still correlational, but eIF2B is worth having on your radar.

One more Alzheimer's item, and it rhymes with a theme from yesterday's show — cognition and plaque coming apart. Clusterin is one of the strongest common genetic risk factors for late-onset Alzheimer's, but nobody's been sure what it actually does mechanistically. This group shows that in microglia clusterin behaves like a brake: expression is low at rest and shoots up when the cells are inflamed, and if you delete clusterin the microglia over-activate and the inflammation runs hotter, while adding recombinant clusterin protein cools it back down. The pointed result is in a human mutant amyloid knock-in mouse, where losing clusterin impaired learning and memory even though amyloid burden went down — and single-nucleus sequencing traced part of that to a disrupted balance between excitatory and inhibitory neurons. So clusterin looks like a negative-feedback regulator that lets microglia resolve rather than smolder, and cognition tracks that resolution rather than the plaque. That's the second time this week we've seen memory get worse while amyloid gets better, which should keep sharpening everyone's skepticism about plaque as the thing to optimize.

Now the computational item, and it's a good one for anyone who works with metabolites or natural products. Mass spectrometry has revealed millions of small molecules across living systems, but the overwhelming majority are unidentified, because annotation leans on expert interpretation and on reference libraries that cover a tiny sliver of chemical space. The tool here, called AIMe — for A-I Molecule Explorer — is a multi-agent, neuro-symbolic system, meaning it pairs explicit chemical reasoning with structure-informed machine learning rather than being one end-to-end black box. Its core trick is to predict a fragmentation spectrum by modeling fragmentation as a sequence of discrete actions — assigning likelihoods to individual bond-breaking steps and building the pathway up — which lets it tie each observed peak to a specific fragment formula and structure, and it beats existing predictors at that. Then they run it at scale: they predicted tandem mass spectra for over a hundred million molecules in PubChem and organized them into a searchable resource, more than eight hundred million predicted spectra, expanding the searchable universe by something like three orders of magnitude over experimental libraries. The framing they use is that this is to metabolomics what sequence-homology search became for genomics — you map an unknown spectrum to its nearest neighbors in a vast predicted space. The obvious caveat is that predicted spectra are not measured spectra, and the real test is prospective identifications that hold up in the lab. But the combination of chemically interpretable, action-by-action predictions with omics-scale coverage is a genuinely nice piece of design, and interpretability is the part that keeps it honest.

And we'll build down with the lightest item, which is quietly about reproducibility. The mu-opioid receptor sits at the center of pain, stress, social behavior, and immune regulation, and there's evidence its signaling shifts with the seasons — shorter days are associated with lower central receptor availability. The open question this paper asks is whether it's the daylength itself that matters, or the fact of daylength changing. So they compared rats kept under a shifting, seasonal photoperiod against rats held at a constant one, and measured receptor density directly with radioligand binding. The result is a little unexpected: the animals on the cycling schedule didn't necessarily have higher or lower receptor density on average — they had markedly less variability between individuals, in the brain, in the cerebellum and striatum, and out in the adrenal glands, with no tissue getting noisier. The reading is that a naturalistic, changing light schedule pulls the opioid system into a tighter, more consistent set point, whereas a flat artificial photoperiod lets individuals drift apart. It's a small study, but it's a nice reminder on two fronts — that seasonal dynamics may shape mood and stress systems through the opioid receptor, and, very practically, that the lighting schedule in your vivarium could be a hidden source of between-animal variance.

That's the show for Friday — a mean-field model linking one potassium-conductance knob to the complexity signatures of the psychedelic brain, and the dissociation between spontaneous and perturbational complexity that falls out of it; a multidimensional dissection of the serotonin 2A agonist TCB-2; ultrasound-uncaged ketamine with real-time neurochemistry; semaglutide switching on orexin and cholinergic systems and raising hippocampal acetylcholine; an IGF-1 sensitizer that rescues cognition in established-disease Alzheimer's mice; a clinical-stage eIF2B activator aimed at the integrated stress response across neurodegeneration; clusterin as a microglial brake where cognition tracks inflammation, not plaque; a neuro-symbolic system charting the small-molecule universe from mass spectra; and a reminder that a changing light schedule tightens up the opioid system.
