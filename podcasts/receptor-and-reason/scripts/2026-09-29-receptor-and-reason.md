## Script

Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Tuesday, September twenty-ninth. Twelve items today, and the thread running through most of them is the same one: what does it actually mean for a computational object to stand in for a biological state? A language model that stands in for a patient. A free energy calculation that stands in for a functional assay. An agent that stands in for the person deciding what to run next. Let's start with the most provocative of those. One housekeeping note first: bioRxiv's metadata API was fully healthy this morning — nineteen hundred and seventy-five records swept day by day, no gaps — but the Cloudflare layer throttled full-text fetches again, so three otherwise-eligible preprints are deferred rather than dropped, including a whole-brain activation map after mu and NOP receptor agonism and a study of spiral cortical dynamics during the DMT experience. Both are worth watching for.

A group has posted a preprint introducing what they call Dementia Language Models, and the framing is genuinely new. The setup is modest: take three open-weight language models with different architectures and pretraining recipes, and fine-tune each one on Cookie Theft picture descriptions from participants with dementia in the Pitt DementiaBank corpus. For each one, also fine-tune a matched Healthy model on control speech from the same task. Then ask a harder question than anyone has asked before, which is not "can this thing imitate impaired speech" but "has it learned a representation of cognitive impairment that transfers off the training distribution."

Three results make this worth your attention. First, the models' internal representations carry information that improves mild cognitive impairment classification in a completely separate cohort. They took the Wisconsin Registry for Alzheimer's Prevention, nine hundred and seven transcripts, a hundred and twenty with MCI, so about thirteen percent prevalence. Conventional baselines — nineteen hand-engineered linguistic measures, or pretrained sentence embeddings — performed essentially at chance, with an area under the curve at or below point five six. But if you push each Wisconsin transcript through both the Healthy and the Dementia model and use the difference between their final-layer representations as your feature vector, discrimination improves. Not dramatically — the best fusion reached point six two eight — but the models were frozen, never saw a Wisconsin transcript, and never saw an MCI label.

Second, and this is the part that should make you sit up: the signal generalizes out of language entirely. They put the Healthy and Dementia models through the Iowa Gambling Task, a hundred trials, four decks, no verbal output at all — just sequential choices. Then they trained a classifier on synthetic sessions only, and applied it cold to ninety real humans, forty-five with MCI and forty-five controls. Area under the curve was point seven two two for one model family, point six two nine and point six one one for the other two. A classifier trained directly on the human data got point six five seven. So synthetic behavior from a model fine-tuned on picture descriptions is about as informative about human MCI as the human data itself. Neither control condition reproduced that — not fine-tuning on unrelated spoken language, not a magnitude-matched random parameter shift.

Third, and this is where the mechanistic reading gets careful. You might be tempted to say the model has become impaired. It has not. Causal activation patching shows the same layers dominate predictions across all conditions, correlation at or above point nine nine eight. Attention entropy barely moves. Depth-wise weight-change profiles are nearly identical between Healthy and Dementia fine-tuning. What dementia fine-tuning produces is not deterioration but a direction: interpolate linearly in weight space from the Healthy checkpoint toward the Dementia checkpoint and predicted MMSE falls smoothly, by roughly one and a half to one point eight five points per unit of interpolation, with the composite linguistic score and the dementia classifier probability rising in step. The authors are honest that linearity per se is not the finding — interpolating toward their control endpoints was also linear, just pointing the wrong way. What distinguishes the real axis is its direction and its clinical interpretation.

Why this matters for your work: it gives you a dial. If cognitive impairment is a controllable continuum in a model's parameters, you can generate graded synthetic populations for powering an assessment instrument, or stress-test a screening tool across severity, without recruiting anyone. The obvious limitation is that the training corpus is one picture-description task from one cohort, and DementiaBank has well-known demographic confounds — the authors do check age, gender, and length as confounders, but this is a one-corpus foundation holding up a lot of building.

Staying with Alzheimer's but moving from language to tissue: Nature Medicine has published the PsychAD consortium's computational framework for phenotype-associated single cells, which they call PASCode. The dataset is the real story here — more than six point three million nuclei from dorsolateral prefrontal cortex across nearly fifteen hundred donors, with Braak stages, Clinical Dementia Rating, cognitive resilience status, and a range of neuropsychiatric symptoms. The method is an ensemble: run several differential-abundance methods, keep the cells they agree on, and train a graph attention network to predict those consensus labels from expression. The output is a per-cell score for how strongly that cell is associated with a given phenotype, which means you can also score cells from donors whose phenotype metadata is missing — which in postmortem brain work is most of them.

The intuition worth carrying is that this decouples cell-type prioritization from cell abundance. Oligodendrocytes are the most numerous subclass and rank low; microglia rank highest, then astrocytes, and vascular leptomeningeal cells come in surprisingly high, which is a real novelty given their role in meningeal lymphatic waste clearance. Somatostatin interneurons show about a tenfold enrichment of control-associated over disease-associated cells, consistent with the reported depletion in Alzheimer's.

For this show the most interesting slice is depression within Alzheimer's. They compared Alzheimer's donors with and without depression, so the Alzheimer's signal itself is subtracted out. Depression-associated cells were distributed across every subclass, but astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, and oligodendrocyte precursors ranked highest — the same three cell types that Maitra and colleagues found depleted in dorsolateral prefrontal cortex in major depressive disorder. Sex-specific patterns lined up too: astrocytes and OPCs in males, microglia in females. Within depression-associated astrocytes, interleukin-eighteen was selectively upregulated. And disease-ontology enrichment found depression-related gene modules in those cells and, notably, an absence of Alzheimer's-related modules, which argues the framework is actually separating the two signals rather than smearing them. The authors float endoplasmic reticulum stress in a specific astrocyte subtype as a possible substrate for why SSRIs have cognitive benefit in Alzheimer's. That's a hypothesis, not a result, but it's a well-posed one.

Precision psychiatry from a different direction. Translational Psychiatry has a multi-omic study from the Mayo Clinic Bipolar Disorder Biobank, four hundred and fifty-seven patients, asking whether kynurenine pathway metabolites and their polygenic scores relate to clinical features and lithium response, scored on the Alda A scale. Measured serum kynurenic acid was nominally associated with better lithium response, and also with higher odds of a lifetime suicide attempt, but none of the metabolite-phenotype associations survived multiple-comparison correction. The finding that did survive was genetic: the polygenic score for kynurenic acid predicted better lithium response, and it was the only result to clear correction. In a joint model, measured kynurenic acid and its polygenic score each predicted response independently. Read that carefully — the genetic instrument outperformed the measured metabolite, which is the pattern you expect when a single serum draw is a noisy read on a lifetime exposure. It reinforces the kynurenine pathway as a target, and it's a small argument that for treatment-response prediction, a polygenic score may be the more stable biomarker than the metabolite it predicts.

A negative result worth knowing, also in Translational Psychiatry. A seven tesla proton MRS study measured glutamate across five regions — anterior cingulate, dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, basal ganglia, hippocampus, and thalamus — in forty-seven antipsychotic-free first-episode psychosis patients against twenty-one controls. Regional glutamate concentrations did not differ. All p-values above point two. At ultra-high field, in medication-naive patients, in the regions where you'd most expect it. What did emerge, in exploratory analysis, is that patients showed significant inter-regional glutamate correlations across six of ten region pairs, correlations in the point three to point four range, and controls showed none. Four of those were associated with positive symptom severity. So the proposal is that glutamatergic dysregulation in early psychosis lives in the covariance structure rather than in the levels. That's exploratory and it needs replication, but it's a useful corrective if you've been reading the regional-concentration literature as settled.

Now some straightforward pharmacology. British Journal of Pharmacology has the first look at whether tirzepatide, the long-acting dual GIP and GLP-1 receptor agonist, touches nicotine. In male mice, tirzepatide attenuated nicotine-induced locomotor stimulation dose-dependently, reduced nicotine conditioned place preference, and cut accumbal dopamine release measured by in vivo microdialysis. More interesting than the acute reward effects: it suppressed reinstatement of place preference and prevented the expression of locomotor sensitization. Ex vivo, it blocked nicotine-induced dopamine elevations in nucleus accumbens, ventral tegmental area, and lateral septum, and restored GABA levels region-specifically, which hints the mechanism isn't purely dopaminergic. The reason this matters beyond "another GLP-1 addiction paper" is the weight-gain problem — post-cessation weight gain is a genuine barrier to sustained abstinence, and a dual incretin agonist is the one class that plausibly addresses both sides at once. Caveat: male mice only, and place preference is not self-administration.

A repurposing result in ACS Chemical Neuroscience that I think deserves more attention than it will get. Fragile X-associated tremor ataxia syndrome is driven by CGG repeat expansion in the five-prime untranslated region of FMR1, producing RNA-mediated toxicity and aggregation of the polyglycine product FMR1-polyG. The group tested itraconazole, the approved antifungal, in combination with lacosamide, the antiepileptic, on the premise that both engage expanded CGG repeat RNA structures. In a Drosophila model the combination reduced polyG aggregation, cut oxidative stress and cell death, improved locomotor performance, and extended lifespan — and did it with a fourfold reduction in the effective itraconazole dose relative to itraconazole alone. They confirmed suppression of aggregation and correction of splicing defects in CGG-repeat-transfected HEK293T and COS-7 cells, and showed selective binding to CGG-expanded RNA biophysically. Two approved drugs, a dose-sparing combination, an RNA-structure target rather than a protein. Flies and cells are a long way from patients, but the translational path here is unusually short.

To structural pharmacology, and a cryo-EM paper on THIK1 that is really a paper about how little chemical similarity guarantees. THIK1 is the two-pore-domain potassium channel encoded by KCNK13, essentially microglia-restricted, and it carries the major tonic potassium conductance that sets microglial resting potential. Blocking it suppresses NLRP3-dependent interleukin-one-beta release, which is why it's a live neuroinflammation target — and why it's more attractive than P2Y12 or P2X7, both of which are expressed well outside microglia. The group solved structures of full-length human THIK1 with two inhibitors: C101248, the original tool compound, and CVN293, Cerevance's clinical candidate that has cleared Phase One with measurable CSF penetration and is heading toward Phase Two. Resolutions of two point nine nine and two point eight one angstroms.

Both compounds bind the same place — the inner vestibule beneath the selectivity filter, formed by transmembrane helices two and four — and both remodel tyrosine 273, the inner gate, pushing it down about three and a half angstroms so the inter-subunit hydroxyl distance opens from two point one to five angstroms. IC-fifty values by whole-cell patch clamp were three hundred and forty-nine nanomolar for the tool compound and four hundred and twenty-eight for the clinical candidate. Same site, same scaffold, near-identical potency. And yet they differ where it counts. In the CVN293 structure the C-terminal helix — a second, more intracellular gate — stays well resolved in its closed conformation. In the C101248 structure it largely disappears, and the authors ruled out particle count by downsampling. The functional consequence is real: deleting that helix cut maximal CVN293 inhibition from about eighty-five to seventy-one percent and collapsed the time to maximal inhibition from a hundred and nineteen seconds to about seven, with much faster and more complete washout. For the tool compound, deletion also accelerated onset but the compound was essentially washout-resistant either way. So the same regulatory element stabilizes the inhibited state for one compound and opposes it for the other. If you are optimizing residence time on this target, the selectivity filter is not where the lever is.

Which sets up the most conceptually interesting computational item of the week. A preprint asks whether you can predict ligand efficacy — not affinity, efficacy — from free energy perturbation. The thermodynamic argument is clean and old: if a receptor has an active and an inactive state, the shift in its conformational equilibrium on ligand binding is just the difference between the ligand's binding free energy to the two states. So compute absolute binding free energies to both conformations, take the difference, and a negative value means agonist, positive means antagonist. They ran seventy-eight protein-ligand pairs across six ion channels from four structural superfamilies: GluA2, the GABA-A rho-one receptor, the alpha-three-beta-four nicotinic receptor, 5-HT-3A, the lysosomal channel TRPML1, and KCNQ2.

Three things make this more than a proof of concept. It separates agonists from antagonists even when the free energy gap is well under one kilocalorie per mole — which should be impossible given typical FEP error bars, and the reason it works is that systematic errors are strongly correlated between two near-identical receptor states and largely cancel in the difference. For the alpha-three-beta-four receptor they estimate that correlation around point nine four. Second, it goes quantitative: for 5-HT-3A the free energy difference tracked measured maximum response along a sigmoid with a root-mean-square error of about six percent in maximum response, and the mechanistic read-out identified aspartate 202 as the residue whose hydrogen bond decides agonism — with one ligand explained by a compensating water bridge it has to break. Third, and this is the claim with the widest reach: local binding-pocket conformation encodes efficacy even when the global channel state is ambiguous. For GluA2 the agonist-bound template had no pore domain at all, and classification still worked. The honest caveats are that the decision boundary is not always at zero — KCNQ2 needed a threshold around minus two kilocalories per mole, plausibly because voltage dependence and PIP2 cofactor effects aren't in the model — so you need a few known ligands to calibrate before scaling. Put this next to the THIK1 paper and the picture is coherent: the pocket is where mechanism lives.

Two agentic items. The first is a preprint called T-REX, for Target-adaptive Rescue-Explore-eXploit, which is an agentic campaign controller for de novo protein binder design. The premise is one that anyone running a compute-constrained pipeline will recognize: there are now a dozen good generative backbone models, inverse-folding methods, and structure evaluators, they differ in productivity and cost by target and by campaign stage, and nobody knows the allocation policy. T-REX has language model agents read accumulating campaign evidence and choose among three moves — rescue a promising design that missed one metric, explore an underused method or setting, or exploit a route that's working — while a deterministic layer validates the proposed jobs, enforces resource limits, and schedules them asynchronously. Across seven targets under matched compute it produced the highest throughput of structurally distinct hits on every target, beating a non-agentic tree-search controller by a geometric mean of two point four three fold and the best generator-only baseline by two point four eight fold. The design choice I'd highlight is the asymmetry it exploits: language model reasoning over compact campaign summaries is cheap relative to running a folding model, so a brief reasoning step can steer expensive computation. What it does not have is a single wet-lab binder — every hit is an in silico hit, and "structurally distinct" is a computed diversity metric, not a measured one.

The second is a benchmark I'd recommend to anyone who thinks about mechanism for a living. It's called MechBench, and it draws a line most autonomous-discovery benchmarks have blurred: recovering a phenomenal law that describes observable behavior is not the same as recovering the mechanism that generates it. Kepler versus Newton. Each task is built from a mechanistic model — a structured set of scientifically meaningful relations whose joint consequences entail an observable law — and the agent sees only observational data and context. Mechanism recovery is then tested with probes that query internal consequences you cannot infer from the phenomenal law alone. To keep memorized textbook answers from carrying the day, they mutate the canonical mechanisms in controlled, still-admissible ways, and screen out instances where competing mechanisms are indistinguishable from the data.

The gap is large. For the strongest configuration they report, phenomenal-law accuracy on the core set was thirty-five percent while mechanism accuracy was thirteen point seven five percent, and mechanism recovery failed in sixty-four percent of cases where the phenomenal law had been recovered correctly. Hand the agent the correct phenomenal law and mechanism recovery still stays below fifty percent. The gap widens as mechanisms get further from their textbook forms. The reason to care specifically: the sixteen task families include four biology families, and one of them is allosteric regulation instantiated as the Monod-Wyman-Changeux two-conformation occupancy model with conformation-dependent turnover. Another is membrane channel flux built from channel-state occupancy coupled to Goldman electrodiffusion. So the benchmark is quite literally asking whether an agent, handed dose-response data, can recover the two-state conformational picture rather than just fitting a Hill equation — which is the exact distinction the free energy perturbation paper we just discussed is built on. The variation the authors find is driven by base model and task family, not by scientific domain and not much by agent harness, which is a useful signal about where the bottleneck actually sits.

Two shorter items to close. There's a preprint on AutoBCI, an agentic framework for discovering EEG decoding architectures. A Designer agent generates and refines architectures across motor imagery, emotion recognition, and sleep staging; a Forecaster agent predicts final performance from architecture code, training protocol, and early learning curves so compute goes to promising candidates. Across fourteen EEG datasets and six language models, the best discovered architecture reached sixty-four point one six percent average test balanced accuracy against sixty-three point eight seven for the strongest baseline. That is a margin of under a third of a percentage point, and I'd read the architecture search as roughly a wash against a well-tuned foundation model. The result I'd actually keep is the forecaster: with ten observed epochs it cut mean absolute error in predicting full-budget validation accuracy from two point two to one point three six percentage points, a thirty-eight percent improvement over just taking the best score seen so far. Early-stopping policy, not architecture novelty, is where the value is here.

And finally, a medRxiv evaluation that is a small masterpiece of the form. The question: if you compose two AI systems that are each individually safe, is the composite safe? The system is a multilingual ambient clinical scribe — speech recognition feeding note generation — evaluated on scripted consultations across seventy-seven languages and five complexity levels, from routine general practice to expert multidisciplinary handover. Note generation from a clean reference script had a three point four percent serious-error rate with no gradient across complexity or language resource level. Run it end to end from the actual speech recognition transcript and serious errors rose from one percent at the simplest level to twenty-four percent at the hardest, and from seven percent in high-resource languages to fifteen percent in low-resource ones, an odds ratio of about one point five per resource tier. Eighty-seven percent of serious in-note errors originated upstream in transcription, and the generation layer amplified those errors about three times as often as it corrected them. Word error rate explained only about a quarter of the cross-language variance. The lesson generalizes well past scribes: component-level metrics do not compose, and the thing you must evaluate is the artifact a human actually acts on.

That's the episode. Twelve items, and the recurring question underneath them is whether your computational stand-in preserves the property you care about — a conformational equilibrium, a mechanism, a cognitive state, or a clinical fact. Three of today's papers say yes under conditions worth knowing, and three say no in ways worth knowing too. See you tomorrow.
