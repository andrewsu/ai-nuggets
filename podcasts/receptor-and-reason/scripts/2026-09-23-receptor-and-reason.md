## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. I'm your host, and this is your daily pass through CNS pharmacology, psychiatric and neurology drug discovery, and the computational work closing in on both. Eleven items today, and there's an unusually clean thread running through the first half: the cholinergic system keeps turning up in places where dopamine used to be the whole story, and every time it does, the pharmacology turns out to be stranger than the target list suggests.

We open with the biggest item of the week, and probably the quarter. SCHEMA 2.0 is out as a preprint from the Analytic and Translational Genetics Unit at Mass General and the Stanley Center — the largest rare coding variant association study of schizophrenia ever run. Eighty-seven thousand nine hundred and fifty-nine cases against a hundred and fifty thousand five hundred and eighty-seven controls after quality control, drawn from a hundred and twenty cohorts, with fifty-eight thousand cases new to this report. That roughly triples the case count from the original SCHEMA. Sixteen genes reach exome-wide significance, and another twenty-four clear a five percent false discovery rate.

Not all of that gain is sample size. They replaced the single missense pathogenicity metric used in SCHEMA 1.0 with a composite rank across multiple predictors, and re-running with the old definition drops exome-wide significant genes from sixteen to thirteen. There's also a quiet vindication of the original study: genes like CUL1, RB1CC1, and SP4 that only crossed the line in SCHEMA 1.0 with de novo evidence layered on are now significant from case-control data alone, and adding de novo information back in buys almost nothing. The de novo signal was prioritizing real genes, not manufacturing them.

Now the part that matters for anyone working on targets. The muscarinic M4 receptor gene comes up at the five percent false discovery rate threshold, and it sits within five hundred kilobases of a genome-wide association index variant. That is convergent common-and-rare evidence on a receptor that is currently the hottest thing in schizophrenia pharmacology. The authors handle this exactly right — they note the approval of xanomeline-trospium, which engages M1 and M4 centrally, and then immediately note the Phase 2 failure of emraclidine, a selective M4 positive allosteric modulator, and conclude that M4 alone may not be sufficient and that combined M1 and M4 action may be what's doing the work. Finding a live drug target sitting at the top of an unbiased rare variant scan is the best available evidence that this class of study can nominate therapeutics rather than just biology.

Two more findings deserve attention. Three genes that were exome-wide significant in SCHEMA 1.0 — GRIN2A, GRIA3, and CACNA1G — no longer clear the false discovery threshold. All three are established developmental delay and intellectual disability genes, and the authors show that protein-truncating carrier frequencies in developmental-delay genes are lower in the newly added cohorts than in the original ones. The most likely reading is ascertainment drift: the new collections are milder or less comorbid cases, diluting cross-disorder signal. That is not a refutation of glutamatergic biology, but it is a caution that rare variant effect estimates are far less stable than common variant ones, because a handful of carriers moves everything. And therapeutically, the authors make a point I'd underline: essentially every carrier is heterozygous, so the mechanism is partial loss of function. If you're designing against these genes, you are modeling knockdown, not knockout, and for the genes expressed predominantly prenatally you may not have a window at all.

Which leads naturally into the second item, because it asks whether the cells implicated by that genetics are actually still there. A group at the Centre for Addiction and Mental Health in Toronto, with Pittsburgh and Stanford, harmonized seven post-mortem prefrontal single-nucleus RNA sequencing datasets — four hundred and sixty-nine donors, two hundred and ninety-eight controls and a hundred and seventy-one cases, about two point four million nuclei — onto a single fine-grained cortical taxonomy, and then meta-analyzed both expression and cell abundance. The question they're settling is decades old: in schizophrenia, are somatostatin interneurons lost, or are they present but molecularly sick?

The answer is both, and the split is by subtype. Within somatostatin cells, somatostatin messenger RNA is down about twenty-seven percent — negative in six of seven datasets, low heterogeneity, and replicated in an independent spatial transcriptomics cohort. That's the sick-but-present part, and because cells were typed from whole transcriptomes rather than from the marker itself, the nuclei with no detectable transcript at all — more common in cases, forty-one percent versus thirty-three — still get counted. Parvalbumin messenger RNA, by contrast, is not reduced within parvalbumin cells in the sequencing meta-analysis at all, which is a notable dissociation from the textbook account. Then the abundance result: a specific set of upper-layer somatostatin subtypes is genuinely depleted, confirmed spatially, alongside an increase in layer six-b excitatory neurons. And the payoff — schizophrenia common variant risk is enriched specifically in the most depleted somatostatin subtypes. Those cells express high levels of the HCN1 channel, partly correspond to the primate-specialized CALB1-positive double-bouquet cells, and are among the first cells lost in Alzheimer's disease. So you have a population that is intrinsically vulnerable across two very different diseases and that carries the genetic risk. If you want a neuroprotective target in schizophrenia rather than a symptomatic one, that is where I'd look.

Staying with human pharmacology but moving to dopamine. A Monash group ran a double-blind within-subject crossover of six hundred milligrams of sulpiride, a D2 antagonist, against placebo in twenty-four healthy volunteers performing an anticipated-response stop-signal task with concurrent EEG. The hypothesis is clean: the indirect pathway is rich in D2 receptors and is thought to carry proactive inhibition — the anticipatory slowing you do when you think a stop signal is coming — so D2 blockade should hit proactive inhibition and spare reactive stopping.

The abstract says that's what happened. The full text says something more interesting. What is strongly supported is a single effect: sulpiride increased the variability of Go response times, with a Bayes factor of a hundred and twenty-eight. Everything else is softer than advertised. Mean Go response time showed no drug effect. The claim that proactive slowing was abolished rests on the Go-probability effect being significant under placebo and not under sulpiride — but the drug by probability interaction was not significant, and its Bayes factor of point one five is actual evidence *against* an interaction. That's the classic error of treating a difference in significance as a significant difference. On the reactive side, stop-signal reaction time went up four milliseconds, p equals point zero six, Bayes factor one point six — that's an inconclusive result, not a demonstrated null. And the electrophysiological drug effect, the attenuation of the frontocentral negativity, is a cluster at p equals point zero three uncorrected. Add that the sample is ninety-one percent male and n equals twenty-four. The honest version of this paper is: acute D2 blockade destabilizes response timing, and the selective-for-proactive story is a hypothesis this study was not powered to adjudicate. Which is still worth knowing, because response-time variability is exactly the kind of measure that gets ignored in favor of means.

From D2 receptors to what happens downstream of them, and one of my favorite results of the week. A group at UC San Francisco asked what basal ganglia output actually does during a dystonic attack. They used the PNKD mouse — a model of paroxysmal nonkinesigenic dyskinesia carrying the human mutation, in which ethanol reliably triggers dystonia. This model has both etiologic and phenotypic validity, which is rare in dystonia, and it lets you time-lock recordings to attacks in a freely moving animal, something you cannot do in a human operating room under general anesthesia.

Firing in the substantia nigra pars reticulata — the main rodent basal ganglia output nucleus — collapses during attacks, and in the parvalbumin-expressing projection neurons, identified by optogenetic tagging, it goes essentially to zero. Onset tracks dystonia onset; when attacks resolve, firing comes back. So far, the classic rate model. The mechanism is the good part. Spikes get smaller and broader in the moments before firing stops — the signature of sodium channels accumulating in steady-state inactivation. Calcium photometry shows intracellular calcium rising during attacks, consistent with sustained depolarization. And the neurons stop responding to direct optogenetic drive. That is depolarization block: the cells are not inhibited into silence, they are depolarized out of the ability to spike. Ethanol appears to reduce direct-pathway medium spiny neuron firing, disinhibiting these output neurons into the block.

The therapeutic inversion is the reason to care. To rescue firing, they delivered pulsatile optogenetic activation of the *inhibitory* striatal input to the output nucleus — and that restored spiking and attenuated dystonia. More inhibition produced more output. If depolarization block is a final common path in dystonia, then the intuition that you should quiet an overexcited circuit is exactly backwards, and it also supplies a clean mechanistic account of why high-frequency stimulation of basal ganglia targets helps.

Second dystonia paper, and it dismantles an assumption about the one drug class that actually works. Antimuscarinics — trihexyphenidyl in particular — are among the most effective pharmacological treatments for generalized dystonia, and the standard story is that they act on striatal cholinergic interneurons, which degenerate in DYT-TOR1A models and are widely blamed for the pathophysiology. A group at UT Southwestern took that story apart with genetics, pharmacology, and targeted ablation in a symptomatic mouse model where torsinA is deleted from all striatal neurons.

Four findings, and they don't fit together the way you'd expect. First, cholinergic interneurons matter developmentally: restoring torsinA selectively in those cells prevents their degeneration and reduces abnormal movements, and the surviving cells become hyperactive right as the motor phenotype emerges. Second, treating with trihexyphenidyl at five milligrams per kilogram daily across the juvenile striatal maturation window produces benefit that persists long after washout — unlike adult treatment, which wears off — and it does this without rescuing a single cholinergic interneuron. Third, infusing scopolamine directly into the dorsal striatum nearly abolishes the abnormal movements, so the striatum is the site of therapeutic action. And fourth, the one that breaks the model: ablating the remaining dorsal striatal cholinergic interneurons with a saporin conjugate neither prevented nor reduced abnormal movements, whether done juvenile or adult — and antimuscarinics still worked just as well afterward, systemically and intrastriatally.

So the cells whose pathology causes the phenotype are not the cells the drug is acting through, and are not required for the phenotype to persist. The authors float the possibilities honestly: constitutive muscarinic receptor activity with the antagonists behaving as inverse agonists, receptor misexpression onto cell types that don't normally carry them, residual cholinergic arbors, or extrinsic input from the pedunculopontine nucleus. Nobody knows yet. But the practical upshot is that subtype-selective M1 and M4 antagonists now exist, and this experiment is the one that tells you the M1-versus-M4 question is worth the money.

Staying cholinergic, moving to Alzheimer's, and to a feasibility question that has been assumed rather than tested. Basal forebrain cholinergic neurons are among the earliest casualties in Alzheimer's disease, and tau deposits there early — but nobody had established whether tau PET can actually measure it, because the basal forebrain sits next to thalamus and pallidum, which are classic off-target binding regions for these tracers. A Neuropsychopharmacology paper works this out in thirteen hundred and ninety-seven cognitively unimpaired older adults across two tracers with different off-target profiles — flortaucipir in five hundred and nine, and MK-6240 in eight hundred and eighty-eight.

The discriminating test is elegant. Basal forebrain tau signal correlates with global amyloid burden in both tracer cohorts, just as entorhinal cortex does — while the adjacent off-target regions, thalamus and pallidum, do not correlate with amyloid at all. Off-target binding shouldn't track amyloid; real tau should. So the basal forebrain signal behaves like pathology rather than artifact, in two chemically distinct tracers. Basal forebrain and entorhinal tau then show essentially interchangeable relationships with age, hippocampal volume, basal forebrain volume, and cognition. And a whole-brain morphometry analysis finds that higher entorhinal tau maps onto lower gray matter volume in a cluster specific to the bilateral basal forebrain, replicated across cohorts. That's a structural correlate of the cholinergic-degeneration story in living, unimpaired people. Keep the effect sizes in perspective — correlations run around point two, so this is a group-level research measure, not an individual biomarker. But if you are running a trial in a cholinergic-adjacent mechanism, you now have a defensible way to measure the substrate.

Now a piece of lipid pharmacology from Sanford Burnham Prebys that reorganizes how you should think about endocannabinoids. The premise: anandamide and 2-AG are ligands for CB1 and CB2, and their termination pathways — fatty acid amide hydrolase and monoacylglycerol lipase — are well-mapped drug targets. This group shows there is a third fate. Phosphorylating anandamide produces anandamide phosphate, which is not a degradation product but a ligand with an entirely different receptor profile: it activates lysophosphatidic acid receptors, most potently LPA1 and LPA5, and it activates MRGPRX4, the primate-specific bile acid sensory receptor, with a measured dissociation constant around twenty-one nanomolar. So phosphorylation is a switch that reroutes an endocannabinoid out of the cannabinoid system entirely.

They find the kinase by activity-guided fractionation of brain versus liver homogenate — the activity is brain-specific — and land on diacylglycerol kinase theta, the only member of the ten-gene family that phosphorylates anandamide appreciably, though several will handle 2-AG. And here is the hook: phytocannabinoids inhibit this kinase, with a sharp structure-activity pattern. Tetrahydrocannabinolic acid — the acidic form present in the raw plant — inhibits by about eighty percent. Delta-nine THC, its decarboxylated counterpart, manages about twenty. Same story for cannabidiolic acid versus cannabidiol, and the shorter three-carbon varin analogs are weaker than their five-carbon parents. The carboxylic acid and the pentyl chain are both doing work. The practical translation is that heating cannabis destroys the activity at this target, which means the acidic cannabinoids in unheated preparations have a pharmacology that the smoked or vaporized product largely does not. Mechanistically, the most provocative speculation is presynaptic: this kinase sits at excitatory terminals, and converting incoming 2-AG into 2-arachidonoyl-LPA could engage LPA2 signaling, which raises glutamate release probability — flipping a retrograde inhibitory signal into a facilitatory one. That's a hypothesis, not a result, but it's the right hypothesis to go test.

Briefly, on delivery. Cell-type restriction in AAV vectors is normally imposed transcriptionally with promoters and enhancers, which eat packaging capacity and often give weak expression. A group at Minnesota engineered targeting into the capsid instead: they ablated heparan sulfate binding in AAV-D-J, which kills infectivity outright, then genetically displayed a nanobody against mGluR5 in a permissive loop of the VP1 subunit, which rescues it. Transduction becomes heparin-insensitive, confirming entry no longer runs through the proteoglycan, and expression is restricted almost entirely to mGluR5-positive neurons. With a strong constitutive promoter, the engineered capsid matches or beats wild-type driven by a neuron-specific promoter, gives eight-fold higher neuronal transduction after hippocampal injection, and produces almost nothing under an astrocyte promoter despite that promoter being active in glia. And in Grm5-null hippocampus, transduction drops — the targeting is receptor-dependent. Capsid-level targeting substituting for transcriptional restriction, with the whole payload budget freed up, is a genuinely useful trade.

Three computational items to close. First, from a Yale group, the AI Neuroscientist — a tool-calling agent wrapped around a functional near-infrared spectroscopy pipeline, with a policy that forces quality control before modeling and correction before any claim of significance. The interesting result is the benchmark: an agent-agnostic six-task suite covering heartbeat-based channel quality control, motion spike detection, and anatomical localization, scored with a reliability penalty for inconsistent tool traces across repeated runs. The specialist agent, running on GPT-5 nano, scores point seven nine overall. The same small model in a general code sandbox scores point one four. Claude Sonnet 5 in the same sandbox scores point four five. On the hardest real-data tasks — poor signal-to-noise quality control and spike detection in real recordings — both sandbox agents score zero while the specialist stays near point nine. But on anatomical localization, all three land at about point five. That is the whole lesson in one table: curated tools substitute for model capability precisely where the domain has encoded knowledge the model doesn't have, and buy you nothing where the task is open-ended reasoning. If you're deciding whether to build tooling or wait for a better model, that's your decision rule.

Second, a nicely scoped virtual screening result from Institute of Science Tokyo. Boltz-2 predicts complex structures and binding affinity together, and the question is what to do with the two or three hundred activity measurements you get back from an initial screen. They fine-tune only the affinity heads on binary active-inactive labels. With three hundred labels across eight targets, average precision improves two point one four fold and actives recovered in the top one percent improve one point seven seven fold, geometric means. It beats DrugCLIP projection-head fine-tuning and it beats gradient-boosted trees trained on the same labels with fingerprint or learned-representation features — which is the comparison that matters, because the cheap baseline usually wins in the low-data regime. The gains hold for compounds with scaffolds absent from training, and restricting the expensive rescoring to the top ten percent of the initial ranking retains almost all the benefit. That last detail is the one that makes it deployable. It is retrospective, on binary labels, from the top of the ranking, and it needs a prospective test — the authors say so.

And to close, a short audit paper that I think is the most useful negative result of the week. Two high school students — and the work is careful enough that this is worth saying plainly — evaluated the EEG foundation models LaBraM and CBraMod on motor imagery under a protocol where every tunable choice, including preprocessing, freeze depth, checkpoint, and temperature, is locked using training-session data only, with the leakage guard encoded in the stored artifacts. On four-class BCI Competition data, every supervised comparator beats every foundation-model configuration, including validation-selected fine-tuning. Then they go after the standard defense, which is that the comparison is confounded because foundation models consume a different input pipeline. They retrain three supervised architectures on the same broadband arrays — and the matched-input correction goes in opposite directions depending on architecture: it helps one network by nearly eight points of accuracy and hurts another by nearly nine. None of the individual terms survives multiple-comparison correction at n equals nine, and the authors are appropriately descriptive about it. The point stands anyway. If the preprocessing confound term flips sign by comparator, then there is no single architecture-invariant way to decompose the pretrained-versus-supervised gap, and every paper that estimated that gap against one baseline was estimating something local. They also show a single validation-fitted temperature restores foundation-model calibration to the supervised range despite much worse accuracy — its own small warning about reading calibration as competence.

That's the episode. The through-line, if you want one: today's papers are mostly about targets that turn out not to be where the drug is working — antimuscarinics that don't need the cholinergic neurons, inhibition that restores firing, an endocannabinoid rerouted into a different receptor family, and a benchmark gap that changes sign depending on what you compare against.
