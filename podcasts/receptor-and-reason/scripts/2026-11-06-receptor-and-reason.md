## Script

Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Friday, November sixth, twenty twenty-six.

Today's thread is geography. Not which target you hit, but where you hit it — and the parallel question on the computational side, which is not what your model knows but what it has access to. Twelve items, and in almost all of them the interesting variable turned out to be location, route, or access rather than identity. A receptor that does two opposite things depending on which patch of membrane it sits in. A brain-delivery vehicle that destroys the drug it was supposed to deliver. A treatment that works by never entering the bloodstream. And a model-auditing result that says the capability everyone found impressive was never about reading the weights at all.

The lead is from Hamburg and Imperial College, and it is one of those results that quietly rearranges a field. We already know GPCRs signal differently from different subcellular compartments — plasma membrane versus endosome versus Golgi. That is settled. The question nobody had answered is why a single receptor subtype, activated in a single compartment, still produces different functional outcomes. The beta-one adrenergic receptor is the canonical case. It drives cardiac contractility, which is good, and it drives hypertrophic remodelling, which is what kills people in heart failure. Beta-blockers suppress both, which is why they work and also why patients at the beginning of therapy feel terrible and why you hesitate to use them right after a myocardial infarction.

The tool here is scanning ion conductance microscopy — a nanopipette that both images the cell surface topography and delivers agonist to a patch of membrane a few hundred nanometres wide — paired with FRET biosensors for cyclic AMP and for protein kinase A activity, targeted to the nucleus. So you can stimulate beta-one receptors at one specific place on a cardiomyocyte and watch where the second messenger goes.

Stimulate the receptors sitting in T-tubules, the deep invaginations that run into the cell interior, and you get contractility. Stimulate the receptors on the crest, the outer non-tubular surface, and you get a much stronger nuclear PKA response and more c-fos, the early-response transcription factor of pathological growth. Same receptor. Same compartment, in the sense that both populations are in the sarcolemma. Opposite functional destinations. And the direction is counterintuitive: T-tubules penetrate deeper into the cell and sit closer to the nucleus, so you would predict the opposite. The segregation is at least partly phosphodiesterase-maintained — inhibiting the PDE3 family abolishes the difference — and they ruled out intracellular receptor pools using a hydrophilic beta-blocker, an OCT3 transporter block, and a dynamin inhibitor. It survives in failing rat myocytes sixteen weeks post-infarction, and in human failing ventricular myocytes.

Then comes the part that makes this a pharmacology paper rather than a cell biology paper. Cardiac T-tubules act as a size-exclusion filter at roughly ten to twelve nanometres. So if you make your beta-blocker too big to fit down a T-tubule, you get a drug that blocks only the crest receptors — the remodelling pool — and leaves the contractility pool untouched. They built it twice. First as atenolol tethered to eighteen-nanometre gold nanoparticles, which blocked isoproterenol-induced hypertrophy in isolated myocytes without touching sarcomere shortening, but failed in vivo because gold nanoparticles go to liver and spleen. Then as atenolol conjugated through albumin to a non-specific immunoglobulin — bigger than twelve nanometres, roughly a seven-day plasma half-life. That construct blocked crest-stimulated signals and not T-tubular ones in the same nanopipette assay, and in two separate mouse heart failure models, infarction and transverse aortic constriction, it reduced hypertrophy and cardiac remodelling without the contractility penalty.

Think about what that is. It is selectivity achieved by steric exclusion rather than by chemistry. The pharmacophore is unchanged — it is still atenolol — and the entire selectivity gain comes from the size of what you hang it on. The obvious caveat is that a weekly injectable antibody conjugate is not going to displace a cheap oral beta-blocker on cost, and the authors hold the patent, so read the enthusiasm accordingly. But as a principle this generalises anywhere a receptor population is physically partitioned by a geometry with a size cutoff, and the nervous system has several.

The counterexample arrived two days later from a group working on brain delivery, and it is the better teaching case because it is a negative result. Fibroblast growth factor twenty-one is a metabolic hormone with real anti-obesity effects that acts both peripherally and centrally, and its central receptors are sparse — so the natural move is to bolt it onto a transferrin receptor brain shuttle and push more of it across the blood-brain barrier. They fused FGF21 to an engineered Fc fragment that binds human transferrin receptor. In wild-type mice, where that Fc does not engage mouse receptor, it produced weight loss identical to plain Fc-FGF21. In transferrin-receptor-humanised mice — the condition the vehicle was designed for — it stopped working entirely, because engaging the receptor collapsed its circulating half-life. Converting from a homodimer with two shuttle arms to a bispecific with a single arm partially restored both half-life and efficacy. Dropping the humanised receptor from homozygous to heterozygous did not help much.

So valency, not affinity, sets the cost. And the deeper point is that for a hormone whose central receptor density is low, sustained systemic exposure was the dominant determinant of effect all along — the brain shuttle was solving the wrong problem and billing you for it in clearance. If you are designing a transferrin-receptor vehicle for anything with a peripheral mechanism, this is the control experiment you need.

Third, a route-of-administration idea I had not seen before, from China Pharmaceutical University. They call it intestinal molecular siphoning. The premise is that if you destroy a small molecule fast enough inside the gut lumen, you create a concentration gradient that pulls it out of blood and into the gut — so you can lower a systemic drug level using an agent that never enters the circulation. Nicotine is the model. They took a NicA2-homologous nicotine oxidase, made it trypsin-resistant by semi-rational mutagenesis at predicted cleavage sites, and got a mutant with a trypsin half-life of about a thousand minutes against twenty-seven for wild type, with a six-fold higher turnover number. Because the enzyme makes hydrogen peroxide — enough to kill intestinal epithelial cells in culture — they co-displayed a manganese catalase, using two different spore coat anchors because sharing one anchor caused steric interference. All of that goes on the surface of Bacillus subtilis spores, germination operon deleted, inside pH-responsive enteric microspheres.

In mice, oral pretreatment cut peak brain nicotine to about thirty percent and peak blood nicotine to about thirteen percent of control. Nicotine-evoked dopamine release dropped from roughly two-and-a-half-fold over baseline to about one-and-a-half-fold. Chronic exposure experiments showed preserved paw withdrawal thresholds, restored locomotion after withdrawal, and — this is the interesting one — prevention of the alpha-four and alpha-seven nicotinic receptor upregulation, which varenicline, used as the comparator, did not prevent even though it rescued the behaviour. The spores cleared from faeces within a week with no microbiome shift.

Two things to hold onto. The nicotine was given intravenously or intraperitoneally, which is the right pharmacological control but not how anyone smokes — an inhaled bolus hits the brain before the gut has any say. And the authors are admirably direct that they never measured transintestinal nicotine flux, which means the siphoning mechanism itself is inferred from the outcome. Still, as a platform argument for uremic toxins, drug overdose, or anything where you want to lower a blood level without putting a drug in the blood, it is a genuinely different lever.

Staying with where the action happens, a Boston group used human induced pluripotent stem cell models to ask what the CD40-CD40 ligand axis actually does inside the CNS compartment in multiple sclerosis. The pathway is well established in peripheral autoimmunity and is already drugged — anti-CD40 ligand antibodies are in late-stage MS development. What has been missing is the CNS-intrinsic consequence. CD40 ligand together with interferon gamma, the combination a reactivated T cell at a CNS border would present, drove human stem-cell-derived microglia into clustering, morphological transformation, heavy interleukin-twelve, TNF-alpha and interleukin-six release, and outright cell death. In a tri-culture of neurons, astrocytes and microglia, that stimulus killed neurons by way of the microglia, and anti-CD40 ligand antibody substantially rescued both. The transcriptional signature they derive correlates with CD40 expression in primary progressive MS brain tissue but not in normal-appearing white matter from controls. It is a dish, with all the usual limits, but it gives the compartmentalised-inflammation argument for CD40 ligand blockade a mechanism rather than an association.

Two Alzheimer's items, both about whether a deficit is cause or consequence. The first, in Molecular Psychiatry, goes after mitochondrial complex four. Complex four dysfunction in Alzheimer's has been reported for decades, and the standing ambiguity is whether it drives pathology or is a downstream adaptation. The clever move is to bypass rather than repair: they expressed alternative oxidase, the single-polypeptide enzyme from lower eukaryotes that short-circuits the complex three-to-four segment of the respiratory chain, and asked whether bypassing the deficit rescues the phenotype. It does — mitochondrial function and synaptic loss improve in both mouse and human-derived models of Alzheimer's risk. And the relationship runs both ways: complex four dysfunction increases amyloidogenic processing of amyloid precursor protein, so the deficit feeds the amyloid that caused it. They close with a proteome-wide association study implicating reduced COX11, a complex four assembly chaperone, in Alzheimer's risk. I would weight that last piece lightly — the significance threshold was an unadjusted p below zero point zero five picked from the top mitochondrial proteins, which is a soft bar. The bypass experiment is the load-bearing result, and it is a nice one, because it converts a correlation into a causal claim without having to rebuild a thirteen-subunit complex.

Also in Molecular Psychiatry, a model of maternal depression that starts before conception rather than during pregnancy. Female mice were socially isolated from weaning through gestation and lactation, and the adult offspring were phenotyped with high-resolution ex vivo structural MRI, diffusion tensor imaging and thalamic transcriptomics. The offspring show robust anxiety-like behaviour, reduced relative thalamic and striatal volume with compensatory expansion of gustatory, visceral and visual cortex, and a multivariate thalamostriatal volume pattern that covaries with the behavioural deficit. The transcriptomics are widely sex-dependent, hitting synaptic organisation, protein synthesis and myelination. The methylation analysis produced nominal pathway enrichments that did not survive false discovery correction, and the authors say so plainly rather than burying it. The reason this matters for drug development is that almost every preclinical model of maternal stress begins the insult during gestation, which means we have been testing interventions against an exposure window that may be the wrong one.

A third Molecular Psychiatry paper takes the ideation-to-action framework — the idea that what predicts a suicide attempt is not the same as what predicts suicidal thinking — and asks whether it holds genetically. Up to seventeen thousand six hundred participants from the Australian depression and bipolar genetics studies, with polygenic scores for ideation, attempt and death modelled jointly across mutually exclusive phenotypes. The ideation and attempt scores behave distinctly: ideation score is associated with lower odds of ideation alone but higher odds of ideation plus attempt, and the attempt score predicts attempt outcomes robustly. The sharpest signal is the transition model, where attempt-polygenic score predicts twenty-three percent higher odds of attempt among people who are already ideating, with an absolute risk difference of nine to twelve percent between top and bottom deciles. The death-by-suicide score showed nothing robust. The discrimination gain is small — area under the curve improves by about two to three hundredths — and the authors do not oversell it. But the structure of the finding is the point: the genetics agree with the clinical theory that ideation and action are separable processes, which is an argument for stratifying ideators rather than screening everyone.

A fourth clinical-measurement item, this one from Nature Mental Health, on EEG markers of depression severity. The framing is a deliberate break with the usual approach: instead of hunting for a focal feature like frontal alpha asymmetry, they assume the information is distributed, and build an index from phase-resetting rate modulation — source-locked, time-lagged modulation of how often phase resets propagate between electrodes. In two hundred seventy-six untreated adults spanning a broad symptom range, an aggregate of individually weak upper-alpha and low-gamma features achieves cross-validated correlation above zero point six with symptom scores and about eighty percent accuracy separating moderate-to-severe from low symptoms. Untreated is the important word — most EEG depression work is confounded by medication. It is cross-sectional, self-reported severity, and needs independent validation in clinically characterised cohorts, which they say. But as a candidate objective severity measure that is mechanistically interpretable rather than a black-box classifier, it is worth tracking.

Now to the computational half, and the same geography question in a different costume. A group at Tsinghua's Institute for AI Industry Research and the company AIPher released PharmAgents2, pitched as autonomous end-to-end drug discovery — a prototype virtual pharmaceutical company. The architecture has two ideas worth knowing. One is Paper2Skill, which extracts transferable decision knowledge from published literature and patents into explicit skills, so that the system's judgment is stated rather than implicit in a prompt. The illustrative example is good: an initial skill says the research contrast is Alzheimer's versus control; the refined skill says specify advanced fibrosis versus mild in the NASH case, and do not simply select the strongest-scoring drug profile, prespecify a rule. The second idea is self-scaling discovery, where parallel subagents inherit accumulated research context rather than receiving it compressed into a prompt.

The wet-lab validation is where you should spend your attention. For the antibacterial target LpxH, the system hypothesised an allosteric site distinct from the established L-shaped substrate pocket, screened an in-stock library against it, and nominated sixty-three compounds. Humans bought and tested all sixty-three. One hit: a two-hundred-eighty-two-dalton molecule with enzyme inhibition at eight point four micromolar, surface plasmon resonance affinity of one point four micromolar, microscale thermophoresis at zero point eight seven, and minimum inhibitory concentrations of thirty-two micrograms per millilitre against efflux-pump-deficient strains only. For JAK1, thirty-four compounds tested, one with activity comparable to the positive control. And in a secondary-target exploration across two thousand six hundred proteins, the top-ranked nomination showed binding at four point six micromolar.

So: a novel-scaffold micromolar hit from sixty-three purchases, no antibacterial activity in a strain with an intact efflux pump, and a binding site that the authors say is still being determined. That is a respectable early hit-finding campaign. It is not a drug, and the gap between "autonomous end-to-end drug discovery" and what was actually demonstrated is the whole medicinal chemistry programme. What I think is genuinely new is not the hit — it is that the system chose an unvalidated allosteric site over the obvious pocket and articulated three testable mechanistic hypotheses for why binding there might matter. That is a research decision, and it is the kind of thing these systems have previously been bad at.

Which sets up the sharpest paper of the day, out of Tianjin Medical University, with a title that is also its conclusion: computation, not weight access. The background is a report from earlier this month that a frontier model could read the raw weights of small neural networks, say what they had been trained to do, write working policy networks, and repair a language model by editing weights without gradient training. The most pointed critique was that the headline conflated two conditions — reading raw weights with no tools, and analysing a larger model by writing and running code. These authors separated them, in pre-specified experiments, on a problem where it matters: auditing clinical prediction models that regulators and reviewers receive without the training data.

They trained tanh networks of one hundred five to thirty-eight thousand weights on simulated subarachnoid haemorrhage cohorts, each with a planted relationship or none, plus one trained on labels that under-recorded deaths in a subgroup. Then five models, each under either a default or an explicit system instruction, and under three access conditions: weights in the prompt with no tools, a calculation-only tool exposing the weights, or the same tool exposing only the network's predictions.

Without tools, the frontier model recovered planted relationships in a three-hundred-fifty-weight network but became unreliable by three thousand — and under the default instruction it declined eleven of twelve audit and repair tasks as unidentifiable. With a calculator, it recovered the planted relationship in all twenty-nine runs up to thirty-eight thousand weights, raised no false alarm in fifteen null-network runs, and found the biased subgroup six times out of six. Black-box access — predictions only, no weights — performed as well as white-box. And a well-designed script with no model in it matched the comparator models on the audit and on the best repair.

Three conclusions, each one load-bearing. First, the impressive capability was computation, and the weights were never the point. Second, elicitation is a confound we are systematically underweighting: one paragraph of system instruction converted near-total refusal into reliable performance, without adding false alarms in the controls. If your evaluation runs a single default-prompt reply, you are measuring a model's willingness to attempt the task, not its ability to do it. Third, the honest comparator — a script — wins or ties, which puts the model's added value in designing and conducting the audit rather than in anything beyond a well-specified program.

Keep that comparator question in mind for the next one, in npj Digital Medicine, which audits LLM-as-a-judge for clinical text in German. Six open-weight models against expert judges, four datasets covering report and nursing documentation summarisation, discharge summary generation and patient letters, three evaluation paradigms and four prompt configurations. No configuration was reliable across paradigms. Every judge showed position and identity bias. Substituting the model for the expert panel shifted fifty-seven percent of pointwise category assignments, swapped forty-two percent of pairwise winners, missed ten percent of clinical errors, and — the bit that should worry you most — inverted clinical priorities, overrating succinctness and apparent accuracy. A pooled agreement metric would have hidden all of that, which is exactly why most of the existing favourable evidence looks favourable. If you are using a model as a judge outside English, this is your prior now.

And to finish, the most abstract item, in Nature Machine Intelligence, which supplies a mechanism for something everyone has experienced. Deep learning training outcomes vary even when you control every external source of randomness — fixed seed, fixed data order, deterministic hardware. The proposal is riddled basin geometry: every initialisation leading to one solution sits arbitrarily close to initialisations leading to a different one, a fat fractal with structure at every scale. In a minimal network they establish this analytically and measure the uncertainty exponent — how fast your chance of predicting the outcome improves as you increase initialisation precision — at about zero point zero one three. In real deep networks it is zero to within two thousandths, on deterministic CPU training as well as non-deterministic GPU. They then extend it from parameter space to function space, where the fraction of inputs on which two near-identically-initialised networks disagree plateaus around three percent and does not shrink with precision.

The practical reading: irreproducibility in deep learning is not entirely a bookkeeping failure you can engineer away with better seeding discipline. Some of it is the geometry of the loss landscape, and for that portion, more precision buys you essentially nothing. Which rhymes, I think, with where we started. The lead paper's whole point was that the same receptor in the same compartment lands in different functional basins depending on a spatial detail nobody was measuring. Here the same architecture with the same data lands in different functional basins depending on a numerical detail that cannot be measured finely enough to matter. In both cases the variable that decides the outcome was never the one on the label.
