## Script

Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Saturday, October third. Ten items today, and the retrieval situation is much better than it has been: bioRxiv's full-text channel, which had been returning nothing but rate-limit stubs for most of a week, started serving PDFs again partway through the run, so several preprints I'd been carrying as deferrals finally got read properly. Today's lineup leans structural and pharmacological at the front, moves through clinical trials and imaging, and closes with computational work and one methodological warning shot.

We start with a structure that has been conspicuously missing from the field. A group at the Shanghai Institute of Organic Chemistry has solved cryo-EM structures of human VIAAT — the vesicular inhibitory amino acid transporter, encoded by SLC32A1, also known as VGAT. This is the protein that loads both GABA and glycine into synaptic vesicles, and it is the only transporter known to do so. Every inhibitory synapse in the vertebrate nervous system depends on it, and until now there was no atomic structure of it at all. They got two states at roughly three-point-three angstroms: an apo luminal-open state and a GABA-bound occluded state.

The first thing that falls out is architectural, and it's a genuine surprise if you've been grouping the vesicular transporters together. The vesicular glutamate, monoamine, and acetylcholine transporters all adopt major facilitator superfamily folds. VIAAT does not — it's an eleven-transmembrane-helix LeuT-like fold, and the closest structural relative in a database search was a lysosomal amino acid transporter with no obvious synaptic connection. So the transporter loading the brain's principal inhibitory neurotransmitters is built on a different chassis entirely, which fits the long-standing observation that VIAAT exploits both the vesicular membrane potential and the pH gradient, whereas the glutamate transporters lean mostly on voltage and the monoamine transporters mostly on pH.

In the central pocket, GABA sits in an aromatic cage of four residues — three phenylalanines and a tyrosine — with its carboxylate pointing toward one pair and its amine wedged between the other. The mechanistic result is that one of those phenylalanines, three-fifteen, does double duty — it contacts the substrate, and it swings away from the pocket in the apo state to open the luminal pathway, then rotates back in when GABA binds, which is what produces the occluded state. One residue, acting as the luminal gate. That is a motif seen before in the choline transporter, and the equivalent positions in the plasma-membrane GABA transporter GAT1 and the taurine transporter are the same two residues — conserved while essentially everything else in the pocket differs. So the field now has a clean story about what is shared and what confers specificity.

What makes this more than a structure paper is the functional arm. They knocked down endogenous VIAAT in cultured hippocampal neurons, abolishing miniature inhibitory postsynaptic currents, then rescued with human VIAAT, which fully restored them — a clean platform for testing variants. Alanine substitutions at tyrosine two-twenty-one and both of those phenylalanines markedly reduced mIPSC amplitude. And the clinically interesting part: a cysteine substitution at the gating phenylalanine three-twenty-two has already been reported in a patient with epilepsy and impaired neurodevelopment, and a methionine-to-threonine change at residue three-thirty, which the structure shows packing against the transmembrane core, is found in genetic epilepsy. The structure explains why those variants break inhibition.

They also deleted the first one-hundred-seventeen residues of the disordered N-terminal region and saw mIPSC amplitude collapse, which suggests that this flexible, unresolved cap is doing real mechanistic work rather than just dangling. The authors are honest that VIAAT is a poor direct drug target — you do not want to globally modulate the loading of all inhibitory neurotransmitters — but a selective ligand would be a genuinely useful PET tracer for imaging inhibitory terminals in Parkinson's or Huntington's, where GABAergic neurons are lost. There has never been a structural framework to design one. Now there is.

Staying with structure and ion channels, there's an elegant paper on TRPM3 from a group combining cryo-EM, molecular dynamics, mutagenesis, and medicinal chemistry. TRPM3 is a calcium-permeable channel in sensory neurons, a validated chronic-pain target, and gain-of-function mutations in it cause developmental and epileptic encephalopathies. The voltage-sensor-like domain — the four-helix bundle that in a true voltage-gated channel would be the voltage sensor, but here serves as an allosteric drug pocket — is where most known modulators bind. They solved structures with three chemically unrelated inhibitors: isosakuranetin, a citrus flavonoid; maprotiline, the tetracyclic antidepressant; and diclofenac, the NSAID. Neutral, cationic, and anionic, respectively. All three sit in the same cavity.

But they engage completely different residue networks inside it, and the mutagenesis confirms it cleanly. Maprotiline's fingerprint is a salt bridge to an aspartate plus specific hydrophobic contacts — alanine substitutions at those positions abolished its inhibition, while mutating residues that merely sit nearby in the structure left potency near wild-type. Diclofenac's carboxylate instead engages two basic residues, a lysine and an arginine, and those are the mutations that kill its effect. The simulations also support a halogen bond from one of diclofenac's chlorines, though they couldn't test it directly because that mutant had no measurable channel activity.

The result that should make a medicinal chemist sit up is the last one. They esterified diclofenac's carboxylate — the single most obvious synthetic modification you can make to that scaffold — and the compound flipped from an inhibitor to a potentiator of wild-type TRPM3. Amidation did the same. And the switch is mechanistically traceable: in the mutant panel, one phenylalanine substitution converted potentiation back into inhibition, while the lysine mutation that destroyed diclofenac's inhibition had no effect on the ester's potentiation. So inhibition and potentiation run through overlapping but distinguishable poses in the same pocket, and the thing that sets the direction is a single functional group. The broader lesson: a pocket that tolerates neutral, cationic, and anionic ligands alike is not a lock-and-key, and efficacy at such a site is not a stable property of a chemotype. Run a structure-activity series against an allosteric pocket counting only potency, and you can walk straight past a sign flip.

Third item, and it's the kind of paper that quietly makes everyone's data more comparable. A group has profiled twenty-two structurally diverse cannabinoid ligands — synthetic cannabinoid receptor agonists, THC analogues, endocannabinoid analogues, and antagonists and inverse agonists — at CB1 and CB2 in matched BRET assays, measuring G-alpha-i-one engagement and beta-arrestin-two recruitment, with every single response normalized to the same reference full agonist, CP55,940. The motivation is the mess the field is in: the efficacy classifications everyone cites were assembled across different labs and assay platforms, often with amplified indirect readouts and different reference agonists, so claims that one ligand is a partial agonist and another full are frequently not comparable at all.

Run on a common scale, the picture tightens, and the headline is a deflation. Twenty-one of the twenty-two ligands showed no meaningful bias at all by the standard transduction-coefficient criterion — not one exceeded the threshold at CB1, and the single biased compound was one synthetic agonist at CB2. The synthetic agonists and THC analogues are high-efficacy at CB1 and also highly efficacious at recruiting beta-arrestin-two; they drive both transducers hard. What varies here is not the balance between pathways but how strongly a ligand drives each receptor. That matters because much of the biased-signaling literature in this family rests on efficacy comparisons drawn across exactly the heterogeneous datasets this paper replaces — and because BRET reads proximal coupling rather than an amplified second messenger, so receptor reserve stops masquerading as intrinsic activity. The most consistent trend across the whole panel was a loss of agonist efficacy going from CB1 to CB2. And among the blockers, the inverse agonists suppressed constitutive CB1 activity more than the neutral antagonists — a distinction that is clinically live given rimonabant's history and the current interest in peripherally restricted and neutral CB1 blockade. This is infrastructure rather than discovery, but a common efficacy scale is the thing that lets the next structure-function argument actually be tested.

From receptor pharmacology to a clinical imaging study. A team at King's College London ran a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover study comparing single clinical doses of guanfacine extended-release and lisdexamfetamine against placebo in youth with ADHD, with fMRI during three tasks — a tracking stop-signal task, an N-back, and a sustained attention task — plus twenty-one matched typically developing controls imaged once to test whether either drug normalizes case-control differences. The design is strong; the numbers are small, and you should hold the findings at that weight: twelve participants on the stop-signal task, eleven on N-back, ten on sustained attention.

What they found is a fairly consistent asymmetry. Lisdexamfetamine produced drug-exclusive increases — right caudate during N-back, putamen during sustained attention, precuneus and posterior cingulate during the stop-signal task relative to guanfacine, and sensorimotor and occipital increases during N-back relative to placebo. Both drugs shared a left superior temporal increase during N-back. On the case-control contrast, under placebo the ADHD group showed the expected underactivation in putamen, insula, parietal and occipital regions during the stop-signal task, and that pattern was attenuated under lisdexamfetamine; under guanfacine, lingual gyrus underactivation persisted and additional precuneus and middle temporal differences appeared. And only lisdexamfetamine improved stop-signal reaction time — significantly faster than placebo, with the ADHD-versus-control performance gap no longer significant on drug.

The honest reading comes with a structural caveat the authors raise themselves, and it's the one that matters most: guanfacine takes weeks to reach clinical effect, so a single-dose design is biased toward the stimulant by construction. Add roughly a dozen participants per task and no correction across the different region-of-interest masks, and no single cluster should be taken to the bank. What it adds is mechanistic texture for choosing between a stimulant and an alpha-2A agonist: the stimulant's effects were broader, included basal ganglia, and tracked an actual behavioral improvement in inhibitory control, while guanfacine's were narrower and did not — consistent with the clinical impression that non-stimulants are a different kind of intervention rather than a weaker version of the same one.

Next, ibogaine — and a mechanistic proposal that I think is more interesting than the usual receptor-binding account. A Dalhousie group looked at what ibogaine does to neuronal energetics. In mouse cortical neurons, ibogaine at three, ten, or thirty micromolar produced a rapid, concentration-dependent suppression of both mitochondrial respiration and glycolysis — inside five minutes. Imaging showed the sequence: mitochondrial membrane potential rises first, then metabolism is suppressed, then mitochondrial reactive oxygen species go up. Inhibitors of the mitochondrial Kv1.3 potassium channel produce concordant changes on the same timescale, and ibogaine is a potassium channel blocker, which is where the mechanistic hypothesis comes from.

The part that gives the paper its shape is what happens next. Wash the drug out at four hours, and twenty hours later energy production rebounds past baseline — overshoot, not recovery. That is the signature of metabolic preconditioning: a sublethal stress provoking an adaptive response that exceeds where it started. BDNF message follows the same dip-then-rebound curve, and in astrocytes and microglia the suppression had already resolved by four hours, so the sustained effect is neuron-selective. They then gave mice oral ibogaine and ran proteomics, and the time course lines up — stress pathways up at fifteen minutes, AMP-activated protein kinase transiently up while mTOR and lipid synthesis are suppressed through twenty-four hours, and by seventy-two hours every synaptic plasticity pathway they tracked sitting above vehicle. Ibogaine also binds the sigma-2 receptor with high affinity, so they nominate mitochondrial Kv1.3 and sigma-2 as the entry points.

What I like about this is that it reframes the question. The dominant framing for psychedelic-adjacent plasticity has been receptor-first — find the receptor, find the downstream signaling, explain the dendritic spines. This proposes that for ibogaine the proximal event is a metabolic insult and the plasticity is the adaptive response to it, with the critical window opening on the rebound rather than during drug exposure. If that's right, the relevant pharmacokinetic variable isn't peak exposure, it's the shape and timing of the recovery — which bears directly on dosing intervals and on whether therapeutic work belongs during or after the acute experience. The caveats are real. The mechanism is inferential — mitochondrial Kv1.3 and sigma-2 are nominated by analogy to what their selective ligands do, not tested with knockouts or blockers. The concentrations are high micromolar. The proteomics is spinal cord, not the forebrain regions you'd care about for addiction. And the plasticity read-out is pathway abundance, with no behavior, no electrophysiology, and no synapse counts behind it.

Now a negative result that should change how people write their introductions. For well over a decade, spinal microglia have been the standard explanation for opioid-induced hyperalgesia and analgesic tolerance — it's in review after review. A group went after that directly. Single-cell transcriptomics of spinal microglia from opioid-withdrawn mice reproduced the morphological changes everyone reports, but the transcriptomic changes were modest — the cells look different without being transcriptionally transformed. Then two sharper tests. Peripherally restricted opioid administration, which doesn't reach the spinal cord, was sufficient to produce both hyperalgesia and the microglial morphology change, decoupling that morphology from any central drug action. And in mice carrying a deletion of the intronic regulatory element in the Csf1r gene — which produces a complete, lifelong absence of microglia, rather than the partial depletions that have muddied this literature before — hyperalgesia, analgesic tolerance, and somatic withdrawal all still developed, with acute morphine analgesia and locomotor response unchanged. That distinction matters because the standard depletion tool, the CSF1R inhibitor PLX5622, interferes with hepatic metabolism of morphine itself. The authors' conclusion is blunt: these data argue against microglial involvement in the development of these phenomena. Complete absence of the cell type you've nominated as the mechanism, and the phenotype is unchanged — that's about as clean a disconfirmation as this kind of model permits, and a fair number of ongoing programs are built on the assumption it refutes.

A clinical trial next: phase one of transcranial magnetic stimulation added to behavioral therapy for tics. Comprehensive Behavioral Intervention for Tics is first-line, but only about half of children and forty percent of adults benefit. Fifty youth aged twelve to twenty-one with moderate-to-severe tics were randomized three ways — one-hertz repetitive TMS, continuous theta-burst, or sham, all to the supplementary motor area, each session immediately followed by the behavioral therapy, ten sessions. Primary outcomes were safety, feasibility, and neural target engagement rather than efficacy, which is the right design for this stage.

It was feasible, with ninety-six percent completion, and adverse events were mild to moderate but more frequent with one-hertz stimulation — twenty-seven percent of sessions, versus about fifteen percent for theta-burst and eleven for sham. On target engagement the two active arms diverged in a way nobody would have predicted from dose-equivalence reasoning. Theta-burst did what you'd hope: it reduced resting-state connectivity between the supplementary motor area and primary motor cortex, and between putamen and primary motor cortex — the cortico-striatal circuit implicated in tics. One-hertz stimulation did something worse than nothing: it blunted the cortico-striatal connectivity reductions that the sham group showed from behavioral therapy alone, and premonitory urges went up in that arm while falling in both sham and theta-burst. All three groups improved on tic frequency and controllability, which is what you expect when everyone gets the active therapy. The take-home is that one of the two standard inhibitory TMS protocols appears to have interfered with the therapy it was meant to augment, and the design — target engagement first, efficacy later — is what caught it. Theta-burst goes forward.

Two computational items to close. The first is the most clinically ambitious agentic system I've seen evaluated carefully. StrokeAgent is a decision-support framework for acute ischemic stroke built around the observation that the pathway is a sequence of decisions under evolving information, not one classification at the end. It ingests clinical records, labs, raw ECG waveforms and source CT, and issues and revises recommendations at triage, at the thrombolysis decision, and at reperfusion planning, each grounded in an auditable chain of findings.

Evaluated on a hundred patients enriched for complex reperfusion scenarios, it reached eighty-six percent concordance with expert-adjudicated final decisions — thirteen points above the mean of three tool-free language models and seventeen above the mean of six resident and attending neurologists, with information matched across comparators. Complete-pathway concordance, meaning agreement at every applicable decision point, was seventy-six percent, with much larger margins. And in twelve hundred blinded clinician assessments it rated higher on clinical utility than every language model, with fewer outputs judged potentially seriously harmful.

But I'd argue the headline number is the least interesting result in the paper. Because every recommendation carries a logged chain, the authors could audit all fourteen discordant cases individually — and that audit is the real finding. Seven were component or tool errors: a misread stroke-scale score, a missed large-vessel occlusion, a botched contraindication screen, a wrong ASPECTS. The other seven were synthesis errors. Which means half the failures were factual mistakes at the perception layer that then propagated into perfectly coherent downstream reasoning — the model arguing impeccably from a wrong number. And ten of the fourteen erred in the same direction: withholding or de-escalating reperfusion. A system with a consistent bias toward undertreatment is a very specific safety profile, and you only know that because the chain was auditable.

A few things keep me calibrated. The cohort was enriched for complex cases, the right stress test but one that inflates the gap against clinicians who ordinarily see mostly straightforward presentations, and a quarter of the records were constructed rather than real. The timing comparison is confounded — clinicians were handed post-processed perfusion maps while the system computed its own, which is why it was actually slower at the final planning stage. And this is retrospective concordance with adjudicated decisions, not outcomes.

The second computational item is a careful piece of pharmacometrics with an unglamorous and useful conclusion. Model averaging — combining several population pharmacokinetic models rather than picking one — is increasingly used for model-informed precision dosing, but nobody has systematically asked how to choose which models go into the ensemble. Using tacrolimus in thirty-seven kidney and liver transplant recipients, this group evaluated every possible ensemble drawn from a pool of twenty-seven published models. Imprecision plateaued beyond three or four: adding more stops helping. And the magnitude is not subtle — the best three-model ensemble reached about nineteen percent relative error, while throwing all twenty-seven in together gave thirty-six percent. Averaging everything published is roughly twice as bad as choosing three models well.

The reason is complementarity rather than individual quality, and there's a lovely illustration of it: one of the three models in the winning trio was, on its own, the single worst performer in the entire pool, at eighty-eight percent error. It earns its place by making different mistakes than its partners. The corollary is that models which look interchangeable are not — substituting models correlated at point-nine still changed the error substantially; you needed correlations above point-nine-eight before swaps were safe. Composition also depends on what data you have: three models from a single trough, seven or eight with richer sampling, and the ensemble optimized on one observation set generalized poorly to others, which is garden-variety overfitting showing up where people may not be looking for it. Only a limited sampling strategy kept imprecision under twenty percent at all; a single trough was not enough. The plateau replicated in infliximab and cefepime. Their one-line summary is the right one: ensemble composition matters more than ensemble size.

Which brings me to the last item, which is short, slightly funny, and genuinely unsettling. A group with an automated home-cage phenotyping system — ninety-six cages recorded continuously — asked whether the act of a human checking on the animals changes the data. Two cohorts got semaglutide at a low dose, three-hundredths of a milligram per kilogram; one was recorded with no post-dosing inspection, the other got a visual health check lasting at most a minute, with no handling at all. Nobody touched a mouse. In the minute after the cage door opened, sleep dropped from a median proportion of seventy-nine percent to fifteen, with walking and rotation up correspondingly, and none of that appeared in either unchecked cohort. More striking, jumping and wall climbing spiked in the minute before the door was even opened — the animals were responding to the experimenter's proximity, not the disturbance. Worse than the added variance: during the observation window, the drug-treated animals' behavior looked more like the undrugged baseline than like the semaglutide reference condition. The observation transiently erased the drug effect it was there to measure.

The title is "Schrödinger's mouse," and the pun is earned. Every one of us has been trained to think of a health check as a neutral read operation. In a home-cage paradigm with minute-scale resolution, it is a perturbation large enough to mask the pharmacology — which means a nontrivial amount of published behavioral pharmacology may have measured the drug under conditions where the drug's effect was suppressed.
