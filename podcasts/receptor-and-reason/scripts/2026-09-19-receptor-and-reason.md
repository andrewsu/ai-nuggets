## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. I'm your host, and this is your daily pass through CNS pharmacology, psychiatric and neurology drug discovery, and the computational work closing in on both. Twelve items today, and the thread running through most of them is the gap between engagement and effect — the repeated finding that showing a drug, a stimulator, or a model has hold of the right target tells you remarkably little about what happens downstream.

The lead makes that point with the cleanest possible design. A group at Mass General ran simultaneous PET and MRI in healthy volunteers and in non-human primates, giving graded intravenous doses of prochlorperazine — the first-generation D2 antagonist most clinicians know as an antiemetic rather than as an antipsychotic — while measuring occupancy with carbon-eleven raclopride and, in the same scan, measuring the hemodynamic response with arterial spin labeling. Two results, and they matter for different reasons.

The first is a straightforward pharmacology finding that ought to change prescribing intuitions. The dose giving fifty percent striatal D2 occupancy in humans was one-point-two milligrams per hundred kilograms. Work that forward: a standard ten-milligram intravenous antiemetic dose puts essentially every patient under two hundred kilograms above eighty percent occupancy, and five milligrams does it for anyone under a hundred. Eighty percent is the conventional threshold above which extrapyramidal symptoms emerge. So the antiemetic dose is not a low dose that happens to touch D2 — it is a full antipsychotic blockade, and that squares with the roughly one in three incidence of akathisia after ten milligrams intravenous, and with the observation that five and ten milligrams produce indistinguishable side-effect rates. Both are far past the ceiling.

The second result is the conceptual one. Occupancy rose smoothly with dose, but the functional response did not follow it. Cerebral blood flow stayed essentially flat until about sixty percent occupancy, then rose steeply — markedly superlinear, and the same shape in both species. Their biophysical model explains it with autoreceptor feedback: blocking presynaptic D2 releases dopamine, that released dopamine competes with the antagonist at postsynaptic sites, and postsynaptic occupancy by endogenous dopamine is therefore held roughly constant until mass action finally wins. The model fits with about a threefold dopamine rise at seventy percent antagonist occupancy, which is in the range microdialysis reports in rats.

What that gives you is an imaging correlate for the lower edge of the antipsychotic therapeutic window — an explanation, in homeostatic terms, for why sixty percent is where clinical benefit starts. And it is a caution for anyone using occupancy as a surrogate endpoint in CNS drug development: at low and moderate occupancies, the system compensates, and target engagement is genuinely not predictive of functional consequence. The authors draw the obvious inference for schizophrenia — that treating striatal hyperdopaminergia with a drug that raises dopamine is a strange strategy, and that xanomeline, which lowers acetylcholine and therefore dopamine without touching D2 at all, or D3-preferring approaches, sidestep the problem rather than fighting it.

Second item, and it stays on the theme that the drug target is only half the story. A collaboration between the Queensland Brain Institute and the Institute of Neurosciences in Alicante asked why male and female mice differ in anxiety-like behavior, and looked not at serotonin receptor or transporter levels — the usual explanation — but at the wiring itself. Using activity-dependent labeling of dorsal raphe serotonergic neurons, they found female-biased recruitment, denser serotonergic innervation of hippocampus and basolateral amygdala, and higher basal serotonin release.

Two things elevate this above another sex-differences paper. One is a methodological point they are explicit about: the behavioral difference is only visible in short time windows. Average over a full open-field session and it disappears; look at the first five minutes and males and females differ in distance, velocity and thigmotaxis, and the pattern reproduces across two laboratories with different housing. Females mount a stronger initial response that normalizes with continued exposure. That is a temporal phenotype, and standard summary statistics erase it.

The other is that they went looking for a molecular handle. Single-cell sequencing of dorsal raphe serotonergic cells with sex as a biological variable pointed at ErbB4, the receptor tyrosine kinase, expressed in about thirty-one percent of serotonergic cells in females versus forty-one percent in males. Conditionally deleting it from serotonergic neurons altered amygdala innervation and shifted behavior — selectively in males. That last detail is the honest one. A wiring gene whose deletion moves only one sex is consistent with their model but also underlines how far this is from a therapeutic story. What it does support is a real claim about sex differences in treatment response: if the circuits differ structurally, not just neurochemically, then a drug acting on the shared neurochemistry will land differently, and that is a mechanistic reason to stop pooling sexes.

Third, a short one from the International Journal of Neuropsychopharmacology, and I should say this is the one item today I am summarizing from a self-contained abstract rather than full text. A group dosed sexually experienced male mice with MK-801, the non-competitive NMDA antagonist, and recorded ultrasonic vocalizations during male-female interactions, classifying calls into eleven categories with an automated tool. MK-801 dose-dependently suppressed the number of calls from the dyad while leaving the treated male's physical proximity to the female unchanged. In the same-sex preference task, social preference survived at every dose even though time near the social stimulus fell at the top dose. Locomotion went up, fecal and urine output went down.

The reason to care is that vocalization and approach dissociate. If you are using NMDA antagonist models for negative symptoms or social deficits, "reduced social behavior" is not one thing — the animal still goes to the conspecific and stops talking. And the concurrent locomotor and autonomic effects are exactly the confounds that make single-readout studies in this model hard to interpret.

Fourth, the largest copy-number study yet in tic disorders, out in Molecular Psychiatry. Three consortia pooled with thirty-two hundred new cases gave fifty-seven hundred Tourette syndrome and persistent tic disorder cases against just under eleven thousand controls, with all CNVs recalled consistently across harmonized array groups rather than meta-analyzed from heterogeneous prior calls. That recalling step is why the sample size more than doubling actually bought power.

They confirm the expected burden signals — more ultra-rare deletions hitting loss-of-function intolerant genes, odds ratio one-point-seven, and more established neurodevelopmental CNVs, odds ratio one-point-four. The new finding is a genome-wide significant locus: duplications at 17q12. Eight cases and one control carried the canonical one-point-four megabase duplication, and one further case carried a hundred-and-ten kilobase duplication inside it spanning only ACACA, acetyl-CoA carboxylase alpha. That single-gene case is the interesting one and also the fragile one — an odds ratio of twenty-seven on one carrier is a pointer, not a result. 17q12 duplication is already a recognized syndrome with variable neuropsychiatric expression; adding tics to its phenotype is the solid contribution here.

Fifth, staying with genetics but moving to the pharmacology-adjacent question of who a risk score actually works for. A Tübingen and Oslo group built a lysosomal-pathway polygenic risk score in Parkinson's disease using the Global Parkinson's Genetics Program data — thirty-two thousand idiopathic cases and eighteen thousand controls across ten ancestries. Pathway-restricted scores are attractive because they promise mechanistic interpretability: if lysosomal variants specifically carry risk, that is a tractable target class.

The score works, modestly, and overwhelmingly in Europeans. Area under the curve was zero-point-six-zero in the European test set and zero-point-six-three in an independent Tübingen validation cohort, versus zero-point-five-one to zero-point-five-nine everywhere else. It separated GBA1-associated Parkinson's from controls and, more usefully, from non-manifesting GBA1 carriers at an AUC of zero-point-six-two — which is the clinically interesting comparison, because it speaks to penetrance. Higher scores tracked earlier age at onset and worse UPDRS scores, though the authors are candid that not all of those clinical associations replicated in the validation cohort.

Two takeaways. The lysosomal pathway does carry polygenic signal above chance, which supports it as a target axis. And a pathway score with an AUC of point-six in Europeans and point-five-one in other ancestries is not a stratification tool yet — it is a description of where the GWAS data came from.

Sixth, still in Parkinson's, and back to target engagement. Transcutaneous auricular vagus nerve stimulation is one of those interventions with a plausible mechanism and an inconsistent literature: stimulate the auricular branch of the vagus, engage the nucleus tractus solitarius, reach the locus coeruleus, modulate noradrenaline. The locus coeruleus degenerates early in Parkinson's and is a reasonable target for non-motor symptoms. But the clinical trials have been scattered, with effects on gait parameters and reaction time and nothing consistent on the motor rating scale.

This group asked a more basic question first: does the stimulation actually reach the brainstem? Twenty-one patients and eighteen age-matched controls, real versus sham in a randomized crossover, with continuous pupillometry as the readout, since pupil diameter indexes locus coeruleus activity. Real stimulation produced significantly greater pupil dilation than sham in the patients, in a window roughly two-point-seven to three-point-six seconds after onset. In healthy older controls, nothing. An auditory oddball control task elicited normal deviant-related pupil responses in both groups, so this is not a story about controls having a dead noradrenergic system.

That is a genuinely odd result and the authors do not over-read it. The honest interpretation is that patients are physiologically responsive to the stimulation in a way healthy older adults are not — possibly a floor-and-headroom effect in a partially degenerated system. Either way, this is the sort of target-engagement biomarker the taVNS field has been missing, and it is cheap.

Seventh, a disease-modification claim in Alzheimer's that I want to look at carefully. Laromestrocel is an allogeneic bone-marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cell therapy; the CLEAR-MIND phase 2a study previously reported effects on composite cognitive scores and reduced brain atrophy. This new analysis goes back to the trial MRI and extracts free-water measures — a diffusion metric increasingly used as a non-invasive index of neuroinflammation — in forty-nine participants with mild Alzheimer's.

The reported effects are large and regionally sensible: reduced progressive inflammation in left hippocampus in both the twenty-five-million-cell and hundred-million-cell four-dose groups, in left temporal and parietal cortex, and in the medulla, at thirty-nine weeks against placebo. Reduced inflammation correlated with reduced atrophy and with better MoCA scores, and plasma neurogranin fell in the lower-dose group, consistent with less synaptic loss.

Here is the caveat, and it is not a small one. The dose groups are ten and eleven participants each against nine placebo, and this is a post hoc imaging analysis across many regions and two doses with correlations reported at p-values in the zero-point-zero-four range. The mechanistic story — peripheral immune engagement driving reduced central inflammation — is coherent and the field badly needs an anti-inflammatory approach that works, since TREM2 activation, TNF blockade and NSAIDs have all disappointed. But at these group sizes this is hypothesis generation with a phase 3 attached to it, and the correlation between an imaging surrogate and cognition at an R of minus zero-point-four is not evidence of disease modification.

Eighth, another intervention in Alzheimer's, much smaller in ambition and much cleaner in execution. Closed-loop auditory stimulation delivers brief sounds phase-locked to the up-state of sleep slow oscillations, and it reliably boosts overnight declarative memory in healthy people. A Spanish group, with Bitbrain and hospitals in Zaragoza and Lleida, ran it in thirty-seven patients with biomarker-confirmed early symptomatic Alzheimer's — randomized, double-blind, sham-controlled, crossover, one night each, with stimulation parameters individualized from a calibration night.

Word-pair retention improved significantly over sham, with an effect size of zero-point-four-two. The stimulation did what it was supposed to physiologically: evoked slow-wave and fast spindle activity increased, and sleep architecture and subjective sleep quality were preserved, which matters because waking these patients up would obviously be counterproductive.

Now the negatives, which the authors report in full and which I think are the most informative part. No effect on visuospatial image recognition. No effect on working memory. And crucially, no effect at all at the seven-day follow-up — the effect size there is exactly zero. So one night buys you an overnight consolidation benefit on one task and nothing that persists. As a proof of physiological mechanism in a biomarker-confirmed population, this is a real advance; as a therapeutic, it tells you the trial that matters is a multi-night one, and that trial has not been done.

Ninth, and this bridges us to the computational items. A team across UNSW, Pittsburgh and Chulalongkorn built a ReAct-style agent with retrieval-augmented generation to classify genetic disease severity — the bottleneck in designing reproductive carrier screening panels, where the requirement that a condition be "severe" is applied subjectively and commercial panels consequently differ wildly in content. The agent applies the ACMG severity tiers and the obstetrics college quality-of-life criteria, retrieves PubMed literature, produces an explicit reasoning chain, and separately verifies its own claims.

Across ten thousand two hundred Human Phenotype Ontology terms it hit ninety-three-and-a-half percent accuracy against expert-curated cohorts with a Matthews correlation of zero-point-nine-two, with eighty-three to ninety-one percent of claims backed by direct evidence or valid inference, and ninety-five percent concordance with the Mackenzie's Mission gene list on external validation.

I am ordinarily skeptical of agent papers reporting numbers this high, but the task shape here is favorable in a way worth naming: the ground truth is a published rubric, the evidence is in the literature, and the expert disagreement being automated away is disagreement about applying criteria rather than about facts. That is close to the ideal case for this architecture. Whether panel composition should be standardized by a language model is a policy question the paper does not touch.

Tenth, the counterweight, and for my money the most important AI item today. A Mass General Brigham group built NUMBERS, a benchmark asking not whether models know clinical numbers but whether they can use them when the case changes. Sixteen configurations, thirteen hundred questions linked to published evidence, a hundred and twenty-five thousand calls.

The pattern is stark. Models recalled evidence-consistent numbers well — seventy-seven percent of off-target diagnostic estimates still fell inside the source evidence's central eighty percent predictive range. But when asked to update a prevalence-dependent quantity, they got it right seventy-nine percent of the time if you handed them the inputs and said "calculate", and thirty-four percent of the time if you asked in ordinary clinical language. Same computation, same model, forty-five points of difference from phrasing alone. Patient-level near-target answers went from thirty percent to seventy-nine percent with explicit inputs and a calculation request.

The direct-action experiment is the one that should worry anyone deploying these. In eleven percent of calls, the model stated a decision cutoff and then chose an action inconsistent with the cutoff it had just stated. Supplying the complete rule raised action accuracy from eighty-six to ninety-nine percent. So the failure is not knowledge, and it is not arithmetic — it is the step where knowledge becomes an act. That is precisely the step autonomous clinical agents are being built to take.

Eleventh, a companion critique from Toronto, on how to read benchmark scores at all. They took seven hundred and ninety-two orthopaedic in-training exam questions and ablated the components — removing images, removing the clinical vignette, removing the question stem — across three open-weight Ministral models and five frontier models.

Removing the images changed nothing for any model, on questions that contain images. Removing the clinical vignette cost about ten points on image questions and nearly twenty on text-only ones. And with only the answer options — no stem, no vignette, no image — every model scored well above the twenty-five percent chance rate, with the best reaching forty-six percent. Nearly half the questions are answerable from the shape of the options alone.

They also checked whether semantic similarity between model explanations and the reference discussion tracks correctness. It does not: similarity stayed high regardless of accuracy, with a Spearman correlation of minus zero-point-one-seven. So the explanation-quality proxy people reach for does not separate right from wrong answers. Read alongside the previous item, the message is consistent — exam performance measures something, and that something is not clinical reasoning.

Closing with the most speculative item, and the one that is pure theory. A group at Washington University in St. Louis and Johns Hopkins built a two-level network in which a fast neuronal layer sits inside a slower astrocytic layer with local, diffusive, gap-junction-like coupling rather than all-to-all connectivity, respecting the actual spatial tiling of astrocytes. They trained it on a hierarchical task where the agent has to infer unsignalled changes in the latent rule from reward feedback.

What emerges is a mechanism worth the name. Reward prediction errors act as bifurcation parameters on the astrocytic dynamics. Reward omission annihilates the current attractor; the state then drifts through a flat region of the landscape until it crosses into a new decision region, which triggers a second bifurcation that reconfigures the field around the new context. The slow drift between bifurcations is evidence accumulation, and the shallowness of the attractors — set by environmental entropy — produces behavioral stickiness, the tendency to persist with a policy for a few extra trials after it stops paying.

The framing they offer is that this is a generalization of integrate-to-bound models in which the bounds themselves move in state space. I think that is the useful contribution: a concrete, biologically-shaped proposal for how a slow, spatially extended, non-spiking system could hold latent state while a fast system executes. It is a model, not a measurement, and astrocyte computation papers have a long history of outrunning their evidence. But it makes contact with real astrocyte biology — calcium timescales, local coupling, neurotransmitter sensing — and it predicts that behavioral stickiness should scale with environmental uncertainty, which is testable.
