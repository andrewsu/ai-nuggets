## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. I'm your host, and this is your daily pass through CNS pharmacology, psychiatric and neurology drug discovery, and the computational machinery closing in on both. Eleven items today, and the lead goes after an assumption so deeply baked into CNS model-informed drug development that most of us stopped noticing it was an assumption.

When you predict receptor occupancy from a pharmacokinetic model, you use the unbound drug concentration in brain extracellular fluid. That is the biophase. It is the number Kp-uu is defined against, the number every free-drug hypothesis argument runs through, and the number that gets compared against PET-derived occupancy in every translational CNS model I can think of. A group at Leiden has now asked whether that is actually right for a receptor that lives in a membrane, and their answer, in Clinical Pharmacology and Therapeutics, is: for most of these drugs, no.

The setup is their multi-compartment CNS physiologically based pharmacokinetic model, which unlike a simple brain-plasma ratio carries separate compartments for microvascular space, brain extracellular fluid, brain intracellular fluid, the brain cell membrane itself, lysosomal fluid, and four cerebrospinal fluid spaces. They extended it with receptor binding kinetics, D2 internalization and recycling, and competition from endogenous dopamine. Then they took eight structurally diverse D2 ligands — haloperidol, risperidone, aripiprazole, brexpiprazole, cariprazine, sulpiride, remoxipride and raclopride — built population pharmacokinetic models for each from published plasma data, and compared model-predicted occupancy against PET occupancy in caudate, putamen and striatum from sixty-six people across eight studies, forty-six of them with schizophrenia.

Brain membrane concentrations aligned better with observed PET occupancy than brain extracellular fluid for five of the eight compounds. That is the headline, and here is the part that makes it a problem rather than a curiosity: lipophilicity does not tell you which five. Brexpiprazole is the most lipophilic drug in the set, it is exactly the compound you would predict accumulates in membrane, and it was one of the two best described by extracellular fluid. Swapping logP for a microsomal membrane partition coefficient improved membrane-based predictions by up to ten percentage points for haloperidol and sulpiride, which says the relevant quantity is real membrane partitioning and not the crude descriptor we substitute for it.

Two more results worth carrying. Including receptor internalization improved predictions for remoxipride and haloperidol and made them worse for five others, so internalization is not a free improvement you bolt on. And endogenous dopamine competition was negligible — not small, negligible — even when they simulated phasic surges at fifty times baseline. The reason is a straightforward affinity argument: dopamine's D2 dissociation constant here is about six point four micromolar, which is somewhere between thirty-three-fold and nearly three hundred thousand-fold weaker than the drugs, so it dissociates long before it matters. That is a modeled result and it sits a little awkwardly next to the displacement literature, which works at tracer doses rather than therapeutic ones, but it is a clean statement of why the two regimes are not the same experiment.

The honest limitations are in the paper. Raclopride, sulpiride and cariprazine still miss badly, with root-mean-square errors of twenty-three to twenty-eight percent. It is a simulation study built on literature pharmacokinetics, not a prospective one. But the load-bearing claim survives all of that: if the biophase for a membrane-embedded receptor is the membrane, then a whole class of occupancy projections has been using the wrong denominator for a subset of compounds you cannot identify in advance from lipophilicity. Adding a membrane compartment to your CNS model is now the cheap experiment.

Staying on targets worth engaging, Molecular Psychiatry has a paper on the cholinergic hypothesis of depression that is timelier than it looks. The hypothesis is old — elevated central acetylcholine in depression, anticholinergics like scopolamine producing rapid antidepressant effects, and the reason nobody could use that clinically being the side effect profile of blocking muscarinic receptors everywhere at once. The muscarinic story has since been rehabilitated by receptor-selective and peripherally restricted approaches, which makes the circuit question live again: where is the acetylcholine coming from, and can you get at the source instead of the receptor?

This group localizes it to the laterodorsal tegmental nucleus, the brainstem's main cholinergic output alongside the pedunculopontine. Under chronic unpredictable mild stress, cholinergic neurons there become hyperactive — consistent with earlier work in chronic social defeat. The new part is the mechanism upstream of that hyperactivity. It is not increased excitatory drive; it is loss of local inhibition. Somatostatin-positive interneurons within the nucleus normally restrain the cholinergic cells, and chronic stress suppresses that synaptic transmission. Activating those somatostatin interneurons during the stress exposure preserved the inhibitory synapse and limited the development of depression-like behavior.

The framing I would take from this is preventive rather than therapeutic, and the authors say so. Driving the cholinergic neurons did not by itself produce depression-like behavior in earlier work — it increased susceptibility when paired with stress. So this is a stress-vulnerability node, not a depression switch. The limitation they flag themselves is that disinhibition could also come from reduced afferent input from other nuclei, and since local GABAergic neurons there express acetylcholine receptors, the cholinergic cells may be regulating their own brake. It is a loop, and they have only cut into one arm of it.

Third item, and it is the one I would actually go read if you work on peptides. A preprint applies sparse autoencoder features from InterPLM to neuropeptide precursor discovery. The problem is a real one: neuropeptides are short, fast-evolving and structurally nondescript, so homology search and motif scanning systematically miss the divergent ones, which are exactly the ones you want. Protein language models help, but a dense ESM-2 embedding spreads any given biological concept across many overlapping dimensions, so you get prediction without any handle on what the model is using.

The move here is to decode those dense embeddings through a sparse autoencoder, score each of the resulting ten thousand-odd sparse features individually for how well it alone separates neuropeptide precursors from other secreted proteins, rank by that, and train plain logistic regression on the top three hundred twenty. The comparison against raw ESM-2 features is the interesting methodological result: ESM-2 per-feature scores pile up degenerately around a single value, while the sparse features spread out with many individually strong ones. That is the disentangling claim, measured rather than asserted.

On the human secretome it recovers about eighty-nine percent of annotated neuropeptides against seventy-eight percent for DeepPeptide, with roughly half DeepPeptide's false positives, and it is far more precise than NeuroPP, which has near-perfect recall and two hundred seventy false positives. Cross-validated accuracy peaks around ninety-two percent. It generalizes cleanly to worm and fly, and only partly to mouse and zebrafish, where the misses are mostly large protein hormones like growth hormone and prolactin — which their own operational definition excludes, so that is a labeling artifact more than a model failure. The broader argument, which I think is the durable one, is that sparse features are a good fit for low-sample-size protein problems precisely because they let a simple classifier work from a compact, labeled-data-cheap representation. Neuropeptide annotation is exactly that kind of problem.

Sticking with tools, there is a new open-source docking platform called PandaDock, and I am including it mostly because of how it reports itself. Torsion-tree ligand flexibility, Monte Carlo with Metropolis acceptance refined by L-BFGS, and rotational gradients taken in closed form through the derivative of the SO(3) exponential map rather than by finite differences — that last bit is a genuine engineering improvement. The affinity grid uses a blocked neighbor-selection scheme that is exact and roughly six to ten times faster than dense evaluation, and caches across ligands sharing a receptor site.

The scoring function is an SE(3)-equivariant graph network trained on seven hundred forty thousand co-folded complexes under target-disjoint splits. Pose recovery within two angstroms is thirty-four percent at rank one and fifty-seven percent within the returned ensemble on eight hundred fourteen complexes. Affinity correlation is a Pearson r of about zero point four on held-out data, zero point four seven transferring to crystal structures with measured binding constants, and zero point six nine for a model trained on PDBbind alone.

Now the part that earns it airtime. They ran an independent thirty-compound series against a single GABA-A receptor target — a within-target ranking problem, which is what you actually face in lead optimization. Their empirical scoring function placed eighth of twenty-five methods, ahead of every AutoDock Vina and Vinardo configuration. Their neural scoring function placed below Vina. They report that, they connect it to a within-target correlation ceiling they identified in their own training data, and they state plainly that the model is unsuitable for pose rescoring. A tool paper that tells you where its neural component loses to a fifteen-year-old empirical function is a tool paper you can calibrate against, and that is rarer than it should be.

There are two regulatory items today, and the first is a small piece of news wrapped around a much bigger one. Clearmind published a US patent application this week covering MDMA combined with N-acylethanolamines — specifically palmitoylethanolamide — for PTSD, anxiety and eating disorders, with the claimed rationale being that the acylethanolamide enhances potency, lowers the required MDMA dose, and widens the therapeutic window. Take the patent itself for what it is; there is no clinical data attached. The reason it is on this list is the thing it is responding to. In July the FDA issued guidance titled "Psychedelic Drugs: Considerations for Clinical Investigations," and that guidance explicitly folds MDMA into the definition of psychedelic drugs as an entactogen alongside the classic serotonergic ones. After the Lykos rejection, the operative question for anyone developing in this space was whether there was a defined regulatory path at all. A guidance document that names the trial design, conduct, safety monitoring and NDA-enabling expectations is that path, and the patent filings following it are the market telling you people believe it.

The second is a clearance rather than an approval. The FDA cleared Roche's Elecsys phospho-tau two-seventeen plasma assay, developed with Lilly, for assessing amyloid pathology in people fifty-five and older with cognitive symptoms. Two things distinguish it. It is a single biomarker rather than a ratio, and it supports both rule-in and rule-out using the same validated cutoffs in primary and specialty care, which is the part that determines whether it actually gets used outside memory clinics. It runs on the more than forty-five hundred cobas analyzers already installed in US labs, so the deployment friction is close to zero. The output is three-tiered — negative, intermediate, positive — and the intermediate band is where the intellectual honesty lives, because it is an explicit statement that a meaningful fraction of patients still need PET or cerebrospinal fluid. It is not standalone, and performance is explicitly not established for predicting future dementia or for monitoring therapy. What it changes is throughput: eligibility screening for anti-amyloid antibodies, and trial recruitment, both of which have been bottlenecked on access to PET.

From there to psychiatric genetics. Nature Mental Health has paired papers on binge eating and anorexia nervosa, and the binge-eating one is, as far as I know, the first properly powered GWAS of the behavior — thirty-nine thousand cases against one point two million controls, six loci. What is worth your attention is not the loci but their company. They have previously been associated with smoking, risk-taking behavior and age at menarche, and the genetic correlations run positive with smoking, general risk tolerance and problematic alcohol use. Loss of control is the clinical core of binge eating, and the genetics puts it in the impulse-control neighborhood rather than the metabolic one.

The BMI question gets handled well. Using GWAS-by-subtraction, the BMI-shared component accounts for only twelve percent of genetic variance in binge eating and ten percent in anorexia; the non-BMI components keep or slightly increase their correlations with psychiatric disorders while their correlations with anthropometric and metabolic traits attenuate. So the FTO signal, which shows up for binge eating and not for anorexia, plausibly runs through the behavior rather than around it.

Now the sobering half, and I want to be direct about it because this is a pharmacology audience. Drug-set analysis returned nothing significant for either phenotype. Drug-class analysis returned exactly one hit, antimigraine preparations for anorexia, which is consistent with prior polygenic overlap with migraine but is one hit out of many tests. Stratified heritability analysis found no significant tissue or cell-type enrichment for either disorder after multiple-testing correction. Polygenic scores explain zero point three percent of liability variance for binge eating and two point three percent for anorexia. And despite raising the effective anorexia sample by sixty-four percent since the last freeze, they added two loci and lost three. The signal is real and the architecture is extremely polygenic; the distance from here to a target is not shrinking as fast as the sample size is growing.

While we are on the theme of studies that need reading carefully, Molecular Psychiatry also published a human gut-brain paper this week that pairs stool metagenomics with magnetic resonance spectroscopy, testing whether the microbiome's genetic potential to make neuroactive compounds tracks brain GABA and glutamate and the excitation-inhibition ratio in the anterior cingulate, dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and inferior occipital gyrus. It is an appealing design and the taxonomic attributions are sensible — Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus for GABA synthesis, Clostridium for p-cresol, Faecalibacterium and Roseburia for short-chain fatty acids.

But the numbers are sixty-one sequenced stool samples, and the reported local false discovery rates on essentially every association sit between zero point two and zero point five five. In other words, almost nothing survives correction. Several effects flip sign between regions — the propionate synthesis pathway associates positively with GABA in anterior cingulate and negatively in occipital cortex — which is presented as regional specificity but is equally consistent with noise. I would file this as a design worth copying at five times the sample size rather than a result. And I would specifically resist the temptation to read directionality into it; the models here predict pathway abundance from brain metabolite levels, which is not the causal direction the gut-brain framing implies.

That is a nice bridge to three papers about measurement being harder than it looks, all in the agentic-AI-for-science space.

The first is a perturbation audit of medical chain-of-thought, and it is the one I would push hardest. The premise is that clinicians read a visible reasoning chain as evidence of reasoning, so the chain has to actually track the evidence it claims to use. The authors built a thirty-operator battery of clinically motivated edits — severity reversal, negation flip, demographic swap, evidence ablation — applied to both the chain and the question, and then, crucially, cross-tabulated whether the chain registered the edit against whether the answer changed. That joint analysis is the methodological contribution, because a low flip rate on its own is ambiguous between a robust faithful model and a model whose chain is decorative.

Fourteen models, four medical benchmarks, roughly three hundred sixty thousand perturbed generations. Their headline metric, the chain-decoupling rate — the chain does not register a clinically destructive edit and the answer does not flip — is seventy-three percent across the panel. Corrupting the chain leaves accuracy unchanged. Removing chain-of-thought prompting entirely does not reduce accuracy. Their worked example is almost too clean: change "acute mountain sickness" to "chronic" in the question, and the model's chain re-asserts the word "acute" and returns the original answer. Two board-certified clinicians re-annotated a hundred ninety-seven perturbed questions and confirmed that ninety-eight and a half percent of the edits leave the gold answer defensible, so this is not an artifact of broken perturbations. Between seventeen and thirty-three percent of destructive flips were judged clinically harmful. The pattern held across medical fine-tuning, reasoning fine-tuning and scale. If you are building anything where a clinician inspects the rationale as a safety layer, this says the safety layer is largely narrative.

Second, a benchmark from Benchling called BenchBench-Protocol, which I like for how the tasks were sourced. Rather than asking experts to write plausible questions, they diffed published wet-lab protocols against the versions scientists actually modified on their platform during real experiments, and turned each difference into a task plus a weighted rubric grounded in the downstream dependencies a correct answer has to address. A hundred forty-nine tasks, ninety-six source protocols, nine domains, every task reviewed by at least two PhD scientists with three-plus years at the bench.

They evaluated nine models, and Claude Opus 5 leads at fifty-nine percent normalized rubric score; everything else lands between thirty-four and forty-seven. At ten attempts the best model still leaves about a quarter of rubric credit unearned, so it is nowhere near saturated. Two findings I would keep. Model ranking is not stable in the number of attempts — two models statistically indistinguishable at one attempt separate cleanly at ten — which is a warning about every single-shot leaderboard you have read. And performance splits by the kind of judgment required: models do comparatively well reasoning from evidence to conclusions and comparatively badly on troubleshooting and adaptation, the tasks that need tacit sense of what an experienced person would consider the most likely approach. The limitations section is appropriately blunt: single-turn only, no human expert baseline, and the grader is one of the nine models being graded.

And to close, something narrower and more immediately practical. A group at Passau documents what they call the compaction cliff. When a long-running agent's context overflows, the runtime summarizes, and it summarizes everything at the same rate — an episodic log and a safety constraint compete for the same tokens and are compressed identically. But only the constraint needs its exact wording to remain enforceable. Measuring this on twenty production agent configurations, they find a standard compaction prompt preserves fifty-three percent of safety rules after one round and ten percent after five. Their fix is to classify each item by type and route each type through its own retention policy, with a verifier that flags the output as unsafe if any constraint went missing. The typed compactor preserves two to four times more safety rules at every compression ratio, with ninety-six percent recall over five rounds, and it beats the production baseline on a medical-compliance benchmark.

Their opening example is the one to remember: an agent reads that the patient is allergic to penicillin, the context fills, the line gets paraphrased away, and three turns later the agent recommends amoxicillin. If you are running long-horizon agents over clinical or pharmacovigilance material, the failure mode is not hallucination. It is that the thing you most needed the model to remember was compressed at the same rate as everything else.

That is eleven. If you take one thing from today, take the lead: for a receptor sitting in a membrane, the membrane may be the biophase, and lipophilicity will not tell you when. Back tomorrow.
