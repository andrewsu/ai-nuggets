## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. I'm your host, and this is your daily pass through CNS pharmacology, psychiatric and neurology drug discovery, and the computational machinery closing in on both. Eleven items today, and the lead gives predictive-processing accounts of psychedelics something they have been missing for a decade — a cell type, a frequency, and a specific computation that breaks.

The setup is a visual change-detection task in head-fixed mice, run on the Allen Institute's OpenScope Brain Observatory. Eight natural images the animal has already learned, each flashed for 250 milliseconds with a half-second gray screen between them, repeated an unpredictable number of times, and then the identity changes and the mouse licks to report it. The design separates the neural code for *which* image you are looking at from the code for *the image just changed*. Layer Neuropixels probes across four visual areas and frontal cortex on top of that — eleven thousand single units — optotag the somatostatin interneurons, and give one milligram per kilogram of psilocybin.

Behavior collapses completely. Perceptual sensitivity goes from an area under the curve of 0.9 before injection to 0.54 after, indistinguishable from chance; hit rate falls from 0.72 to 0.07. And this is not sedation — running speed is unchanged, and an automated motion-sequencing analysis that decomposes body-part trajectories into sub-second behavioral syllables finds no difference anywhere. Pupil dilates, as it does in humans. One mouse out of seven was simply resilient, held baseline performance, and — hold onto this — also showed almost none of the neural signature.

Now the neural side, and the first surprise is what *doesn't* break. Receptive fields are unchanged. Image identity decodes from the population just as accurately under psilocybin as under saline. Regular-spiking firing rates drop modestly, about one spike per second, with fast-spiking and somatostatin rates flat. Basic visual representation is intact. What appears instead is a four-hertz oscillation locked to visual stimulation, and it is wildly cell-type-biased. The fraction of neurons showing it goes from six percent to thirty-five percent in regular-spiking cells, ten to forty-six in fast-spiking, and ten to fifty-nine percent in somatostatin interneurons. It is confined to sensory areas and essentially absent frontally.

Then the part that makes it a mechanism rather than a phenomenon. They used mutual information at baseline to split neurons into an identity-encoding ensemble and a change-encoding ensemble — two largely non-overlapping populations, with change encoding enriched in interneurons. The oscillation preferentially captures the change-encoding cells: the correlation between oscillation strength and change-information is 0.44 in somatostatin neurons, against 0.09 and non-significant for identity-information. So during an *expected* image repetition, under psilocybin, the ensemble that normally fires only when the world changes gets recruited anyway. Project the population into a state space defined before injection and the drug pushes no-change responses along the same trajectory a genuine image change would produce, and in change-encoding somatostatin cells the first fifty milliseconds of that trajectory match the true change response almost exactly.

The last piece is the one I'd have bet against: the magnitude depends on the picture. They defined a contour fragmentation index — discrete contours divided by their average length, so smooth continuous edges score near zero — and it accounts for fifty-six percent of the within-session variance in oscillation strength across the eight images. Images with clean continuous contours drive the effect hardest, and those are the images that recruit the largest change-encoding ensembles. So the drug is not flipping cortex into a uniform altered state; it is amplifying a specific, feature-tuned population, and how much you get depends on what you are looking at.

The honest limitations: six animals in the main comparison, and the oscillation-to-behavior link is correlational. There's also a real puzzle the authors flag — hits fell without false alarms rising, so an internal bias toward "something changed" does not translate straightforwardly into reporting changes. Their proposal is that the aberrant recruitment destroys the stable context the learned report depends on, which is plausible and untested. But the field's story about psychedelics has been overwhelmingly about layer-five pyramidal cells, and this says the interneuron that computes sensory mismatch is where the action is, in visual cortex rather than frontal cortex. That is a testable relocation of the target.

Staying with cannabinoids and long-range consequences, Neuropsychopharmacology has a short paper from the Marsicano group in Bordeaux with Busquets-Garcia in Barcelona that closes a loop people have been trying to close for twenty years. The epidemiology linking adolescent cannabis to adult psychopathology is solid and the mechanism has never been. They gave mice THC daily from postnatal day thirty-five to forty-five — the adolescent window — and tested at day seventy. Males showed hyperlocomotion, reduced sociability, and impaired spontaneous alternation. Females showed essentially nothing.

The mechanistic candidate is mitochondrial complex one, specifically phosphorylation of the NDUFS4 subunit at serine 173, which this group had previously shown mediates THC's acute social deficits in adults through astrocytes. Here, adolescent THC at five milligrams per kilogram reduced that phosphorylation in hippocampus and prefrontal cortex — not cerebellum — measured immediately at the end of exposure. And by adulthood it was gone. Fully normalized. In females it never appeared at all.

So you have a transient molecular signature and a permanent behavioral one, which is exactly the shape of a developmental insult, and the causal test is the good part. They delivered a systemically-acting adeno-associated viral vector retro-orbitally before adolescence, driving a phosphomimetic NDUFS4 mutant specifically in astrocytes — meaning the subunit is locked into the phosphorylated state THC would have removed. That vector, on its own, did nothing to baseline sociability or locomotion. But it completely prevented the adult deficits from adolescent THC. A ten-day bioenergetic perturbation in astrocytes, during a window, is sufficient to explain a lifelong behavioral change.

It's four pages, and the limitations are proportionate: they cannot say astrocytes are the only effector, the female-resilience explanation is hand-waved toward antioxidant capacity and estrogen, and nothing links the transient dephosphorylation to adult behavior. But as a therapeutic entry point it is unusually concrete, because the window is defined and the target is a phosphorylation site rather than a receptor you'd have to block during the exposure itself.

From a drug you'd rather adolescents didn't take to one nearly everyone over seventy already does. A bioRxiv preprint takes on trazodone as a disease-modifying candidate in Alzheimer's, which is a claim that has been floating on retrospective cohort data for years without a proper preclinical package behind it. Trazodone is the most prescribed sleep medication in the United States, it robustly promotes slow-wave sleep, and slow-wave sleep is now a credible therapeutic target for amyloid.

They dosed APP NL-F knock-in mice with sixty milligrams per kilogram daily, voluntarily in palatable food, for sixty days, starting either at nine months or fourteen months — early and intermediate amyloid. Sleep did what you'd want, and better than you'd want: more NREM and more slow-wave power during the rest phase immediately after dosing, and, unexpectedly, more wake during the active phase. That is consolidation rather than sedation, and it is the clinically valuable version of the effect. Hippocampal plaque burden in the fourteen-to-sixteen-month cohort was forty-five percent lower than vehicle. Microglial and astroglial activation were lower, mostly in males. Phospho-tau at serines 396 and 404 was lower in the early-stage cohort. And a separate behavioral cohort treated about four months showed better short- and long-term novel object recognition.

Two things temper it. The mechanism is unresolved and the authors say so: trazodone is a 5-HT2A antagonist, an alpha-1 antagonist, a serotonin transporter inhibitor, and an unfolded-protein-response modulator, and chronic blockade of several of those independently reduces plaque. So "sleep is doing this" is a hypothesis, and the experiment that settles it — matching the slow-wave enhancement optogenetically, without the drug — is proposed rather than done. Second, and this one is not discussed: the mice were food-restricted to eighty-five to ninety-seven percent of baseline body weight to make voluntary oral dosing work. Caloric restriction on its own moves amyloid in these models. Both arms were restricted, so it isn't a confound on the comparison, but the effect sits on top of an intervention that is already doing something.

Now a methods paper more important than the finding it reports. Molecular Psychiatry has a study on neuromelanin-sensitive MRI, pushed hard as a non-invasive proxy for midbrain catecholamine synthesis capacity in psychosis, depression and Parkinson's. The standard analysis normalizes substantia nigra signal against a reference region, the crus cerebri, as a contrast-to-noise ratio — which presupposes that reference region is biologically invariant.

In a transdiagnostic sample of a hundred and one adults aged eighteen to forty, they compared conventional ratio normalization against a residual-based approach with a data-driven reference region. Conventional normalization: no sex differences anywhere, all adjusted p-values above 0.20. Residual-based normalization: males higher than females in substantia nigra pars compacta, ventral tegmental area, and locus coeruleus, all adjusted p below 0.001. And the reason is directly measurable — the crus cerebri itself differs by sex, at p below 0.001. Males accumulate more iron in white matter, the reference region is myelin- and iron-dense, and you are dividing by a quantity that carries the very effect you're testing for.

The sex finding lines up with dopamine synthesis capacity differences conserved from Drosophila upward, but the transferable point is the arithmetic: when the denominator varies systematically with a grouping variable, a ratio can attenuate a real effect, amplify a null, or invert it. That likely explains a chunk of the inconsistent neuromelanin literature, and it probably spared the Parkinson's studies only because age-related nigral loss is large enough to swamp reference-region noise. If you're using this modality as a biomarker, the normalization scheme is not a preprocessing detail. Caveat on their own sample: twenty-eight males against seventy-three females, and a narrow enough age band that no age effects emerged.

Sticking with objective measurement in psychiatry, a bioRxiv preprint attacks a problem that anyone who has watched deep brain stimulation programming will recognize. Ventral capsule and ventral striatum stimulation for refractory psychiatric illness has real potential, but choosing among the contacts on the lead is a slow, subjective, trial-and-error process driven by patient self-report. The proposal here is to close the loop with a multi-armed bandit over contacts, scored by raw reaction times on the Multi-Source Interference Task, a cognitive control task where this stimulation is known to produce site-specific improvement.

The results are all in simulation — a synthetic patient generator across a thousand replicates — and I'd normally discount that heavily, except that the useful finding is a negative one about architecture. Prior versions of this idea inserted a state-space sensor model between the raw reaction times and the bandit, to estimate a latent cognitive state. Removing it helps substantially, because the sensor model amplified high-frequency noise, inflating state variance by fifty-two percent at baseline and a hundred and forty-eight percent in conflict trials; drop it and the contact-ranking failure rate falls from thirty-two percent to twelve. Running continuously instead of resetting daily preserves accumulated samples, drives regret down steadily over roughly four thousand trials, and lets the system re-converge when the underlying response silently shifts. And a rolling four-contact sub-tournament scales to eight-contact arrays without losing accuracy, which matters because real hardware can't stimulate every contact at once.

Treat it as a design argument rather than a result — but "the latent-state model was making it worse" is the kind of design argument that generalizes past this application.

From closed-loop optimization on behavioral readouts to language models trying to read behavioral readouts, which goes considerably less well. BALMS, out of Dartmouth, is the first systematic benchmark of agentic language models for longitudinal mental health sensing. The premise is genuinely appealing — wearables give you months of continuous sleep, activity and heart-rate data, self-report scales give you sparse snapshots, and an agent that could reason over the former to predict and *explain* the latter would be worth having. Three real longitudinal datasets, two task families — predict the wellbeing score, and generate a rationale graded by a language-model judge — three agentic paradigms and five backbones.

The headline is bleak and useful: zero-shot agents rarely beat a mean predictor that ignores the sensor data entirely and outputs the training-set average. Claude Haiku 4.5 with a prompt-based system reaches 0.42 mean absolute error on one dataset against a 0.58 baseline — a real win. A tool-using agent gets 0.54 against a 0.48 baseline, a loss. Smaller open models routinely fail to match the mean. Much of the predictive power in daily self-report is dataset-level prior, and agents mostly rediscover the prior.

Two subsidiary findings are sharper than the headline. The tool-based agent works only when the tool interface matches the data schema — fine on Fitbit-style daily aggregates, brittle code on raw mobile sensor streams. That's an interface limit, not a language capability limit, and it's fixable. The one to carry is on the rationale task: open-source instruct backbones produce fluent, plausible explanations while their scores for temporal alignment and structured evidence use sit near zero. They are not slicing the right time windows or tracking cross-channel correlations; they are imitating the surface form of temporal reasoning. Only the frontier model closed both the quality and the grounding gap. If you are building anything that shows a patient a narrative explanation of their own sensor data, the fluency of that narrative tells you nothing about whether it came from the data.

A more constrained agentic task next, and a better outcome. TrialCode Agent, on medRxiv, tackles a genuine bottleneck in real-world evidence: turning free-text clinical trial eligibility criteria into computable code sets across ICD-9, ICD-10 diagnosis and procedure codes, LOINC, and RxNorm. This is the step that makes target trial emulation reproducible, and it is currently expensive manual curation. Their system pairs language-model interpretation with deterministic terminology verification — the model proposes codes, the terminology server checks that they exist, belong to the right vocabulary, and are acceptable, and then a controlled expansion walks the code hierarchy.

Against expert-built reference sets on forty criteria from eleven trials, the best configuration was Claude with the plain non-retrieval pipeline: precision 0.76, recall 0.62, F1 0.68. GPT-5.5 followed at 0.61, Qwen with biomedical retrieval at 0.55, MedGemma at 0.38. Two negative results are the interesting ones. Retrieval-augmented generation improved aggregate F1 for exactly one model — but it lifted GPT-5.5's RxNorm performance from 0.32 to 0.91, so retrieval is decisively worth it for medication mapping and roughly neutral everywhere else. And family expansion, walking up the code hierarchy to catch siblings, reliably raised recall and reliably cost more precision than it bought.

What carried the system was the deterministic layer. The staged ablation takes micro-F1 from 0.25 before verification to 0.51 after; existence and vocabulary checking alone deleted two thousand five hundred and ninety-four false-positive codes, and acceptance filtering another nine hundred and fifty-two. An unglamorous lesson — the model's job is clinical interpretation, and every claim it makes about the terminology should be checked against the terminology. At an F1 of 0.68 this is not replacing a curator, but as a first pass with an audit trail it's clearly useful.

Scaling that question up as far as it currently goes: BixBench3, from Edison Scientific, asks whether agents can execute an entire computational biology study. Each of twenty tasks hands the agent a research objective, the methodological guidance from a published paper, and that paper's raw data, then grades the artifacts it produces — peak call matrices, differential expression tables — programmatically against the artifacts in the original study. A hundred and thirty-eight graded artifacts across seventeen assay types and nine domains.

Thirteen frontier models score from zero to 0.48 — so the best reproduce roughly half the requested artifacts well enough to preserve the original scientific interpretation. Performance degrades exactly where you'd predict: 0.36 on tasks under a hundred gigabytes of raw data against 0.10 above it, and 0.36 at one or two dependency steps against 0.24 at three or more. Errors introduced early propagate, and nothing recovers them. The resource numbers are worth hearing out loud — an average of six point eight hours, a hundred and two million tokens, and forty-three dollars per task, with the worst run consuming twenty-four hours, over a billion tokens, and five hundred and twenty-five dollars. Spending more did not help: the top scorers used fewer tokens and cost less than several models below them.

The limitation the authors state plainly is the one that matters for how you read the number. Every task prescribes the method, because the grading requires comparability to the published artifact. So this measures whether an agent can execute a specified pipeline end to end — not whether it can decide which analyses are worth running. That is the harder half, and it's not in here.

Two computational pharmacology items to bring it down. First, a neat, narrow result from the Estonian Biobank on calling copy number variants in CYP2C19 — the pharmacogene behind clopidogrel, voriconazole, proton pump inhibitors, and a long list of antidepressants. There are recognized star alleles for whole-gene and partial deletions, with cumulative frequencies up to two percent in Estonian and Finnish populations, and a missed deletion is a metabolizer-status error that propagates straight into dosing. Whole-genome sequencing calls these reliably; genotyping arrays, which is what biobanks and clinics actually run, do not, because you're inferring structure from probe intensities at low resolution.

They trained a fully-connected network to predict deletion state probe by probe from log R ratio and B-allele frequency, with labels from long-read sequencing, and compared it against PennCNV, the hidden Markov model everyone uses. On held-out test data the network hit a hundred percent accuracy while PennCNV missed a hundred and six of six hundred and twenty-five deletion positions. Across the whole biobank the two agreed on most individuals but disagreed on about three hundred and eighty, and PCR on the conflicts gave the network ninety-seven percent precision against PennCNV's twenty-three. The explanation is visible in the gradient attributions: the network uses probes in the flanking regions, not just probes inside the deletion — long-range dependencies a hidden Markov model structurally cannot exploit. It's locus-specific, it handles essentially only heterozygous deletions, and every new region needs a new model. But for a pharmacogene this consequential, on data that already exists in every biobank, that is a cheap fix.

Second, an ADMET paper from CUHK whose framing I like more than its benchmark numbers. The observation is that ADMET is not one prediction problem — solubility, permeability, protein binding, clearance, transporter activity and toxicity are governed by different molecular signals, so no single representation should be optimal across all of them. Instead of another universal encoder they build a candidate pool — SMILES-based, graph-based, geometry-aware, and plain chemical-descriptor models — and select per task. Across twenty-two Therapeutics Data Commons benchmarks it exceeds the published top method on ten and lands in the top ten on twenty-one.

The ablation is the honest part. The largest single contribution came from the chemical descriptor models — classical cheminformatics features, still carrying the load after a decade of learned molecular representations. And the authors are clear that this is not a new encoder but an argument about evaluation: ADMET leaderboards should report the model setup used for each task rather than a single architecture name, because task identity carries information about assay mechanism, dataset size, and metric. They also list what selection cannot fix — bioavailability, lipophilicity, volume of distribution, drug-induced liver injury, hERG and acute toxicity all stay negative-margin, limited by endpoint noise and label uncertainty rather than architecture. That's yesterday's alpha-2 selectivity lesson from a different direction: the benchmark is often measuring something other than what you think.

And to finish, a piece of straightforward pharmacology with a regulatory sting, from the Taffe lab here at Scripps. Six-methyl nicotine is a nicotine analog that occurs in tobacco at about four orders of magnitude below nicotine, and which has recently become the primary psychoactive ingredient in some e-liquids, oral pouches, and at least one non-tobacco cigarette — marketed on the premise that it isn't nicotine, and therefore isn't covered by nicotine regulation. The pharmacology question underneath that pitch had never been properly asked.

They ran vapor inhalation in male and female rats across concentrations from five to thirty milligrams per milliliter, then a head-to-head against nicotine. Six-methyl nicotine produced concentration-dependent hypothermia of about one degree and increased warm-water tail-withdrawal latency, at magnitudes essentially indistinguishable from nicotine at matched vapor concentrations. Vapor self-administration was equivalent between the two. Pre-treatment with mecamylamine increased responding for six-methyl nicotine vapor, which is the compensatory pattern you expect if the reinforcement is nicotinic-receptor-mediated. And injected, it was somewhat *more* potent than nicotine at suppressing wheel activity.

The reinforcement data are the softest part — responding was variable across individuals, probably because the training concentration was low — and there's a tolerance confound in the mecamylamine temperature arm from the order of experiments. But the top-line conclusion is not subtle: this compound does what nicotine does, at roughly nicotine's potency, by what looks like nicotine's mechanism. And independent analyses have found actual six-methyl nicotine content in these products running at ten to thirty percent of the labeled concentration. So the "more potent than nicotine" positioning is marketing, and the "not nicotine" positioning is a legal argument with no pharmacological content behind it.
