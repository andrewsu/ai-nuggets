## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. I'm your host, and this is your daily pass through CNS pharmacology, psychiatric and neurology drug discovery, and the computational machinery closing in on both. Eleven items today. The thread, if you want one, is the gap between optimizing a number and understanding a system — which turns out to be the same complaint whether the thing being audited is an AI agent, a pupil diameter, or a surface plasmon resonance trace.

We open with the most important paper of the day, from the Broad Institute, with Eric Lander and Pardis Sabeti among the corresponding authors. They introduce what they call science sandboxes, and the premise is a criticism of every agentic-AI-for-science benchmark now in circulation. Those benchmarks score a final answer. But science isn't answer production — it's rule inference. An investigator who finds a high-scoring sequence without learning why it scores high has produced an epicycle, not a theory. So the sandbox separates the two. An agent submits specimens, a hidden oracle assays them and returns a report, the agent revises its hypotheses and picks the next experiment, and crucially it keeps a lab notebook the experimenters read. Oracles come in three flavors — wet, damp and dry: real physical measurement, a model trained on real measurement, or invented rules with no relationship to biology at all.

They built two. The first asks an agent to design a fifty-thousand-sequence reporter-assay library that will best train a downstream regulatory-activity model, benchmarked against fourteen human expert strategies. Claude Opus won, with a median Pearson correlation of point seven seven against point seven six for the best human strategy, and every one of its five replicates met or beat that human mark; GPT and Gemini both fell short of it. When they gave the winner thirty rounds instead of one, the score barely moved — and that's the finding. The gain was in the notebook, not the number. The agent predicted a fully random DNA library would score near zero, observed a surprisingly high score, and rewrote its theory on the spot: a large part of the evaluation signal is free and composition-driven, so the design problem is teaching regulatory grammar beyond composition. Unprompted, it drifted toward one-variable-at-a-time controls, and in one run caught itself having moved two variables at once and drawn the wrong conclusion.

The second sandbox is where it breaks. This one hides an invented genetic code — an alphabet, a codon length, a mapping to hydrophobic or polar residues, a folding rule — and tells the agent nothing. Maximizing the score is trivial: every run hit the ceiling within ten rounds, because a repeated character eventually lands on a hydrophobic codon. The interesting question is the remaining four hundred and ninety rounds. With four letters and short codons the agent filled in the table. When one codon position was silent and the others interacted, it assigned effects to individual letters, hit a result that broke that theory, changed strategy, and recovered the interaction rule — asking a better question beating collecting more examples. But with a six-letter alphabet it never discovered that codons existed at all; it built an elaborate and genuinely predictive theory of individual nucleotides and short runs that missed the simpler structure underneath. Eight letters worked better than six, so this isn't difficulty scaling — it's a specific failure of induction. Current agents search brilliantly inside a familiar conceptual frame and can't leave it. That's a constraint on which problems you hand an agentic discovery loop, not a benchmark score to wait out.

Which leads naturally into a Neuropsychopharmacology paper doing the same kind of inference on mice instead of models. The group, at the University of Minnesota, asked whether prefrontal NMDA receptor hypofunction specifically — not brain-wide blockade — is sufficient for the value-based decision-making deficits seen in schizophrenia. They used a CRISPR-Cas9 viral strategy to ablate the obligate GluN1 subunit centered on medial prefrontal cortex, which eliminates all NMDA receptor function in transduced neurons, and ran the animals on a two-armed restless bandit in a touchscreen chamber. Local ablation was sufficient. Performance dropped after surgery relative to control virus, stably across eight weeks, and the deficit grew as the value difference between options grew — so these animals are specifically bad at exploiting a clear advantage, not uniformly impaired. Negative outcome weight, the extent to which an unrewarded trial drives a switch normalized to baseline switching, was lower throughout.

Then they gave everyone MK-801 to compare local with global. Systemic blockade collapsed both groups to the same impaired level, and simulated agents from the fitted models became nearly indistinguishable. Here's what I want to flag, because it's unusually honest for this literature: the authors say explicitly that convergence is consistent with partial occlusion of a shared mechanism but cannot be distinguished from compression toward a performance floor, because the virus-by-drug interaction wasn't significant. Separately, a tissue-processing failure means viral spread couldn't be quantified across the cohort, so the manipulation is described as prefrontally targeted rather than anatomically restricted. Both caveats sit in the results, not buried. That's the right way to publish an occlusion design.

Staying with the machinery of updating, a preprint from Romain Goutagny's group in Strasbourg asks whether the locus coeruleus projection to hippocampus supports spatial learning generally or updating specifically. They optogenetically activated LC terminals in dorsal hippocampus during object-location recognition and an appetitive radial maze with an initial learning phase and then a reversal. The dissociation is clean: object-location memory improved and reversal improved — fewer total errors, fewer repeat visits to already-sampled arms — while initial acquisition and retention of the reference memory didn't change at all. And the reversal benefit came without a change in latency, so the animals weren't just moving faster; they sampled more efficiently after the contingency changed. Two honest limits: LC terminals co-release noradrenaline and dopamine and this design can't separate them, and the extended acquisition protocol may have left controls no room to improve. But the shape of the result — neuromodulatory gain that matters when your model of the world is wrong, and not otherwise — is a reason to think about arousal-targeted compounds as state-contingent rather than tonic enhancers.

Now a preprint that should make anyone using pupillometry as a noradrenergic readout uncomfortable. A Cornell group collected simultaneous pupillometry and multi-echo functional MRI in eighty-nine adults aged nineteen to seventy-eight during a visual oddball task. Oddball-evoked pupil responses were robust in every age group and largest in middle age. But locus coeruleus BOLD responses were weak, and — this is the part that matters — they did not predict individual pupil responses. So they went whole-brain, and found that pupil size and its first derivative couple to BOLD on different timescales, about two and a quarter seconds versus five, and in different spatial patterns. The derivative behaves like a hemodynamic response to discrete events; pupil size is already positively correlated at zero lag, behaving less like a response and more like a slowly varying state variable — which is what you'd expect if size is the running integral of change.

Then they regressed those coupling maps against nineteen PET receptor and transporter maps. Derivative-specific coupling correlated uniquely with norepinephrine transporter density — so far so good for the standard story. But size-specific coupling correlated with mu-opioid and cannabinoid CB1 density instead, and only the derivative relationships weakened with age. So tonic pupil diameter and phasic pupil change are not two readouts of one nucleus; they index different neurochemical systems, and if your outcome measure is baseline pupil size you may be indexing opioid and endocannabinoid tone rather than noradrenergic gain. Given how many psychopharmacology studies use pupillometry as a cheap locus coeruleus proxy, that's worth taking seriously.

From measuring a receptor system to delivering to one. A preprint reports an intranasal liposomal formulation of nor-binaltorphimine, the long-acting kappa opioid receptor antagonist, and the setup is the interesting part. Kappa antagonism given into the brain prevents pain-induced negative affect in rodents, but systemic kappa antagonists carry peripheral liabilities. The authors first show that systemic nor-binaltorphimine actually made motivational deficits worse in rats with persistent inflammatory pain from complete Freund's adjuvant. So the drug isn't simply underdosed centrally — the route is generating an effect in the wrong direction. Their fix is nose-to-brain: drug-loaded liposomes suspended in a thermosensitive, in-situ-gelling mucoadhesive hydrogel with sustained release.

The pharmacology is checked properly rather than asserted. Intranasal delivery produced negligible plasma levels compared with intraperitoneal. In-vivo microdialysis showed it blocked a kappa agonist's suppression of nucleus accumbens dopamine release, which is the functional proof of central target engagement. Behaviorally it restored sucrose motivation in the pain model. And repeated intranasal dosing left mechanical thresholds unchanged in pain-naive animals, where systemic dosing did not. So the claim is specifically that route-restricted delivery converts a compound that worsens the phenotype into one that improves it. For a receptor whose antagonists have repeatedly stalled on tolerability, a delivery answer rather than a chemistry answer is a genuinely different bet.

Sticking with opioid receptors, a molecular dynamics preprint from an Indiana group asks how cannabidiol — which they previously characterized as a negative allosteric modulator at the mu receptor — interacts with fentanyl binding as a function of receptor state. They ran fentanyl alone and fentanyl plus cannabidiol in three mouse receptor backgrounds: an active-like model, an inactive-like model, and a computational fifty-percent morph between them, three independently seeded two-hundred-nanosecond trajectories each, eighteen total, with the trajectory as the unit of replication rather than the frame. Consensus cannabidiol contacts were strongly state-dependent — nine residues in the inactive background, eleven in the intermediate, eight in the active, with essentially different residues in each.

But the reason I'm including this is the negative result and how it's handled. They looked for a cannabidiol-associated shift in the classic intracellular activation metric, the distance between transmembrane helices three and six, and the answer depended on which geometric definition they used. Measured alpha-carbon to alpha-carbon between two specific residues, no receptor background showed the same direction in all three replicates. Measured between helix-segment centroids, the shift was consistent. Same trajectories, opposite verdicts. The authors label the whole thing descriptive, decline to claim significance from directional concordance across three trajectories, and say outright that three replicates per condition has limited inferential power. I'd rather read this than a paper that ran the same simulations and reported a mechanism. The lesson: an activation coordinate is not a measurement, it's a choice of coordinate, and the choice can flip your sign.

Which brings us to a paper I did not expect to see on arXiv this week: David Colquhoun, with James Higham at Cambridge, on the interpretation of ligand-receptor binding kinetics. It's an update of his own nineteen-eighty-one treatment, and its target is a habit that has become universal since surface plasmon resonance became cheap. You measure an onset rate and an offset rate, take their ratio to get an equilibrium constant, compare it to the concentration producing half-maximal binding at equilibrium, and treat agreement as validation. Colquhoun's point is that this inference assumes binding produces no conformational change — which, for an agonist, is definitionally false.

He works through a three-state mechanism where binding is followed by a conformational change, in the three regimes where the time course looks near-single-exponential. Any such model relaxes with more than one exponential component, but one usually dominates in amplitude, so single-rate fits look fine. The equilibrium constant you get from those rates is never the microscopic binding constant — the one that tells you about the agonist binding site. It approximates a macroscopic constant that folds affinity and efficacy together, so agreement with your measured half-maximal concentration confirms nothing about either separately. The exception is instructive: when binding is much faster than the conformational change, the rates give you neither and agreement wouldn't be expected — but the amplitude of the initial fast jump, if you can resolve it, does give you the microscopic constant. That's the actionable piece. The information you want sits in an amplitude you're probably discarding, not in the rate constants you're fitting.

From receptor theory to industrial screening. Nature Biotechnology has the AdaptiveFlow platform for ultralarge virtual screening, and the interesting idea is what they call adaptive target-guided screening. They curated the Enamine on-demand space — sixty-nine billion molecules — into ready-to-dock form, computed twenty-eight molecular properties and used eighteen of them to disperse the library across a high-dimensional property grid with roughly twelve million occupied cells. Instead of docking everything, you dock a small prescreen to find which regions of property space this particular site likes, then concentrate there. A twelve-million-molecule prescreen to navigate sixty-nine billion is over five thousand-fold less compute than exhaustive docking, and their target-guided primary screens of a hundred thousand molecules matched standard million-compound random screens for most targets — a tenfold reduction at equal docking performance. An optional active-learning layer closes the loop on top.

They validated experimentally on two targets, and this is where it earns the page. For poly-ADP-ribose polymerase 1, an enzymatic screen returned seven inhibitors, four under two hundred and fifty nanomolar, the best at an IC-fifty of eight point eight nanomolar — comparable to olaparib. For ferroptosis suppressor protein 1, targeting the coenzyme-Q site, inhibition constants came in comparable to or better than the handful of previously described inhibitors. Both series were confirmed by high-resolution co-crystal structures. Prospective potency plus structures is the bar for this kind of paper, and they cleared it. The honest framing is that the intelligence here is a property-space heuristic, not a model of binding — but a heuristic that removes three orders of magnitude of docking cost is worth more than most learned scoring functions.

A useful counterweight from Arizona, a preprint evaluating AlphaFold 3 and Boltz-2 from a medicinal chemist's point of view rather than a pose-prediction leaderboard's. They combined newly determined soluble epoxide hydrolase co-crystal structures with matched activity data and a curated post-training-cutoff set spanning kinases, allosteric modulators, covalent systems, degraders, molecular glues, fragments, membrane proteins and activity-cliff pairs. Both models handled canonical orthosteric enzyme and kinase complexes, including the right activation-loop states. Allosteric sites, membrane proteins and induced-proximity complexes remained hard — which is the set most relevant to CNS targets, so read that as a caution.

Three findings I'd act on. Pharmacophore root-mean-square deviation was often lower than whole-ligand deviation, meaning the key recognition contacts survive even when the overall pose is misaligned — so a mediocre-looking prediction may still support a structure-activity argument. AlphaFold's predicted aligned error did track pose accuracy, and values below about nine tenths of an angstrom were strongly enriched for correct poses, which makes it usable for triage. But confidence scores showed no relationship to experimental activity at all, so do not read a confident structure as a potent one. Boltz-2's affinity module did capture relative activity trends and even resolved an activity-cliff pair, though inconsistently across series. Confidence is about geometry; potency needs its own model.

One more agentic item, and it's the one where an agent actually produced a hypothesis I'd want to chase. A preprint applies a language-model agent to exhaustive cross-tissue proteomics. Every prior cross-tissue co-abundance study picked its tissue pair from prior biology; the authors ran all eight hundred and twenty pairwise combinations of forty-one human tissues and biofluids, finding eighteen hundred and thirty-three conserved co-abundance clusters across four hundred and six pairs. The agent's job is explicitly evidence integration and hypothesis organization, not autonomous conclusion — a distinction they state up front and honor. The result for this audience is brain-and-gut, a twenty-protein cluster whose strongest enrichment is extracellular exosome, and whose most striking pairing is sepiapterin reductase with thioredoxin reductase 1 — the final step of tetrahydrobiopterin synthesis sitting alongside the central thioredoxin enzyme. Tetrahydrobiopterin is the cofactor for tryptophan hydroxylase in both central neurons and enterochromaffin cells, and there's published work showing a gut microbial metabolite downregulating thioredoxin reductase and both tryptophan hydroxylases without addressing cofactor supply. Only two of a hundred and ninety possible pairs in that cluster have documented interactions, and no complex database contains it. The limitation is stated plainly: co-abundance is not interaction, not causation, not inter-tissue communication. The value is a better-organized set of questions.

And to close, the lightest item, which is a trial design worth watching. Axsome dosed the first patient on the thirty-first in a Phase 3 called SUMMIT, testing dextromethorphan-bupropion for smoking cessation — the combination already approved as Auvelity for major depression and for Alzheimer's-related agitation, an NMDA antagonist and sigma-1 agonist paired with an aminoketone that's also a CYP2D6 inhibitor. Twelve weeks, one-to-one-to-one against bupropion and placebo, primary endpoint abstinence at week twelve. What makes this a real experiment rather than a line extension is the active comparator: bupropion is itself an approved smoking cessation drug, and it's a component of the combination. So the trial cannot succeed on bupropion's known effect. It has to show that adding dextromethorphan — whose exposure the bupropion is partly there to raise, by blocking its own metabolism — buys something on top. That's an unusually clean question to ask in a Phase 3, and the kind of design that produces an interpretable failure as well as an interpretable success.

That's the episode. Eleven items, and the thread I'd leave you with is the one Colquhoun and the Broad both make from opposite ends of the field: a number that agrees with your expectation is not a validation. The agent that hit the score ceiling in ten rounds still never found the code. See you tomorrow.
