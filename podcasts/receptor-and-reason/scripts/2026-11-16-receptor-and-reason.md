## Script

Welcome to Receptor & Reason for Monday, November sixteenth. There's a thread running through almost everything on today's list, and it's an uncomfortable one for anyone who designs interventions: the thing you aimed at is frequently not the thing that moved. A compound built on one hypothesis works for what looks like another reason. A model built to predict brain responses gets its best score from a scanner artifact. A disease gene gets silenced and the pathological state comes back anyway. Today is a good day for that kind of humility.

We start with a preprint out of the Shanghai Institute of Materia Medica and Vigonvita that takes a genuinely clever swing at the ketamine problem. The setup: ketamine and its S-enantiomer give you rapid antidepressant action, and they also give you psychotomimetic effects and abuse liability, and nobody has cleanly separated those. The group started from an observation — escitalopram, given before S-ketamine, blunts the S-ketamine-evoked dopamine transient in the nucleus accumbens, measured with the GRAB-DA fluorescent sensor. So enhancing serotonergic tone damps the mesolimbic response to NMDA receptor blockade. From there they did the obvious medicinal-chemistry thing: build the serotonin transporter inhibition into the NMDA receptor antagonist itself. Starting scaffold was 3-methoxymorphinan, which already had some activity at both targets. The output is a compound they call VV147.

And the pharmacology behaves. VV147 inhibits both the GluN1-GluN2A receptor and human SERT in cells. In freely moving mice it produces no detectable rise in accumbal dopamine, no conditioned place preference at ten or thirty milligrams per kilogram where morphine clearly does, no locomotor hyperactivity, and no rotarod deficit — all three of which S-ketamine produces in the same hands. Meanwhile, a single dose raises sucrose preference and cuts immobility in forced swim across both chronic unpredictable mild stress and chronic restraint stress. Rapid efficacy, no reward signature. Pre-treating with VV147 at forty milligrams per kilogram also blunts the S-ketamine dopamine response, which closes the loop on the original observation.

Here's where it gets interesting, and to the authors' credit they report it plainly. They depleted brain serotonin with para-chlorophenylalanine and the antidepressant effect of VV147 on sucrose preference was not significantly attenuated. And they tested a sibling compound — compound six — which keeps the NMDA receptor activity but has substantially weaker SERT inhibition, and it also produced significant antidepressant effects. So the serotonergic arm, which is the entire design rationale, may not be carrying the therapeutic effect. It may be carrying the safety effect and nothing else — which, to be fair, would still be a real and useful result. But the paper's own data separate the design hypothesis from the efficacy mechanism, and that separation is the thing worth sitting with. The dopamine dissociation is the finding. Why VV147 is an antidepressant is still open.

Second, staying with the question of whether you can pull apart a drug's wanted and unwanted effects, a nice amperometric study from the NIDA intramural program. Nitazenes — the two-benzyl-benzimidazole opioids that have been appearing in the illicit supply — were synthesized in the nineteen-fifties and abandoned as analgesics because they were as good at stopping breathing as at stopping pain. This group put oxygen sensors in the nucleus accumbens and in the subcutaneous space of freely moving rats and compared three nitazenes against fentanyl. Etonitazene, the prototype, and fluornitrazene both behave exactly like fentanyl: dose-dependent peripheral hypoxia, and in the brain a biphasic response at doses above about ten micrograms per kilogram — robust hypoxia followed by a weaker rebound hyperoxia. Etonitazene at thirty micrograms per kilogram produced the deepest and longest brain hypoxia of anything tested, with no rebound at all.

The exception is the derivative they call DFNZ — N-desethyl-fluornitrazene. At one hundred micrograms per kilogram it has essentially no effect on brain oxygen. At three hundred it produces minimal change. At a full milligram per kilogram, roughly thirty times the dose rats will self-administer, it produces profound sedation and still only a weak oxygen response. A structurally close analog of a drug that reliably suffocates animals does not suffocate them, while retaining analgesic potency. The honest framing is the one the authors use: this is a single derivative, measured with brain tissue oxygen rather than direct respiratory measurement, and oxygen in the accumbens is a downstream readout that mixes respiratory depression with cerebral blood flow changes. But it's a good argument that "nitazene" is a chemical class, not a toxicological verdict, and that the category should not be categorically written off for therapeutic development.

Third, one of the strongest designs on the list. The 22q11.2 deletion is one of the largest known genetic risk factors for schizophrenia, but it's incompletely penetrant — carry the deletion and your risk is somewhere around one in four, not one in one. Which means the deletion carriers who don't develop psychosis are the single most informative control group in psychiatric genetics, and almost nobody uses them. A group spanning Children's Hospital of Philadelphia, Penn, UC Davis and Zhejiang did. They took iPSC-derived neurons from deletion carriers with schizophrenia and from deletion carriers without, and asked why the first group fails to compensate for its weaker mitochondrial energetics while the second group upregulates mitochondrial biogenesis genes.

The answer runs through the lysosome. Neurons from the psychosis group carry older, more depolarized mitochondria. Transcriptomics and proteomics point at mitophagy pathways being down. Live imaging shows elevated lysosomal pH — the lysosomes are insufficiently acidic, fewer in number and enlarged, which is what you'd expect if undegraded cargo is piling up. So the defect isn't that these neurons fail to make mitochondria; it's that they fail to clear the bad ones, and the clearance failure sits upstream of the biogenesis failure. The therapeutic experiment is the satisfying part: poly-lactide-co-glycolide acidifying nanoparticles, seventy-two hours, and lysosomal pH in the psychosis-group neurons drops to the level of the no-psychosis group, degradative function recovers on a DQ-BSA assay, mitochondrial membrane potential normalizes, the aged mitochondrial pool shrinks — and biogenesis transcription factor expression goes up, without anyone touching biogenesis. Three days after washout the depolarized fraction had fallen further. This is a dish, the cohorts are small, and iPSC neurons at thirty days are not adult cortex. But as a demonstration that the modifier separating penetrant from non-penetrant carriers is a druggable cell-biological process rather than a second genetic hit, it's about as clean as this field gets.

Fourth, and this is the item that most directly earns today's theme. A preprint from Core Biotherapeutics in Berkeley applies a transcription-factor network metric they call the Core Coefficient — which quantifies a factor's functional dominance within a network rather than its expression level — to more than a hundred post-mortem neuronal transcriptomes from Huntington's disease and control brains. The HOX family is the only transcription factor family that is uniformly quiescent in controls and uniformly, significantly dominant in vulnerable neurons in every Huntington's donor they looked at. HOX dominance is already present at Vonsattel grade zero, before overt atrophy; it scales with grade and with the CAG-age product; and its magnitude correlates with age at onset, with exponential fits around point-seven-seven.

Now the part that matters for the field's current therapeutic bet. They applied the same analysis to a published dataset in zQ175 mice treated with a zinc-finger repressor that knocked mutant huntingtin production down by more than eighty percent in striatal neurons. Sustained lowering cut HOX dominance by about fifty-five percent after four months — and then HOX regained comparable dominance over time, whether treatment started at two months or at six. The transcriptional state outlives the protein that started it. That's the self-sustaining-network claim, and it's a direct challenge to the assumption that lowering mutant huntingtin resolves the downstream biology. They then built a decoy oligonucleotide carrying five families of HOX-bound DNA motifs — COR-H800 — which concentrates in striatal nuclei after intrathecal dosing, eliminates HOX dominance in R6/2 medium spiny neurons, and corrects about half of the altered transcription factor coefficients toward wild type, with a forty-two percent average reduction in deviation. Behaviorally, treated R6/2 mice nest, dig, show fewer neurological signs, and clasp later.

Now the caveats, because they are large. The single-nucleus sequencing is one to two mice per group. The behavioral work is four per group, powered for a thirty percent effect. R6/2 is an aggressive model and the authors note that HOX accounts for only about forty-five percent of the transcription factor alterations in these mice, where in human donors the alteration is essentially HOX alone — so the mouse is not reproducing the human finding faithfully. And this is a company preprint about its own asset. Treat the mouse pharmacology as a proof of concept and the human post-mortem analysis as the real contribution. The human result stands on its own and it's a genuinely uncomfortable one.

Fifth and sixth, two swings at Alzheimer's that approach the same frustration from opposite ends. From Penn's Center for Neurodegenerative Disease Research, SilencioMA: a single molecule built by covalently linking the passenger strands of two fully-modified small interfering oligonucleotides, one against MAPT and one against APP, through a polyethylene glycol linker, with the guide strands carrying five-prime vinylphosphonate. The linking is the design idea — it forces the two guides into the same cells rather than hoping two separate drugs distribute identically. Every modification except the vinylphosphonate is already used in approved siRNAs, and it needs no lipid nanoparticle for CSF delivery. One intracerebroventricular injection into 5xFAD-crossed-PS19 mice, which carry both severe amyloidosis and tauopathy, and two months later: MAPT message down about twenty-two to twenty-six percent, APP message down about twenty-five to twenty-eight percent, total tau protein down forty-six percent, phospho-threonine-231 tau down fifty-nine percent, and the three amyloid-beta species down between twenty-five and fifty-six percent. Pathological tau staining across the whole brain fell about sixty percent, though that one only approached significance. The authors' framing on safety is worth noting — because this suppresses production rather than recruiting immune clearance, it shouldn't carry the ARIA risk that the anti-amyloid antibodies do.

Against that, a Spanish-led preprint delivering a p38-MAPK-phosphorylation-resistant human E2F4 variant by AAV to homozygous 5xFAD mice — and critically, dosing after functional impairment is already established, which almost nobody does. E2F4 sits at the cell-cycle-reentry node that keeps showing up in degenerating neurons, and making it resistant to the stress kinase that normally phosphorylates it is the intervention. They report restored hippocampal long-term potentiation, restored memory, reduced amyloid and phospho-tau, and prevented hippocampal neuron loss, with proteomic and phosphoproteomic remodeling across metabolic, redox, neuroimmune and GSK3-beta-associated pathways. The detail I liked most: after contextual fear conditioning, treated animals show broader learning-associated synaptic proteomic signatures — the treatment doesn't just restore a resting state, it restores the capacity to respond to an experience. Put the two next to each other and you have the field's live argument in miniature. SilencioMA says remove both culprits at the source. E2F4 says the culprits have already triggered a downstream program and you should go treat the program. Both are 5xFAD, which overexpresses mutant human transgenes and recapitulates neither the timescale nor the complexity of the human disease — a limitation the SilencioMA authors state outright.

Seventh, something more basic and, I think, a small textbook correction. Synaptotagmin-1 is the calcium sensor for fast synchronous release, and its two C2 domains were each proposed to bind calcium through five acidic residues. The received view is that calcium binding to C2A is dispensable and C2B is where the action is. A group did the unglamorous and necessary experiment: mutate all ten putative ligands, one at a time, and compare calcium binding against evoked glutamate release side by side in neurons. All ten are crucial for evoked synchronous release. C2A calcium binding is not dispensable. Calcium binding to C2B is specifically essential for synchronization, so the asymmetry is real but it's an asymmetry of role, not of requirement. They also show that two newly described human mutations at C2B calcium ligands are dominant-negative, which matters for how you interpret a heterozygous patient variant. And four new crystal structures make a methodological point with teeth: which neutralizing substitution you pick — the charge-conserving choice versus the sterically conservative one — differentially alters the structure and therefore the function. Some of the field's accumulated "this residue doesn't matter" results may be artifacts of mutagenesis choices made for convenience thirty years ago.

Eighth, a human-physiology item with a clean negative inside it. Across two sites, forty-six women were classified as pre-menopausal, perimenopausal, post-menopausal, or users of combined continuous hormone therapy, and given paired associative stimulation — the transcranial magnetic stimulation protocol with a twenty-five-millisecond interstimulus interval that induces spike-timing-dependent plasticity in motor cortex. Baseline corticospinal excitability and short-interval intracortical inhibition were indistinguishable across groups. The plasticity response was not. Pre-menopausal women facilitated by forty-eight percent. Perimenopausal and post-menopausal women showed no facilitation at all. Hormone therapy users showed a smaller, non-significant thirty-two percent rise that nonetheless separated them from the blunted perimenopausal and post-menopausal responses. And the negative: the magnitude of plasticity was not associated with any single serum hormone concentration, or with chronological age, across the sample. So menopause stage predicts plasticity capacity but the hormone panel doesn't explain it — which is both the most interesting result here and the reason to be careful with it, since forty-six participants across four groups is thin and the hormone-therapy group is self-selected. Still, if you design motor-learning or rehabilitation protocols, the assumption that a fifty-five-year-old cortex responds to a plasticity induction protocol like a thirty-year-old one looks unsafe.

Ninth, the agentic AI item, and it's one of the better ones I've read this year. A Microsoft Research team with collaborators at Hopkins, Columbia and NYU introduce interpretable weight-space programming. The idea: give a frontier coding agent a small transformer and have it write the weights directly, in code, with training of any kind forbidden — no backpropagation, no gradient step, no fitting loop. An auditing agent judges whether each edit is human-interpretable. Deprived of gradients, the agent has to reason from prior knowledge about what computation would help, implement it explicitly, see the score, and revise. It's the workflow of a human scientist, enforced by constraint. They validate it on thirteen algorithmic tasks, then turn it on brain encoding models in vision, language and audition.

The vision result is that a hand-written model built on one fMRI dataset outpredicts interpretable baselines on another and separates early from higher visual areas. The auditory ECoG result is that the agent's features — cochleagrams with slow context, modulation filter banks, spectral flatness — explain variance that Whisper does not, in four of six patients, and improve on Whisper when combined with it. But the result that should make the rounds is the language fMRI run. The agent's score was crawling along below the GPT-2 XL baseline of point-oh-seven-nine correlation, then jumped from point-oh-six-seven to point-one-oh-four in a single iteration. The features it had just added encoded nothing about word meaning. They encoded a word's position in the story. Those position features alone reach about point-oh-nine-oh — above the strong black-box baseline — because the voxel-averaged response starts abnormally high and ends abnormally low across every scan, in all three subjects. That's a scanner-session artifact, and a black-box encoding model would have absorbed it silently as "language processing." The interpretability wasn't a tax paid for transparency; it was the only reason anyone noticed. The authors are honest about the ceiling: their agents recombine known motifs — Gabors, divisive normalization, modulation filters — rather than inventing new computational principles, and the models stay shallow, which is why they match pretrained networks in early visual cortex and trail them deeper.

Tenth, a preterm rabbit model of neonatal intraventricular hemorrhage, which in extremely premature infants leads to white-matter injury and cerebral palsy. The new anatomical observation is that blood and iron accumulate specifically in the hippocampal dentate gyrus, because the dentate sits in the path of unidirectional CSF flow and its immature vasculature is vulnerable — a mechanistic reason the hippocampus should be in the injury picture, which it largely hasn't been. Sulforaphane, an Nrf2 activator, plus deferoxamine as an iron chelator, reduced iron deposition, free hemoglobin and glial activation, with the gene-expression profile pointing at ferroptosis rather than apoptosis or autophagy as the death pathway being suppressed. There's a regional specificity worth flagging: no astrogliosis in CA1 through CA4 either way. This stops at early mechanism — no long-term outcomes, no behavior, and the authors say so and say it should be done next.

And to close, something lighter but useful: a review out of St. Jude on what they call the knowledge boundary of biological foundation models — the range of inputs, tasks and conditions over which a model's outputs can actually be trusted. They argue that boundary is set by five things: the data, the architecture, the training scheme, the evaluation scheme, and the post-analysis. The framing matters more than it sounds, because agentic systems now call protein language models and single-cell foundation models as tools, and an agent invoking a model has no way to know it's outside that model's competent range unless the range has been written down in a standardized form. Which is the review's actual proposal — not a benchmark, but a disclosure format. If you've ever watched a pipeline hand a protein language model something it was never trained to handle and then confidently report the number, you know why that's worth doing. And it rhymes with where we started: the aim and the hit are different things, and the work is in telling them apart.
