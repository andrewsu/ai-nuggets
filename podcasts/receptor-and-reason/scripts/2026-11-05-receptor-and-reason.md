## Script

Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Thursday, November fifth, twenty twenty-six.

Today's thread is the reference condition — the thing your number is a number relative to. A model looks skillful until you ask what it is skillful compared to. A receptor's basal activity is uninterpretable until you put every receptor on one scale. A serum element difference survives or dies on what you normalize it by. And the agent papers fail precisely because the systems quietly substitute a remembered reference for the one in front of them. Pick the comparator badly and you can run a flawless experiment that measures nothing.

The lead is the cleanest piece of methodology I have read this year, and it is aimed squarely at something a lot of people are currently selling. Patient world models — systems that claim to simulate clinical trials — are usually placed on a four-rung ladder: temporal prediction, action-conditioned prediction, counterfactual rollouts, and planning. The problem the author identifies is that a system's position on that ladder is currently assigned by reading its paper. Nobody measures it.

So here is the measurement. From roughly thirty thousand completed phase two and phase three trials with results posted to the public registry, construct within-trial contrasts — treatment arm minus comparator arm on the primary endpoint, as a risk difference or a standardized mean difference. The audited release holds just under ten thousand contrasts from seven thousand two hundred trials. Now the instrument itself: for any simulator, train a second, identical copy on the same trials with every intervention-identifying field withheld. Endpoint, condition, eligibility, design, population, comparator type all stay. The drug goes. Interventional skill is one minus the ratio of the full model's mean squared error to that blind model's. The logic is borrowed straight from hypothesis-only baselines in natural language inference and question-only priors in visual question answering: if your prediction survives deleting the treatment, it was never a prediction about a treatment.

The results. On a temporal split — trained on results posted before twenty twenty-three, tested after — a registry-trained gradient-boosting simulator removes about a fifth of the error of a trivial baseline. Read as a leaderboard, it predicts treatment effects. Its interventional skill is minus zero point one two, interval minus zero point two one to minus zero point zero five over five seeds. Withholding the drug makes it better. And the author killed the easy explanations: a different endpoint family, four alternative featurizations, retrained temporal cuts, placebo-controlled and active-controlled strata separately, same-molecule retrieval. No configuration produces an interval above zero. The honest reading is not "negative skill" as a finding; it is no detectable skill at all.

A frontier language model reading only the registered protocol gets zero point two zero, interval zero point zero eight to zero point three one. A smaller model of the same family and an open-weight seventy-billion-parameter model both land at zero point zero eight, at the limit of detection. And the frontier model is using the drug as a drug: swapping which arm is which reverses the sign of its prediction in eighty-six percent of contrasts, and substituting an unrelated compound is worse than withholding the compound entirely — which is what you want, because a model that merely pattern-matches on the presence of drug-shaped text would not care.

Two details lift this above a benchmark paper. First, the author turned the instrument on itself and found the blind reference is not actually drug-blind: prompted with the redacted protocol, a model recovers the withheld drug class forty-five percent of the time against a two percent chance rate, and the exact agent twenty-one percent. Eligibility criteria leak mechanism — so interventional skill is a conservative lower bound. Second, the ceiling: sampling noise in posted trial results caps attainable skill at zero point nine one. So the frontier model has a foothold on the counterfactual rung and is nowhere near the top of it, and the registry-trained model is not on the rung at all. If you are evaluating a trial simulator, this belongs next to held-out accuracy the way a majority-class baseline belongs next to a classifier.

Which brings us to agents substituting memory for data. A group at the University of Ghana, with a collaborator at Michigan, did the first forensic audit of what extended-reasoning models actually think inside a bioinformatics sandbox. The puzzle they started from is real and documented: the authors of BixBench, the autonomous bioinformatics benchmark, excluded reasoning models from their primary agentic evaluation because those models performed worse in multi-turn environments than ordinary instruction-tuned ones. Frontier proprietary models hide their chains of thought, so nobody could say why. This group ran open-weight models whose monologues you can read — a fourteen-billion reasoning distill against a matched fourteen-billion coder control — in containerized sandboxes, and captured every token.

Two failure modes. The first they call the PubMed Prior Trap, and it is the one worth the attention. Inside its own reasoning stream, the model talks itself into believing the workspace files are missing — and then, rather than failing, substitutes recalled literature for computation. It estimates adjusted p-values from what typical experiments of this kind report. This is epistemic rationalization happening in the latent monologue, invisible in the final answer, and it is the exact pathology of the lead paper transplanted into an agent: a plausible number produced without reference to the data it claims to describe. The second mode is duller but fixable — the models conflate agent-level XML action tags with Python syntax and produce interpreter errors.

Their fix, which they call dual-track decoupled reasoning, evicts the internal monologue from multi-turn history, injects explicit empirical grounding guardrails, and intercepts tool invocations at runtime. It takes the reasoning model from zero percent to nine percent accuracy and cuts code execution errors from seventy-three percent to fourteen. Now, the honest accounting: nine percent is two out of twenty-two, against one out of twenty-two for the control. That is a difference of one problem, and I would not take the doubling claim seriously at that n. The execution-error reduction and the steps-to-solution drop from five point two to one point eight are better supported. But the contribution here is not the scaffold's score — it is the transcript evidence that the failure is grounding, not capability.

The third item completes the triptych by getting the reference condition wrong in the simplest possible way. A group at Mass General Brigham and Harvard built MERIT, a multi-agent clinical decision support system for early cognitive decline in primary care — retrieval-augmented generation over multimodal electronic health records, maintaining what they call a virtual cognitive status, developed through co-design with a primary care provider, tuned on one physician's panel and tested on four others'. The target problem is real: fewer than half of dementia cases are identified, and only eight to twenty percent early.

The evaluation is five-point Likert ratings by experts. Correctness four point oh for the cognitive summaries, three point nine for the decision support; completeness, readability and adoptability in the low fours. The automated readability work is genuinely well done — patient summaries score as college-level text, the education materials as high-school level. But there is no ground truth anywhere. Nobody asks whether the patients MERIT flags actually have early cognitive decline, or whether it misses the ones who do. A correctness score of four point oh tells you a reviewer reading the output found it plausible — which, after the previous two papers, is precisely the quantity we have just learned not to trust. The system may well work. This evaluation cannot tell you, and the plan to integrate it into Epic makes that gap load-bearing rather than academic.

Now to receptor pharmacology, and a genuinely surprising repurposing result. A collaboration between the Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam, the Luxembourg Institute of Health, Medical College of Wisconsin and IIT Kanpur screened twenty-three hundred approved compounds against ACKR3 — the atypical, intrinsically beta-arrestin-biased chemokine receptor that scavenges CXCL12 and CXCL11 — in a fluorescent-chemokine binding competition assay. The hit is abemaciclib, the approved CDK4/6 inhibitor for breast cancer. Nanomolar, with affinity comparable to the field's reference ACKR3 agonist.

What makes this more than a screening artifact is how thoroughly it is nailed down, and that the selectivity runs in two directions. Within the CDK4/6 class, palbociclib, ribociclib, dalpiciclib and trilaciclib are all inactive — so this is abemaciclib the molecule, not CDK4/6 inhibition as a chemotype. Across chemokine receptors, there is no detectable activity at CXCR4 or ACKR5, the other receptors for the same chemokine. It is a full agonist: recruits both beta-arrestin one and two, drives rapid internalization, with potency and efficacy like the reference agonist. Methionine carbon-thirteen HSQC NMR confirms direct engagement and shows a conformational signature distinct from the chemokine-bound state, and a cryo-electron microscopy structure at three point two angstroms shows an inverted U-shaped pose in the orthosteric pocket with the activation rearrangements in transmembrane six, seven and helix eight. Functionally it blocks ACKR3-mediated chemokine uptake, and oral dosing in mice raises plasma CXCL12 over time, which is the in vivo target-engagement readout you would ask for.

Two consequences. The major CYP3A4 metabolites retain activity at reduced potency, so in a patient on abemaciclib there is sustained exposure of this receptor to an agonist nobody is modeling. And ACKR3 is a receptor where the therapeutic concept in demyelinating disease is antagonism — blocking the scavenger raises local CXCL12 and promotes remyelination. Abemaciclib does the opposite. Longtime listeners will remember the macrocyclic peptide platform we covered on the twenty-seventh, which pulled ACKR3 agonists and CXCR4 antagonists out of the same library. This receptor keeps turning out to have a much wider chemical neighborhood than anyone assigned it.

Staying on basal receptor behavior, with a paper from the Manglik and Coyote-Maestas labs at UCSF that fixes a measurement problem rather than discovering a mechanism. Constitutive activity is the baseline that agonists raise and inverse agonists lower, it causes disease when mutations shift it, and for orphan receptors it is the only functional readout that exists. The trouble is that every receptor's basal activity is reported relative to its own baseline, so no two are comparable. They paired a barcoded library of all human GPCRs with a transcriptional cAMP reporter and scored nine hundred one constructs in one pooled experiment, putting the whole family on a single scale for the first time.

Basal activity turns out to be a continuous distribution rather than a class of special receptors, many of the top scorers have prior literature reports of ligand-independent activity — a nice internal validation — and orphans are strongly overrepresented at the high end. They then took one of those orphans, GPR161, and mutated it exhaustively, measuring protein abundance, surface expression and cAMP signaling across five hundred twenty-eight positions. Here is the finding I would carry: only ten positions specifically alter signaling independent of expression. Constraint on basal signaling is dominated by receptor biogenesis. Which means a single-phenotype mutational scan of a GPCR — the standard design — is mostly reading out whether the protein got made and got to the surface, and will attribute to signaling what is actually folding and trafficking. You need the multiphenotype scan to separate them. That is a methods warning with teeth for anyone running deep mutational scanning on a receptor.

Two items now on sex as a pharmacological variable rather than a covariate. First, a nice piece of work from Pittsburgh, with CAMH and Toronto, on the subgenual anterior cingulate cortex in people without psychiatric illness. Using sequencing from laser-capture microdissected neurons — excitatory pyramidal cells in layers two-three and five-six, and somatostatin, parvalbumin and VIP interneurons, ten males and nine females — they found sex differences that are cell-type-specific and in opposite directions: parvalbumin cells show stronger female transcriptional enrichment, somatostatin cells male. Co-expression network modules follow the same split.

Then they did the part that makes it pharmacology. Since GPCRs are the druggable surface, they ranked sex differences across every receptor-by-cell-type combination, and the muscarinic M2 receptor in parvalbumin cells came out second strongest, enriched in females. They took that to mouse prefrontal cortex and patch-clamped it: M2 activation increased maximal firing rate in parvalbumin cells from female mice and not from males. A transcript-level sex difference in human postmortem tissue, carried into a functional electrophysiological difference in the same cell type in a different species. The sample is small — nineteen subjects — and the validation is cross-species rather than in the human tissue itself, which is the standard compromise. But the structure of the argument is the right one, and the implication is that a muscarinic agent acting on cortical interneurons could have genuinely different circuit consequences by sex.

The companion item is short, and it is the fourth installment of a series we have been following. The Falk group in Arizona has shown that sub-anesthetic ketamine reduces levodopa-induced dyskinesia through striatal TrkB signaling, that its neuroprotective effect does not run through BDNF, and — in the paper we covered Tuesday — that the anti-dyskinetic effect survives a D2-like agonist challenge but not a D1-like one. All of that was male rats. This preprint runs the female cohort. Ketamine at twenty milligrams per kilogram slowed the development of dyskinesia, and in established dyskinesia produced acute reductions in abnormal involuntary movements persisting up to forty-nine days. Crucially, forelimb adjusting steps were not impaired — it does not blunt levodopa's motor benefit — and ketamine alone improved stepping. Same caution as Tuesday about a repeated dissociative dose and ordinal scoring without described blinding. But filling in the female arm of a translational program, rather than publishing the male result and moving on, is what the field keeps saying it will do and mostly does not.

Two clinical pharmacology items on dose and route. First, a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in twenty adults aged sixty-five and over — the first to measure the trade-off between analgesia and abuse liability from delta-nine-THC in older adults, the fastest-growing group of cannabis users, for whom pain is the most common reason. Three eight-hour sessions, counterbalanced: oral THC at placebo, five and ten milligrams, or vaporized at placebo, two and four milligrams, with quantitative sensory testing for pain, subjective drug effects and reinforcement value for abuse liability, and plasma THC and its active metabolite.

The pattern dissociates the two things cleanly. Oral five milligrams relieved pain relative to placebo — though the oral arm's overall dose effect was not significant, so hold that lightly — with relief at every assessment from one to six hours, while its pleasurable effects peaked once at two and a half hours. Oral ten milligrams did nothing. Vaporized THC at either dose did not change thermal pain sensitivity at all, produced pleasurable effects within thirty minutes, and at four milligrams was the only condition that raised reinforcement value and the only one producing adverse events. Across the trial, abuse liability tracked THC exposure and analgesia did not. The practical reading: route and dose are separable levers, the oral low dose is where the therapeutic ratio sits, and more is worse in both directions. Twenty people and a laboratory pain model, so this is a signal to take into a clinical population, not a prescribing guide.

Second, a translational-model fix from Pittsburgh. Buprenorphine-naloxone sublingual film is first-line for opioid use disorder, but rodent studies use routes that do not match it, so the preclinical pharmacokinetics do not resemble what patients get. They gave actual sublingual film to unanesthetized rats, at a dose scaled from the sixteen-milligram daily clinical dose by body surface area and corrected for species differences in sublingual bioavailability. Plasma buprenorphine peaked at four nanograms per milliliter at two hours and was still at zero point nine at twenty-four — spanning the range reported as therapeutic in patients. Twenty-one days of daily dosing changed no serum liver enzyme or liver histology. A feasibility paper, male rats only, but matching the route is the unglamorous groundwork that decides whether anything built on the model transfers.

Then a biomarker result that lives or dies on its normalization, which is why it belongs in today's episode. A group measured nine elements by mass spectrometry in serum from seventy people with treatment-resistant schizophrenia and fifty-three controls. The complication they handled properly: people with schizophrenia are prone to psychogenic polydipsia, so hydration status can distort every concentration. Potassium did not differ between groups, so they normalized to it — and then also, independently, to total protein. Selenium and iron are down and rubidium is up, significantly, under both normalizations and with age, sex, tobacco, alcohol and BMI as covariates. Selenium's effect size is large, Cohen's d of minus one point one eight. Copper, which looked different before normalization, did not survive it.

Two things make this more than a correlation. Neither age nor serum clozapine level nor their interaction correlated with any of the three elements — the obvious confound in a clozapine-treated cohort, checked directly. And selenium and iron correlate with each other in controls and do not in the patients; the relationship decouples, which is a different claim than either level alone. Rubidium going up is the odd one, with a long inconclusive history in mood disorders. Cross-sectional, one cohort, no replication, and diet and smoking are only partly addressed by covariate adjustment. But the normalization discipline is exactly what the inconsistent prior literature on trace elements in schizophrenia has been missing.

A short therapeutic item. A Boston University and Bedford VA group tested ripasudil, a potent ROCK inhibitor approved in Japan for glaucoma, in Alzheimer's models. It gets into mouse brain fast, peaking half an hour after injection. Three hours after a thirty-milligram-per-kilogram dose in amyloid knock-in mice, quantitative proteomics shows significantly reduced total tau and phospho-tau at serine three-ninety-six. In patient-derived neural stem cells, it raises basal oxygen consumption, ATP-linked respiration, maximal respiration and reserve capacity. The bioenergetic half is the more interesting one, because mitochondrial dysfunction and tau pathology are usually pursued separately and this links them under one compound. The caveats are large and the authors do not oversell: a three-hour single-dose exposure is target engagement, not efficacy, there is no behavior, and ROCK inhibition is pleiotropic enough that attributing the tau change to any one pathway is premature.

And for the closer, a small, careful negative result that is the day's theme in its purest form. Two students at St. Xavier's College in Nepal asked whether rescoring docked ligands with short molecular dynamics and end-point free energy — the M-M-G-B-S-A workflow that a great many lead-optimization pipelines run as a matter of course — actually beats docking alone on a congeneric series. Twenty-nine neutral p-thirty-eight alpha kinase inhibitors from a curated public benchmark, potencies from a single publication spanning about three point eight kilocalories per mole. Each ligand docked with three random seeds, and two independent twenty-nanosecond replicas simulated from the benchmark pose.

Docking scores correlated with experiment at a Spearman rho of zero point two eight, interval minus zero point one five to zero point six two. Replicate-averaged M-M-G-B-S-A came in at zero point two four, interval minus zero point one eight to zero point six two. The paired difference is minus zero point zero three, interval minus zero point five zero to zero point four three. The methods are indistinguishable. Raising the interior dielectric from one to two changed the ranking essentially not at all — the two score sets correlate at zero point nine six with each other.

Here is the number that makes the paper. Two independent replicas of the same ligand differed by three point three kilocalories per mole on average. The entire experimental affinity range of the series is three point eight. The measurement noise is the size of the signal. And in an exploratory check, ligand size alone correlated with affinity at rho zero point six zero — better than either computed score, neither of which tracked size. Thirty ligands gives you wide intervals, this is one target and one force-field combination, and the authors are explicit that they are not claiming M-M-G-B-S-A never helps. What they are claiming, correctly, is that a single-replica rescoring run on a narrow-range congeneric series cannot resolve what it is being asked to resolve, and that reporting it without replicate variance is reporting a draw as a result.

Which is where we started. Every number today was a number against something. The ones that held up were the ones where somebody bothered to build the comparison on purpose.
