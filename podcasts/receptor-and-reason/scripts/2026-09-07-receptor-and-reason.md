## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. I'm your host, and this is your daily pass through CNS pharmacology, psychiatric and neurology drug discovery, and the computational machinery closing in on both. Eleven items today, and the thread running through them is the sign. Over and over on this week's pile, the finding is not that an effect exists but that it runs the opposite way from what the mechanism's name would lead you to expect: a stimulation protocol that works by quieting the cortex it stimulates, a dissociative anaesthetic that treats opioid relapse by turning the opioid system up, a receptor deletion that matters early and then stops mattering, a peptide whose disappearance is the inhibitory signal, and a family of models that memorises more the harder you let them think.

We open with Molecular Psychiatry and the first day-by-day neurophysiological account of Stanford Neuromodulation Therapy in treatment-resistant depression. Recall what the protocol is: high-dose accelerated intermittent theta burst stimulation, ten sessions a day for five days, with the left dorsolateral prefrontal target chosen per patient to be maximally functionally anticorrelated with subgenual anterior cingulate. The sham-controlled trial gave a sixty-two percent mean reduction in depression severity in five days. Nobody had watched what the cortex was doing while it happened. This group recorded single-pulse TMS with EEG at baseline, before and after every treatment day, and at one month, in twenty-four patients from the original double-blind trial — twelve active, twelve sham, fourteen sessions each.

Two things had to be established before any of it means anything. First, that evoked potentials are stable enough across an intensive five-day protocol to serve as a measuring stick at all. They are: intraclass correlations of point eight one, point seven eight and point eight three across the early, middle and late windows, and within-subject waveform similarity of point seven three against point four zero between subjects, in both arms equally. These are genuine individual fingerprints, not treatment artefacts. Second, spatial specificity, which they get by also stimulating a vertex control site where nothing changed in either group.

Then the result, and the direction is the point. Early-window global mean field amplitude at the treatment site fell steadily in the active group — to seventy-two percent of baseline by day three, sixty percent by day five, still sixty-six percent immediately post-treatment and seventy percent at one month. Sham sat between ninety-eight and a hundred and one percent the whole time. Cortical reactivity at the stimulated site goes down, not up, and it does so progressively rather than crossing a threshold. Source-localised subgenual activity fell too, from the first post-stimulation session onward and still significantly reduced at one month, while depth-matched control regions gave nothing. Middle and late windows did not survive correction.

I'd read this as the strongest available argument that accelerated theta burst is doing something cumulative rather than tripping a switch, which has direct dosing consequences: if the curve is still moving on day five, the protocol length is a choice nobody has optimised. The interpretive question is whether reduction means normalisation of a hyperexcitable frontolimbic circuit or plain suppression, and with no healthy comparator group this design cannot say — the authors are appropriately careful about that. Two caveats they flag themselves and I'd weight heavily. The network-level results, reductions in default mode, frontoparietal, dorsal attention and limbic networks, were not corrected across the seven networks tested. And the headline biomarker claim — baseline subgenual activity predicting improvement at a correlation of minus point six seven — rests on twelve patients with no correction for multiple correlations. That is a hypothesis, not a stratification tool.

From a device to a drug, and a preprint from Yasmin Hurd's group at Mount Sinai with Aya Osman at Albany that inverts the pharmacology you'd expect. The clinical puzzle behind it is old: naltrexone blocks ketamine's antidepressant effect, ketamine binds mu and kappa receptors, a cryo-EM structure has it engaging mu directly, and ketamine and its metabolites both behave as positive allosteric modulators of opioid receptors. So what does a single dose of ketamine do to the endogenous opioid system, and does it help opioid relapse?

In male Long-Evans rats, one intraperitoneal dose at ten milligrams per kilogram raised transcription of all four of the opioid-system genes in nucleus accumbens — the mu receptor, the kappa receptor, prodynorphin and proenkephalin — at three days, and the effect was still there at seven days only at the higher dose. Nothing in prefrontal cortex or dorsal striatum. Subregional dissection put it in the shell, not the core. The protein followed: mu receptor abundance up in two independent cohorts, DAMGO-stimulated GTP-gamma-S binding enhanced seven days later, so more receptor and more G protein coupling per receptor, and leu-enkephalin elevated in both free and total pools. Then the behaviour — heroin self-administration, ketamine given during forced abstinence, and a heroin-primed reinstatement session seven days later where ketamine-treated animals pressed the active lever significantly less.

Sit with the mechanistic shape of that. Proenkephalin is one of the most reliably induced genes after chronic antidepressant treatment, but it normally takes two weeks or more of daily fluoxetine to move the peptide. One ketamine dose does it for seven days. The authors' account is a fast allosteric phase at the receptor followed by a slow transcriptional phase, which fits the metabolite data, since the metabolites keep the allosteric activity and lose the orthosteric binding. What makes me cautious is the same thing that makes it interesting: the intervention for opioid use disorder is upregulating mu receptor number and signalling. That is a plausible route to restoring a depleted endogenous tone, and it is also the direction you would worry about for opioid sensitivity. And the behavioural effect was on primed reinstatement only, not cue-induced, in male rats, with four animals per group in the first molecular cohort.

Stay on mu receptors, because a preprint out of the pain field argues that in chronic neuropathic pain the receptor is not where the action is. The rostral ventromedial medulla is the descending pain hub, densely mu-expressing, and morphine injected there is potently analgesic. This group first showed that chemogenetically activating either glutamatergic or GABAergic medullary neurons is antinociceptive at baseline, but after nerve injury the glutamatergic population switches sides and enhances pain responses. Then they used CRISPR to delete mu receptor selectively from each population.

The result is a clean dissociation in time. Mu knockout in either cell type precipitated earlier onset of neuropathic hypersensitivity — and had no effect on the intensity of established chronic pain. So endogenous opioid signalling in the medulla is holding the line during the transition into a chronic state, and once the state is established it is largely irrelevant to how much it hurts. If that holds, it reframes why systemic opioids underperform so badly in chronic neuropathic pain: the descending circuit they are supposed to recruit has already stopped using that lever. It also suggests the therapeutic window for anything working through this node is early, which is exactly the window in which nobody wants to start an opioid. Mouse, one injury model, reflexive endpoints.

Neuropathic pain and mood run together clinically, and a Molecular Psychiatry paper takes the epitranscriptome at that junction. The target is YTHDF1, the m-six-A reader, in anterior cingulate. Knockouts look normal at baseline — no locomotor change, no anxiety, no anhedonia — and then two bouts of acute social defeat unmask a full phenotype: reduced velocity in social approach and open field, which they read as psychomotor retardation, reduced sucrose preference, an anxiety phenotype in the light-dark box, and it persists four weeks after a single acute stress exposure. Hyperalgesia comes along with it.

The mechanism has two nice inversions. First, this reader is not doing decay. Genome-wide stability analysis and actinomycin D chase both say target transcripts are not destabilised; YTHDF1 is enhancing translation. Second, the effector direction. The downstream target is cyclase-associated protein one, which works with cofilin to accelerate actin filament depolymerisation. Lose the reader, lose the protein — messenger unchanged, protein down — lose depolymerisation, and the filamentous-to-globular actin ratio in cingulate neurons rises. And the regulation sits in a narrow early postnatal window: reader protein peaks in the first five days of life and declines, its target climbs in mirror image, and cingulate protein is already reduced at postnatal day three. Loss of the reader also doubles cingulate c-fos, about eighty-five percent of it in glutamatergic neurons, which lines up with the well-established story that cingulate hyperactivity drives the anxiodepressive component of chronic pain. Reader expression is reduced in cingulate and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex of patients with major depression, which is what gives the model its claim on relevance. What I'd want before believing the developmental window is the adult-onset conditional — if the critical period is really perinatal, the target is a vulnerability marker rather than something to drug in a depressed adult.

Now the item where a peptide's absence is the signal. A preprint from Luping Yin's lab at Westlake asks why aggressive male mice attack a rival before mating while socially dominant-but-nonaggressive males mate immediately. The circuit they land on is a kappa receptor circuit, and it inverts twice. Estrogen-receptor-one neurons in ventrolateral ventromedial hypothalamus recruit kappa-expressing GABAergic interneurons in medial preoptic area, which inhibit the preoptic mating neurons and delay mounting. They recruit those interneurons two ways: direct glutamatergic excitation, and disinhibition — by suppressing dynorphin release from arcuate prodynorphin terminals. Kappa receptor is Gi-coupled, so removing the peptide is what turns the interneuron on. Using a genetically encoded kappa sensor they show that during rival encounters the dynorphin decline is larger and the interneuron activity stronger in aggressive males, and CRISPR knockout of arcuate prodynorphin abolishes the competition-induced mating delay.

For anyone tracking the kappa antagonist field — aticaprant, navacaprant, and a decade of the "dynorphin equals dysphoria" framing — this is a useful corrective. Here the tonic dynorphin signal is permissive for behaviour and its withdrawal is inhibitory, in a hypothalamic circuit at the same receptor. Whatever a systemic kappa antagonist does, it is not doing one thing. Animal numbers are small throughout, three to eight per group, which is normal for this kind of circuit dissection and still worth knowing.

Adjacent to that, and useful mostly as an assay contribution, Neuropsychopharmacology has a new social competition test that gets female mice into the picture. The design is a platform in cold water; occupancy is the resource, and relative rank comes out of who holds it. Ranks were stable in both sexes, and the structure they recover is messier than the classic tube test allows — some cages with clearly separated dominants and subordinates, others with tightly clustered ranks. Dominant animals of both sexes shared the platform less. The regional result is the interesting one: prefrontal activation, despite subregion differences, was not associated with overall rank, which sits awkwardly with the canonical prefrontal-synaptic-efficacy account of dominance. What tracked rank was lateral habenula — greatest activation in dominant females, and a significant association with platform occupancy in males. It is a c-fos study, so this is a correlation with a circuit, not a manipulation of one. But if you are choosing a dominance assay for a pharmacology experiment in both sexes, this one resolves structure the standard tests cannot.

Two target-discovery items now, starting with a genome-wide screen from Aimee Kao and Martin Kampmann's groups at UCSF with Simon Newstead at Oxford and Lilly. The question is what sets neuronal lysosomal pH, which matters because lysosomes alkalinise with age and in neurodegeneration, and because even modest pH drift changes protease substrate preference for tau, TDP-forty-three and alpha-synuclein. They ran CRISPR interference genome-wide in iPSC-derived neurons with a genetically encoded lysosomal pH sensor, recovered the known regulators, and pulled out two unexpected pathways — protein UFMylation and mitochondrial homeostasis, specifically mitochondrial translation and complex one assembly, which fits recent work on lysosome-mitochondria contact sites and the plain fact that maintaining a proton gradient is expensive.

The lead hit is PQLC2, a lysosomal cationic amino acid exporter. Losing it blocks acidification — and does so independently of amino acid transport, which is the inversion here: a transporter that turns out to matter for something other than what it transports. They then bring in a PQLC2 variant found in a tauopathy patient, leucine forty-one to proline. Expressed in knockout neurons it fails to rescue lysosomal pH, and tau accumulates. A second rare variant, previously linked to an age-associated retinitis pigmentosa, also fails to rescue. Neither transports arginine, and both express poorly in the oocyte assay, so I would not yet call the pH function cleanly separable from a folding problem — the two variants could simply be broken proteins. The screen is the durable contribution; the human genetics is a promising anchor that needs more alleles.

Turning to the computational half of the show, and a paper that should change how you read any claim about language models predicting molecular properties. The setup: benchmark accuracy cannot distinguish a model that predicts a value from one that remembers it. The authors' detector is elegant because it sidesteps the usual requirements. No logits, no canaries, no ordering — instead you ask for three significant figures, count how many one-figure agreements survive to two and how many of those survive to three, and test that retention against the best a molecule-blind guesser could do knowing only the label distribution. The premise is that no model predicts the third digit of an experimental measurement, because that digit is inside the measurement noise. So retention above the floor is memory.

They ran twenty-two frontier models across twelve benchmarks on the same five hundred molecules. On five datasets more than half the models show verbatim retrieval; elsewhere it is isolated. The anchor example is worth remembering: the solvation benchmark assigns most entries a default experimental uncertainty of point six kilocalories per mole, and one model reproduces it with a median absolute error of point zero two five. That is twenty-four times better than the measurement it is being scored against, which is not a prediction of anything. Claude Opus 5 reproduces forty percent of that benchmark to three figures; the textbook boiling-point positive control runs at eighty-five percent across most of the panel.

Three findings I'd carry forward. Retrieval is not the benchmark file being crawled — how often a benchmark's molecules appear across documents in open pretraining indexes tracks its retrieval rate at a correlation of point eight eight, while the benchmark's own column headers track it at point two zero. So it is coming from secondary sources, encyclopaedia entries and handbooks, which you cannot decontaminate by removing a file. Second, and this is the sign inversion: the same models on the same molecules with the same prompt were flagged eighty-nine percent more often at higher reasoning effort. More thinking, more remembering. Third, older models are less contaminated than current frontier ones, and suppressing retrieval by transforming the structure strings compresses the models' relative error differences — so some of what looks like capability spread on these benchmarks is memorisation spread. If you have a language-model baseline in an ADMET or property-prediction paper, this is the control you now owe a reviewer.

From evaluation to execution, Alán Aspuru-Guzik's group with Varinia Bernales has an agentic layer for self-driving laboratories, and the design decision is the interesting part. Bayesian optimisation is the standard planner for closed-loop chemistry and it is still hard to deploy because someone has to wire the optimiser to the instrument, configure the campaign and interpret its trajectory. Rather than putting a language model in the optimisation loop, they separate the two: the agent constructs and supervises the campaign, the campaign holds persistent optimisation state and runs its own repetitive loops, and control returns to the agent only when progress needs interpretation or the campaign needs revising. Every decision stays auditable, and the loop does not inherit language-model variance.

They took it to two physical platforms. In a closed-loop contact angle campaign it detected and corrected a mid-run measurement failure, drove the contact angle from seventy-one point four degrees to sixty-seven point eight against a target of sixty-five plus or minus one, and then — the part I like — correctly concluded the target was probably unreachable with the reagents on hand and recommended changing the formulation. In a five-day multi-objective flow chemistry campaign it took yield from thirty to fifty-nine percent in twenty-three experiments, and despite substantial inference cost came in cheaper than the human-directed comparison on both money and starting material, at a more mass-efficient operating point. An agent whose useful output is "stop, your target is infeasible" is a better demonstration of judgment than one that reports a win.

Two short items to close, both about the durability of computational claims. First, a bibliometric study from the Medical College of Wisconsin on model deprecation. They pulled biomedical papers applying a specific language model from PubMed, used an extraction agent over sixty-one thousand abstracts to identify model names, and compiled lifecycle data for the fifty most-used models. Across nearly nine thousand paper-model mentions in five thousand two hundred publications, seventy-eight percent cite a commercial closed-weight model, and forty-two percent involve a model already retired at publication or scheduled to retire within two years of it. Median interval from publication to retirement: five hundred thirty-eight days. Whatever you think about reproducibility in general, this is a specific and fixable reporting failure — the artefact under study evaporates about eighteen months after the paper describing it appears, and almost nobody preserves outputs or reports access dates well enough to reconstruct it.

And last, a short commentary from Elliot Murphy at UTHealth Houston that is worth reading if you follow model-brain alignment work, responding to a recent proposal that language models can serve as mechanistic models of language processing. His argument is compact: an encoding model fits an unconstrained linear map from model representations to neural responses, and an unconstrained map is free to shear and rescale, so what survives it is collinearity and parallelism, not the distances and neighbourhood structure that "shared geometry" is normally taken to mean. Worse, both systems are responding to the same structured stimulus, so alignment can be inherited from the input rather than from any shared operation. And even granting shared geometry in the strongest sense, a geometry fixes neither the operations that produced it nor the operations defined over it. He makes the same point with surprisal, which predicts neural responses beautifully while demonstrably failing to identify the syntactic structure that generates it. A strong descriptor is not a mechanism. It is a philosophy-of-inference argument rather than a new result, and the same argument transfers intact to every foundation model we discuss on this show that claims to have learned biology because it predicts a biological measurement.

Eleven items, and the recurring lesson is to check the sign before you check the p-value: stimulation that heals by quieting, an anaesthetic that treats opioid relapse by raising opioid tone, a receptor that matters only before the disease becomes chronic, a peptide whose withdrawal is the inhibitory signal, a transporter that regulates pH without transporting, and models that remember more the harder they think.
