# Receptor & Reason — 2026-08-16

## Script

Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Sunday, August sixteenth. After yesterday's journals-only run — the bioRxiv feed was down — the preprint well is full again, and today is a genuinely rich pharmacology day: ten items, heavy on mechanism, a run of psychedelics and biased-receptor signaling in the middle, and a clean regulatory win to close on. One theme is going to keep resurfacing, so I'll flag it now: sex. Three separate papers today only make sense if you split males from females, and pooling would have hidden the result. Let's get into it.

We open with the most exciting opioid preprint I've read in a while, out of a Cornell-led group, and it goes straight at the oldest problem in the field: can you keep morphine's analgesia while throwing away its reward and its tolerance? Everyone knows those three effects all flow from the same mu opioid receptor but then diverge downstream — into different signaling pathways, different cell types, different circuits — which is exactly why building a clean opioid has mostly failed. Their move is to point at a single upstream node: the RNA demethylase FTO, the fat-mass-and-obesity gene product, an enzyme that strips methyl marks off RNA. Delete FTO genetically, or inhibit it pharmacologically, and morphine reward — measured by conditioned place preference — drops, and antinociceptive tolerance to both morphine and fentanyl drops, while the analgesia itself is completely preserved. That's the dissociation people have been chasing for decades. And the mechanism is the surprising part. FTO is famous for erasing m6A on messenger RNA, but here messenger-RNA m6A didn't move across more than twenty thousand sites. What changed was a chemically related mark — m6Am, that's N6,2'-O-dimethyladenosine — on small nuclear RNA, the spliceosome's own RNA. In mice engineered so they can't make m6Am at all, losing FTO stopped helping — which is about as clean a causal chain as you'll see in a v1 preprint. They even dissected the anatomy: knocking FTO down in the nucleus accumbens shell killed reward but not tolerance, while knocking it down in the dorsal root ganglion killed tolerance but not reward. So the pitch is FTO inhibitors as an adjuvant to opioids: same pain relief, less addiction liability, less tolerance. The obvious caution is that FTO touches an enormous amount of biology — metabolism, other neuropsychiatric processes — so systemic inhibition won't be free of consequences. But as a concept, this is a big swing, and a credible one.

Staying with the theme of prying a drug's therapeutic effect apart from its unwanted one — psychedelics. DMT and 5-MeO-DMT are structurally close, short-acting tryptamine psychedelics, both now in clinical development, and this preclinical comparison teases apart their acute trip from their lasting plasticity. In the head-twitch response — the standard rodent proxy for 5-HT2A hallucinogenic activity — DMT gives a bell-shaped dose-response curve while 5-MeO-DMT climbs monotonically, so they're already pharmacologically distinct at the front door. Antagonize 5-HT2A or 5-HT1D, or turn on 5-HT1A, and you flatten the head-twitch for both compounds without abolishing their effect on marble burying, an OCD-like screening assay — meaning the hallucinogenic signature and at least one therapeutic-like behavior come apart under the right receptor manipulation. On the plasticity side, both raise TrkB phosphorylation and increase synaptic proteins twelve days after a single dose, but DMT engages more broadly across default-mode and hippocampal territory — and, interestingly, DMT uniquely lowers hippocampal BDNF and reprograms frontal-cortex glutathione and energy metabolism, so "more plasticity everywhere" is too simple. The take-home is the rational-design one this audience cares about: if you can retain the plasticity while dialing down the 5-HT2A-driven hallucination, you move toward a tolerable, scalable, take-home psychedelic. It's mechanistically busy and it's preclinical, but it's exactly the dissociation the field needs to be mapping.

Third, MDMA — and a paper that's really about scalability rather than a new mechanism. MDMA-assisted therapy has strong PTSD efficacy data, but the protocols are long and therapist-intensive, and that cost is the real bottleneck to deploying it. This group used a learned-helplessness model — mice that keep failing to escape after inescapable stress, which serves as a trauma-susceptibility screen — and asked whether pairing MDMA with a brief exposure to the trauma cue beats either alone. It did: MDMA alone cut the behavioral deficits, and MDMA plus exposure produced rapid, sustained recovery on active avoidance, with dose-dependent effects on anxiety- and depression-like measures. Now, this is proof-of-principle in rodents, and "reverses PTSD-like features" is doing some heavy lifting in that title, so temper it accordingly. But the useful idea is concrete: the drug may let you compress the psychotherapy into something far shorter, and compressing the therapy is precisely the thing standing between MDMA and broad clinical access.

That trauma-and-extinction thread carries straight into a lovely circuit-and-pharmacology paper on oxytocin. Social trauma leaves animals — and people — avoiding social contact, and the failure to overwrite those aversive associations is the core barrier in social anxiety disorder. Using social fear conditioning in mice, the group finds a discrete population of oxytocin-receptor neurons in the caudal lateral septum that acts as the hub for extinction learning. And the coding is remarkably precise: successful extinction is best predicted by these neurons being transiently silenced at exactly the moment of social contact — the mice that couldn't silence them at that instant didn't extinguish. Chemogenetically shutting the neurons down at the wrong time impaired learning, so it's the timing, not just the level, that matters. Then the part this audience will care about most: when they dissected downstream oxytocin-receptor signaling, it was the Gi-coupled arm, not the Gq-coupled arm, that accelerated extinction and promoted social approach. That is a biased-agonism target in the making — an oxytocin-receptor ligand tuned toward Gi signaling could be a genuine handle on treatment-resistant social anxiety, and it's a reminder that "activate the oxytocin receptor" is far too blunt an instruction.

Biased signaling is the natural bridge to the next one. Beta-arrestin-2 is the other face of the GPCR coin — the scaffold that desensitizes G-protein signaling and then runs its own downstream program — and it's ubiquitous in the brain. This preprint asks whether it plays a sex-specific role in Alzheimer's, using the APP/PS1 mouse. Delete beta-arrestin-2 and, in males, you get less amyloid-beta oligomer burden, more autophagic flux, quieter astrocytes and microglia, and broad cognitive rescue — spatial working memory, spatial learning, cognitive flexibility, recognition memory. In females: amyloid and astrogliosis unchanged, microglia if anything more reactive, and improvement limited to recognition memory. The male effect tracked mechanistically with reduced Akt and GSK-3-beta phosphorylation and enhanced autophagy through a GSK-3-beta–ZBTB16 route. The message isn't so much "beta-arrestin-2 is a target" as it is a warning: a GPCR-targeted Alzheimer's therapy that works through this node may help one sex and do nothing — or something worse — in the other, and if you run the study pooled you will conclude, wrongly, that it doesn't work at all. That's sex-stratified design point number one of three today.

From individual targets to a target theory — a deliberately provocative framework piece in Progress in Neuro-Psychopharmacology. Schizophrenia pharmacology is still, fundamentally, dopamine blockade, and that leaves negative symptoms, cognition, and real-world function largely untouched. The author proposes what he calls the polyamine stress response: the idea that stress resets intracellular polyamine pools, and that reset — running through the arginine–nitric-oxide–agmatine–polyamine axis — drives oxidative load, glial reactivity, and membrane and circuit instability. Agmatine, a decarboxylated-arginine metabolite with its own neuromodulatory activity, sits near the center, with the enzyme agmatinase proposed as a controllable regulatory node, and he points to irreversible inhibitors of the upstream entry steps borrowed from plant biology as a way to actually manipulate the flux. Let me be honest: this is a hypothesis paper, not data, and parts of it are frankly speculative. But it's the kind of non-monoamine reframing schizophrenia badly needs, and unlike most manifestos it names concrete, measurable, druggable set points and lays out a roadmap that starts with polyamine-flux modulators as pharmacological probes in animals before committing to selective drugs. Worth having in your head as a hypothesis to shoot at.

Another druggable-metabolism angle, and this one comes with a methodological warning shot. In a hundred and seventy-three people with bipolar disorder from the French FACE-BD cohort, researchers measured kynurenine-pathway metabolites — the tryptophan-breakdown arm that produces neuroactive species like kynurenic acid and quinolinic acid — against which psychotropics each patient was actually taking. The drugs left clear fingerprints. Valproate users had broadly lower tryptophan, kynurenine, xanthurenic acid, and quinolinic acid. Lithium users had higher kynurenine and a higher kynurenine-to-tryptophan ratio. Lamotrigine users had more quinolinic acid. And these survived adjustment for age, sex, BMI, tobacco, and mood symptoms. Antipsychotics and antidepressants showed no signal. The direct implication is a caution the whole field should absorb: a decade-plus of kynurenine biomarker studies in psychiatry may have been partly reading medication effects as disease biology. If you work on this pathway — and a lot of people are, because it's such an attractive druggable target — current drug exposure is a confounder you have to model explicitly, not wave away.

Eighth, drug repurposing with — you guessed it — a sex-differences twist, in Neuropharmacology. The prenatal valproate rat is the workhorse model for autism-like phenotypes. This group first shows the model's own cortical phenotype is sex-specific: different pyramidal-neuron complexity, spine density, and morphology, and divergent gene-expression signatures — males up on neuropeptide-signaling genes like prodynorphin and the dopamine D2 and adenosine A2a receptors, females down on vascular-permeability and inflammation genes. Then they repurpose dimethyl fumarate — the Nrf2 activator that's an approved multiple-sclerosis drug — and in females it rescued object recognition, anxiety, and social behavior, corrected the abnormal cortical oscillatory power and cross-frequency coupling, and suppressed microglial activity, that last effect selectively in females. So you get a repurposing candidate with a plausible antioxidant-and-anti-inflammatory mechanism, and — sex-stratified point number three — a clear demonstration that if you'd tested this in males only, or pooled the sexes, you might have concluded it doesn't work. The honest caveat is that it's one model and the female-specific benefit needs testing elsewhere, but as a repurposing lead it's a nice one.

Two quicker ones to finish. On the computational side — because agentic AI for discovery is squarely in this show's lane — a system called LungChat, a conversational multi-agent setup in which a supervisor decomposes a natural-language question into parallel, tool-grounded tasks: single-cell and spatial analysis, literature and clinical-trial synthesis, and drug repurposing. The clever piece is a module they call direction-aware repurposing and targeting, which distinguishes compounds that reverse a disease's transcriptional program from those that reinforce it — cell type by cell type — which is a safety check most connectivity-based repurposing screens skip entirely. In their case studies it independently re-derived saracatinib — a Src-family kinase inhibitor — as an idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis candidate, matching a real clinical trial, and recovered an established COPD steroid through a single orchestrated analysis. Their ablations argue that the hierarchical orchestration is what buys them token efficiency and, importantly, grounded abstention — knowing when to say it doesn't know. It's lung, not brain, so I flag it as adjacent rather than on-target — but the transferable idea is the architecture: verifiable, tool-grounded, willing to abstain, with a directionality-aware safety layer on the repurposing. That's the direction computational pharmacology is heading, whatever the tissue.

And to close, a concrete regulatory win. The FDA has approved florquinitau — a fluorine-18 tau-PET tracer, brand name Tauklarify, the agent many of you know from the literature as MK-6240 — for imaging tau neurofibrillary-tangle pathology in adults with cognitive impairment being evaluated for Alzheimer's. It's positioned to complement amyloid PET, not replace it, and it is explicitly not validated for non-Alzheimer's tauopathies, which is a meaningful limitation to keep in mind. The approval leaned on the HEAD study — a prospective, within-participant head-to-head published in the Lancet this spring — plus blinded-read studies across several hundred participants. Why it matters beyond the individual clinic: a validated, standardized tau imaging agent is really trial infrastructure. It's how you enrich and stratify anti-tau drug trials and how you read target engagement over time. So this is quietly good news for tau-directed therapeutics, not just for diagnosis. That's the show — ten items, a lot of good mechanism, and a standing reminder threaded through half of them: stop pooling your sexes. Back tomorrow.
