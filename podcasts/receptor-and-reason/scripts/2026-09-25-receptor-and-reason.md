We open in Dublin, with a study that does something the epilepsy field keeps promising and rarely delivers. The claim that neuroinflammation drives drug resistance in epilepsy is about fifteen years old and has a large, consistent animal literature behind it. The compound class everyone points to is antagonists of the P two X seven receptor, the ATP-gated ion channel that sits on microglia and gates interleukin one beta release. What has never been shown is that any of it works in human brain. This group, at the Royal College of Surgeons in Ireland with the neurosurgery service at Beaumont Hospital, took tissue resected during epilepsy surgery, cut acute slices, and tested the drugs there.

The preparation is worth describing because its internal controls are the best part. Slices were bathed in modified artificial cerebrospinal fluid with high potassium and low magnesium, which evokes seizure-like events beginning about forty minutes in and persisting for at least two hours. Crucially, those events appeared only in tissue from patients who had actually had seizures — temporal lobe epilepsy, frontal lobe epilepsy, or tumour patients with a seizure history. Tissue from tumour patients who had never seized produced nothing. So the model is reading something about epileptic tissue rather than just about potassium.

The headline result is a negative one followed by a positive one. The P two X seven antagonists on their own did not suppress seizure-like activity at all — if anything amplitude and power drifted upward, consistent with the slow worsening the preparation shows over time regardless of treatment. Add carbamazepine, the sodium-channel blocker, at a dose that by itself produced only mild suppression, and the combination cut both the number and the duration of seizure-like events. A second, structurally distinct allosteric antagonist reproduced the effect. Switch the anti-seizure medication to lorazepam, which works through an entirely different mechanism as a positive allosteric modulator at the GABA-A receptor, and the potentiation held. Then the mechanism: antagonist treatment lowered interleukin one beta, and betaine — which blocks interleukin one beta release rather than touching the purinergic receptor at all — mimicked the effect when paired with carbamazepine. Finally, mice with P two X seven deleted specifically from microglia responded better to carbamazepine during status epilepticus.

Why this matters more than another positive rodent study: P two X seven is one of those receptors with marked species differences in sequence and function, and the lead compound here has substantially higher affinity for the human receptor than for the rodent one. Testing in human tissue isn't a courtesy, it's the only way to know the clinical candidate engages the clinical target. And the result reframes the trial. This is not an anticonvulsant. It is a resistance-reversal agent, which means the endpoint is conversion of non-responders, not seizure freedom on monotherapy, and the comparator has to be the existing regimen rather than placebo. The caveat I'd flag is that carbamazepine was deliberately used at a sub-maximal dose. That leaves headroom for a combination effect to appear, and it is the design choice that most flatters the hypothesis. A full dose-response for the anti-seizure medication alone, with and without the antagonist, is the experiment that would settle it.

That first paper is about a receptor that only matters because of the cell type it sits on. The second is about receptor geography at a much larger scale, and it's the one I'd hand to anyone still describing schizophrenia pathology as a dopamine story. A Seville-led group, with Cambridge, has a cohort of three hundred fifty-seven first-episode schizophrenia-spectrum patients and a hundred ninety-six controls, followed with structural imaging and clinical assessment out to twenty years. They segmented thalamus, hippocampus, amygdala and brainstem into subregions, modelled volume against diagnosis, antipsychotic dose and symptoms, and then did something I wish more imaging papers did: they took the resulting maps of effect sizes and asked which normative neurotransmitter receptor and transporter distributions those maps spatially line up with.

The anatomical findings are sensible on their own. The earliest components of the hippocampal trisynaptic circuit — dentate gyrus, C A four, C A three, molecular layer — carry the largest first-episode deficits, consistent with input-pathway vulnerability early rather than progressive decline afterward. Amygdala reductions are widespread already at first episode, largest in the accessory basal nucleus, and they track positive symptom severity strongly, with the hippocampal-amygdalar transition area showing some of the biggest effects. Negative symptoms barely register against subcortical volume at all.

The receptor mapping is where it gets interesting. The diagnosis map co-localised with cannabinoid one, histamine three and serotonin six densities. The interaction between diagnosis and age — the regions where patients diverge further from controls as they get older — lined up with dopamine transporter, D two, histamine three, mu-opioid, norepinephrine transporter and serotonin transporter. And the medication map, meaning the hippocampal subregions where cumulative antipsychotic dose tracked volume loss, co-localised with muscarinic M one, cannabinoid one, GABA, glutamate and serotonin one A densities — while showing the opposite relationship with D two, alpha four beta two nicotinic and mu-opioid. Read that again. The hippocampal territory where antipsychotic exposure and volume loss travel together is territory that is relatively poor in D two receptors and rich in cholinergic, cannabinoid and amino-acid-transmitter markers.

I'd be careful how much to make of that. These are spatial correlations between group-level statistical maps and group-level PET and autoradiography atlases from entirely different people; they cannot speak to mechanism in an individual, and confounding between illness severity and prescribed dose is exactly the confound this design cannot break. Attrition is substantial too — a hundred fifty-two retained versus a hundred eighty-one dropped by the five-year mark, though baseline symptoms and dose didn't differ between them. But as a hypothesis generator it is pointed. If the volumetric cost of long-term antipsychotic treatment is concentrated where D two is sparse, that cost is probably not a D two-occupancy effect, and the muscarinic entry on that list is hard to ignore in a year when M one and M four agonism has become the most interesting thing in the antipsychotic pipeline.

Third, a depression neuroimaging paper in Molecular Psychiatry that earns its place mostly by being willing to report a null. The Marburg-Münster Affective Disorders cohort, eighteen hundred and four participants, roughly split between healthy and depressed. The authors define resilience not as the absence of illness despite adversity, but as a residual: fit a ridge-regularised model predicting depressive symptom severity from twenty-two risk and protective variables, then take how much better than predicted each person actually did. Cumulative risk explained just under fifty percent of symptom variance at baseline, and the residual showed moderate two-year stability at a correlation of about point three two.

Then they looked for brain structure. At baseline, nothing — neither region-of-interest nor whole-brain analyses found any morphometric correlate of resilience. Prospectively, in the eight hundred and eight people with two-year follow-up, higher baseline resilience predicted smaller gray matter volume in the left inferior orbitofrontal gyrus and temporal pole, with a partial correlation of about point one eight, and no cortical thickness effect at all. Smaller. The existing literature overwhelmingly reports the opposite direction — greater volume in prefrontal and limbic regions among resilient individuals.

The authors offer two readings, greater neural efficiency or a delayed biological cost of adapting, and are candid that they can't distinguish them. I'd add a third that they partly acknowledge: the earlier literature's direction may have been an artifact of defining resilience against a single stressor in small, diagnostically-selected samples, and once you define it dimensionally against cumulative risk in a large cohort, most of the effect evaporates. The detail that stuck with me is that sixty percent of the most resilient group still met criteria for lifetime major depression. Better-than-expected is not the same as well, and the field's habit of treating those as interchangeable is exactly what this residual framing is built to break.

Fourth, and staying with cohort imaging but moving to a pharmacology question with a genotype in it. A CAMH and Toronto group asked whether the decades-long mess of menopause hormone therapy and cognition is partly an exposure-definition problem — whether lumping formulations together has been washing out real effects. Using the National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center data, eight thousand seven hundred forty-one participants, they separated estradiol, estradiol plus a progestogen, conjugated equine estrogens, and conjugated equine estrogens plus medroxyprogesterone acetate, and crossed all of it with APOE epsilon four status.

The cognitive results are formulation- and domain-specific in a way that supports the premise. Conjugated equine estrogens users had worse episodic memory than both estradiol users and non-users, but better category fluency than non-users. Both estradiol and conjugated estrogen users outperformed the combination-with-medroxyprogesterone group on naming. These survived bootstrap resampling and propensity-score-weighted sensitivity analysis. The pharmacology is plausible — conjugated equine estrogens are a mixture with a very different receptor and metabolic profile from bioidentical estradiol, and medroxyprogesterone acetate has a long history of being the component that spoils outcomes in this literature.

Then the imaging subset, and here I want to be blunt about what the data will and won't carry. In nine hundred thirty participants, estradiol use interacted with APOE epsilon four status across eight structural measures — inferior parietal, supramarginal, parahippocampal and entorhinal thickness, accumbens, ventral diencephalon and hippocampal volume, and white matter hyperintensities — all pointing the same way, with more favourable structure in carriers taking estradiol and not in non-carriers. Consistent direction across eight measures is genuinely more persuasive than any one p-value. But the estradiol imaging group is fifty-nine people, of whom roughly twenty-one are carriers, the p-values run from point zero zero three to point zero three five without multiple-comparison correction, and in the much larger cognitive analysis the formal hormone-by-APOE interaction was not significant at all — the carrier story there is descriptive. So the honest summary is that the formulation effect on cognition is the well-supported finding, and the genotype interaction is a hypothesis with suggestive imaging behind it. That is still a useful result, because it says the next trial should stratify on both formulation and APOE rather than continuing to ask whether hormone therapy, undifferentiated, is good for the brain.

Fifth, and this is a measurement paper disguised as a psychiatry paper. John Torous's group at Beth Israel Deaconess looked at nine hundred ninety-seven person-days from twenty outpatients taking aripiprazole through the digital-medicine version, the one with the ingestible sensor and the wearable patch. Of three hundred twenty-two days with no detected ingestion, fifty-three point seven percent had insufficient patch wear. The patch simply wasn't on long enough for a dose to have been observable.

The consequence is arithmetic and large. Score every no-detection day as a missed dose and apparent nonadherence is thirty-two point three percent. Condition on adequate wear and it's eighteen point one percent. Because adherence during patch-off periods is unobservable, the true rate is bounded between about fifteen and thirty-two percent — which is to say the study cannot distinguish a reasonably adherent population from a poorly adherent one. Their proposal is a three-state outcome: detected ingestion, adequately observed non-ingestion, and indeterminate. They also ran exploratory behavioral analyses across the seven hundred forty-four adequately worn days, finding ingestion more likely on days resembling a participant's usual activity pattern, odds ratio one point five two, and when they spent more time at home around the habitual dosing hour, one point three one.

Those behavioral associations are the part everyone will quote and the part that matters least. The important claim is that digital adherence measurement conflates not taking the drug with not being watched, and that every downstream analysis — intervention effects, predictive models, regulatory claims about objectively measured adherence — inherits that conflation. Twenty patients is small. The logic does not depend on the sample size.

From measurement discipline to tooling. In CPT: Pharmacometrics and Systems Pharmacology, a platform called PMxAgent, an open-source agentic layer for pharmacometric analysis. I should say up front that the publisher's site is blocking me today, so unlike the rest of today's items I'm working from the abstract rather than the full paper, and I'll keep it correspondingly short. The architecture is the interesting part: Docker orchestrating an R-based API server alongside a Python model-context-protocol server that auto-generates agent-callable tools from OpenAPI specifications. Five tools ship with it, covering non-compartmental analysis, exposure-response modelling, pharmacokinetic simulation, data standardisation, and population pharmacokinetic dataset generation from an existing model library.

The evaluation is the reason to mention it. They benchmarked the non-compartmental analysis workflow against four frontier AI agents across a hundred eighty-two drugs and eighteen hundred twenty simulated subjects, with a commercial package as reference. PMxAgent reached ninety-eight point three percent accuracy, matching or beating every frontier agent tested. But the finding with legs isn't the accuracy number — it's that PMxAgent was deterministic, while the general-purpose agents solved the problem by writing fresh code on every run and therefore produced different answers each time. For a regulatory submission, that distinction is the whole ballgame. And the lesson generalises past pharmacometrics: if you want an agent's output to be auditable, don't let it author the analysis, let it call an analysis somebody already validated.

Which is a good bridge to the seventh item, a benchmark from Beth Israel's neurology department called LitBench, testing whether AI systems can actually do literature retrieval in a clinical domain — epilepsy, specifically. The construction is careful. Nineteen hundred eighty open-access papers, a thousand holding answers and nine hundred eighty decoys from unrelated fields, and nine thousand four hundred seventy-two human-reviewed facts, yielding two thousand one hundred eighty-eight single-paper questions and a hundred seventy requiring one fact from each of two papers. Difficulty ramps by burying the answer among up to two thousand distractors, and then by running over live PubMed Central. And then the condition almost nobody else tests: they re-ask every question with the answering paper removed, so that refusing is the correct response.

The results are a useful corrective to anyone who thinks retrieval is solved. Single-paper accuracy ranged from roughly fifty-six to seventy-two percent for a four-billion-parameter Gemma, sixty-one to seventy-six percent for the twelve-billion version, and ninety to ninety-one percent for Sonnet five. Two-paper synthesis: five point seven percent, seventeen point one percent, and thirty-seven point eight percent respectively. The frontier model fails the two-source task nearly two times in three, and the logs point at two separate bottlenecks — failing to retrieve the second paper at all, and then failing to extract and combine once both are in hand. On the evidence-absent conditions, the twelve-billion Gemma's refusal rate collapsed from eighty-eight percent to forty percent as the candidate pool grew, the smaller model essentially never refused, and DeepSeek V4 Flash refused constantly but also refused on eighty percent of matched controls where the answer was present — which isn't calibration, it's abstention. Facts requiring interpretation were consistently harder than facts stated verbatim. If you are evaluating a literature-search tool for clinical use, this is the shape the evaluation should have.

Eighth, and staying with clinical AI safety, a design report in npj Digital Medicine that I think is more useful than most empirical papers on the topic. A team working in primary-care-based opioid use disorder treatment in Massachusetts built a recovery-support chatbot and had to solve the problem nobody publishes: what the system does when a patient says something alarming. Their architecture routes every incoming message through a safety classifier before anything else sees it, sorting into three tiers — explicit suicide or self-harm risk, ambiguous distress without overt suicidality, and neither. Tier one triggers a crisis response scripted by clinicians, with an explicit instruction to avoid phrases like "you are not alone" so the system doesn't imply human presence. Tier two routes to a node whose only job is to gather more context. Only tier three reaches the conversational agent at all. Behind all of it sits a human monitoring and triage workflow using standard suicide-risk instrumentation.

It's a comment piece rather than a trial, and the authors say plainly that the design needs validation. What makes it worth your time is the specificity — the tier definitions, the routing logic, the tone instructions, the business associate agreement and zero-data-retention arrangement that made it HIPAA-compatible. The population matters too: opioid use disorder carries very high rates of suicidal ideation and co-occurring depression, and addiction medicine is exactly the capacity-constrained setting where a chatbot is most tempting and most dangerous. This is the first implementation description I've seen concrete enough that another group could copy it and then argue with it.

We'll close on something slower-moving. Molecular Psychiatry has published interdisciplinary recommendations from GENERATE, the German research network on autoimmune encephalitis, on how to evaluate psychiatric presentations for an underlying autoimmune cause. The clinical problem is real: some patients with autoimmune encephalitis present with isolated or predominant psychiatric symptoms and get a primary psychiatric diagnosis instead. The proposed workup is stepwise — clinical assessment, MRI, EEG, blood work, cerebrospinal fluid analysis, and antibody testing in serum and cerebrospinal fluid together, with fluorodeoxyglucose PET, live-cell-based assays and volumetric imaging reserved for ambiguous cases.

The reason I'm ending here rather than filing this under clinical housekeeping is the argument underneath, which is a straight Bayesian one and applies far past this diagnosis. Autoimmune causes of psychiatric syndromes are rare to ultra-rare, while primary psychiatric disorders are common. At that pre-test probability, even a test with respectable specificity has poor positive predictive value — so the widespread practice of ordering serum-only antibody panels, and of treating an isolated blood-cerebrospinal-fluid barrier abnormality or diffuse EEG slowing as supportive evidence, will generate far more false attributions than true diagnoses. The authors are explicit that they are not defining a new disease entity, and that terms like autoimmune psychosis should be read as heuristics for triggering further evaluation rather than as diagnoses. In a subfield where the incentive runs hard toward finding the treatable cause, a consensus document whose main contribution is restraint is genuinely unusual, and worth reading for the reasoning even if you never order the panel.
