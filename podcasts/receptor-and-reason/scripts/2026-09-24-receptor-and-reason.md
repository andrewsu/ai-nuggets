## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. I'm your host, and this is your daily pass through CNS pharmacology, psychiatric and neurology drug discovery, and the computational work closing in on both. Ten items today, and a housekeeping note first: bioRxiv's servers were unreachable from here all morning — both the metadata API and the subject feeds — so today's preprint tier is arXiv only, and the neuroscience preprint sweep is a genuine gap rather than an editorial choice. The journals more than carried the day, and there's a thread running through the first half that's worth flagging up front: three separate papers where the preclinical prediction was not just wrong but inverted, and in each case the inversion is the useful part.

We open with a paper from Marburg's psychiatry department that I think is the most interesting framing of the week, even though its results are modest and the authors are unusually honest about that. The question is the one that separates neuroimaging from therapeutics: not how do I decode a brain state, but how do I specify the change I want. They build what they call an AI-driven neural surrogate — a subject-specific model linking stimulus-evoked functional MRI activity to the latent space of a generative image model — and then use it to propose candidate representational changes and predict what those changes would look like perceptually, all without stimulating anyone.

The framing is control theory, and the authors are scrupulous about which part of the problem they're solving. A full closed-loop neuromodulation system needs a forward model from stimulation parameters to induced fields to neural response, plus neural dynamics. They have none of that. What they have is the upstream target-identification step, posed as a static, local inverse-design problem: before you pick a stimulation protocol, you have to be able to say what representational change you're aiming at and whether hitting it would actually matter behaviorally.

The implementation uses more than thirty-six thousand image-and-scan observations from four deeply sampled participants in the Natural Scenes Dataset — seven-tesla data, which is why four subjects is enough. Subject-specific ridge models map visually responsive cortex onto the latent vector of a frozen variational autoencoder, recovering coarse generative structure at two-way identification of point seven nine to point eight eight against a chance level of point five. Then they push the decoded latents along a steering direction for emotional valence or for memorability, reconstruct the perturbed latents back into images, and have people rate them. Valence and memorability are explicitly worked examples, chosen because persistent negative valence bias is central to major depressive disorder and overly salient memory is central to PTSD.

The evaluation is where this earns its keep, because they didn't stop at automated scorers. Seven thousand two hundred human trials across eighteen participants. Under the automated assessors, valence moved from about minus point six to plus one standard deviation and memorability moved even further. In humans, valence ratings moved in the predicted direction — a mean slope of about point zero four standard deviations per unit of perturbation strength, confidence interval scraping zero at the bottom, positive in sixteen of eighteen participants. Perceived memorability did not move reliably at all. Baseline agreement between the automated valence scorer and human ratings was point three, and for memorability point one.

So: a small, real, directionally correct effect on one attribute and nothing on the other. Three caveats matter more than the effect sizes. The steering directions are population-level empirical vectors — quartile centroids — not per-image optimal gradients, so the local linear approximation the math is built on isn't actually what gets applied. The diffusion refinement stage that turns coarse reconstructions into viewable images means the rated stimuli aren't end-to-end brain-derived. And pushed hard enough, the perturbed images stop resembling the original stimulus at all, which means intended change trades directly against fidelity and there's a bounded window where this is meaningful. I'd still argue the contribution is real: this is a falsifiable way to generate and behaviorally test a neuromodulation target before anyone builds a coil, and the field has been badly short of those.

From designing targets to a target that turned out to do the opposite of what everyone assumed. In Biological Psychiatry, a study of GluN2D-containing NMDA receptors in the Fmr1 knockout mouse, the standard model of Fragile X syndrome. The setup is textbook: Fragile X involves cortical circuit hyperexcitability, sensory-induced seizures, and deficits in sensory-driven cortical synchrony. GluN2D-containing NMDA receptors are enriched in cortical inhibitory neurons. So the obvious hypothesis — and the one the authors state they went in with — is that selectively activating GluN2D would boost inhibition and fix the circuit.

It's the reverse. Pharmacological inhibition of GluN2D-containing receptors corrects the hyperexcitability, reduces sensory-driven seizures, and improves EEG synchrony. The explanation is a cell-type switch: in the Fmr1 knockout, the functional expression of GluN2D-containing receptors moves out of inhibitory neurons and into excitatory ones, and it is that ectopic excitatory-neuron expression driving the hyperexcitability. They back it with the transcript — Grin2d messenger RNA is up in Fmr1 knockout excitatory neurons — and then with the clean genetic test: reducing Grin2d specifically in excitatory neurons corrects the circuit measures.

This is the item I'd put in front of anyone doing subunit-selective NMDA receptor pharmacology. The entire therapeutic logic of subunit selectivity rests on the subunit marking a cell type. Here the disease moves the subunit to a different cell type, which means the direction of the correct intervention flips sign. A compound screened in wild-type tissue for the "right" effect would have been developed backwards. Worth noting the ceiling: this is circuit physiology and EEG in a mouse model, not behavior or cognition, and Fragile X has a long history of mouse rescues that didn't translate. But the mechanism is specified well enough to be checked in human tissue, which is the bar that matters.

Second inversion, same journal, human this time. A positron emission tomography study of fatty acid amide hydrolase — FAAH, the enzyme that terminates anandamide signaling — in post-traumatic stress disorder, using the irreversible carbon-11 tracer CURB with arterial input quantification. Eighty-six participants: thirty-five with PTSD, fifty-one controls. The preclinical literature is consistent that elevated FAAH activity means less anandamide and heightened stress responsivity, so the prediction was more FAAH in PTSD.

They found less. Amygdala FAAH was about ten percent lower in PTSD than in controls, p equals point zero two, and the effect was anatomically specific — no differences in the other corticolimbic regions. FAAH levels did not correlate with symptom severity. An exploratory analysis pointed somewhere else entirely: childhood trauma was associated with about twelve percent higher corticolimbic FAAH regardless of diagnosis.

The authors draw the line I'd draw, which is that this may inform why FAAH inhibitor trials have underdelivered. If amygdala FAAH is already reduced in the disorder, inhibiting it further is not obviously corrective, and a trial enrolling on diagnosis is averaging over people whose enzyme is up and people whose enzyme is down. The childhood-trauma association is the more actionable signal, because it suggests the stratifying variable is developmental history rather than the diagnostic label — but with twenty-eight versus twenty-four participants carrying trauma-questionnaire data, that is a hypothesis-generating result and the authors treat it as one. A ten percent regional difference in an in vivo enzyme measure is also right at the edge of what this methodology resolves.

Staying with stress and relapse, and to Neuropsychopharmacology, where a group extends earlier work on the dynorphin and kappa opioid receptor system in opioid withdrawal. The clinical premise is uncontroversial — negative affect during withdrawal drives relapse — but the preclinical evidence tying those two things together causally has been thin. They establish the correlation first: mice showing stronger morphine conditioned place preference also show more severe depressive-like behavior in withdrawal, and then more pronounced reinstatement after forced swim stress.

Then they do the causal work in both directions, which is what makes this worth your time. Inhibiting kappa receptor activation in the basolateral amygdala, or activating the amygdala-to-accumbens projection, attenuates the withdrawal-induced depressive behavior and blocks the subsequent stress-induced reinstatement. Overexpressing kappa receptors in that circuit makes both worse; genetically ablating them is protective. And they identify the likely source of the ligand — a population of prodynorphin-expressing prelimbic cortical neurons projecting to the amygdala, active during both withdrawal and the stress challenge, whose inhibition alleviates the depressive behavior and blocks reinstatement.

Bidirectional manipulation at the receptor plus identification of the projection supplying the peptide is about as complete as this kind of circuit story gets. The honest caveat is that kappa antagonism has been a therapeutic near-miss for over a decade — aticaprant and navacaprant both disappointed in depression — and the lesson from those programs may be that the kappa system matters most in a specific state rather than as a tonic target. This paper is an argument for exactly that reading: the effect is on withdrawal-associated negative affect and its downstream relapse risk, not on baseline mood. Male mice only, which for a stress-and-dynorphin study is a real limitation given the known sex differences in kappa signaling.

Now something structurally different, from Molecular Psychiatry — a bipolar disorder paper that takes subtype seriously in a way the molecular literature usually doesn't. Serinc2 is a bipolar susceptibility gene encoding a serine transporter that feeds membrane lipid synthesis. Working across patient plasma, iPSC-derived forebrain organoids from patients, and knockout and overexpression mice, they find Serinc2 expression is increased in bipolar one and decreased in bipolar two.

That opposite-direction finding is the whole paper, and they treat it as a hypothesis to test rather than a curiosity. Bidirectional manipulation in mice produces opposite changes in synaptic transmission and depression-related behavior, and the mechanism running underneath is lipid-dependent: phosphatidylserine and sphingomyelin composition changes, which redistributes NMDA receptor subunits within the postsynaptic membrane. Upstream, thioredoxin binds the Serinc2 regulatory region and represses its transcription, and differential chromatin accessibility at the thioredoxin locus tracks the subtype-associated expression pattern.

Membrane lipid composition as the variable that positions receptors, rather than receptor expression as the variable, is an underexploited axis in CNS pharmacology, and it connects to the GluN2D story earlier — both are cases where the relevant fact about an NMDA receptor is where it is, not how much of it there is. The thing to hold lightly is the subtype claim itself: bipolar one versus bipolar two is a clinical distinction with imperfect reliability, and a gene whose expression goes opposite directions across that boundary is a strong claim resting on how the cohorts were split.

One more from Neuropsychopharmacology, and it's a null result that I think is more useful than most positives. A systematic review and meta-analysis of proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy studies of ketamine in depression — does the rapid antidepressant shift GABA, glutamate, or the combined glutamate-plus-glutamine signal, and do those shifts track clinical response? Eleven studies survived screening out of over a thousand identified. The meta-analyses found no statistically significant treatment-related change in any metabolite.

Two things make this worth the minute. First, the authors explicitly flag an assumption the field has been running on without examination: the convention of treating the glutamate signal as excitation and the GABA signal as inhibition is, in their words, an assumed convention rather than a validated physiological equivalence. Static metabolite concentrations measured by spectroscopy are pools, not fluxes, and they do not directly index neuronal excitation or inhibition. Second, the heterogeneity is enormous — infusion protocols, which metabolites were measured, acquisition timing relative to dose, and region of interest all vary across the eleven studies, and only a handful related metabolite change to ketamine blood levels. So the correct reading is not "ketamine doesn't change cortical glutamate." It's that this literature, as built, cannot answer the question. The one encouraging signal is from a single randomized trial where the percent reduction in the glutamate-to-GABA ratio correlated with symptom improvement — the ratio as a response predictor rather than the absolute levels as a mechanism.

Three computational items, and then a practical one to close. The first is a short paper on what the authors call false-science induction in autonomous discovery, and it identifies a failure mode I hadn't seen named. In a closed-loop system — a self-driving lab, a robotic chemistry platform — record binding stops being bookkeeping and becomes part of the apparatus. If a real measurement gets attached to the wrong object, you have manufactured a false scientific statement, and a surrogate model trained on that archive will learn it faithfully. Nothing is malfunctioning; the model is correctly fitting a corrupted record.

Their contribution is showing that the variable that governs the damage is not how many errors there are but how coherent they are. They run two loops — green fluorescent protein fitness, and materials band-gap prediction — and compare clean data, same-budget random label swaps, and targeted coherent misbinding. In the protein loop, five rounds of a hundred acquisitions from forty-five thousand candidates, where the target slice is about half a percent of the pool, base rates predict roughly two point seven selections out of five hundred. Coherent misbinding redirects thirty-six to forty-seven. Same-volume random swaps produce essentially zero — point zero to point one. Same pattern in materials. The label distribution is provably unchanged in both conditions, so every marginal sanity check you'd run passes.

The practical output is that monitoring the acquisition trace for over-concentration gives you a pre-execution stop signal, and feedback-conflict triage recovers the corrupted axis nine or ten times out of ten. If you run any active-learning loop over an assay database — and increasingly that describes compound screening cascades — the takeaway is that your provenance audits are probably tuned to error frequency when they should be tuned to error structure.

Which pairs directly with the second item, a benchmark paper called CellAudit that goes after the same gap in agent-discovered models. The setting is AI virtual cells: models that predict cellular response to a perturbation. The uncomfortable observation is that held-out predictive performance does not establish that the model used the perturbation information at all — it can score well off cellular context and control profiles alone. In agentic discovery, where a language model generates and revises predictors against a score, that gap becomes a machine for producing models that look right for the wrong reason.

Their demonstration is brutal and specific. On a paired morphology-and-transcriptomics benchmark, an agent-selected predictor reaches a mean held-out correlation of point three one five — and is exactly invariant to replacing the compound. A predictor using only control profiles reaches point three one four. Source inspection found the compound query pathway blocked by a degenerate single-key attention step. Across forty-eight generated candidates, forty-seven changed their predictions when the compound was swapped, but only twenty showed an actual gain in target loss with intervals above zero on both folds. So the naive invariance check passes for nearly everything while the contribution check fails for more than half. Their audit asks three separate questions — can the input reach the computation, do predictions depend on it, does that dependence improve prediction — and adding that falsification layer to the agent's feedback recovers genuine compound contribution. The framing they propose, moving from generate-score-revise to discover-falsify-revise, is the right one.

Third, a benchmark for tool-using chemistry agents called MolDesignBench, from Korea University with LG AI Research. Two thousand molecular generation and optimization instances built as scenario narratives rather than parameter lists, so the agent has to infer implicit constraints from a design brief the way a medicinal chemist reads a project document, satisfy explicit property and functional-group constraints simultaneously, coordinate seventeen chemistry tools over multiple turns, and — the part I like most — recognize when a specification is chemically infeasible and refuse. About ten percent of instances are deliberately unsatisfiable.

Best frontier performance is around forty-three percent success. The failure analysis is more informative than the number: the two dominant bottlenecks are implicit-constraint interpretation and infeasibility detection, and the models fail them in different styles. One frontier model almost never returns a refusal — it will design something for an impossible brief rather than say the brief is impossible. An open-weight model optimizes the explicit numeric targets almost exclusively and ignores the constraints embedded in the narrative, which is why its constraint-satisfaction score tracks the two or three explicit terms and nothing else. Both behaviors are exactly what you don't want in a design agent, and neither shows up on benchmarks that hand the agent a clean property range.

And to close, something you can use this week rather than argue about. CPT: Pharmacometrics and Systems Pharmacology has published a genuine end-to-end tutorial on physics-informed neural networks for inverse problems in pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, and quantitative systems pharmacology. The motivation is the regime pharmacometrics actually lives in — sparse and noisy data, only some states measurable, stiff or discontinuous dynamics, and gray-box systems where you know part of the model and want to learn the rest. Three worked case studies: recovering constant parameters in a three-compartment system, discovering an unknown right-hand-side function in a gray-box setting, and inferring a time-varying chemotherapy efficacy function under partial observation. It covers the things that actually decide whether these models converge — collocation point design, loss weighting, constraint enforcement, numerical precision, residual-based attention — and it discusses when Kolmogorov-Arnold networks are worth swapping in for a standard multilayer perceptron. Code and notebooks are released as a library. Tutorials rarely make this show, but the gap between "physics-informed networks are promising for pharmacometrics" and a reproducible workflow has been wide for about four years, and this closes a useful part of it.
