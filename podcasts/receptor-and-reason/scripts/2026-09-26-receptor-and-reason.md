## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. I'm your host, and this is your daily pass through CNS pharmacology, psychiatric and neurology drug discovery, and the computational work closing in on both. Eleven items today, and one housekeeping note: bioRxiv's metadata API is still returning empty responses, a second consecutive morning, and medRxiv's feeds are down with it, so there is no medRxiv tier at all today. The per-subject bioRxiv RSS feeds and the journal side both worked, and the day's best item came out of the preprint server anyway.

We open with a small single-author preprint from University College Cork that asks a question the agentic-biology literature has been carefully avoiding. When you build an LLM agent to run a scientific analysis, the model is doing two completely different jobs. It decides what to measure next — call that orchestration. And it decides what the accumulated measurements mean — call that adjudication. Almost every agent paper you have read evaluates these together, end to end, and reports one number — and that number cannot tell you which capability you bought. A bad orchestration decision wastes compute and gets corrected by the next analysis. A bad adjudication decision propagates straight into the reported biological conclusion.

The setup is deliberately unglamorous: deciding whether a bacterial genome carries the tetracycline efflux determinants tet A or tet B. Both are major facilitator superfamily members, so detecting a transporter is easy and discriminating the right family from its neighbours is the actual problem. Every sequence measurement comes from frozen deterministic tools. The model never touches them; it can only request follow-up analyses from a predefined registry, and the endpoint call is made by a separate layer that can be swapped out.

As a workflow controller, GPT five point six Sol did well. Across a prospective, blinded, balanced cohort of twenty genomes it got eighteen of twenty — exactly what a fixed policy got, and exactly what exhausting every available analysis got — while running thirty-nine follow-up analyses against fifty-seven for the fixed policy and seventy-four for exhaustive. The precursor sixty-case run makes the same point harder: changing the controller altered the final evidence record in thirty to forty-five of sixty cases and changed the final call in zero of sixty.

Then the role swap, which is what makes this paper worth your time. The exact evidence record the model had assembled — checksum-identical, nothing recomputed — was handed back to the model instead of to the rule-based judge. Accuracy fell from eighteen of twenty to eleven of twenty. Sensitivity held at eight of ten. Specificity collapsed from ten of ten to three of ten. Exact McNemar, P equals point zero one six. And the shape of the failure matters: all nine disagreements were cases the rules called absent and the model called unresolved. Seven of those nine the rules had right, and the model corrected neither of the two errors the rules made. It did not hallucinate a gene that wasn't there. It refused to commit that a gene wasn't there.

One design choice does inflate the effect: unresolved was prespecified as incorrect, and in a clinical setting a calibrated abstention has real value. But it's the right choice here, because the deterministic layer answered the same question from the same bytes and answered it correctly — hedging that adds no information over a rule that already works is a cost, not caution. Note the comparator too: they ran Jev, the non-LLM typed decision model, as a controller. Same thirty-nine analyses, same eighteen of twenty, at four point six times lower latency and a hundred and thirteen times lower cost. Even the job the LLM did well, it did not do uniquely. Twenty genomes and one target family is thin. But the design — hold the evidence fixed, move the decision authority — is what the field should be copying.

Second paper, same failure mode, clinical setting, higher stakes. A New South Wales group with clinicians from St Vincent's and Royal Prince Alfred asked what happens when you stop evaluating triage models on completed records and start evaluating them the way triage actually happens, as a conversation unfolding turn by turn. They score six models at five checkpoints on four hundred twenty-five model-generated and fifty clinician-written nurse-patient conversations, predicting an Emergency Severity Index level from a growing prefix.

Offline, on the complete structured record, the numbers match the existing literature: quadratic weighted kappa of point seven zero for the best closed model, point six four for the best open-weight one. That's the ceiling the sequential trajectory would approach if the model integrated evidence as it arrived. It doesn't. Every model drops to fair-to-moderate agreement, performance plateaus at the very first checkpoint, and several models get worse as the conversation continues.

The perturbations localise it. Scramble or delete the chief-complaint exchanges and the label moves; do the same to any later turn and it doesn't. The decision is committed at the opening and everything after is decoration. Then they close the loop information-theoretically: the models demonstrably extract the relevant content from later turns — they can name the red flags — yet the surprisal of the true label rises monotonically across checkpoints, by three quarters of a nat to nearly two nats. Evidence arrives, the model reads it, and the posterior moves away from the right answer. On the fifty clinician-authored conversations, three expert clinicians reached kappas between point eight nine and point nine three by waiting for later turns. The best model reached point two nine five.

Put those two side by side and you have today's thesis. Language models are good at deciding what to look at next and bad at deciding what they have found. In the genome case the failure was over-hedging, in triage it was premature commitment — which look opposite but are the same defect: neither system updates a belief in proportion to the evidence in front of it. The design implication is identical. Keep the model in the loop for action selection, and put something explicit and auditable at the point where evidence becomes a call.

Which makes the third item well-timed counterprogramming. Clinical Pharmacology and Therapeutics has a tutorial from Sanofi's translational modelling group with Certara and Leiden on combining quantitative systems pharmacology with formal causal inference. The framing is worth stealing even if you never build either. QSP asks: given these mechanistic assumptions, how does the system behave under intervention? Causal inference asks: given these data, what are the effects of intervening in the real world? And the authors are explicit that mechanistic structure does not by itself confer causal validity — a QSP model is causally interpretable only conditional on structural adequacy, parameter identifiability, and how informative the calibration data are. That's more honest than that literature usually manages.

What lifts it above a review is two case studies with outcomes attached. Lunsekimig, a bispecific nanobody against TSLP and interleukin thirteen for asthma: the model was given only binding affinity and predicted pharmacokinetics, made a genuinely blind prediction of the Phase 1b biomarker package before readout, got it right, and the program went from Phase 1b straight to 2b, saving an estimated one to two years. Olipudase alfa in acid sphingomyelinase deficiency: digital-twin simulations were submitted as primary evidence for the type A population, children too rare and too sick for a dedicated trial, and the FDA accepted them. That's the highest-water mark I know of for in silico evidence in a regulatory filing. For a CNS audience the obvious question is when this becomes tractable for brain targets, and the answer here is: not yet, for exactly the structural-adequacy reason they name.

From simulating trials to simulating receptors. A two-person group at the University of Warsaw has a preprint on detecting biased agonism computationally — worth solving, because experimental characterisation of bias is slow, expensive, and tells you almost nothing about why a ligand is biased. Their method runs two microseconds of all-atom membrane molecular dynamics on a receptor-transducer complex with a known biased ligand, identifies conserved molecular switches whose conformational changes track activation of that pathway, then uses the frames at those switch-change moments as launch points for shorter five-hundred-nanosecond runs with the ligand swapped for an oppositely biased one. The intuition is good: instead of waiting for a rare transition in a long trajectory, find the moments where the receptor is already committing and restart from there. They test beta one adrenergic with xamoterol and carvedilol, C X C R three with two chemokine tool ligands, and mu-opioid receptor, and in at least two of three replicas per system the swapped simulations separate in the expected direction.

I like the idea more than the current implementation, and the authors are candid about why. The microswitches they used to select frames turned out not to be good markers of transducer activation in the shorter runs — they fell back on RMSD of the G protein helical domain in one system, arrestin finger-loop conformation in another, hydrogen-bond analysis in a third. The readout is chosen per system, post hoc, on retrospective cases with known answers. That is not yet an assay. And for C X C R three they had to use partially rather than fully biased tool ligands, because fully biased ligands barely exist — which is the field's real bottleneck, not compute. The prospective test, predicting the bias of a ligand nobody has assayed, is the paper I want next.

One step down the pipeline, a company called AlpheraBio published a chemical-space mapping study around their peripherally restricted alpha two A adrenergic receptor agonist. The pharmacological premise is sound: alpha two A agonists are real analgesics whose ceiling is set by the sedation, hypotension and hypothermia that come from central exposure. They took four seed structures — the lead plus three variants differing in linker length, polar-group orientation and linker hopping — and applied about thirty thousand precedented medicinal-chemistry transformation rules to defined modifiable regions while freezing the receptor-interacting core. Out comes a virtual library of sixteen point six million compounds.

The retrospective claim is that forty-three compounds from their earlier hand-designed program, about two ten-thousandths of one percent of the library, were all recovered as exact matches. Read that carefully: the seeds were drawn from those same series, so complete recovery is close to guaranteed by construction. The experimental work is more interesting — three compounds pulled straight from the library came back at ten, twenty-four and two nanomolar affinity with single-digit to mid-teens nanomolar potency, and five produced thirty-nine to fifty-six percent anti-allodynic effect in spared nerve injury at one to two milligrams per kilogram orally.

The honest gap is prioritisation. Nothing here ranks the sixteen point six million; three compounds were chosen by hand and the paper offers no prospective method for making that choice. And the most striking result argues enumeration alone can't get you there: resolving one compound into enantiomers gave one thousand thirty-seven nanomolar and two point eight five nanomolar. A three-hundred-sixty-fold stereochemical cliff the enumeration has no way to see. Enumeration gives coverage, and coverage without a scoring function is a bigger haystack.

Two on drug disposition. First, MetExPred, from a Chinese University of Hong Kong Shenzhen group — a unified model over seventeen metabolism classification endpoints and two excretion regression endpoints, covering the major cytochromes, glucuronidation, transporters, microsomal stability, clearance and half-life, on about two hundred thousand records. The architectural idea worth noting is a third representational view alongside the usual sequence and graph embeddings: protein language model embeddings of the compound's annotated pharmacological targets, with masking so the same architecture runs with or without them. The premise is that by lead optimisation you already know what your compound binds, and that pharmacology might carry information about pharmacokinetics. Mean area under the ROC curve of point eight four across classification is respectable. Two numbers temper it. Half-life has an R-squared of point two five, which is not a usable half-life predictor. And the target-aware view, the paper's actual novelty, delivers what the authors call a modest improvement, with no consistent benefit for half-life at all. Right idea, small effect — worth knowing before anyone spends a year on it.

The second disposition paper goes after the layer above the enzyme. AetherXeno, from China Medical University in Taichung, is an allele-resolved atlas of predicted receptor occupancy for three ligand-activated transcription factors that govern drug-handling genes: the pregnane X receptor, the farnesoid X receptor, and the aryl hydrocarbon receptor. The problem is genuinely underserved. Pharmacogenomics is overwhelmingly a coding-variant discipline, the cytochrome star-allele business, but a great deal of interindividual variation in disposition sits in the noncoding machinery that sets how much enzyme and transporter you express — and for a noncoding variant, allele frequency and generic regulatory annotation tell you neither which receptor's program is perturbed nor in which direction.

They take a frozen AlphaGenome sequence trunk, fit a separate ChIP-seq prediction head per receptor on primary human hepatocyte data, then run exhaustive in silico saturation mutagenesis across eighty-seven hundred supported elements, linking seventeen million variants to allele-matched external evidence. On held-out chromosomes the receptor-specific heads beat native AlphaGenome and Enformer outputs, with Spearman gains from point zero four to point three three. What I'd underline, though, is how disciplined the framing is. The normalised score is explicitly not a probability, not a percentile, not a pathogenicity call. Evidence attached to one allele is never propagated to other substitutions at the same position. A pharmacist reviews each proposed receptor-to-gene mechanism against the cited literature before release, scoped to mechanistic pharmacology rather than dosing advice, and clinical response prediction is declared out of scope. That is the correct amount of epistemic modesty for a hypothesis-generation resource and it is rarer than it should be. The caveat: the aryl hydrocarbon receptor dataset rests on a single biological replicate, so treat that third of the atlas with more suspicion than the other two.

Now to wet pharmacology, and a preprint from Milwaukee, Cyprus and London that does something the addiction field keeps meaning to do — four receptor systems, one withdrawal time course, one set of animals. Mice on a ten-day escalating Lieber-DeCarli ethanol diet, then one, four or seven days of withdrawal, with quantitative autoradiography for oxytocin receptor, mu-opioid receptor, metabotropic glutamate five and the translocator protein as a glial marker, alongside motor and recognition-memory testing.

The temporal dissociation is the finding. Motor incoordination appears during exposure and persists into early withdrawal. The recognition-memory deficit does not appear until day seven, after the physical withdrawal syndrome has resolved. The receptor changes are equally staggered: oxytocin receptor up in septum with a transient basomedial amygdala dip at intermediate withdrawal that normalises, mu-opioid receptor transiently up in striatum at day four specifically, glial activation early and transient, and metabotropic glutamate five up in accumbens core, basolateral amygdala and hippocampus, with caudate-putamen and amygdala binding correlating with the memory deficit in a stage-dependent way.

The detail with teeth is methodological. They went to human postmortem hippocampal transcriptomic data from people with alcohol use disorder and found no consistent change in the metabotropic glutamate five transcript. If you had run this as a transcriptomics study — which is how most of us would run it now — you would have concluded there was nothing there. The regulation is post-transcriptional and only a binding assay sees it; every time someone proposes replacing receptor pharmacology with expression profiling, this is the counterexample. Male mice only by design, and regional binding correlating with behaviour is not causation — but negative allosteric modulators at that receptor exist, and this hands you a specific window in which to test one.

Item eight, in the British Journal of Pharmacology, is a nice example of a channel changing address. T R P M three is known as a peripheral heat and pain sensor sitting in dorsal root ganglia, and for most of us that is the whole story. But gain-of-function mutations cause a human neurodevelopmental syndrome with epilepsy, which implies a central role nobody has characterised. This group went looking in the lateral amygdala.

It's there, in excitatory neurons, and functionally active. Agonist depolarises, antagonist hyperpolarises — and the fact that the antagonist does anything at rest means a fraction of the channel is constitutively open and setting resting membrane potential. Knockouts sit six millivolts more hyperpolarised with roughly double the rheobase. Long-term potentiation at cortex-to-lateral-amygdala synapses is largely abolished in knockouts and equally abolished by acute antagonist in wild-type slices. The behavioural dissociation is clean: anxiety unchanged across three assays, foot-shock threshold unchanged — which matters, because otherwise you cannot rule out that a pain-sensor knockout simply felt the shock less — but contextual and cued fear memory both substantially reduced. In the kainate model, knockouts had roughly forty percent fewer spikes and about a third less seizure time during status epilepticus, and none of ten went on to develop spontaneous seizures against three of ten wild-types. Crucially, agonist alone into the lateral amygdala produced no seizures; it only worsened things alongside a subthreshold kainate dose.

That last result makes this a modulator story rather than a driver story, and it sets the therapeutic frame. An antagonist here would not be an anticonvulsant. It would be a threshold-raising adjunct, which is a different trial with a different comparator. The postmortem human work — the channel is present in glutamatergic neurons of the human lateral amygdala — is what makes it worth someone's money.

Two short ones to close. Also in the British Journal of Pharmacology, a group has engineered E. coli Nissle to produce L-DOPA under an inducible, titratable promoter as a gut-to-brain catecholamine delivery system. The premise is that locus coeruleus degeneration is an early Alzheimer's event and a plausible driver of the prodromal picture, so they lesioned the noradrenergic projection with a dopamine-beta-hydroxylase immunotoxin into prefrontal cortex of an Alzheimer's rat model and gave six weeks of daily gavage with the engineered strain plus benserazide. Stable colonisation, sustained plasma and brain L-DOPA, raised cortical and hippocampal noradrenaline, improved spatial and working memory — and, less expectedly, reduced forebrain amyloid plaque load and class two microglial burden with increased PSD95. I could not get the full text, so I'm working from the abstract and flagging that; treat the amyloid claim as the one most in need of scrutiny. But a titratable living L-DOPA source that sidesteps the peripheral decarboxylation problem is an interesting delivery idea even if disease modification doesn't survive.

And last, a short paper in ACS Chemical Neuroscience that is really about a measurement artefact worth internalising. Synaptic vesicle glycoprotein 2 is the levetiracetam target family, and the S V two C paralog is of interest in Parkinson's. A UCB collaboration had previously found one candidate S V two C ligand lost about tenfold affinity going from four degrees to thirty-seven, so they asked the same question of the successor compound, which is a hundredfold selective for S V two C over S V two A. Binding assays plus molecular dynamics: affinity for S V two A falls substantially at physiological temperature while S V two C holds. The simulations reproduce it — the ligand rattles loose in the S V two A pocket at thirty-seven degrees and stays anchored in S V two C — and attribute the difference to a tyrosine that is less solvent-exposed in S V two C and holds a persistent hydrogen bond with a neighbouring asparagine. The lesson to keep: selectivity measured on ice is not selectivity. Here the temperature dependence happened to work in the compound's favour. It could as easily have gone the other way, and a great many published selectivity ratios were measured at four degrees.

That's eleven. If there's a through-line today it's that the interesting work is all about where a system commits — which evidence a model acts on, which conformation a receptor settles into, which enzyme a variant turns up or down, and whether the number you measured survives being measured at body temperature.
