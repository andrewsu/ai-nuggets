## Script

Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Friday, November the twentieth. The thread running through today's papers is the gate. In item after item the interesting variable isn't the signal itself but the thing that decides whether the signal is allowed to have an effect — a denervation threshold, a hormone, a layer of extracellular matrix, a timescale you weren't recording on. Measure the signal and you see nothing; find the gate and the whole phenomenon snaps into place. Twelve items.

The lead is a Parkinson's paper that answers a question the field has been circling for thirty years: why do dopamine replacement therapies stop treating and start causing dyskinesia. The standard framing blames cumulative drug exposure, but the clinical evidence has never fit that cleanly — dyskinesia shows up first in the limb contralateral to the most denervated striatum, delaying levodopa at diagnosis doesn't delay its onset, and in the rare MPTP poisoning cases it arrives within days of the first dose. So the suspicion has been that denervation severity, not treatment history, sets the clock. The problem is that in rodents you lesion an animal and get one severity per animal, which is a terrible design for finding a threshold.

The trick here is spatial. They infused six-hydroxydopamine slowly into the dorsolateral striatum to make a gradient of dopamine axon loss across the tissue, then quantified denervation from the point of view of each individual neuron — fifty-micron regions of interest tiled across the whole dorsal striatum, scoring what fraction of the neuropil still carried dopamine-transporter-positive axons. Because a direct-pathway spiny projection neuron's dendritic field is small relative to that gradient, every animal becomes its own dose-response curve, with severity varying over space rather than over time.

And the answer is that the transition is not graded at all. Give a first-ever dyskinesiogenic dose of levodopa, and immediate-early gene induction in direct-pathway neurons goes up only in regions with no detectable dopamine axons at all, reaching about eighty percent of those neurons. Where there is any residual innervation — including regions at ninety to ninety-nine percent denervation — levodopa does what you want it to do and restores activity toward normal. The induction follows the contour of the denervation boundary and plateaus within a hundred and fifty microns of the nearest surviving axon. They reproduced it with a selective D1 agonist, which rules out a presynaptic story about levodopa conversion, and they re-analyzed a longitudinal calcium imaging dataset and got the same split functionally: fields of view with a few axons left returned to pre-lesion activity, fields with none overshot past anything seen in a normal animal.

Then the mechanism. The obvious candidate is the well-documented rise in intrinsic excitability after dopamine depletion — but that rise is already present at seventy to ninety-five percent denervation, on the safe side of the boundary, so it can't be what distinguishes the two zones. What does distinguish them is the loss of D1 receptor signaling itself. Deleting D1 receptors from direct-pathway neurons in otherwise intact, fully innervated animals and then driving those cells through a Gs-coupled DREADD reproduced the pathological response — eighty-three percent positive for c-Fos, ninety percent for Arc — in animals whose dopamine axons were perfectly healthy. The cell is counting its own Gs signaling, not the dopamine in the tissue. And the converse worked too: sustaining Gs-coupled signaling chemogenetically through the drinking water, before and during the lesion, prevented the maladaptive shift, and starting that treatment three weeks after the lesion, once the shift was established, reversed it.

That last result is the one to hold onto. It reframes dyskinesia risk as a cell-autonomous, reversible adaptation to prolonged absence of Gs tone rather than as accumulated damage, and it implies something counterintuitive for the clinic — that continuous low-level D1-like signaling during the pre-dyskinetic window might be protective, where the field's instinct has been to minimize exposure. Caveats: this is c-Fos and Arc, not movement; there is no dyskinesia scoring in these experiments; and a chemogenetic actuator is not a drug regimen. But the threshold result is clean and the design is the reason it's clean.

Staying in the striatum, and staying with the question of what signal a spiny projection neuron is actually integrating. A second preprint takes on tonic dopamine — the slow, minutes-long component that every computational model of motivation leans on and almost nobody has measured properly, because the sensors optimized for sub-second transients are the wrong instrument for a drift over ten minutes, and vice versa. They used fluorescence lifetime photometry at high temporal resolution, which sidesteps the usual intensity-based artifacts — photobleaching, differences in sensor expression — because lifetime is a ratiometric quantity, and that stability is what lets you trust a slow baseline.

Two findings. First, tonic dopamine is not a static background level; it fluctuates on the scale of tens of minutes, and those fluctuations predict how long an animal will keep working on a progressive ratio schedule before disengaging. Chemogenetic manipulation made that causal, and the care taken there is worth noting — they titrated the actuator dose while watching both timescales, because the higher doses everyone uses raise tonic dopamine while flattening phasic release, which makes the resulting behavioral change uninterpretable. Second, and this is the part that connects to the lead, the downstream consequence is cell-type-specific. Slow dopamine changes directly engage PKA activity in D2-receptor neurons, while in D1-receptor neurons they don't drive PKA themselves — they set how much effect a phasic transient has. Same molecule, two populations, two completely different readouts: a level in one, a gain control in the other. Which is roughly what the tonic-phasic literature has been asserting on theoretical grounds since the nineteen-nineties, now with the signaling measured.

Third, a computational pharmacology paper on ketamine that I like more for its framing than for any single result. The approach is called historeceptomics, from the Cardozo group at NYU: take every quantitative drug-target affinity you can assemble — ChEMBL, PubChem, the NIMH psychoactive drug screening program, the IUPHAR guide, BindingDB — convert them to a log potency, multiply by the z-scored expression of that target across human nervous-system tissues, and then use an outlier test to pull out the target-tissue pairs that stand out. The output is an anatomical fingerprint rather than a target list.

For ketamine you get NMDA receptor subunits in prefrontal cortex and amygdala, GABA-A subunits in occipital and parietal cortex and amygdala, and in prefrontal cortex a muscarinic receptor, 5-HT2A, and phosphodiesterase 4A. The comparison against thirty-four other psychoactive drugs is the useful part. Prefrontal 5-HT2A is shared with LSD, psilocin and psilocybin — and also with clozapine and olanzapine. The dopamine transporter in hypothalamus is shared with bupropion, fluoxetine and sertraline. Delta-opioid receptor in thalamus is shared with morphine. Several NMDA and GABA-A pairs, plus the prefrontal phosphodiesterase, aren't shared with anything in the reference set.

The honest reading is that this is hypothesis generation off bulk-tissue expression — it cannot see cell types, which is precisely where the ketamine mechanism lives, since the canonical story runs through NMDA receptors on GABAergic interneurons rather than on pyramidal cells. And the authors say the quiet part themselves: drugs with opposite perceptual effects share the same receptor in the same region. So the fingerprint is a map of where a drug could act, and the gate — cell type, circuit, dose, which enantiomer or metabolite is actually present — decides what that engagement does. As a tool for asking why ketamine's social-functioning benefit and its dissociative effects might be anatomically separable, it earns its place. As an explanation, it isn't one yet.

Fourth, and still on depression mechanism, a reanalysis of the psilocybin-versus-escitalopram randomized trial that changes what the images are made of. Conventional whole-brain modelling fits a connectivity matrix to the BOLD signals of regions. This group instead works in what they call vortex space: parcellate finely, then characterize each macroregion not by its mean signal but by the local phase synchronization among its constituent parcels — a local Kuramoto order parameter — and fit the generative model to connectivity between those synchronization patterns. Same data, same anatomy, same oscillator model; the only change is what the node variable represents.

Vortex space was the more sensitive representation in most analyses, including distinguishing pre- from post-treatment states and predicting escitalopram response from baseline connectivity. The treatment contrast is the interesting one: psilocybin produced widespread increases in vortex-space effective connectivity and reduced network modularity, and escitalopram produced nothing comparable. Yet in both arms, greater increases in turbulence — spatiotemporal variability in local synchronization — tracked greater symptom reduction, with a significant post-treatment increase in responders. Two drugs reorganizing dynamics in visibly different ways while sharing the dynamical feature that correlates with getting better. That is a more interesting claim than either "psychedelics increase entropy" or "they work the same way", and the obvious caution is that this is a modest phase-two sample reanalyzed with a new method whose sensitivity advantage is itself one of the paper's claims.

Fifth, a neuromodulation trial in young people that is worth hearing precisely because its mechanistic result is negative. Fifty youths aged fifteen to twenty-five with current depression received continuous theta burst stimulation to a personalized dorsomedial prefrontal target, twice a day for ten days, paired with positive-affect training between sessions. The rationale is specific: anhedonia has been linked to hyperconnectivity between dorsomedial prefrontal cortex and nucleus accumbens, and a single session of this stimulation had previously reduced that connectivity with corresponding improvement in positive affect.

Forty-two percent responded. Depression, anhedonia and anxiety all improved. But the connectivity that the whole design targets did not change on average. What held up was the individual-difference relationship — participants whose connectivity fell further improved more on anhedonia — and responders beat non-responders on symptoms without beating them on connectivity. The authors then did the thing that rescues the paper: they modelled the electric field actually delivered and asked how well each person's real target network matched the intended reward network, and that similarity predicted who showed the connectivity decrease. So the mechanism may well be right and the targeting is the gate. It's a small open trial and that's a post-hoc modelling result, but it is the correct next experiment stated as a finding, and it is a useful corrective to reading a group-level null as a dead mechanism.

Sixth, the paper that gave me the spine for today. A group in Vienna asked how spinal astrocytes modulate acute nociceptive transmission, and whether that differs by sex. Chemogenetically activating distinct astrocytic G-protein-coupled pathways in rats produced pathway-specific synaptic depression or potentiation at spinal nociceptive synapses in males — and in females, nothing detectable. Not a smaller effect; no effect.

Then two manipulations identified the gate. Gonadectomy abolished astrocyte-induced plasticity in males, and testosterone treatment in ovariectomized females enabled Gq-dependent synaptic depression. Separately, females showed more prominent perineuronal net labelling, and enzymatically degrading extracellular matrix components also enabled the Gq-dependent depression in females. So hormonal status and matrix organization each license the same astrocytic signal to reach the synapse. And the behavioral result cuts across all of it: despite having no detectable astrocyte-induced plasticity, females developed mechanical hypersensitivity comparable to males. The plasticity you can measure in the slice and the pain behavior you can measure in the animal are dissociable, which should make everyone slightly more careful about reading a synaptic mechanism as the pain mechanism. Given how much of the sex-differences-in-pain literature is microglia-versus-T-cells, an astrocytic axis gated by matrix is a genuinely new place to look.

Seventh, briefly, on the genetics side. A whole-genome sequencing study across twenty-five pedigrees with schizophrenia, psychosis, bipolar disorder, depression, OCD and substance use disorder went looking in a place exome sequencing structurally cannot: microRNA genes. Five rare variants segregated with schizophrenia and related psychoses — two in seed regions, one in a mature region, two in precursors — including a mature-region variant in miR-4794 segregating in two unrelated pedigrees and appearing again in a parent-offspring pair from a third. One pedigree also carried a seed-region variant predicted to shift target specificity, losing synaptic gene targets. And the pathway test is what makes it more than a list: targets of three of these microRNAs are enriched for schizophrenia rare-variant risk genes from the SCHEMA exome collaboration, with six of those genes targeted by miR-96. A seed-region change doesn't break one gene, it re-points a regulator at a different target set — which is exactly the kind of effect a coding-variant pipeline is blind to. Small pedigree counts, prediction-heavy, no functional validation; but a reasonable template for how to interrogate non-coding variation without drowning in it.

Eighth, a resource that belongs on the radar of anyone doing quantitative pharmacology. Pharmacolibrary is an attempt to turn the published pharmacokinetic, pharmacodynamic and pharmacogenomic literature into executable models. A multi-agent workflow does LLM-assisted retrieval and extraction, then deterministic validation, ontology mapping and provenance tracking, and an engineer agent instantiates the extracted parameters into one of eight structural templates and exports to Modelica, SBML, CellML, MATLAB or FMI, with browser-based simulation. The current release screened about a hundred and thirty-five thousand publications, judged twenty-three thousand relevant, and extracted at least one population PK record for nine hundred and forty-seven drugs, PD records for sixteen hundred and ninety, and pharmacogenomic records for a hundred and fifty. Their own evaluation says staged extraction beats single-pass, and that local open-weight models were at least comparable to frontier commercial ones — preliminary, their word.

The detail I'd flag is the one they're transparent about: a standing list of twelve thousand three hundred publications whose abstracts look highly relevant but whose full texts are behind paywalls, which they estimate holds another nine thousand four hundred extractable records. Roughly a third of the resource is missing for reasons that have nothing to do with the science. Everyone building a literature-derived knowledge base is hitting this wall; almost nobody quantifies it.

Ninth, a safety result for clinical language models that reframes where to look. The concern with prompt injection in a clinical setting is the attack that leaves the output looking entirely ordinary — the model is told to omit a pathology, and it returns a fluent, reassuring note with nothing to flag. This group ran a multimodal model's residual-stream activations through a natural-language autoencoder to render its internal state as plain text, then had an input-blind judge read that thoughts channel and the answer separately.

For the omission attack in text, the judge caught the injection in forty-nine of fifty cases from the internal state and two of fifty from the answer. The disagreement ran entirely in one direction — dozens of cases flagged from thoughts alone, not a single one from the answer alone — and the same pattern held on images and replicated on a second model. For the covert drug-insertion attack, where the answer does carry a trace, the answer channel did better but the internal state was still more sensitive, thirty-nine of fifty versus twelve. False positives on clean inputs stayed near zero on both channels. And in a separate experiment, a safety appraisal was visible in the internal state while the model was still reading a harmful request, before any refusal appeared in the output. The practical appeal is that none of this requires retraining: an off-the-shelf verbalizer and a prompted general-purpose judge, so adapting to a new attack family means editing a prompt rather than collecting labelled activations.

Tenth, from the same corner of the field but arguing almost the opposite case. Decision2Care works up an acutely ill patient without generating any free text at all. A decision model assigns probabilities over a fixed menu of permitted actions — examine this system, order this panel, this imaging study, this drug at this dose — and fixed rules execute the most probable one, with a differential posterior updated at each step. On five hundred and forty-six emergency admissions across twelve diseases it reached eighty-six and a half percent diagnostic accuracy, against eighty-six percent for a text-based agent on one open-weight backbone and about ninety and ninety-two percent on two stronger ones, with over half the gap concentrated in a single disease, cholecystitis, where most errors were gallstones without cholecystitis. Weighted to a matched disease mix, four board-certified physicians working up a sample of the same benchmark got seventy-eight percent. Median time per work-up was two point two seconds against ninety-two to a hundred, at roughly a fiftieth of the cost, and run-to-run variability was half a percentage point. You give up some accuracy and you get an audit trail that is the decision rather than a narration of it — which is the right trade for anyone who has tried to reconcile an agent's stated reasoning with what it actually did.

Eleventh, a genuinely prospective test of trial forecasting. The system assembles standardized evidence dossiers from public sources and has a frontier model estimate the probability that a trial meets its prespecified primary endpoint. Crucially, the forecasts were recorded before disclosure. Of six hundred and ninety-four ongoing trials with results expected within six months, sixty-nine had been adjudicated by late October, giving an area under the curve of point eight two. But look at the error structure: twenty-seven of twenty-nine failures correctly identified, and seventeen of forty successes wrongly called failures. It is a good pessimist and a poor optimist, which is roughly what you would expect from a model reasoning over the published evidence base — and it means the number that would actually be worth money, a confident call that a trial will succeed, is the one it can't make. Sixty-nine adjudicated outcomes is a small denominator and the confidence interval is wide, but they've archived forecasts on nearly six thousand more trials as a public benchmark, so this will self-adjudicate.

And to close, a benchmark that tests the ability everyone claims and nobody measures. Existing AI-scientist evaluations check whether an agent can execute a given workflow, or choose the next measurement from a predefined menu. TwinLab-Bench asks whether it can design its own experiment, by building digital twins of fifty-two tasks from real laboratories across the physical sciences, life sciences and engineering, supporting over a hundred experimental action types, and scoring the agent's final report against a hidden answer taken from the lab's actual records. The best agents score in the mid-forties out of a hundred and the field spreads from five to forty-five, with score rising roughly with the logarithm of cost. The fidelity work is what makes it credible: seventy-six percent of returned results come from the record itself or from calculators calibrated on that lab's data, swapping an extrapolation choice for an equally plausible one changes few outcomes, and held-out conditions are reproduced to within about one percent interpolated and two and a half percent extrapolated. In successful runs, more than a third of requests were experiments the expert route never contained. So the twin is permissive enough to let an agent go somewhere the humans didn't — which is the only version of this benchmark worth having, and a reasonable note to end on.
