## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. It's Saturday, July twenty-fifth, twenty twenty-six, and it's a dopamine-and-reward heavy day — ten items. We open on the molecular basis of a side effect clinicians know well but can't predict: why dopamine agonists trigger impulse-control disorders in some patients and not others. From there we stay on dopamine — how the geometry of its release splits movement from learning, and how astrocytes in the ventral tegmental area gate cocaine associations. Then we cross into aversion, with an endogenous-opioid brake on fear learning and a habenula circuit that decides whether uncontrollable stress becomes helplessness. We do a piece of hard receptor biophysics on what actually makes some AMPA receptors slow, a pain-circuit dissection that separates hurting from caring, and we close on the computational side: two takes on why AI-driven drug discovery keeps tripping over its own benchmarks, and a small study trying to predict who responds to brain stimulation.

We open on a molecular story about a side effect that clinicians see constantly but can't forecast. Dopamine D2 and D3 agonists like pramipexole — the workhorse dopamine-replacement drug in Parkinson's disease and restless legs syndrome — carry a striking risk of impulse-control disorders: pathological gambling, compulsive buying, hypersexuality. In Parkinson's patients on these agonists the rate runs close to eighteen percent. But only a subset of patients ever develop it, which has always pointed to some pre-existing vulnerability. A group working in rats went looking for the molecular substrate of that vulnerability. They stratified animals by baseline impulsivity on a delay-discounting task — the standard measure of whether you'll wait for a bigger reward or grab the small immediate one — then gave subchronic pramipexole and sequenced microRNAs in dorsal striatum and nucleus accumbens. The behavioral result already tells you something: pramipexole pushed the low- and mid-impulsive rats toward impulsive choice, but the already-high-impulsive rats didn't move at all — a ceiling effect, as if the drug carries you toward a trait you may already be sitting at. One microRNA, microRNA-10a-5p, tracked that pattern precisely: constitutively high in the trait-impulsive animals, and driven up by pramipexole exactly in the animals whose behavior changed. Then the causal test — they overexpressed microRNA-10a-5p with a virus, confirmed it knocks down the PI3-kinase–Akt–mTOR and BDNF pathways, and showed that doing this in the accumbens alone, not the dorsal striatum, was enough to make rats impulsive, reproducing the drug's effect without the drug. Why it matters: it turns "individual vulnerability" from a shrug into a specific, accumbens-localized molecular lever, and it nominates a microRNA-directed therapeutic angle for a disorder we currently treat mostly by borrowing from the addiction playbook. The honest caveat is that this is a rat delay-discounting model standing in for a human behavioral syndrome, and any microRNA touches hundreds of transcripts — but the region-restricted rescue is about as clean a causal claim as this kind of work gets.

Staying with dopamine but pulling back to a more fundamental question: how does a single neuromodulator control both movement and learning at the same time, when those can obviously come apart? A new preprint argues the answer is in the geometry of release. They show striatal dopamine comes out in two distinct modes — a diffuse mode that builds up bulk extracellular dopamine, and a spatially restricted, point-to-point mode. When they selectively eliminate the release driving bulk accumulation, they unmask that point-to-point mode, which keeps phasic dopamine-receptor activation going on striatal projection neurons. And the two modes do different jobs: killing the diffuse signal drops striatal excitability and reduces locomotion, while the point-to-point transmission is enough on its own to maintain dendritic spine density and support motor and associative learning. The framing — that the same cell multiplexes different messages by where it releases, not only when — is a genuinely useful way to think about why dopaminergic drugs can dissociate motor from cognitive effects, and why bulk-measurement techniques might be blind to half of what dopamine is doing.

Next, a reminder that astrocytes are not just packing material in addiction circuits. A group looked at astrocytes in the ventral tegmental area and used chemogenetics to switch on their Gq signaling while rats learned to associate a context with cocaine. The result is nicely history-dependent. In drug-naive rats, activating those astrocytes made a subthreshold dose of cocaine enough to build a place preference — it facilitated learning the association. But in rats that already had cocaine experience, the very same astrocyte activation suppressed that preference, and in a self-administration setup it cut voluntary cocaine intake. So one manipulation reads out in opposite directions depending on drug history, which both flags ventral tegmental astrocytes as a real therapeutic node and warns that any astrocyte-Gq approach would have to respect where a patient sits in the trajectory from first use to established use.

From reward to aversion, and to endogenous opioids doing something subtle. We've known that dopamine in the lateral amygdala drives fear acquisition and that opioids restrain it, but this group actually watched the opioid signal. Using a sensor for met-enkephalin, they show it's released locally in the amygdala during auditory fear conditioning, and the signal migrates over training — from the shock itself to the cue that predicts the shock, the same backward shift you see in dopamine prediction-error signals. The source is the amygdalo-striatal transition zone, a strip of medium spiny neurons getting strong auditory thalamic input, and the enkephalin spreads by volume transmission — diffusing over to the lateral amygdala rather than acting at one tidy synapse. Knock the enkephalin down there and fear learning gets stronger, so this diffuse peptide is a brake on fear memory, and it works by suppressing dopamine release through mu-opioid receptors. It's a clean example of volume-transmitted neuropeptide signaling setting how much you learn from something frightening — and a concrete mechanistic handle on the opioid–dopamine balance that's thought to go wrong in anxiety disorders and PTSD.

Sticking with aversion and its aftermath: the lateral habenula is the brain's disappointment-and-punishment hub, and its activation is required for the learned-helplessness state that follows inescapable stress — the classic model where uncontrollable, but not controllable, stress produces a susceptible phenotype. This paper asks which input to the habenula does the work, and points at glutamatergic axons arriving from the ventral tegmental area. Those axons fire to aversive stimuli, and — interestingly — that response doesn't depend on prior stress history; they seem to report the current aversive event rather than encoding whether you've been through it before. But when they optogenetically silenced those glutamatergic axons in the habenula during each inescapable-stress trial, they prevented the social and non-social downstream consequences of that stress. So this input acts as a gate on whether uncontrollable stress gets translated into a helplessness phenotype at all — a fairly specific circuit entry point for stress-related depression, and a nice dissociation of reporting an aversive event from committing to its long-term behavioral cost.

Now a receptor-biophysics piece that quietly overturns a textbook line. AMPA receptors are supposed to be the fast receptor in the brain — millisecond currents that let synapses track individual spikes. But this group found abundant slow AMPA responses in CA1 pyramidal cells, distributed like a mosaic, differing even between neighboring dendrites, and they went after the molecular cause. The answer is an understudied auxiliary subunit called cornichon-3, or CNIH3. It sits in a gradient — common in ventral CA1, sparse in dorsal, absent in dentate granule cells — and it does the job on its own: a short-hairpin RNA against it largely ablates the slow responses in ventral CA1, and overexpressing it in dorsal CA1 introduces slow responses all over. Their framing is that cornichon-3 converts a synapse from a fast follower into what they call a detonator — a slow, summating input that can by itself drive its target — which means a single auxiliary protein decides whether a synapse does coincidence detection or temporal integration. For anyone thinking about AMPA-receptor pharmacology, it's a pointed reminder that the auxiliary-subunit landscape, not just the pore, defines what these receptors actually do region by region.

Then a pain-circuit dissection with a clean translational logic. The mediodorsal thalamus shapes pain through its projections to prefrontal cortex, but it usually gets treated as a single unit. This group split it into a medial-central and a lateral subdivision and asked whether they separately control the sensory-discriminative versus the affective-motivational sides of pain. Using subdivision-selective lesions and pathway-specific optogenetics into anterior cingulate and prelimbic cortex, they found a double dissociation. Both subdivisions matter for mechanical and thermal hypersensitivity — the raw sensory side — but only the medial-central subdivision, acting through cingulate cortex, controlled pain-related avoidance, which is the affective side. The two subdivisions even preferentially target different cortical interneuron types. That maps "how much it hurts" and "how much you care that it hurts" onto separable thalamocortical channels — exactly the separation you'd want if the goal is to treat the suffering component of chronic pain without simply numbing sensation.

Shifting to the computational side, and to a paper making an argument I think is basically right about why AI has stalled on small-molecule drug discovery. The pitch is that the field keeps hoping scaling will work the way it did for language and for protein structure, but the small-molecule structural data is tiny and historically biased — the molecules in it are the ones somebody already knew how to synthesize. This group's framework, called Target Preference Maps, tries to sidestep the memorization trap. Instead of feeding the model whole-ligand topology, it learns atom-type-specific spatial preference maps from local protein microenvironments — essentially, given this little pocket of protein atoms, what kind of ligand atom likes to sit here. Because it never sees the whole molecule, it can't just memorize known ligands, and the learned preferences transfer across targets. The maps recover chemically sensible things — bridging waters, metal-dependent environments — and they validated the approach both retrospectively and prospectively on a genuinely hard protein-protein interface target, using it to guide real medicinal-chemistry optimization. It's interpretable, and it's designed to feed downstream docking or generative pipelines rather than replace the chemist — which is a more honest framing than end-to-end AI designs your drug.

Staying in computational drug discovery, with an ADMET model and, more importantly, a lesson about benchmarking. A company reported a foundation model called CHIMIYA-1 against the Therapeutics Data Commons ADMET benchmark — the twenty-two-endpoint standard for predicting absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity, which is where an enormous fraction of late-stage drug failures come from. The model does well — first in the field on four endpoints, top decile on twenty of the twenty-two. But the part worth your attention is the rigor. They reported every score as the mean of five independently seeded runs, which is the benchmark's own stated minimum, and — they note — a bar that not all public leaderboard entries actually clear. And they audited for train-test structural overlap and found none. The uncomfortable subtext is that several of the top-ranked public comparators on this leaderboard don't survive that scrutiny. Take the specific model with a grain of salt, since it's proprietary, but the methodological point — that ADMET leaderboards may be inflated by data leakage and by a single lucky seed — is one worth carrying around every time you read one.

And we'll close on something small and speculative, but pointed in the right direction. Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation works for treatment-resistant depression, but the response is frustratingly variable and we don't have a good way to predict it in advance. This is a single-investigator study — twenty-five patients — applying network control theory to the structural connectome. Prior work mostly looked at how controllable individual brain regions are; the twist here is to look at edges, the connections themselves. Baseline edge-controllability of specific connections centered on the middle frontal gyrus — links to superior frontal gyrus, hippocampus, angular gyrus — correlated with how much depression scores improved after five weeks of prefrontal stimulation. With twenty-five patients this is a hypothesis, not a biomarker, and edge controllability is an abstract quantity you have to squint at. But it's exactly the kind of circuit-level, individualized predictor the field needs if personalized neuromodulation is ever going to be more than trial and error.
