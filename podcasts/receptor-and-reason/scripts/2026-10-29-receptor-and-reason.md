## Script

Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Thursday, October twenty-ninth, twenty twenty-six.

There's a thread running through almost everything on today's list, and it isn't a subject-matter thread. It's about what the load-bearing comparison in each paper is actually being made against. In a good experiment the thing you compare to is present, contemporaneous, and measured under the same conditions. In today's papers, over and over, the comparison is to something that isn't there — a human arm that was never recruited, a covariate that was never entered, a density nobody can assign, a denominator that quietly shrank to twenty, a label describing a plant that isn't in the bottle. None of these are frauds, and most are disclosed. But in every case the headline sentence is doing more work than the control behind it can support.

Let's start with the best structural pharmacology of the week, out of Shanghai Jiao Tong and the Shanghai Institute of Materia Medica. The subject is the intracellular allosteric pocket of neurotensin receptor one, and the parent compound is S-B-I five-five-three, the beta-arrestin-biased modulator that's been the field's favorite demonstration that you can impose bias from the cytoplasmic face rather than the orthosteric site. The team made four analogs, each one perturbing a different sub-region of that pocket — the entrance-facing head, the mid-pocket core, the cytoplasmic-facing side chain, the deep anchoring region — and then profiled all five compounds across beta-arrestin two and five separate G protein pathways.

The pharmacology is genuinely striking. None of the compounds activate G-q. One analog, N-oh-six-five, produced no detectable G protein activity at all, making it the cleanest beta-arrestin-biased agent in the set. They then solved cryo-EM structures of neurotensin receptor one bound to beta-arrestin two with each of three modulators, at roughly two-point-five to two-point-nine angstroms, and the structures show what you'd want them to show: the same cavity, subtly different depths and orientations, N-oh-six-five uniquely forming a halogen bond on the TM7 face that the other two don't make. Alanine scanning then found a handful of residues around the pocket that shift transducer preference in opposite directions depending on which ligand is bound.

Then they went into mice, and this is where the comparison gets interesting. N-oh-six-five produced rapid, sustained hypothermia; S-B-I five-five-three produced none. The paper reads this as bias translating into physiology. But look at the mechanism they themselves propose: neurotensin receptor one hypothermia runs through G-q, neither compound touches G-q directly, and the explanation is that S-B-I five-five-three is a strong negative modulator of neurotensin-evoked G-q signaling while N-oh-six-five is a weak one. So the headline in vivo separation is not produced by what the biased ligand does. It's produced by what the other compound blocks and this one doesn't. That's a real and interesting result, but it's a story about residual endogenous signaling through the one pathway neither molecule engages, which is close to the opposite of the framing.

And then there's the exposure data. They explicitly test whether the divergent effects are just pharmacokinetics, and report that S-B-I five-five-three has substantially higher brain exposure and brain-to-plasma ratio while N-oh-six-five has greater plasma exposure. They conclude this argues against a pharmacokinetic explanation. But the phenotypes sort exactly along that axis: S-B-I five-five-three is the one with the central effects — impaired Morris water maze, reduced Y-maze alternation — and N-oh-six-five the one with hypothermia and suppressed food intake. Higher brain exposure, more central phenotype. The exposure comparison doesn't exclude the confound; it lines up with it. What survives intact, and is genuinely newsworthy, is that S-B-I five-five-three — a compound on a translational track for psychostimulant use disorder — impaired spatial and working memory in mice and accelerated weight gain in diet-induced obese animals over a month of daily dosing. That's a liability signal on a clinical-interest molecule, and it's in the supplement.

Second, from Oxford, Kiel, Queen Mary and Cerevance, cryo-EM structures of the microglial potassium channel THIK-one in complex with C-V-N two ninety-three. This matters because C-V-N two ninety-three is a real clinical candidate — a brain-penetrant THIK-one inhibitor that's cleared Phase one and is being pursued for Alzheimer's and A-L-S on the theory that blocking potassium efflux damps inflammasome-driven microglial activation. Nobody knew where it bound. The answer here is that it binds deep in the inner cavity below the selectivity filter and acts as a pore blocker, with the two tyrosines of the so-called Y-gate rotating down about fifteen degrees to accommodate it. Molecular dynamics keeps it stably in that site, and functional work supports it.

Now, we covered a competing THIK-one structure on September twenty-ninth, and this paper is in part a direct rebuttal of it. That earlier work assigned a low-resolution density at the intracellular mouth of the pore to the distal C-terminus acting as a second, more intracellular gate, and argued that this segment is what distinguishes the clinical candidate from the earlier tool compound. The Oxford group says no: the same density appears in maps from truncated constructs that have no C-terminus at all, and in excised patches, with drug applied directly to the intracellular face, they see no difference in block kinetics that could support the rival model. Their conclusion is that the C-terminus does not plug the pore — it controls the Y-gate allosterically and acts as a hub for signaling input.

That rebuttal is probably right. But note the symmetry problem, because the honest caveat is also in this paper: at three-point-four to three-point-six angstroms, sitting on the pseudo-symmetry axis, they state plainly that they could not unambiguously resolve the orientation of either compound. So they build a drug and a mechanism into one unassigned density while using a second unassigned density to dismantle somebody else's model. Both moves may be correct. They are not symmetric in epistemic cost, and the paper's own closing line — that the identity of that intracellular density remains to be determined — applies to its own ligand pose more than it lets on.

Third, staying on muscarinic pharmacology after yesterday's M-four work, a circuit paper on how acetylcholine sustains short-term memory in prefrontal cortex. The premise is a real biophysical problem: mediodorsal thalamic inputs to prefrontal cortex show profound frequency-dependent short-term depression, so repetitive thalamic drive progressively loses the ability to fire cortical neurons — which is awkward for any model where a thalamo-cortical loop maintains delay-period activity. Their answer is that acetylcholine doesn't fix the synapse. Cortical acetylcholine rises at cue onset and stays elevated through the delay, muscarinic activation leaves the depression untouched, and instead raises intrinsic excitability of prefrontal neurons so they stay responsive to a weakening input. A postsynaptic-gain solution to a presynaptic problem.

The causal behavioral test is systemic atropine. It reversibly drops accuracy to chance, and they run the right first control — a zero-delay version of the task, where performance stays intact, ruling out sensory and motor explanations. They also show the deficit survives restricting to completed trials, so it isn't only disengagement. What's missing is the control the mechanism actually requires: local prefrontal infusion. Everything cellular in this paper is prefrontal and postsynaptic; the causal manipulation is brain-wide muscarinic blockade in an animal with muscarinic receptors in hippocampus, striatum, thalamus and periphery. And in the population analyses there's a smaller version of the same thing — having established that saline doesn't differ from baseline, the decoding comparisons largely run pre-injection against atropine, so the vehicle arm, which exists, drops out of the analysis that carries the conclusion.

Fourth, from Columbia, a result I liked a great deal, partly because it surprised the authors. The model is early developmental fluoxetine exposure in mice, during a window equivalent to the human third trimester — an established risk factor framing for later depression. The adult phenotype they find is a deficit in goal-directed effort. Then they tried to rescue it, and the obvious candidates failed: chronic adult S-S-R-I treatment did nothing, and neither did tianeptine, the atypical antidepressant that works as a partial mu opioid receptor agonist. What worked was going the other way — methocinnamox, a long-acting mu opioid antagonist, reversed the deficit. Knocking down mu opioid receptor in nucleus accumbens reproduced the rescue. And critically, both manipulations only worked in mice that had the early fluoxetine exposure; in controls they did nothing.

That last clause is what makes it a real finding rather than a nonspecific motivational effect, and it's a genuinely odd mechanistic claim: a developmental window of elevated serotonin signaling appears to reset the sign of accumbal mu opioid receptor's contribution to effort in adulthood. If it holds, it argues that motivational anhedonia and mood may need separate pharmacology, and that patients who don't get effort back on an S-S-R-I are a mechanistically distinct group rather than simply under-treated.

Fifth, a small but well-targeted PET study from Turku. The question is whether childhood adversity leaves a measurable imprint on the adult endocannabinoid system. Thirty-five participants — fifteen with first-episode psychosis, twenty healthy — scanned with a selective fluorine-eighteen CB1 tracer with arterial input, distribution volume across thirteen regions. Participants reporting two or more adverse childhood events had lower CB1 availability, with the effect strongest in amygdala, temporal cortex, hippocampus and insula; eta squared around point three in each. A voxel-wise analysis replicates it without region-of-interest constraints, and the effect survives adjustment for recent adverse events, depression severity, age and body mass index.

Here's the covariate that never enters any model: smoking. It's the single demographic variable that differs between groups in this study — two of twenty controls smoked versus seven of fifteen patients — and it splits unevenly across the adversity groups too. Chronic smoking is one of the better-replicated downregulators of CB1 availability in human PET, with its largest effects in cortical and limbic regions, which is the same spatial pattern reported here. The authors adjust diligently for four other things and never for that one. With thirty-five subjects you may simply not be able to, and the finding may well be real — the preclinical literature predicts it, and a second preprint using a different tracer points the same way. But the comparison that would make it convincing is the one that wasn't run.

Sixth, briefly, from Bordeaux and Montreal: chronic twenty-five percent fructose in drinking water for eight weeks produces anxiety-like behavior and memory deficits in male mice alongside the expected metabolic changes, hippocampal transcriptional changes concentrated in microglia, and — the mechanistic claim — microglia-specific knockdown of the fructose transporter Glut5 prevents the anxiety. What's worth knowing is scope. The knockdown rescue is demonstrated in the novelty-suppressed feeding test, and the paper says explicitly that the knockdown could not be evaluated in the elevated plus maze or the splash test because fructose didn't induce the phenotype in that cohort. So the behavioral battery that established the phenotype did not replicate in the experiment that tested the mechanism, and the rescue rests on the single surviving assay — which also happens to be the anxiety assay most sensitive to appetite and metabolic state. The molecular phenotype is the more interesting part anyway: fructose didn't raise microglial cytokine expression, it blunted the response to lipopolysaccharide. A hypo-reactive microglial state, not an inflamed one.

Seventh, a large autism stratification paper from Toronto and U-S-C. Fourteen clinical measures in sixty-three hundred autistic children in SPARK, reduced by a tree-based dimensionality-reduction method into three subtypes — high support needs, emotional-behavioral support needs, and low support needs — plus continuous scores so individuals aren't forced into one box. The framework projects onto twenty-seven hundred children in the Simons Simplex Collection and reproduces the phenotypic and medication-use patterns, which is the hard part and they did it. A-D-H-D and major depression polygenic scores track the emotional-behavioral score in both cohorts; de novo and rare copy-number burden track the high-support-needs score.

The part to hold loosely is the heritability ordering, which is how this will get cited. Common-variant heritability comes out at point two two for the emotional-behavioral score, point one seven for high support needs, point oh six for low support needs — with standard errors of about point oh five on each. Those intervals overlap substantially, and no test of the difference between them is reported. That ordering is a ranking of three point estimates, not a demonstrated difference. The cell-type enrichments are likewise uncorrected, and one of the two for the emotional-behavioral score is fetal endodermal cells, which for a behavioral phenotype tells you how much weight that analysis carries.

Eighth, a drug-supply surveillance report from U-C-L-A and N-I-S-T worth tracking. Medetomidine — the veterinary alpha-two agonist whose single enantiomer, dexmedetomidine, we use in intensive care — is arriving in West Coast fentanyl. Community drug checking in Southern California, thirty-four hundred samples by direct-analysis-in-real-time mass spectrometry, first detection in May twenty twenty-five, thirty-two positives since. Two findings are solid. Mean concentration about point four percent mass fraction, which sounds trivial until you remember medetomidine is estimated at up to a hundred times the potency of xylazine in animals. And all thirteen samples that got chiral analysis were racemic — equal parts dexmedetomidine and the non-pharmaceutical enantiomer — good evidence this is synthesized for the illicit market rather than diverted from human supply.

The number that will travel, though, is the trajectory: six percent of fentanyl samples in the second quarter of twenty twenty-five to twenty-five percent by August of twenty twenty-six. Six percent is nine of a hundred forty-nine. Twenty-five percent is five of twenty. The denominator fell by a factor of seven, and the final figure is the final month of a quarter, not the quarter. The direction is almost certainly right and the public health implication — a sedative that complicates overdose reversal and produces a worse withdrawal syndrome than xylazine, now in a market that largely skipped xylazine — stands on its own. The fourfold increase is five samples.

Ninth, and this is where the theme becomes the paper's own title. A group spanning U-S-C, Western Ontario and ResearchHub inserted fourteen known methodological errors into randomized trial reports and asked fifteen language models to review them. Detection of the nine major errors ranged from fifty-three to ninety-six percent on the classic manuscript set, and fifty-one to ninety-nine percent on three trials published in twenty twenty-six — chosen to postdate most of the models' training cutoffs, which is the right precaution and they document it trial by trial. Open-weight models landed sixty-six to eighty-seven percent. Review times ran one-point-four to sixteen minutes.

The title says these are errors that human peer review misses. The human number in that comparison is twenty-seven percent, and it comes from a two thousand eight study, on different manuscripts, with reviewers who were never re-run. No human reviewed the twenty twenty-six trials. The authors say this in their limitations, and they annotate it in the figure legend, which is more than most would do. But the structurally important consequence is in the control arm, and it's the best thing in the paper: they added unmodified manuscripts, which the original human study never had, and found models flagging between two and twenty-nine percent of error types in papers with no inserted errors. Sensitivity and specificity were inversely related across models, Spearman's rho of minus point seven seven. The most sensitive model was also the least specific. Since no human specificity exists anywhere in this literature, there is no way to place the human point on that tradeoff curve at all — the twenty-seven percent is a sensitivity with no companion number. The useful reading of this paper is not models beat humans. It's that detection rate alone was always the wrong summary statistic, for models and for us, and this is the first study with the control arm to prove it.

Tenth, the same lesson in radiotherapy, which has gone further toward deployed agents than most of medicine. OpenGray adds tasks with known answers to a head-and-neck planning dataset: two requests containing dose goals proven mathematically incompatible, and — the key design move — a feasible control, a hard but achievable case with a stored plan that meets every hard goal. Four systems on thirty patients. Decision accuracy came out at ninety-five and ninety-two percent, which is the number that would go in an abstract. But on the contradictions requiring arithmetic over the anatomy, the systems escalated for review only fifty and twenty-five percent of the time. And on the feasible control every system submitted a plan, yet twenty-four percent of those plans missed a hard goal the stored plan had met. The authors also read logs from thirty-nine real stereotactic radiosurgery runs of a deployed clinical planning agent: in four of them a goal failed at every attempt, the agent correctly named the failure in every reply, and had no action available for stopping. Favorable aggregate scores concealing specific decision failures — and the only reason you can see it is that somebody built the feasible control.

Eleventh, a companion from the AI-scientist side, asking whether automated novelty checks actually work. The framework uses temporal ground truth: about a thousand proposals built from published papers, reviewer disputes, and generated ideas that match things the field subsequently published. The finding is the same shape as the peer review paper — existing novelty checks with high rejection rates on genuinely non-novel work also have high false alarm rates on ideas later realized by human researchers. Rejection rate without the false-alarm arm is uninterpretable. Their claim-level module catches ninety-seven percent of deliberately disguised republished papers at a one-and-a-half percent false alarm rate, and cuts a language-model judge's false alarms from nearly twelve percent to that same one-and-a-half. Whether or not that module is the answer, the evaluation design is the contribution.

And to close, a short research letter from Ole Miss that is the purest version of today's theme. They bought forty-six unit-dose products labeled as cat's claw — an herbal supplement with no opioid activity — from online retailers and three Mississippi gas stations, after Utah and Tennessee reported overdoses and deaths involving tablets with that label. Not one product declared kratom alkaloids. Every single one contained at least one. Mitragynine in eighty-nine percent, seven-hydroxymitragynine in seventy-six percent, and in eighty-five percent either mitragynine pseudoindoxyl or dihydro-seven-hydroxymitragynine — both of which the D-E-A temporarily placed in Schedule One in August. Five products contained tianeptine, not approved in the United States, at twenty-eight to forty milligrams a unit. Eight products carrying explicit claims of no kratom or no seven-hydroxymitragynine contained both. The seven-hydroxymitragynine content across products ranged from a hundredth of a milligram to ninety-five milligrams per unit — a ten-thousand-fold range, so even knowing the compound is present tells a clinician essentially nothing about exposure.

And the detail that makes it complete: twenty-eight percent of these products did contain some Uncaria alkaloids, but not one showed the exclusive oxindole profile of authentic cat's claw bark. So the label names a plant that is, in the strict sense, not in the package, attached to a dose range spanning four orders of magnitude, on products that are also carrying an unapproved antidepressant with mu opioid activity — the same tianeptine that failed to rescue anything in the Columbia experiment we talked about earlier. If you are working up unexplained opioid toxicity and the patient reports an herbal supplement, that is now a real differential.

That's the show. The common lesson, if you want one: a detection rate, an accuracy, a prevalence, an effect size — none of them mean anything on their own. What they mean depends entirely on the thing you compared them to, and whether that thing was actually there. See you tomorrow.
