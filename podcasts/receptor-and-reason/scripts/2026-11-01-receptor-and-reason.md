## Script

Welcome to Receptor and Reason for Sunday, November first, twenty twenty-six.

Today's through-line, if there is one, is that the scaffolding we build around a system — whether that system is a language model, a kinase assay, or a pathology stain — keeps turning out to be the thing that determines the answer. We start with a hospital that let four AI agents quietly dose a narrow-therapeutic-index antibiotic for two months, and the configuration that won is not the one you would guess.

A Stanford group silently deployed a set of language-model agents for inpatient vancomycin dosing at Stanford Hospital over two months, running them in parallel with the real pharmacists and never touching patient care. The design is a two-by-two: high autonomy versus low autonomy, each with and without the institution's own dosing guideline. Low autonomy means developer-written code fixes the sequence — pull the labs through the health-record interface, assess for acute kidney injury, call the commercial pharmacokinetic engine, have the model pick among the options it returns. High autonomy means the model runs its own loop, writing and executing Python, deciding which tools to call and when it is finished. Same underlying model in all four arms. Across four hundred and three dosing events in three hundred and sixteen patients, the high-autonomy agent's mean absolute percentage error against the treating pharmacist's order was twenty-one point eight percent; the tightly engineered low-autonomy workflow was thirty-seven point eight percent. Within a twenty-five percent tolerance, high autonomy landed seventy-three percent of the time versus fifty-one percent.

Two things make this more than a scoreboard. First, the guideline did nothing — supplying the institution's own protocol moved the error about one percentage point in either arm, not significant. All the signal was in runtime autonomy, none in clinical specification. Second, they calibrated the tolerance against a human reference: a second, blinded pharmacist agreed with the treating pharmacist within twenty-five percent in ninety percent of cases. So the bar is ninety, and the best agent is seventy-three. Pharmacists then reviewed a sample of out-of-tolerance recommendations and flagged a safety concern in about two-thirds of them, with low autonomy carrying more than double the odds of a flagged concern.

The error taxonomy is where this becomes a lesson rather than a benchmark. The single biggest category in both arms was misreading dosing history and levels — and the cause was infrastructural, not cognitive. The hospital's standard record interface does not reliably expose true medication administration times, so both systems misaligned vancomycin concentrations with the doses that produced them. Every tool-execution failure in the study occurred in the low-autonomy arm, because a fixed workflow cannot recover when the pharmacokinetic engine returns a transient error, whereas the agent in the loop simply retried. The high-autonomy arm's distinctive failure was the opposite kind — correctly reading the pharmacokinetics and then choosing a clinically inappropriate regimen, an aggressive dose for an underweight elderly patient, an interval inconsistent with the estimated half-life. That is a real finding for anyone building clinical agents: constraining the model's path buys you less than it costs, and the bottleneck is data access and plumbing at least as much as reasoning. Neither configuration is close to unsupervised dosing.

Staying with autonomy for a moment, a consortium built around the Galaxy project posted an argument that agentic data analysis is heading for a reproducibility problem, and backed it with an experiment I find genuinely alarming. They handed thirty independent coding agents — ten each from three vendors — the same published bacterial RNA-sequencing study and asked each to design a replication plan. Twenty-nine of the thirty diverged from the published workflow, and the thirty plans splintered into eighteen distinct pipelines, with eleven incompatible designs across just the five choices that actually change gene counts and p-values: reference genome, ribosomal RNA filtering, aligner, annotations, and whether samples are pooled. The published paper had left two of those choices unstated, which is the ordinary condition of the methods section, and the agents filled the vacuum thirty different ways.

Then they had one agent per vendor actually run its plan, and divergence got worse at execution. All three recovered the headline result, which is the seductive part — but the reference assembly silently changed which toxins were detectable. One assembly missed cytotoxin K entirely because it lacks the locus; the sequenced isolate found it transcribed in every library. The three runs left behind twenty-six unversioned glue scripts and twelve hundred lines of non-portable code. Their proposal is to move agentic analysis onto shared public infrastructure where every step is a containerized tool call with a persistent history. I am less interested in the platform than in the demonstration that a headline finding can replicate while the underlying measurement quietly does not.

A third item on the same theme, from the machine-learning literature. A group at Stevens built a benchmark called STRATA for a task that sits squarely in pharmacovigilance: testing temporal hypotheses over clinical timelines — after a given vaccine, does one adverse event tend to precede another. The point of the benchmark is that it grades every decision on the way to the verdict, not just the verdict. Eight thousand seven hundred hypotheses over vaccine adverse-event reports, each with a gold-standard analysis plan, a gold evidence set — which reports count in the denominator — a gold p-value, and a verdict, all derived from a published codebook and reproduced by a deterministic oracle.

What they find is what they call the verdict–evidence gap, and it is the kind of result that should change how we read any "the model got it right" claim. With a mid-size open model, only about forty percent of the verdicts it got right rested on the correct set of records; strip out the lucky ones and its real trace accuracy is about a fifth. Systems fail predominantly at deciding which records bear on the hypothesis, and accuracy alone conceals that entirely. Their staged approach, which forces the plan, the evidence set, and the test out as separately inspectable artifacts, closes most of the gap and brings a thirty-one-billion-parameter open model to within a hundredth of frontier models. One failure mode is worth savouring: one model scored sixty-nine percent on a family of hypotheses with every evidence set exactly right, because on nearly all the negated hypotheses its plan kept the test tail pointing the same direction after marking the hypothesis negated — so supported and refuted simply swapped. No check in the pipeline compared the tail with the negation. The right conclusion from the wrong arithmetic, and no evidence panel would catch it.

Turning to Parkinson's disease, and to computation of a different kind. A group at Emory posted a framework they call DIMER that tries to connect two things that usually live in different rooms: network-based drug repurposing and target-directed virtual screening, by making both take the same disease gene sets as input. Applied to Parkinson's, they used a hundred and seventy-five genetic risk genes plus around five hundred disease-associated genes partitioned into six mechanism modules — inflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, autophagy, protein aggregation, proteasome dysfunction, oxidative stress — and ran network proximity against drug–target annotations, layered on transcriptional reversal and cell-type-resolved networks. Four hundred and twenty-three repurposing candidates came out, plus a consensus virtual screen of one point eight million compounds against seven Parkinson's-associated proteins that narrowed to twenty-two. Four of the repurposing candidates — polydatin, fedratinib, sorafenib, and venlafaxine — reduced the loss of dopamine neuron markers in human midbrain organoids exposed to the catecholaminergic toxin 6-hydroxydopamine.

Two observations matter more to me than the hit list. Seventy-one percent of the qualifying drug–module associations ran through targets outside the disease gene set, and half the genes shared across their genetic and mechanistic sets have no direct drug-target annotation at all. Restrict discovery to compounds that bind disease proteins directly and you discard most of the available pharmacology. And the honest caveat they state themselves: both established Parkinson's treatments and drugs known to cause parkinsonism came out network-proximal to the same gene sets. Proximity tells you a drug touches the module, not which direction it pushes — which is why the transcriptional reversal layer exists, and its answer depends on which reference transcriptome you pick. One more thing: the canonical symptomatic-treatment targets are absent from all eight gene sets. Genetics does not point at dopamine.

A nice companion from Stanford pathology on the pharmacology side of Parkinson's. Pathogenic LRRK2 variants increase kinase activity, and essentially the entire therapeutic effort has been type I inhibitors that bind the active, closed conformation. The field's standard cellular biomarker for target engagement is phosphorylation at serine 935 — a site LRRK2 does not phosphorylate itself, but which falls when a type I inhibitor forces the conformation that releases the 14-3-3 adapter proteins and exposes the serine to phosphatases. This group screened type II inhibitors — compounds that bind the inactive conformation — and found a clean dissociation. Their lead chemotypes, derived from hesperadin and from GNF-7, potently inhibited the true autophosphorylation site at serine 1292 and inhibited phosphorylation of the physiological Rab substrate, with the two tracking almost linearly, while showing no inhibition of serine 935 up to ten micromolar. More striking, one series went the other way: it increased phosphorylation at the interdomain heterologous sites and restored 14-3-3 binding in variants where that binding is lost.

Why this matters right now is that the most advanced type I LRRK2 inhibitor recently failed in phase 2b, and one hypothesis in the field is that pathogenic variants cause trouble partly through destabilized tertiary structure and lost adapter binding, not purely through excess kinase output — and a type I inhibitor cannot fix that, because the conformation it enforces is the one that releases 14-3-3. These compounds are tools, not candidates, and the paper does not show neuroprotection. But the measurement point is sharp: a standard biomarker that reports conformation rather than catalysis will tell you an active inhibitor is inactive.

Sticking with Parkinson's, and with a pathology marker that may be lying to us from the other direction. Of the six well-characterized familial alpha-synuclein mutations, three accelerate fibril formation in the test tube and three slow it — and A53E, which slows fibrillization, produces an earlier onset and a more aggressive course than most. A group at Lewis and Clark, with collaborators at Oregon Health and Science, looked at this in living neurons rather than in vitro, using fluorescence recovery after photobleaching at presynaptic terminals as a readout of how much of the protein is mobile versus locked into assemblies. Wild-type and the fibril-accelerating E46K variant progressively lose mobility over days, exactly as you would expect from a protein creeping toward aggregated states. A53E stays mobile throughout.

The mechanistic hook is the part with teeth. A53E showed no detectable phosphorylation at serine 129 — the modification that defines Lewy pathology in every neuropathology lab in the world and is the near-universal readout for synucleinopathy burden. When they engineered a phosphomimetic at that residue into A53E, its mobility dropped. So serine-129 phosphorylation is doing causal work on the aggregation trajectory, not merely marking it. The uncomfortable implication is that the most aggressive familial variant is the one that both aggregates least and carries the least of the stain we use to measure aggregation. If soluble oligomers rather than fibrils are the toxic species, slowing fibrillization is not a therapeutic goal, and a phospho-serine-129 count is not a severity measure.

On dopamine pharmacology, a short but genuinely useful methods paper from Vanderbilt. Measuring ligand binding at the D2 receptor still leans on radioligands or on wash-dependent fluorescent assays that disturb equilibrium and preclude kinetics. Resonance energy transfer assays solve that, but they have been limited at D2 by tracer availability, and the existing tracers are agonists, which activate and traffic the receptor you are trying to measure. This group characterized three far-red fluorescent antagonist tracers, all with nanomolar affinity and saturable specific binding, and showed one of them supports equilibrium, kinetic, and competition measurements with assay statistics good enough for compound profiling.

The piece that extends beyond D2 is the bystander configuration. Conventional assays fuse the luminescent donor to the receptor's extracellular amino terminus — a substantial modification of the thing you are characterizing. Instead they anchored the donor to the plasma membrane on its own and let lateral diffusion bring the unmodified receptor and its bound tracer transiently into range. That worked at untagged recombinant D2, with low background from nonspecifically membrane-associated tracer, and then at endogenously expressed receptors in differentiated SH-SY5Y cells, abolished by excess raclopride. One caution the authors raise and I would underline: the tracer also binds D3 with higher affinity than D2, yet gives a smaller signal window there. Signal amplitude in these assays depends on donor abundance, distance and orientation — so differences in window between receptor subtypes must not be read as differences in affinity.

Now a paper I think is the most clinically consequential of the day after the first one, from the Burdakov lab at ETH Zurich, on why we disengage from tasks. They trained mice on a decision task with shifting reward size, extracted latent engagement states and the transitions between them, and asked which neurochemical signal predicts leaving and returning. The answer is two separable axes. Pupil-linked arousal and dopaminergic utility coding each predicted transitions, but along different dimensions — and the pharmacology dissociates them cleanly.

Almorexant, a dual orexin receptor antagonist of the same class now widely prescribed for insomnia, disengaged mice specifically at low reward, and the effect was statistically mediated by pupil-linked arousal with no detectable contribution from dopamine. Semaglutide did the opposite: it reduced the gain with which accumbal dopamine integrates reward history, and that reduction predicted the progressive disengagement it caused, with no contribution from arousal or from orexin. They also separate fast dorsolateral striatal from slow accumbal dopamine codes, each predicting engagement on its own timescale. For two drug classes in enormous and growing use, this gives a mechanistic frame for the motivational complaints that attach to both — and it says the orexin-antagonist version and the incretin version are not the same phenomenon and should not respond to the same countermeasure. The caveat is the obvious species and task one: engagement in a mouse operant task is not apathy in a clinic.

Staying on motivation, a Bordeaux group asked what makes adolescent sugar exposure different from adult exposure, which has been a stubborn observation — motivational deficits follow adolescent overconsumption and not adult. They used a retrovirus to tag and chemogenetically control dentate granule neurons born specifically during adolescence, then tested motivation in adulthood with a progressive-effort lever task. Inhibiting those adolescent-born neurons in adulthood impairs motivation for reward, which establishes the causal role in the first place. Sucrose overconsumption during adolescence then abolishes that function, with selective remodeling of the synaptic inputs to those neurons and disrupted functional integration — rather than a simple loss of neurogenesis. It is a clean demonstration that a developmental window can be targeted by a dietary exposure in a cell-cohort-specific way, and it gives a concrete substrate for the epidemiology linking adolescent sugar intake to later mental health outcomes.

From the Pizzagalli group, a magnetic resonance spectroscopy study that complicates the glutamate story in depression. Using proton and phosphorus spectroscopy at four tesla in the same medial prefrontal voxel, they measured glutamate, glutamine, their sum, and the ratio of oxidized to reduced nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide as an index of redox balance, in seventy-one women across four groups: healthy controls, current depression without childhood sexual abuse, current depression with it, and remitted depression with it. Glutamate and the combined glutamate-plus-glutamine signal were lower in both abuse-exposed groups. Depression without that history did not differ from controls.

Read that carefully, because it inverts the usual framing: the prefrontal glutamatergic deficit tracked the maltreatment exposure, not the diagnosis. Within the exposed women, greater maltreatment severity went with lower glutamine and greater multiplicity of maltreatment types with lower glutamate, specifically in those currently depressed. And the redox measure behaved differently again — a lower baseline ratio predicted greater perceived stress six months later, but only in the depressed women without the abuse history. The groups are small, fourteen to twenty-seven, and this is women only, so I would hold the subgroup interactions loosely. But the headline is a useful corrective to treating glutamatergic abnormality as a feature of depression per se when it may be a feature of a particular developmental route into it.

A method paper now from Stanford radiology, extending something that has been building for a few years. The ideal tool for causal mapping of brain function would be noninvasive, reversible, targetable anywhere, and mechanistically transparent — which describes nothing we currently have. This group had previously made an acoustically activatable liposomal formulation of the local anesthetic ropivacaine and used it for peripheral nerve blocks. Here they give it intravenously and apply transcranial focused ultrasound to the brain: the ultrasound uncages ropivacaine at the focus, and because ropivacaine crosses the intact blood–brain barrier, it anesthetizes the sonicated region without opening the barrier.

Uncaging in mouse primary visual cortex suppressed visually evoked cortical calcium responses for about an hour, with no effect from ultrasound alone, drug alone, or drug plus ultrasound aimed at an off-target prefrontal site. In rats, uncaging over hindlimb somatosensory cortex raised mechanical withdrawal thresholds within fifteen minutes in the contralateral hindlimb only, with the ipsilateral limb near baseline. No acute histologic injury, no open-field behavioral abnormality. The mechanism is the attraction here — sodium channel blockade is about as well understood as a mechanism of inactivation gets, which is more than can be said for ultrasonic neuromodulation on its own. Functionally this is a noninvasive, repeatable, reversible version of the Wada test, and if it translates it changes what a pharmacological test lesion costs.

I will close with something lighter, and the kind of paper that makes you happy about the shape of biology. A Harvard group resolved the biosynthetic route to harmala alkaloids — the beta-carbolines that inhibit monoamine oxidase and make ayahuasca pharmacologically possible — in both Syrian rue and the ayahuasca vine. These two plants sit in lineages that diverged more than a hundred million years ago on different continents, and both ended up making the same monoamine oxidase inhibitors, and both were independently adopted by human cultures for the same purposes.

The convergence runs deeper than the molecules. The committed enzyme in each lineage is a tryptamine six-hydroxylase, and in each lineage it arose independently by duplication and neofunctionalization of tryptamine five-hydroxylase — the enzyme that makes serotonin and melatonin, which is conserved across the angiosperms. In the ayahuasca vine the new gene still sits fifty kilobases from its functional parent. Moving the hydroxyl one position around the ring is the difference between the serotonin pathway and a class of psychoactive monoamine oxidase inhibitors, and the shift is small: swap four amino acids back to the ancestral residues and both enzymes revert completely to five-hydroxylase activity — though, nicely, it is a different set of four substitutions in each plant. Two lineages, a hundred million years apart, pulled the same lever on the same ancestral enzyme by different means.
