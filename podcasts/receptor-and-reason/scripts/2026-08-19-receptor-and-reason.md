## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. I'm your host, and this is your daily tour through CNS pharmacology, psychiatric and neurology drug discovery, and the computational tools closing in on both. Eleven items today, and if there's a thread running through the first half it's a question about specificity — not whether a drug works, but which piece of it you're actually getting, and whether the good part can be pulled apart from the bad part. The second half moves outward: getting molecules into the brain, finding them in the first place, and predicting whether they'll survive a trial.

We open with a preprint from Daniel Brierley's group at University College London that does the cleanest job I've seen of asking whether semaglutide's therapeutic effects and its nausea are separable at the circuit level. The framing is familiar: GLP-1 receptor agonists are extraordinarily effective, and roughly the same brainstem territory — the dorsal vagal complex, containing the area postrema and the nucleus tractus solitarius — has been implicated in both the weight loss and the vomiting. The received account, built on Cre-line manipulation of whole receptor-defined populations, is that area postrema GLP-1 receptor neurons drive satiation and aversion while nucleus tractus solitarius receptor neurons drive satiety without aversion. The move here is to stop assuming that the neurons expressing the receptor are the neurons the drug actually recruits. They used activity-dependent genetic tagging — the TRAP system, which permanently labels neurons that were active during a defined window — with a moderate sixty-microgram-per-kilogram dose of semaglutide as the tagging stimulus. Call it Sema-TRAP. Two findings make the paper. First, in the area postrema, semaglutide recruits mostly GLP-1 receptor-expressing neurons, as expected. But in the nucleus tractus solitarius, the large population it recruits mostly does not express the receptor at all — the fraction of receptor-positive nucleus tractus solitarius neurons that got activated went from ten percent at baseline to nineteen percent, which is close to nothing. So most of the nucleus tractus solitarius response to the drug is indirect. Second, and this is the elegant part: chemogenetically reactivating those tagged nucleus tractus solitarius neurons on their own reproduced satiation, reproduced conditioned flavor avoidance at the same magnitude as the drug itself, and suppressed motivation to work for a high-fat high-sugar diet in a progressive ratio task — and then, when they knocked down GLP-1 receptor expression specifically in the area postrema before tagging, all three of those effects vanished, while semaglutide's effect on satiety and its effect on body weight survived. So there's an area postrema receptor to nucleus tractus solitarius pathway that bundles satiation, nausea, and reward suppression together, and a separate, still-unidentified mechanism that carries satiety and a good chunk of the weight loss. The implication for drug design is uncomfortable but useful. If you want the reward-suppressing effects — the ones people are excited about for alcohol and other substance use disorders — you may be tethered to the nausea, since they run through the same upstream receptor. Satiety is the arm that looks separable. The authors are careful to say they can't yet tell whether reward suppression and aversion are parallel outputs or whether reduced motivation is simply what a negative valence state does to the value of food, and that's the experiment that decides how tunable this is.

Staying on reward but moving to humans and to dopamine, there's a trial in Neuropsychopharmacology giving a single hundred-milligram dose of amisulpride — at that low dose acting mainly as a presynaptic autoreceptor antagonist, so net dopamine-enhancing — or placebo to a hundred and fifteen people, depressed and healthy, before scanning them on a social incentive delay task where the reward is a smiling face. The headline is a treatment effect in the left ventral tegmental area during reward anticipation, and essentially nothing in the ventral striatum, which is where the field's priors say the anticipation signal lives. The depressed group showed the expected blunting in the right putamen, but it didn't survive correction. I'd read this as a mostly negative result presented politely: a single low dose moved the dopaminergic source nucleus and did not propagate to the terminal fields where anhedonia is supposed to be indexed, in a sample whose mean Beck score was sixteen — mild. The lesson isn't about dopamine, it's about design. A one-shot low dose in mildly depressed volunteers with no crossover is not a fair test of a dopaminergic hypothesis of anhedonia, and the striatal null shouldn't be read as evidence against it.

A better-designed pharmacological study of the same neurotransmitter systems comes from Joe Kable's group at Penn, and it's preregistered, double-blind, placebo-controlled, and within-subject crossover across three visits. The question is persistence — how long you'll keep waiting for a reward that arrives at an uncertain time. They frame this as structurally the same problem as foraging, where the standing theory is that dopamine tracks the opportunity cost of time and pushes you to leave, while serotonin promotes staying. So they gave forty-two people L-dopa or escitalopram or placebo and measured willingness to wait. The serotonin prediction held: a single dose of the SSRI made people wait longer, which as far as I know is the first demonstration of that in humans, and it lines up with a decade of rodent and macaque work. The dopamine prediction did not — L-dopa did nothing to persistence in the preregistered analysis. Two wrinkles worth carrying. The SSRI effect was moderated by state anxiety, showing up only in people who didn't get more anxious after dosing — an obvious confound to design around, given how commonly acute SSRIs make people jittery. And in exploratory analysis controlling for age, L-dopa reduced persistence and increased exploration in younger participants and did the opposite in older ones, an inverted-U story that everyone in dopamine pharmacology nods at and almost nobody powers for. Their sample topped out at forty-four, so treat that as a hypothesis, not a finding.

Serotonin again, but at a scale nobody has attempted before. Natasha Mason's group at Maastricht ran an EEG hyperscanning study in twenty-five romantic couples who took fifty micrograms of LSD together — randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, crossover, with both partners recorded simultaneously. LSD broadly raised feelings of connectedness, closeness, love, trust, and being in sync, and lowered loneliness. The detail I find most interesting is that this affiliative shift came uncoupled from the pharmacokinetics: subjective drug intensity rose and fell with plasma concentration, while connectedness rose and stayed elevated as the drug washed out. On the neural side, LSD increased inter-brain theta amplitude-envelope coupling across four region pairs during shared rest. The reason to take this seriously rather than filing it under pretty-picture neuroscience is the control structure. They compared real couples against surrogate dyads built by pairing people across different couples in the same session and drug condition, which rules out the trivial explanation that LSD just makes everyone's EEG look alike; all four region pairs beat that surrogate under drug and only one did under placebo. And a temporal-shift surrogate confirmed the coupling depended on moment-to-moment alignment rather than shared slow drift. Two caveats. The effect appeared during rest and not during the structured tasks — affective touch and joint drawing — which the authors attribute to task demands pulling attention away from each other, but which also means the positive result came from the one condition where behavior wasn't controlled. And the correlation between synchrony and felt connectedness, the bit that makes the story, did not survive correction. Still: this is the first demonstration that a psychedelic changes brain-to-brain coupling between people, and given how much of psychedelic-assisted therapy rests on the therapeutic relationship, a measurable dyadic signal is more than a curiosity.

From how a drug works to why it doesn't. There's a paper in Molecular Psychiatry that I think is the most mechanistically satisfying item this week, on lithium non-response in bipolar disorder. Only about a third of patients are excellent lithium responders and we have very little idea why. The group started from human genetics: a curated core clock gene set was significantly associated with lithium response and — importantly — not with bipolar risk itself, with BHLHE41 the top gene-based hit. BHLHE41, also called DEC2 or SHARP1, is a clock output gene, best known from the short-sleep variant, rather than a component of the core transcription-translation loop. Then they took it to mice, using a double knockout of BHLHE40 and 41 and chronic lithium in the drinking water. The knockouts were, in a specific sense, lithium non-responders: lithium improved contextual fear memory and serial reversal learning in wild types and did nothing in the mutants, while lithium's blunt suppression of locomotor activity worked fine in both. So it's not that lithium isn't getting in. The cellular story is where it gets convincing. In wild-type cortical neurons, lithium reduces excitability, and in the mutants it doesn't. In slices, lithium reduces instantaneous firing frequency in wild types and not in mutants, and the genotype-by-treatment interactions on short-term and long-term potentiation are enormous. Single-nucleus sequencing of anterior cingulate localizes the lithium-responsive transcriptional program to layer two-three excitatory neurons, and it's a coherent program: up on mitochondrial ATP synthesis and ribosomes, down on postsynaptic scaffolds and cation channel genes — neurexins, neuroligins, Kcnip4, Cacnb2. And a heterozygous BMAL1 deletion, which carries bipolar risk, responded to lithium normally, so this isn't a generic circadian story. What you get is a mouse in which lithium reaches the brain and fails to produce its cellular signature — exactly the model you'd want for testing what to give a non-responder. The transcriptomics are underpowered at three per condition and nobody has shown BHLHE41 variants predict response prospectively in patients, but the genetics-to-slice-to-circuit chain is unusually complete.

Sticking with mechanism specificity, a preprint from Diego Pafundo's group at the University of Buenos Aires takes on excitation-inhibition balance, which the field treats as a scalar property of a neuron. They used mice with NMDA receptors deleted early from corticolimbic GABAergic interneurons — the standard hypofunction model — and asked whether the resulting imbalance in prefrontal pyramidal neurons is global or input-specific. Using pathway-specific labeling and optogenetic circuit mapping, they find the imbalance is confined to ventral hippocampal inputs; callosal inputs from the other hemisphere are untouched. Structurally it's restricted to hippocampal synapses on apical, not basal, dendrites. Functionally the excitation-shifted ratio comes from a failure to recruit feedforward inhibition — fast-spiking interneurons specifically lose their preferential drive from hippocampal inputs. And the functional consequence is a loss of temporal control: blocking GABA-A receptors prolongs hippocampally evoked potentials in wild types but not in mutants, because the inhibition that was doing the shortening is already gone. Presynaptic release dynamics were unchanged, so this isn't a terminal problem. Yesterday we covered the SETD1A model, where hippocampal-prefrontal theta synchrony was intact and the deficit sat in the thalamic reuniens pathway. Put these side by side and the message arrives from two directions: the useful unit of analysis is the afferent pathway, not the region, and a global excitation-inhibition number can average away the entire phenotype.

Now to the delivery problem, with a substantial preprint from Ionis on getting oligonucleotides across the blood-brain barrier. The vehicle is a monovalent VHH-Fc fusion — a llama single-domain antibody fused to an Fc, binding transferrin receptor one — carrying either antisense oligonucleotides or siRNA. Two things distinguish this from the crowded transferrin-receptor field. The binding site, confirmed by cryo-EM, spans the interface between the two receptor monomers and does not overlap the transferrin site, and consistent with that, systemic dosing didn't move serum iron, transferrin, hemoglobin, or red cell indices. And they did the engineering sweep properly. Affinity has a sweet spot rather than a maximum — best cortical knockdown came from conjugates in the ten-to-a-hundred-nanomolar range, with the sub-nanomolar binders performing worse in the brain while working fine in muscle and heart. Drug-antibody ratio of two was worse in the central nervous system than a ratio of one, and better in liver, which is the tradeoff you'd predict but hadn't been shown for brain. Conjugation site mattered a lot for antisense oligonucleotides — C-terminal attachment gave almost nothing while position two thirty-nine in the CH2 domain cleared fifty percent knockdown in cortex and spinal cord — and linker chemistry mattered essentially not at all across seven variants. The translational payoff is MAPT knockdown across frontal and temporal cortex, hippocampus, and amygdala in non-human primates, with subcutaneous dosing matching intravenous. Tau-lowering by systemic injection rather than intrathecal delivery would meaningfully change what's clinically feasible. They also saw knockdown in dorsal root ganglia and peripheral nerve, which opens the pain and neuropathy space with the same construct.

Two computational items, and then one on how we decide what to trust. The first is from Yinglong Miao's lab at UNC Chapel Hill, benchmarking docking protocols for GPCR allosteric modulators. The premise is that allosteric pockets are shallow, transient, and poorly represented by whatever single conformation happens to be in the Protein Data Bank, so structure-based screening for allosteric modulators has no validated protocol. They generated conformational ensembles with Gaussian accelerated molecular dynamics for four class A receptors — muscarinic M2 and M4, beta-2 adrenergic, and the chemokine receptor CCR2 — and screened known modulators against property-matched decoys with four programs. Ensemble docking beat single-structure docking at every target under at least one program, and Glide improved early recovery at all four, in one case placing nearly all known actives in the top half percent. The genuinely useful finding is a rule for how to re-rank an ensemble, and it's keyed to the free-energy landscape: when sampling is dominated by one low-energy state, rank by the best binding energy across the ensemble; when the landscape is flat and multi-populated, rank by the average. Take the union of both at the earliest percentiles and you maximize scaffold diversity, because different programs have strong and reproducible scaffold biases. The result people will argue about is Boltz-2, the deep-learning co-folding and affinity model, which was almost insensitive to the templates it was given — it collapsed back to the cryo-EM ground state no matter what conformation you handed it, making ensembles pointless for it — and underperformed conventional docking, while recovering the most chemically diverse set of actives. Complementary, not a replacement, at least for allosteric sites.

The second computational item is about trial outcomes, and it lands a methodological punch I want to flag for anyone reading this literature. The model is called MERIT, and it predicts whether a trial will fail on efficacy or on safety using only pre-trial biology — tissue-specific binding, pathway engagement, target-disease genetic support, off-target liabilities, pharmacokinetics — across seven hundred and fifty-three small molecules and three thousand one hundred and thirty-three trials. Reported area-under-the-curve in this field runs from about zero point seven two to zero point eight eight, but those numbers come from splits where the same compound sits in both training and test, so a model can score well by memorizing which drugs succeed. The authors name this the memorization gap and measure it. Holding out compounds by structure, a leading prior method drops to zero point six three; MERIT gets zero point seven seven; and the highest published figure in the field, reproduced under a fair split, falls from zero point eight eight to zero point seven five and turns out to ride on trial registration metadata — sponsor, trial size, program size — rather than biology. That finding alone is worth the paper. The other half is nicer. Because the model scores mechanism-disease fit and never sees the compound's identity, you can run it backward: for a drug that failed, ask which disease its mechanism does fit. Across sixty compounds that failed one indication and are approved for another, it ranked the approved indication higher in eighty-three percent, recovering tamoxifen to breast cancer and imatinib to chronic myeloid leukemia from mechanism alone. And trained on pre-2016 data, it anticipated later repositionings — selinexor to myeloma, siponimod to multiple sclerosis — though with only eight to twelve confirmed successes per cutoff, that's corroboration, not a headline. One systematic error they own: host-directed drugs repurposed against COVID are badly over-credited.

Our agentic AI item today is on arXiv and it's a single-author paper — Travis Smith, working independently from the Azores — called The Little Scientist, and I like it because it argues against the dominant paradigm and then backs it up. The dominant paradigm is evolutionary: FunSearch and AlphaEvolve mutate code populations guided by a scalar fitness score. The claim here is that a scalar score is nearly information-free about where to search next, which is why those systems need island models and enormous sample counts to maintain diversity. The alternative is to force the agent through the actual scientific method: write a hypothesis, write a numeric prediction to a file before you write any code, implement, evaluate, and then reconcile the prediction against the result in the next iteration. The author is blunt that this requires what he calls walking the dog on a short leash, because language model agents left to themselves write the hypothesis after the fact and rationalize surprises. The environment returns per-instance diagnostics rather than one number, so the agent can see that a change improved forty benchmark cases and broke twelve, and that the twelve share a structural feature. And when the search plateaus, a second agent — the Kuhn agent, after Thomas Kuhn — proposes a paradigm-level reframing paired with a cross-disciplinary analogy, to push the model into a different region of its own latent space. Results: on DNA motif discovery it wrote an algorithm from scratch that beats STREME, the MEME Suite default, across a hundred and thirty-two ENCODE transcription factors, mean area-under-curve zero point eight four two versus zero point eight zero three, and runs eleven times faster. On protein fitness prediction it produced an ensemble calibration strategy that currently ranks first on the ProteinGym zero-shot leaderboard across all five official metrics, beating the second-place model by zero point zero three three mean Spearman across two hundred and seventeen assays. Total cost: two hundred and twenty-nine million tokens on one virtual machine with no GPUs, against FunSearch's reported two million programs for a single instance. In both cases the Kuhn agent supplied the reframing that everything else was built on. The author is candid about what's missing — two case studies, no wet-lab validation, no controlled ablation, because isolating each component would take billions of tokens. The strongest evidence for his central claim is accidental: at one point the per-instance diagnostic output was silently deleted, the search stalled, and restoring it restarted progress. One natural experiment, not an ablation, but it's the right kind of evidence for the right claim.

We'll close on something plainer. A meta-analysis in Biological Psychiatry pools a hundred and eighty-one samples from a hundred and forty-nine studies — eighteen thousand cannabis users, fifty-three thousand non-users — on age at onset of psychosis. Cannabis use is associated with onset two and a half years earlier, which is roughly the number the field has been quoting for a decade. The new part is the moderator. In samples whose mean onset was under twenty, there was no difference at all. In the twenty-to-twenty-five band it was one point six years, twenty-five to thirty was four point four years, and thirty and above was six point three years — a strong, statistically independent gradient. The deflationary reading is regression to the mean, and I'd want that ruled out. The substantive reading is more interesting: in people whose illness was going to arrive early anyway — presumably the high-liability group — cannabis adds nothing, because the ceiling is already reached, whereas in later-onset cases the exposure has room to move the trajectory. That's a straightforward risk-modification model, and it argues that the population where prevention messaging would change something is not the one everyone targets.
