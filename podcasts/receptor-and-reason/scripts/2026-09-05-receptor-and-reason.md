## Script

Welcome back to Receptor and Reason. I'm your host, and this is your daily pass through CNS pharmacology, psychiatric and neurology drug discovery, and the computational machinery closing in on both. Nine items today, and the thread running through them is the instrument rather than the molecule. Almost every paper on the pile turns out to be about what a readout actually measures — a radioligand, an evoked current, a postmortem assay, a benchmark score — and in several cases the readout is doing more of the work than anyone intended.

We open with the paper that makes that case most directly: a meta-analysis in Molecular Psychiatry of ninety-two carbon-eleven raclopride positron emission tomography studies, sixteen hundred and forty participants, and the first attempt to put every intervention class on a common axis. The assay is thirty-five years old and still the field's workhorse — endogenous dopamine competes with the radioligand at D2 and D3 receptors, so a drop in binding potential is read as dopamine release. What nobody had done is ask how big that drop is for amphetamine versus a cognitive task versus alcohol, in the same units.

The answers are not subtle. Amphetamine drops striatal binding by ten point nine percent, oral methylphenidate by eleven point five, ketamine by nine point eight, alcohol by four point eight, and cognitive challenges by two point seven — so a psychostimulant produces roughly four times the signal of a demanding task and more than twice that of alcohol. Regionally, psychostimulants spare the caudate, five point nine percent for amphetamine there against twelve point two in putamen, while alcohol is the mirror image, preferentially ventral. And route matters more than formulation: intravenous methylphenidate produces roughly twice the striatal signal of oral.

The finding that should change practice is the negative one. Cognitive tasks with a reward and cognitive tasks without a reward produced statistically indistinguishable dopamine release — three point zero versus two point two percent in whole striatum, two point nine versus two point five in ventral striatum, every comparison above point one. Twenty years of ventral striatal reward imaging with this tracer has been running on an assay that cannot separate reward from task engagement and attentional load. Combine that with the effect-size table and you get a second, more uncomfortable point the authors make explicitly: studies of cognitive and alcohol challenges have often enrolled the same number of subjects as amphetamine studies while chasing an effect a quarter the size. That is a field-wide power problem hiding inside a methods section, and the central calibration stands even though the caudate result is partly vulnerable to partial volume and motion artefact and nearly all intravenous methylphenidate data come from a single group.

Stay with the tracer, because a preprint from the Turku PET Centre identifies a variable the meta-analysis could not control for: what time it was when you scanned. Two historical cohorts of healthy volunteers — a hundred eighty-eight scanned with carbon-eleven carfentanil for mu-opioid receptors, a hundred eighty-four with raclopride for D2 — were reanalysed with time of scan as the predictor and daylength and trait anxiety as modifiers. The two systems move in opposite directions across the day: mu-opioid availability rises from morning to afternoon across medial and dorsolateral frontal cortex, medial temporal lobe and putamen, while D2 availability falls in bilateral putamen and left nucleus accumbens, with caudate at trend.

Availability is a composite — receptor density, affinity and occupancy by endogenous ligand all collapse into one number — so a diurnal swing could be trafficking or tonic transmitter tone, and this design cannot separate them. But that ambiguity is exactly why it matters for the assay. Every dopamine-release study computes a difference between two scans and assumes the baseline is stationary. The D2 slope here is small — a few thousandths of a binding-potential unit per hour in putamen, well under a percent — but the cognitive-task effects we just enumerated sit at two point seven percent in total, so a scan schedule that differs systematically between two groups is not obviously negligible.

The opioid arm is the more interesting biology. The diurnal pattern interacted with seasonal daylength, and it ran in a direction I did not expect: the morning-to-afternoon rise was clearest under long daylength and flattened under short days. In the hundred participants who had trait-anxiety measures it interacted with anxiety as well, the rhythm most visible in the least anxious and blunted in the most anxious, the two interaction maps overlapping in anterior cingulate, orbitofrontal and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, insula and putamen. The seasonal interaction came back null for dopamine, and anxiety was only ever tested against the opioid arm. So a flattened diurnal opioid rhythm looks like a correlate of anxious temperament. The design is cross-sectional and retrospective — different people scanned at different times, so a between-subjects contrast stands in for a within-subject rhythm, and confounding by who volunteers for an eight a.m. slot is not excluded. Somebody should now scan the same people twice.

From receptor availability to receptor state, and a paper in Nature Neuroscience that is the quietly most consequential piece of receptor pharmacology this week. The standard model of an excitatory synapse treats the AMPA receptor as a fixed-conductance element: glutamate arrives, channels open, current scales with how many opened. This group asks what happens when glutamate is not saturating, and the answer is that how many of the four subunits are liganded determines which conductance state the channel visits. Fewer liganded subunits means smaller unitary conductance, not merely fewer open channels.

They show it in cerebellar molecular layer interneurons by comparing two inputs onto the same cell. Parallel fibre and climbing fibre synapses produce essentially the same current — minus three hundred fifty-one picoamps versus minus three hundred four, nowhere near significance — and yet unitary conductance is twenty-three point one picosiemens at the parallel fibre synapse and twelve point four at the climbing fibre, with the same split preserved when desensitization is blocked with cyclothiazide. Kynurenic acid, a low-affinity competitive antagonist whose block is relieved by high glutamate, inhibits the climbing fibre current more than the parallel fibre current, seventy-one percent against fifty-one, confirming the climbing fibre synapse simply sees less glutamate. Rectification index tracks it too — point four one at the parallel fibre synapse against point seven two at the climbing fibre — because partially occupied receptors interact differently with the intracellular polyamine block.

Sit with what that does to the interpretation of an evoked current. Two synapses with identical amplitudes are running fundamentally different receptor populations, and the difference will surface as differential sensitivity to any drug whose binding depends on channel state — including the polyamine-site blockers used to assay calcium-permeable AMPA receptors. Their own data make the point: sensitivity to the polyamine antagonist NASPM tracked occupancy rather than current size. If you have ever inferred subunit composition from a rectification measurement, this paper says that inference is confounded by the glutamate transient. It is one cell type, and the kinetic scheme assumes independent binding across subunits, so the quantitative model will move. The qualitative claim — that concentration selects conductance state — will not.

Which brings us to the co-agonist site, and a preprint from Jean-Pierre Mothet's group at Paris-Saclay on fast-spiking parvalbumin interneurons in the prelimbic prefrontal cortex of adolescent mice. NMDA receptor hypofunction on these interneurons is the load-bearing assumption behind a large fraction of schizophrenia pharmacology, and yet almost nothing was known about which co-agonist — D-serine or glycine — actually occupies the GluN1 site there.

First they establish the receptors are present at all, which has been genuinely contested in mature animals: intracellular MK-801 reduces interneuron excitability on its own, implying a tonically active population; the NMDA-to-AMPA ratio at these synapses is about one; and both the GluN2C/D negative allosteric modulator NAB-14 and the GluN2B blocker Ro 25-6981 cut the current, so these are GluN1-GluN2B-GluN2D receptors. Then the co-agonist experiments, done with enzymatic scavengers rather than antagonists — a D-amino acid oxidase to strip D-serine, a glycine oxidase to strip glycine. Stripping D-serine cuts the NMDA-to-AMPA ratio by about forty percent and speeds the rise time. Stripping glycine does nothing. Blocking GlyT1 to raise ambient glycine does potentiate the current, so glycine can act there — it is simply held below the effective concentration by transport.

The elegant part is what activity regime does to that. Trains at two hertz depend on D-serine; at twenty and fifty hertz the dependence disappears, meaning glycine gets recruited as the synapse works harder. And in serine racemase knockouts, the standing genetic model of NMDA receptor hypofunction, basal synaptic transmission looks entirely normal — same current, same ratio — because glycine has taken over as a spare co-agonist, which they prove by showing that glycine oxidase, inert in wild-types, now cuts the current by sixty percent. What glycine cannot rescue is plasticity: high-frequency stimulation induces robust long-term potentiation in wild-types and the knockouts lose it, though the short-term potentiation ten minutes after the tetanus survives in both, and rate tuning breaks at twenty hertz.

The therapeutic reading is uncomfortable for the obvious drug class. If glycine can substitute at rest but not for plasticity, then GlyT1 inhibitors — the sarcosine-and-bitopertin lineage that keeps failing in schizophrenia trials — may be topping up a co-agonist that is competent for tonic function and incompetent for the thing you actually want back. And if you assay hypofunction by measuring basal transmission, the knockout looks normal. One species, one cortical area, one developmental window, and some personal-communication immunogold data doing load-bearing work on receptor localization. But it reframes what hypofunction is even supposed to mean.

Then a systematic review that reads like a warning label: a group mapping every human postmortem study of metabotropic glutamate receptors across neuropsychiatric disease. Five hundred thirty-two records screened, fifty-seven reports included, spanning schizophrenia, major depression, Alzheimer's, autism, fragile X and alcohol use disorder. Their own summary is that most analyses yielded null findings and there is no uniform or disorder-specific molecular signature.

I would push it harder than they do. Inside schizophrenia alone, where twenty-four studies exist: GRM5 transcript unchanged in prefrontal cortex, hippocampus, thalamus and nucleus accumbens, but increased in one small study of Brodmann area eleven; mGluR5 protein unchanged nearly everywhere it was measured, yet total mGluR5 in CA1 increased by forty-two percent alongside its trafficking partners Norbin and Tamalin — while mGluR1a in the same tissue series fell by thirty-three percent. Dimeric mGluR5 up in dorsolateral prefrontal cortex in one study, binding unchanged in an overlapping cohort. Group two is no better: mGluR3 protein reduced in two prefrontal studies, unchanged in others, transcript null nearly everywhere, and one study finds mGluR2 reduced regardless of antipsychotic exposure while the mGluR3 reduction was confined to unmedicated cases.

The authors' own explanation is the useful one, and it is a measurement argument rather than a biology argument: transcript abundance, total protein, receptor assembly, subcellular localization and ligand binding are five different quantities, and a study measuring one of them and reporting a null has not tested the same hypothesis as a study measuring another. mGluR5 can be unchanged in abundance and disturbed in dimerization and scaffolding at the same time. For anyone with an mGluR2/3 or mGluR5 program, the honest posture is that postmortem tissue has not delivered a target-engagement rationale, and probably cannot at the resolution most of these studies used.

A structural paper now, about where a drug's selectivity actually lives. Xiangyu Liu's group at Tsinghua has what they believe are the first cryo-EM structures of G protein-coupled receptors bound to endogenous inverse agonists. The melanocortin system is the textbook case: agouti signalling protein suppresses constitutive activity of the melanocortin-1 receptor to switch pigment synthesis from eumelanin to pheomelanin, and agouti-related protein suppresses melanocortin-4 tone in hypothalamic neurons to drive feeding. Both carry the same arginine-phenylalanine-phenylalanine pharmacophore on an inhibitor cystine knot — the disulfide scaffold you find in conotoxins and spider venoms — and yet agouti-related protein barely touches melanocortin-1. Nobody knew why.

They solved the agouti-signalling-protein complex with melanocortin-1 at two point nine four angstroms, using a conformation-specific nanobody raised in alpacas as a structural chaperone, and the agouti-related-protein complex with melanocortin-4 alongside it. The shared mechanism is nicely physical: the cysteine-rich domain caps the extracellular vestibule as a molecular cork, and the arginine of the pharmacophore drops into the conserved acidic triad and substitutes for the calcium ion that normally coordinates agonist binding, occupying the pocket without triggering the microswitch rearrangements that activate the receptor. Silencing is imposed from above, through a remodelling of the extracellular surface centred on transmembrane helix three.

Then the selectivity result, which is the part with drug-discovery consequences. Selectivity is not in the orthosteric pocket, which is nearly identical across subtypes and has defeated subtype-selective medicinal chemistry for years. It is a clasp formed by the agouti-signalling-protein carboxyl-terminal loop onto extracellular loop one and transmembrane helix two, a region that diverges between subtypes. They then did the experiment that makes it stick: swap extracellular loop one of melanocortin-1 for the melanocortin-4 sequence and their new nanobody antagonist stops working; graft the melanocortin-1 loop onto melanocortin-4 and the previously insensitive receptor becomes inhibitable. A single alanine at the top of transmembrane three, unique to melanocortin-1 where the other subtypes carry aspartate or glutamine, is the selectivity hotspot — mutate it either way and the nanobody dies. With setmelanotide approved and a crowded melanocortin-4 obesity field behind it, the message is that the pocket everyone models is the wrong place to look for subtype selectivity, and the receptor periphery is druggable if you bring a modality large enough to engage it.

Into the clinic, and the trial a lot of people have been waiting for. The Ket-BD study in JAMA Psychiatry is a double-blind, midazolam-controlled randomized trial of intravenous ketamine in treatment-resistant bipolar depression, run across three Ontario sites from July twenty twenty-two to November twenty twenty-five. Sixty-eight adults with bipolar one or two, a current moderate-to-severe episode and at least two failed evidence-based trials, randomized one to one to four flexibly dosed forty-minute infusions over two weeks at point five to point seven five milligrams per kilogram, or midazolam, on top of a stable mood stabilizer or antipsychotic. Sixty-three reached the day-fourteen primary endpoint.

The result is positive and the effect size is respectable: a between-group difference of seven point three points on the Montgomery-Asberg scale, confidence interval two point five to twelve, Cohen's d of point seven. No mania, no hypomania, no psychosis, no suicide attempts in either arm; one case of subthreshold mixed features per group. That safety line is the substantive contribution, because the reason bipolar depression has been carved out of the ketamine and esketamine evidence base is precisely a switch risk this trial did not observe. Two caveats. Midazolam is the right active control and still an imperfect one — forty-seven percent of participants correctly guessed their assignment after the first infusion. And the endpoint is day fourteen, at the end of dosing, which tells you nothing about durability, which is the actual clinical problem with serial ketamine. Sixty-three patients is a proof of efficacy, not a practice change, but it is the first randomized evidence of its kind in this population and it moves the prior.

Staying with sleep and psychiatry, a network meta-analysis in the International Journal of Neuropsychopharmacology compares two dual orexin receptor antagonists head to head — vornorexant against lemborexant — across seven randomized trials and two thousand eight hundred and five participants, mean age fifty-five, sixty-nine percent female. On subjective time to sleep onset at week two, all four active arms beat placebo: lemborexant five milligrams twelve minutes faster, lemborexant ten fifteen minutes, vornorexant five eleven minutes, vornorexant ten just under ten.

Notice the shape of that. Lemborexant scales with dose and vornorexant does not; the ten-milligram vornorexant arm is if anything slightly worse than five, and it fails to separate from placebo on subjective wake after sleep onset at either week. Meanwhile the tolerability signal runs the other way — both lemborexant doses carried more adverse events and more somnolence than placebo, while neither vornorexant dose differed from placebo on any safety outcome. So within one mechanism, at the same target, you get a genuine efficacy-tolerability trade and a dose-response curve that is flat for one compound and not the other. Two structural caveats: this is indirect comparison across trials that never randomized patients against each other, and the endpoints are sleep-diary self-report rather than polysomnography, which systematically flatters hypnotics. Still, if you prescribe in this class, the useful takeaway is that the choice is not orexin antagonist versus not, it is dose within molecule.

One computational item to close, and it is a benchmark story with a randomized controlled trial attached, which is rare enough to be worth your time. The ALLIANCE trial, on medRxiv from Johannes Knitza's group in Marburg with Hamburg and the Charité, is the first randomized trial of a medically certified large language model clinical decision support system in any specialty. Eighty-two physicians across seven hospitals in two countries, twenty-one of them rheumatologists, randomized to conventional resources plus the certified system or conventional resources alone, each working three rheumatology vignettes before and after assistance.

The primary endpoint is flat. Top-one diagnostic accuracy went from twenty-two point two to thirty-three point three percent with the model, and from twenty-three point three to thirty-five percent without it — adjusted odds ratio point nine nine, p equals point nine seven nine. Top-three accuracy, diagnostic reasoning score and confidence all likewise failed to separate. What did move was time: ninety-four seconds per assisted case with the model against two hundred six without, plus significantly higher ratings for information timeliness and perceived support quality.

Two exploratory numbers are the ones I would put in front of anyone deploying this. First, the standalone system scored thirty-three point three percent on its own, beating unassisted non-rheumatologists at twenty-one point three — and after assistance, physicians in both arms landed at almost exactly the model's own number, around thirty-four percent. The tool did not lift clinicians past itself; it pulled them to it. Second, over-reliance — the fraction of incorrect model suggestions physicians accepted into a wrong final answer — was point nine five, against under-reliance of point one zero. Calibration got worse in both arms as accuracy rose, with overconfidence climbing from sixteen to twenty-four percent in the intervention group. Trust in the system was reported at sixty-four percent, but only thirty-six percent of users agreed that mistakes could be corrected easily. The measurement lesson is the one this whole episode keeps circling: benchmark accuracy was not the quantity that determined clinical value, and the endpoint that did move was a secondary outcome.
