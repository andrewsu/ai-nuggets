# Felzartamab — anti-CD38 antibody clears its first global approval, in China for myeloma; Biogen's real bet is antibody-driven kidney disease

Paper link: https://endpoints.news/biogens-monoclonal-antibody-felzartamab-wins-first-global-approval-in-china/

## Script

Today's pick isn't a data readout. It's an approval that tells you more about a strategy than about a drug. China's regulator has approved felzartamab — a humanized anti-CD38 monoclonal antibody — for second-line multiple myeloma, given with lenalidomide and dexamethasone. It's sold there as Jingfei, made by TJ Biopharma in Hangzhou, and it's China's first domestically produced anti-CD38 antibody for myeloma. Here's the first thing worth noticing, and the reason it caught my eye for your brief: this is felzartamab's very first approval anywhere in the world.

For a myeloma drug, that's a strange place to make your debut. Anti-CD38 is not new ground. Daratumumab — Darzalex — owns this class: something like nine out of ten of every anti-CD38 dose used globally, around seven billion dollars a year. Felzartamab is walking into a mature, crowded field as roughly the fourth entrant, and its pitch is convenience and cost, not novelty. TJ Biopharma reported efficacy and safety comparable to daratumumab in the lenalidomide-dexamethasone combination, but with an infusion of about an hour and a half instead of the up-to-seven-hour intravenous slog of the original daratumumab, plus a domestic-sourcing price edge inside China's procurement system. The honest caveat is that daratumumab's subcutaneous version already cuts dosing to three-to-five minutes, so that convenience advantage is thinner than it first looks. Analysts see felzartamab taking maybe fifteen percent of a fairly small Chinese myeloma market by the end of the decade. On its own terms, this is a validation event, not a revenue event.

So why should you care? Because of who owns the rest of the molecule, and why they bought it. Outside greater China, felzartamab belongs to Biogen — and Biogen did not pay up for a fourth-place myeloma antibody. It paid over a billion dollars up front, back in 2024, to acquire Human Immunology Biosciences, and what it wanted was a plasma-cell-depleting antibody pointed at antibody-driven kidney disease. It's the same mechanism you already know from myeloma: CD38 marks the long-lived plasma cell. But flip the logic. In autoimmune and transplant settings, those plasma cells are the factories churning out the pathogenic antibodies that wreck the kidney — so depleting them should shut the antibody off at its source, which is exactly what anti-CD20 drugs like rituximab can't reach because they spare plasma cells. Biogen is running three global Phase 3 programs on this idea — antibody-mediated rejection in kidney transplant recipients, IgA nephropathy, and primary membranous nephropathy — with FDA Breakthrough Therapy and orphan-drug designations in two of them, and Phase 2 data already showing dropping proteinuria and stabilized kidney function.

Here's the framing I'd hold onto. Felzartamab's first approval on Earth landed in the indication that matters least to the company that owns most of it — a me-too anti-CD38 in Chinese myeloma — while the indications Biogen actually paid the premium for are still reading out. But that myeloma label is not nothing to you, because it's the first real-world proof that this specific antibody depletes CD38 plasma cells safely and effectively enough to satisfy a regulator. That's the identical mechanistic bet sitting underneath the kidney franchise. The oncology approval quietly de-risks the molecule; the autoimmune programs are where the value actually lives. For your beat, this is the cleanest current example of a single plasma-cell-targeting antibody straddling oncology and autoimmunity at once — the CD38 target migrating from killing cancer cells to silencing the plasma cells behind antibody-mediated disease. Watch the Phase 3 kidney readouts. That's the swing factor that decides whether felzartamab ends up a footnote or a franchise.
