# BI-1808 — first-in-class anti-TNFR2 antibody takes FDA Fast Track in platinum-resistant ovarian cancer on a Treg-depleting, checkpoint-reviving mechanism

Paper link: https://www.bioinvent.com/en/press/bioinvent-receives-fda-fast-track-designation-bi-1808-treatment-ovarian-cancer-2472829

## Script

It was a genuinely thin weekend on your beat — no fresh preprints on target, no clinical readouts, mostly quarterly earnings — so today's pick is a regulatory milestone from Friday that happens to sit on a mechanism I think is worth your attention. BioInvent said the F-D-A granted Fast Track designation to a molecule called B-I-eighteen-oh-eight, in combination with pembrolizumab, for platinum-resistant ovarian cancer. And I want to spend the time on the target, because it's a differentiated answer to a problem you think about constantly — how to make a cold, checkpoint-resistant tumor respond to P-D-one blockade.

The target is T-N-F-R-two, the second receptor for tumor necrosis factor. This is a tricky, almost contrarian target, and that's what makes it interesting. T-N-F-R-two is heavily upregulated on the regulatory T cells that sit inside tumors — the intratumoral T-regs that are among the most suppressive cells in the whole microenvironment. It's also on some myeloid cells and, in the periphery, on effector cells, which is exactly why the field spent years arguing about whether you should agonize this receptor or block it. BioInvent's bet is a specific one: B-I-eighteen-oh-eight is a ligand-blocking antibody that also engages Fc-gamma receptors, so it does two jobs at once. It blocks T-N-F-R-two signaling, and because it's Fc-engaging, it physically depletes the T-regs that overexpress the receptor — rather than just quieting them down. In the process it reprograms the myeloid compartment and lets C-D-eight T cells wake back up. So instead of releasing a brake on the T cell, the way P-D-one or C-T-L-A-four blockade does, it goes after the suppressive population itself and clears it out of the tumor.

If that logic sounds familiar, it should — it's the same theme as the ipilimumab story I walked through last week, where the real action turned out to be T-reg destabilization inside the tumor rather than checkpoint blockade on effector cells. This is another swing at the T-reg as the thing standing between a cold tumor and a checkpoint response, just with a depleting antibody against a receptor the T-regs happen to overexpress.

Now the data behind the Fast Track, because a designation on its own is just a faster review lane, not evidence. The support comes from an interim Phase 2a cohort presented at A-S-C-O this year, in heavily pretreated, platinum-resistant ovarian cancer — patients who've already progressed through platinum. The combination of B-I-eighteen-oh-eight plus pembrolizumab, and notably without any chemotherapy, produced a twenty-four percent confirmed objective response rate and a fifty-six percent disease control rate, with a preliminary median progression-free survival of ten-point-three months. Responses showed up across both high-grade serous and clear cell subtypes, and several were still ongoing past ten months.

Here's the number that gives those results their weight. Pembrolizumab by itself in this population historically gets you about an eight percent response rate. Ovarian cancer is the textbook cold tumor — it just doesn't respond to single-agent checkpoint blockade. So going from roughly eight percent with pembro alone to twenty-four percent by adding a T-reg-depleting antibody, chemo-free, is a real signal that the mechanism is doing something, not just riding along. That's the kind of triple-the-response-rate delta that justifies chasing the target.

Now the caveats, because this is early and single-arm. Twenty-four percent confirmed responses is meaningful against an eight-percent benchmark, but it's still a minority of patients, and this is a small, non-randomized Phase 2a cohort with no control arm — cross-trial comparisons to historical pembro monotherapy are suggestive, not definitive. Fast Track itself is a process designation about unmet need and rolling review; it says nothing about whether the drug ultimately works. And T-N-F-R-two remains a genuinely double-edged target — the same receptor that's suppressive on T-regs is co-stimulatory on effector cells in other contexts, so the therapeutic margin between depleting the bad cells and hitting the good ones is something only a bigger, randomized dataset will settle. The expansion cohorts in high-grade serous and clear cell are enrolling now, with another data cut expected in the second half of this year, and that's the readout that matters.

So why flag it. Ovarian cancer is one of the hardest places to make immunotherapy work, and the most credible path through it right now is not a better checkpoint antibody — it's remodeling the suppressive microenvironment so the checkpoint drug you already have starts working. B-I-eighteen-oh-eight is a clean example of that thesis, aimed at a target most companies have been afraid to touch. Put it on the watch list for the back-half data, and watch whether the T-reg-depletion story holds up when they add patients.
