# In vivo CAR-T — J&J options Sail Biomedicines in a bet worth up to three-and-a-half billion dollars on off-the-shelf immune reset for autoimmune disease

Paper link: https://www.jnj.com/media-center/press-releases/johnson-johnson-announces-collaboration-with-sail-biomedicines-to-advance-in-vivo-car-t-programs-and-transform-autoimmune-disease-through-immune-reset

## Script

On Tuesday, July 29th, Johnson & Johnson took an exclusive option to buy Sail Biomedicines, a Flagship company building in vivo CAR-T therapies for autoimmune disease. The structure tells you how serious this is: seven hundred eighty-five million dollars up front, including a four hundred sixty-five million dollar equity stake, another hundred and forty million in near-term milestones, and then two-and-a-half billion more to actually exercise the option and take the company. Call it three-and-a-half billion dollars, all pointed at immunology, not oncology. And here's the detail I want you to sit with: the very same day, AbCellera and Vertex signed a separate deal to build multispecific T-cell engagers, also for autoimmune disease. Two big-pharma bets, same day, both on redirecting T cells to reset the immune system. That's not a coincidence — that's a field deciding where it's going.

Let me set the backdrop, because it's the whole reason this is worth three-and-a-half billion dollars. The most striking autoimmune data of the last few years didn't come from a biologic — it came from CD-nineteen CAR-T cells borrowed straight from oncology. Take a lupus patient, or myositis, or systemic sclerosis, wipe out their B cells with a CD-nineteen CAR, and a meaningful fraction go into drug-free remission. Not better-on-therapy — off therapy, immune system rebooted, autoantibodies gone. That result reorganized how a lot of people think about autoimmune disease. The problem is delivery. Making an autologous CAR-T product means apheresis, weeks of manufacturing, lymphodepleting chemotherapy, and a six-figure price tag — for a disease that isn't cancer. So the biology is spectacular and the logistics are impossible, and that gap is exactly the opening everybody is now racing to fill.

Sail's bet is to make the CAR inside the body instead of in a factory. Their platform pairs circular RNA — they call it endless RNA — encoding the CAR, with targeted lipid nanoparticles that carry it to T cells in vivo. You inject it, the nanoparticle drops the RNA into a T cell, the cell transiently expresses the CAR, goes and kills B cells, and then — because it's RNA, not integrated DNA — the expression fades on its own. Nothing is permanently rewritten. No apheresis, no manufacturing per patient, no lymphodepletion of the usual kind. In principle it's a vial you pull off a shelf and dose like a biologic.

Here's the scientific thesis worth arguing about, and it's the reason I'd flag this to you specifically. The entire approach rests on the idea that in autoimmune disease you don't need durable engraftment to get a durable effect. In oncology you often want the CAR cells to persist for years, surveilling for relapse. In autoimmune, the emerging read is that you just need a deep, transient B-cell sweep — knock the autoreactive compartment down hard enough, and the immune system repopulates from naive precursors and comes back tolerant. If that's true, then a transient, self-limiting in vivo CAR is not a compromise — it might actually be the better tool, because you get the reset without a permanently engineered cell living in the patient forever. That's a genuinely elegant argument, and it's the crux of the whole deal.

Now the caveats, because they're real. This is a platform bet, not a data readout — Sail's lead autoimmune program is still preclinical, heading into IND-enabling work, so there is no human evidence yet that in vivo reprogramming achieves the depth of B-cell depletion that ex vivo CAR-T does. And in vivo T-cell targeting is genuinely hard: getting lipid nanoparticles to deliver to the right cells at the right dose, hitting a potent-enough CAR level, and controlling cytokine release without the monitoring scaffolding of an inpatient CAR-T program. Sail itself has had a bumpy ride — Flagship merged two struggling platforms to create it and it's been through layoffs. So J&J is buying an option, not a sure thing, and the option structure lets them walk if the first program stumbles.

For context on the buyer: J&J already knows CAR-T from the oncology side through Carvykti with Legend, and they'd previously reached into in vivo cell therapy with Kelonia before Lilly bought that out from under them. So this is them buying back into a frontier they lost once, and doing it in immunology, where their commercial muscle already is.

So the one-line take: the next CAR-T battle isn't about the target — CD-nineteen is settled — it's about delivery, and it's being fought in autoimmune disease, not cancer. J&J and Vertex just told you, on the same day, that the prize goes to whoever can take those spectacular ex vivo remissions and turn them into something you can inject. In vivo CAR-T is the boldest version of that idea, and this is the biggest check anyone has yet written on it.
