# IM1240 — a "capped-CD3" trispecific that engages both T cells and NK cells posts preclinical data aimed squarely at the T-cell-engager cytokine-release problem

Paper link: https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/08/07/3341033/0/en/purple-biotech-reports-second-quarter-2026-financial-results.html

## Script

Today's pick is a preclinical story, and I want to be upfront about that, but it lands right on one of the modalities you follow most closely — trispecific T-cell engagers — and it was the freshest genuinely on-target item on the board. Yesterday, Purple Biotech, in its quarterly update, disclosed new data on a molecule called I-M-twelve-forty. And the design is worth walking through, because it's a clean example of the field's current best answer to the problem that has kept T-cell engagers boxed into blood cancers.

Here's the shape of the molecule. It has three arms. The first is a C-D-three arm, so it recruits T cells — standard engager stuff. The second is a five-T-four arm; five-T-four, also called trophoblast glycoprotein, is an oncofetal antigen that gets switched back on across a wide range of solid tumors and is mostly silent in normal adult tissue, so it's the tumor-targeting handle. The third arm is the interesting one: it grabs N-K-G-two-A. That does double duty. N-K-G-two-A sits on natural killer cells and on some C-D-eight T cells, so the arm physically pulls NK cells to the tumor alongside the T cells; and because N-K-G-two-A is itself an inhibitory checkpoint — tumors put up its ligand to switch killer cells off — occupying it also lifts a brake. So one molecule bridges two different killer populations and relieves a checkpoint at the same time.

But the headline word here is "capped." The C-D-three arm is masked — Purple calls the platform CAPTN-three — so the engager stays quiet until it reaches tumor tissue, where the mask comes off. And this is the whole ballgame for solid-tumor engagers. The reason C-D-three bispecifics have struggled outside hematology is the therapeutic window: crank the dose high enough to kill a solid tumor and you get systemic T-cell activation and cytokine release. Masking is the bet that you can decouple those two things — kill in the tumor, stay quiet in the blood.

The primate data are the reason to actually take this seriously. In non-human primates, the capped version showed roughly an eight-fold longer half-life and sixteen-fold greater systemic exposure than the uncapped version — which is what you'd expect if the mask is keeping the drug from being consumed by on-target activation out in the periphery. And the safety separation is dramatic. The capped molecule produced minimal interleukin-six and T-N-F-alpha at ten milligrams per kilogram, while the uncapped version drove robust cytokine release at three hundredths of a milligram per kilogram. That's more than a two-log shift in the dose that triggers a cytokine storm. If that holds up in people, it's exactly the window-widening the class has been chasing.

On the tumor side, they tested patient-derived samples and reported that all of them responded — including P-D-one-resistant head and neck cancer and checkpoint-inhibitor-resistant bladder cancer. Those are the populations where you'd most want a mechanism that doesn't lean on an intact endogenous checkpoint response, so that's a sensible place to show your hand. In one lung sample they saw the drug induce tertiary lymphoid structures while pulling in both C-D-eight T cells and NK cells — the kind of organized immune architecture you actually want to see, not just a killing readout in a dish.

Now the caveats, because this is early. This is preclinical data disclosed in an earnings release — not a peer-reviewed paper, not a clinical readout — and the molecule isn't slated to enter the clinic until 2027. Primate cytokine profiles and patient-sample killing are encouraging, but the graveyard of solid-tumor T-cell engagers is full of molecules that looked clean preclinically and then lit up in patients. Masking strategies in particular have two things to prove: that the mask is tight enough in circulation, and that it's reliably cleaved in the tumor — and that the protease logic holds across tumor types, not just the handful you chose to put in the deck.

So why flag it anyway. The combination of conditional activation, plus NK engagement, plus a checkpoint-relieving arm is a genuinely differentiated take on the therapeutic-window problem — and it's the exact engineering conversation happening across the whole trispecific field right now, just with the targets swapped. If capping works, it's a template that generalizes far past this one asset. I'd put it on the watch list for the 2027 first-in-human data, because that's when we learn whether the mask holds up where it actually counts.

One bit of context on the rest of Purple's pipeline, since it tells you how far ahead this is: their lead clinical assets are C-M-twenty-four, a C-E-A-CAM-one-blocking antibody that reported improved outcomes across efficacy endpoints in a Phase 2 pancreatic study, and N-T-two-nineteen, a dual I-R-S one and two and STAT-three inhibitor that finished Phase 2 in head and neck cancer. Those are the near-term shots on goal. But I-M-twelve-forty is the one built for your beat.
