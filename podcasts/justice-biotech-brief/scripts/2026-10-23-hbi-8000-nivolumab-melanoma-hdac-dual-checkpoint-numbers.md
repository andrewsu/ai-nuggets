# HBI-8000 — an oral HDAC inhibitor plus nivolumab wins frontline melanoma, landing on CheckMate 067's dual-checkpoint numbers

Press release: https://www.biospace.com/press-releases/huyabio-international-announces-hbi-8000-and-nivolumab-combination-reduces-the-risk-of-disease-progression-by-27-in-patients-with-advanced-melanoma-in-phase-3-trial

## Script

Good morning, Justice. ESMO opened in Madrid this morning, and the first late-breaker worth your time goes up this afternoon: a randomized Phase 3 showing that adding an oral HDAC inhibitor to nivolumab beats nivolumab alone in frontline advanced melanoma. The drug is H-B-I eight thousand, from HUYABIO, a San Diego company that licensed it from Chipscreen in China. You may know the molecule better as tucidinostat, or as chidamide — it's a selective class one HDAC inhibitor that's been approved in China since twenty fourteen and in Japan since twenty twenty-one for relapsed peripheral T cell lymphoma. So this is not a new chemical entity. It's an old Asian oncology drug taking a swing at a Western frontline immunotherapy indication.

The headline numbers first. Four hundred four patients, a hundred thirty-nine sites across fifteen countries, randomized one-to-one, double-blind, placebo-controlled, stratified by PD-L1 status and LDH. Patients were checkpoint-naive, unresectable or metastatic, non-uveal. Primary endpoint was progression-free survival by blinded independent review — which is the version of that endpoint you actually want. Median PFS was eleven point seven months on the combination versus seven point four on nivolumab plus placebo. Hazard ratio zero point seven three, p equals zero point zero one four. That's a win, and it's the first time a randomized Phase 3 has shown an HDAC inhibitor adding anything to PD-1 blockade. Everything before this lived in small single-arm studies in the refractory setting — entinostat plus pembrolizumab, for instance, got a nineteen percent response rate in PD-1-refractory melanoma in a small early-phase study. Nobody had converted that into a randomized frontline win. So give them that.

Now here's the thing I want you to sit with, because it reframes the entire pitch. Go pull up CheckMate oh-six-seven, the four-year follow-up. Nivolumab plus ipilimumab: median PFS eleven point five months. Nivolumab alone: six point nine months. Grade three-to-four treatment-related adverse events: fifty-nine percent on the doublet, twenty-two percent on nivolumab alone.

Now this trial. Combination: eleven point seven months. Control: seven point four months. Grade three-to-four treatment-related events: fifty-four point eight percent on the combination, seventeen point nine percent on nivolumab plus placebo.

Those two sets of numbers are very nearly superimposable. Efficacy and toxicity, both arms, all four cells of the table. It's cross-trial, a decade apart, so don't take it as a comparison — but as a sanity check on where this combination actually sits, it's hard to unsee. H-B-I eight thousand plus nivolumab appears to land almost exactly where dual checkpoint blockade already is.

Which matters because of how the result is being framed. The company's lead message, and the investigator's quote, is that this offers a frontline option that doesn't rely on dual immune checkpoint inhibition — the implication being you get the efficacy without the toxicity. But look at the grade three-to-four rate: fifty-four point eight percent. That is not a gentler regimen. Thirty-eight percent of patients needed a dose reduction. The common events were diarrhea, nausea, cytopenias and fatigue — classic HDAC class effects, exactly what you'd predict. They did not avoid the toxicity burden of a doublet. They swapped the category of toxicity.

And that swap is genuinely defensible — it's just a different argument than the one in the headline. The best line in the release is the quietest one: no increase in adrenal insufficiency or hypophysitis. Those are the two immune-related events that are frequently permanent. A patient who gets hypophysitis from ipilimumab is on hormone replacement for life. A patient who gets neutropenia and diarrhea from an HDAC inhibitor gets a dose reduction and recovers. If you're choosing a frontline regimen, trading irreversible endocrinopathy for reversible myelosuppression is a real and meaningful trade. That's the argument. It's about reversibility, not incidence, and I'd rather they led with it.

Three things keep me cautious. First, the comparator. This trial was designed in twenty twenty-one against nivolumab monotherapy. Nivolumab plus relatlimab was approved in twenty twenty-two. For a lot of frontline melanoma patients today, single-agent nivolumab is not what they'd be offered, so beating it establishes activity but doesn't establish a place in the sequence. Second, the confidence interval on that median: seven point three to twenty-one point one months. That is an enormously wide band, and the point estimate of eleven point seven should be held loosely. The hazard ratio is also quoted with a ninety-six percent confidence interval rather than ninety-five, which is the signature of alpha having been spent at an interim look — a detail worth confirming when the manuscript lands. Third, overall survival is immature. Melanoma has a long history of PFS gains that didn't convert, and HDAC inhibitors carry myelosuppression into subsequent lines of therapy.

The mechanism claim — that HDAC inhibition turns a cold tumor hot — is asserted, not demonstrated here. No biomarker data in the release. But the trial stratified on PD-L1 expression, which means the subgroup breakdown exists, and that's the first thing I'd look for this afternoon. If the benefit concentrates in PD-L1-negative tumors, the epigenetic-priming story gets real support. If it's flat across both, then this is a cytotoxic-flavored add-on with a nice mechanistic story attached, and the comparison to dual checkpoint blockade becomes the only frame that matters.

Manuscript is submitted for peer review. The company says it's taking the data to the FDA.
