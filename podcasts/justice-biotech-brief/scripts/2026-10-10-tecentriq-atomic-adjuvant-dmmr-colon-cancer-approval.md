# Tecentriq — first adjuvant checkpoint approval in colon cancer, won on disease-free survival with overall survival flat

Paper link: https://www.biospace.com/press-releases/fda-approves-genentechs-tecentriq-in-combination-with-a-fluoropyrimidine-and-oxaliplatin-for-the-adjuvant-treatment-of-a-certain-type-of-stage-iii-colon-cancer
Paper link: https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2507874

## Script

Yesterday the FDA approved atezolizumab, Genentech's PD-L1 antibody sold as Tecentriq, in combination with a fluoropyrimidine and oxaliplatin, for the adjuvant treatment of stage three mismatch-repair-deficient colon cancer. It is the first immunotherapy regimen approved after surgery in colon cancer, and the company is calling it a new standard of care. The approval rests on ATOMIC, an Alliance trial run through the National Clinical Trials Network, published in the New England Journal of Medicine in March. I want to walk through what that trial actually shows, because the headline number and the number that matters are not the same number.

The design first. Seven hundred and twelve patients with completely resected stage three colon cancer, mismatch repair deficient by immunohistochemistry, randomized one to one. One arm got six months of FOLFOX. The other got six months of FOLFOX plus atezolizumab, and then another six months of atezolizumab alone — a full year of checkpoint blockade. Primary endpoint was disease-free survival. At a median follow-up of about forty-one months, three-year disease-free survival was eighty-six percent with atezolizumab and seventy-six percent without, a hazard ratio of zero point five. That is a large, clean effect, and it is why this got approved.

Now the number that didn't make the press release. Overall survival is flat. Thirty-one deaths in the atezolizumab arm, thirty-three in the control arm, five-year survival of about ninety percent versus eighty-eight percent, a hazard ratio of zero point nine with confidence intervals running from zero point five five to one point four seven. The trial is not powered for survival and the follow-up is short, so this is not evidence of no benefit. But there is a specific reason to take it seriously here, and it is immunological. These are mismatch-repair-deficient tumors. When they recur, they recur into a setting where PD-1 blockade is already the standard first-line treatment and works well — that is KEYNOTE-177. So the control arm patients who relapsed mostly went on to get a checkpoint inhibitor anyway. The honest framing of a zero point five hazard ratio for recurrence with a flat survival curve is that we may be moving immunotherapy earlier, not adding a cure. For some patients, avoiding a recurrence is worth a great deal on its own. For the health system, and for the patient weighing a second six months of infusions, that distinction is the whole decision.

Then there's the cost side, which is larger than the usual checkpoint story because the comparator is chemotherapy rather than nothing. Grade three and four adverse events hit eighty-four percent of the atezolizumab arm versus seventy-two percent of the control arm. Over half the patients in the atezolizumab arm discontinued early, against about a third in the control arm, and the dropouts cluster in the back half — the atezolizumab monotherapy phase, after the chemotherapy has stopped. Colitis of any grade ran about seven percent versus half a percent. Hypothyroidism, twenty percent versus four. These are familiar, mostly manageable toxicities, but they are being handed to people who have had their tumor resected and may never have recurred.

There is one subgroup finding worth sitting with. The investigators pre-specified a look at chemotherapy duration, and the atezolizumab benefit shows up almost entirely in patients who received more than six cycles of FOLFOX — hazard ratio zero point four one. In patients who got six cycles or fewer, the hazard ratio is zero point nine seven. No benefit at all. That is a subgroup analysis in a trial with a hundred and twenty-seven events, so don't over-read it. But it does point at the question ATOMIC never asked, which is whether the chemotherapy backbone belongs there in the first place. Mismatch-repair-deficient colorectal cancer is the classic case of intrinsic fluoropyrimidine resistance. This trial added a checkpoint inhibitor on top of chemotherapy; it did not test the checkpoint inhibitor against chemotherapy, and it did not test it alone.

Which brings me to what I think is the real argument. ATOMIC enrolled from 2017 to 2023, and it was designed before the neoadjuvant data existed. In NICHE-2, a hundred and fifteen patients with locally advanced mismatch-repair-deficient colon cancer got two doses of nivolumab plus ipilimumab over roughly six weeks before surgery. Pathological response in ninety-eight percent. Complete pathological response in sixty-eight percent. Grade three or four immune-related toxicity in four percent. Not one recurrence at a median follow-up of twenty-six months. NICHE-3, with nivolumab plus relatlimab, got ninety-seven percent pathological response in fifty-nine patients. Those are single-arm phase two trials in a somewhat different population, and I am not going to pretend they are a randomized comparison. But the mechanism is on their side. Checkpoint blockade needs antigen and it needs the lymph nodes that drain the tumor. Give it before surgery and both are present; give it after, and you have resected the antigen depot and the draining nodes and are asking the drug to work on micrometastatic disease. Six weeks of neoadjuvant therapy generating ninety-five percent major pathological responses is a different proposition from a year of post-operative therapy that moves recurrence without yet moving survival.

The field clearly agrees, because the registry is now full of neoadjuvant trials in this exact population testing organ preservation and watch-and-wait — trying to find out whether these patients need the operation at all. An editorial in Nature Reviews Clinical Oncology this past July asked, in its title, whether ATOMIC really improved on the standard of care. My read: this is a real result and a reasonable approval, and it is also an eight-year-old question answered after the field moved past it. If you are designing anything that depends on T cells recognizing a tumor, the transferable lesson is about sequence. For a hypermutated, T-cell-inflamed tumor, when you give the drug relative to the surgeon may matter more than which checkpoint you block.
