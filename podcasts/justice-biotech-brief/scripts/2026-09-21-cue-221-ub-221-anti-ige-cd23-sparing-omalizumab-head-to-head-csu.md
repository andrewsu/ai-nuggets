# CUE-221 — an anti-IgE antibody engineered to leave the CD23 brake intact beats omalizumab numerically in a head-to-head Phase 2, and the gap widens after dosing stops

Paper link: https://www.biospace.com/press-releases/cue-biopharma-announces-positive-topline-results-from-cue-221-phase-2-study-in-chronic-spontaneous-urticaria

Mechanism paper: https://www.jci.org/articles/view/157765

Trial link: https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03632291

## Script

Good morning, Justice. One pick today, and it is not a T cell engager — bioRxiv gave us a genuinely empty weekend, so this is a news pick. It is an antibody-engineering result, it landed on the wire late last night, and the design lesson in it transfers directly to the molecules you build.

Cue Biopharma reported topline Phase 2 data for C-U-E two-twenty-one in chronic spontaneous urticaria. The molecule you may know by its original name, U-B two-twenty-one. It is a humanized anti-I-g-E monoclonal, and the trial was a hundred forty-five patients with moderate-to-severe disease still uncontrolled on antihistamines, run in China by Genesis Life Sciences. Five arms — three subcutaneous doses of the antibody every four weeks, placebo, and, critically, omalizumab at three hundred milligrams every four weeks as an active comparator. Sixteen weeks of treatment, then twenty weeks of follow-up off drug.

The headline numbers first. The primary endpoint was complete resolution of hives at week twelve. Fifty-four percent at the top dose of four milligrams per kilogram, fifty-three at two, forty-three at one, against eleven percent on placebo and forty-one percent on omalizumab. Complete response on the urticaria activity score, the key secondary, was forty-six percent against eleven on placebo and twenty-nine on omalizumab.

Now, do not spend your attention on that Xolair comparison. The omalizumab arm had seventeen patients in it, and the protocol says in plain language that omalizumab was included to enable comparative efficacy without planned statistical testing. So there is no p-value against it at the primary endpoint, by design. A thirteen-point numerical lead on seventeen controls is a press release, not a finding.

The finding is the shape of the curve over time, and to see why it matters you need the engineering premise.

I-g-E has two receptors that matter here. The high-affinity one, F-c-epsilon-R-one, sits on mast cells and basophils and is the one that makes you itch. The low-affinity one, C-D-twenty-three, sits on B cells, and for thirty years the field has proposed that I-g-E binding C-D-twenty-three is a negative-feedback brake on I-g-E synthesis — the cell sensing its own product and turning the tap down. Omalizumab blocks both. Its footprint on I-g-E substantially overlaps the site where C-D-twenty-three docks — the mechanism paper puts the overlapping atomic volume at roughly twenty-eight hundred cubic angstroms. So Xolair neutralizes the receptor you want blocked and, incidentally, the brake you would rather leave alone.

C-U-E two-twenty-one was built to split those. It binds a mixed protein-and-carbohydrate epitope spanning the C-epsilon-two and C-epsilon-three domains, holding I-g-E in an extended conformation that leaves the C-epsilon-three to C-epsilon-four junction — where C-D-twenty-three binds — clear. In the assays, free antibody bound C-D-twenty-three-occupied I-g-E with a half-maximal concentration of thirty-nine nanograms per milliliter, against three hundred ninety-six for ligelizumab, while omalizumab was inert. And in stimulated human B cells it knocked down new I-g-E protein by about seventy percent and I-g-E messenger R-N-A by about seventy-five, where the other two managed somewhere between five and thirty.

Here is why I think this is worth your five minutes rather than being another me-too anti-I-g-E. The field already ran the control experiment on the obvious alternative hypothesis, and it lost. Ligelizumab binds I-g-E about four times more tightly than this molecule and roughly fourteen times more tightly than omalizumab. Novartis took it into two Phase 3 trials, PEARL-one and PEARL-two, in exactly this disease. It beat placebo comfortably and it did not beat omalizumab — the treatment differences against Xolair were fractions of a point on the activity score, in either direction depending on the trial. Novartis said it was disappointed and moved the asset elsewhere. So more potent neutralization of the receptor everyone agrees is causal does not buy you a better drug. That axis is spent. This molecule is not the most potent binder in the class; it is the one that declines to block the second receptor.

And the timing of the effect is what a C-D-twenty-three mechanism predicts. This is the part I find genuinely persuasive, because it was written down in advance. The twenty twenty-two mechanism paper said explicitly that a synthesis-suppression benefit would only become apparent under repeat dosing over a substantially long period, because a single dose mostly reports neutralization. Four doses over sixteen weeks later, complete hive resolution did not plateau at week twelve — it kept climbing to sixty-nine percent at week twenty-two. And at week twenty-eight, twelve weeks after the last injection anybody got, sixty percent of the top-dose patients were still hive-free against twenty-four percent of the omalizumab patients. A thirty-six-point gap, with a p-value under point-oh-five. Post hoc, which I will come back to, but a prospectively stated mechanistic prediction matching the direction, the magnitude and the timing of the observed effect is about as good as clinical inference gets outside a properly powered trial. Safety was unremarkable in the good way — no treatment-related serious events, no anaphylaxis, injection-site reactions almost all grade one.

Now the discount rate. That week-twenty-eight comparison is post hoc. The whole study is a hundred forty-five patients across five arms, so every cell is small. It ran entirely in China, and the standard of care and the population are not a U-S registrational setting. The trial is not on ClinicalTrials-dot-gov — only the older, smaller studies are, three Phase 1s and a twenty-five-patient intravenous Phase 2 — so you cannot read the protocol yourself, which for a result this interesting is annoying. The earlier work was intravenous and this is subcutaneous. And dosing is weight-based against omalizumab's flat three hundred milligrams, which is a real asymmetry at the top dose.

Then the competitive honesty, because the chief executive's phrase was best therapeutic option and that is not established. On raw complete response at week twelve, Celldex's barzolvolimab — an anti-KIT antibody that simply deletes the mast cell — already reported up to fifty-one percent in Phase 2, deepening toward seventy percent over a year, and it has nineteen hundred patients enrolled in two Phase 3s. Remibrutinib was approved a year ago as the first oral B-T-K inhibitor here, with roughly a third of patients clear at twelve weeks. So on response rate this is competitive, not dominant. The defensible claim is narrower and more interesting than the one they made: nobody else in this disease is arguing that the effect should outlive the drug for a reason you can point to in a structure.

Two things to read when the full data set arrives, and the first one is not the response rate. Read the off-drug window. If the separation from omalizumab at week twenty-eight replicates in a Western population under a pre-specified analysis, that is a disease-modifying claim in an allergic disease and it is a big deal. The second is the total I-g-E curve, and they have promised the I-g-E analyses from the full thirty-six-week study. With omalizumab, total I-g-E goes up, because complexed I-g-E clears slowly. If the C-D-twenty-three brake is actually engaged, repeat dosing should bend total I-g-E down instead of letting it accumulate. That single curve is the cleanest available test of whether the mechanism story is true or just a nice structural narrative attached to a lucky small trial.

The read-across for your work is the general one, and you have heard me make it twice this month already. Four days ago it was mosunetuzumab, whose C-D-twenty arm uses rituximab's own epitope and therefore cannot be co-dosed with rituximab, while epcoritamab was deliberately engineered onto a different face so it could. Last week it was targeted I-L-two, where selectivity came from geometry rather than from mutating the receptor interface. This is the same lesson a third time. When you pick a binding site you are not only choosing what to block — you are choosing what you leave accessible, and the thing you leave accessible can be a mechanism in its own right. For an engager or a conjugate, that means asking what your epitope forecloses: which co-receptor, which combination partner, which feedback loop. The potency axis is usually the one that gets optimized and it is usually the one that is already saturated.

One last note, on the business, because it is a small story with a strange shape. Cue licensed this antibody in April for fifteen million dollars up front against up to six hundred seventy-six and a half million in milestones, outside greater China. A company whose identity was a T-cell-targeting platform now leads with a licensed allergy antibody, and the plan has already moved — in April the global play was food allergy after the urticaria data; this morning they are going to a Phase 2b-slash-three in urticaria first. Fifteen million dollars for the asset that is now the company. Watch whether they can fund a Phase 3 in a disease that already has an approved oral and a fully enrolled competitor.
