# MUC17 kCAR-T — a CAR-T cell that secretes a human protease to strip the tumor's mucin coat, and the epitope-position rule that says when debulking helps and when it eats your binding site

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.09.25.754490v1

Prior work on glycocalyx thickness and immune attack (Nat Mater 2024, via PubMed): https://doi.org/10.1038/s41563-024-01808-0

MUC17 x CD3 half-life-extended BiTE, Phase 1, terminated: https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04117958

HALO-301, pegylated hyaluronidase in hyaluronan-high metastatic pancreatic cancer, Phase 3, terminated: https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT02715804

## Script

Today's pick is a preprint that went up yesterday from Marcela Maus's group at Mass General, done with Matt Paszek's lab. It is not a trial readout, it is a design paper, and I picked it because it attacks a habit almost everyone in this field has. The habit is choosing a CAR or engager target by counting antigen — copies per cell, antibody binding capacity, immunohistochemistry score. More is better. This paper shows that for a whole class of targets, more is not better, and it shows you where the curve turns over.

Here is the setup. Epithelial tumors wear a glycocalyx — a dense forest of glycoproteins and proteoglycans standing off the plasma membrane, and the tallest trees in it are the transmembrane mucins, MUC-one, MUC-sixteen, MUC-seventeen, with enormous extracellular domains carpeted in O-linked glycans. This group showed two years ago that the thickness of that layer, in nanometers, sets how well a cytotoxic cell can attack, and that differences of about ten nanometers are enough to change the outcome. The glycocalyx is a steric barrier sitting precisely where the immune synapse needs to form.

Now put the two ideas together. If your CAR target is itself a mucin, raising target expression does two opposite things at once: it gives you more binding sites, and it raises the barrier around them. The experiment is clean — a doxycycline-inducible mucin system so they can dial expression, and a CAR against the truncated tumor glycoform of MUC-one. At zero doxycycline, killing was twenty point two percent. At one hundred nanograms per milliliter, thirty-two point four. At one thousand nanograms per milliliter — more antigen, unambiguously more antigen — killing fell back to twenty-four point five percent. Non-monotonic. They then built a thermodynamic adhesion model with a polymer-brush repulsion term, it reproduces the hump, and it says something you should take seriously: raising CAR affinity or CAR expression shifts the balance but does not rescue you at high mucin density. You cannot out-affinity a physical barrier.

The second half of the argument is about where your epitope sits, and this is the part I would write on the whiteboard. They treated cells with the bacterial mucinase S-T-C-E, which chops the mucin ectodomain off, and asked what happened to antibody binding. For the epitope in the MUC-one tandem repeat — out in the branches — binding did not improve, and at high expression it got slightly worse, because you are shedding the thing the antibody grabs. For the membrane-proximal MUC-one C-terminal domain, binding rose about one point seven-fold. For endogenous MUC-seventeen, where the CAR epitope is juxtamembrane, it rose two point seven-fold on a pancreatic line and two point three-fold on a gastric line. They confirmed it functionally with an acoustic force avidity assay — pull harder, and more CAR-T cells stay stuck to the target if you cleared the mucin first — and with killing.

So the rule is: cutting the forest down helps if your target is a stump, and hurts if your target is a leaf.

Then comes the translational move, which is the reason this is more than a nice biophysics paper. S-T-C-E is a protease from enterohemorrhagic E-coli. It is immunogenic, it has a narrow tolerability window given systemically, and it is never going into an autologous product as a payload. So they went looking for a human enzyme, screened recombinant human kallikrein-related peptidases four, five, six, seven and thirteen for mucin cleavage, and K-L-K-five won. It cleaves MUC-one, and also MUC-sixteen, MUC-seventeen, and C-D-forty-three, the glyco-barrier recently described on myeloid leukemia. It does not chew everything glycosylated — P-S-G-L-one and C-D-forty-five were largely spared, so there is some selectivity. And measured directly by interference microscopy, it thinned the glycocalyx by about fifteen nanometers. Remember that ten nanometers was the number that mattered.

Then they built it into the cell, two ways. First, membrane-tethered — and the tethering itself turned out to be the hard part. A fusion to a C-D-twenty-eight transmembrane domain through a short flexible linker did not express at all. What worked was borrowing the architecture of ADAM-ten, an endogenous membrane protease: keep its roughly two-hundred-sixteen-amino-acid extracellular stalk, swap its catalytic module for K-L-K-five. That is a reusable result on its own — to display an enzyme on a T cell, a real membrane protease's stalk beats a glycine-serine string. The second design was simply to secrete the enzyme, and they call those k-CAR-T cells.

Secretion won. In a gastric xenograft, head to head, the secreting MUC-seventeen k-CAR-T gave the best tumor control and clearly the longest survival, beating both the tethered version and the conventional CAR. Same direction in a pancreatic model, and it generalized — a mesothelin CAR secreting the same enzyme outperformed a conventional mesothelin CAR in a glycan-rich pancreatic model. Their guess for why secretion beats tethering is that a soluble enzyme reaches glycocalyx beyond the contact patch, which they honestly flag as unproven; they did not measure where the enzyme went.

The control that makes me believe the whole model is the failure. They also put secreted K-L-K-five into a CAR against the truncated MUC-one glycoform — whose epitope lives inside the tandem repeat region that K-L-K-five happens to cut. Modest benefit in a dish, no clear benefit in an animal. The enzyme ate the binding site. That is the prediction from the first half of the paper coming true in the last figure, and it turns an observation into a design rule you can apply before building anything.

Where I would push back. The secretion is not antigen-gated. Supernatant enzyme rose about four-fold after tumor coculture, from one point four to five and a half nanograms per milliliter, but that is activation and expansion, not a circuit — the construct has no gate, and the authors say so. That matters more than usual, because K-L-K-five is the desquamation protease of human skin, held in check by an endogenous inhibitor, and when that inhibitor is lost genetically you get Netherton syndrome, a skin barrier that falls apart. The tolerability data on offer are forty-two days of stable weight and clean histology in immunodeficient mice given recombinant enzyme systemically. Reasonable first pass. Not the same experiment as a persisting living cell secreting a human protease for months in a patient. Everything here is also xenograft — no host immune system, no real stroma, and the wins are better tumor control and longer survival, not cures. One small honest negative worth respecting: they screened A-I-designed K-L-K-five variants, and none beat the wild-type enzyme.

Two pieces of context for your world. First, we have tried degrading a tumor's physical defenses with an enzyme before, and it did not go well — pegylated hyaluronidase in hyaluronan-high metastatic pancreatic cancer ran a four-hundred-ninety-two patient Phase three that was terminated without a survival benefit. The distinction being drawn here is scale and delivery: not bulk stroma dosed into the bloodstream, but the few tens of nanometers at the synapse, delivered by the cell doing the killing. Whether that distinction survives contact with a human tumor is the entire bet. Second, MUC-seventeen already has a graveyard. Amgen ran a half-life-extended MUC-seventeen by C-D-three engager through a fifty-eight patient Phase one in gastric, gastroesophageal, colorectal and pancreatic cancer; it was terminated in 2023 and no results were ever posted to the registry. If part of why big-mucin engagers underdeliver is steric exclusion rather than biology, that is a testable retrospective — and an awkward one for engagers, because an antibody cannot carry its own protease. A CAR can.

Last week on this show I covered the Outpace mesothelin CAR and argued that spacer geometry — how far the receptor holds the two membranes apart — may have been quietly responsible for results the field blamed on binders. This is the same physics approached from the other side. You can tune the receptor, or you can edit the ligand's neighborhood. What is new here is that the T cell can now do the editing itself, and that whether you should let it depends on one question you can answer on a napkin: after the enzyme cuts, is my epitope still there?
