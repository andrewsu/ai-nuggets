# EB103 — CD19-redirected ARTEMIS synthetic-TCR T-cell therapy posts durable complete responses with low CRS in relapsed/refractory B-cell lymphoma

Paper link: https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260724356360/en/Estrella-Immunopharma-Announces-First-Patient-Dosed-in-Dose-Expansion-Phase-of-STARLIGHT-1-Trial-and-Reports-Durable-Response-Rate-in-Advanced-B-Cell-Non-Hodgkins-Lymphoma

## Script

Today's pick is a cell therapy story, and the reason it's worth your attention isn't the response rate — it's the receptor. Estrella Immunopharma just dosed the first patient in the dose-expansion phase of its STARLIGHT-one trial, and alongside that milestone reported durable complete responses from the dose-escalation portion in relapsed or refractory B-cell non-Hodgkin's lymphoma. The drug is E-B-one-oh-three, and it is a C-D-nineteen-redirected T-cell therapy — but crucially, it is not a car-T.

Here's the distinction, because the whole thesis rides on it. A conventional car-T takes an antibody-derived binder and bolts it onto an artificial signaling tail — C-D-three-zeta plus a costimulatory domain like four-one-B-B or C-D-twenty-eight — all fused into one synthetic chimeric receptor. That design works, spectacularly, but it signals in a way a natural T cell never does: it's prone to tonic, always-on background signaling, and when it engages a big tumor burden it can dump cytokines all at once. That's the origin of the two toxicities that define the car-T experience — cytokine release syndrome and the neurotoxicity we call I-CANS. Estrella's platform, called Artemis, takes a different route: instead of a fused artificial tail, the C-D-nineteen binder is wired to recruit the cell's own native T-cell-receptor and C-D-three signaling machinery. The pitch is that you get a more graded, more physiologic activation — closer to how a real T-cell receptor fires — and therefore far less of the explosive cytokine burst.

So does the clinical signal back that up? Early, and in small numbers, yes. In the dose-escalation cohort — nine patients — the second dose level produced complete responses in all evaluable patients, and those complete responses were sustained out to the six-month assessment in the patients without central-nervous-system involvement. Roughly eighty percent of these patients met high-risk criteria, and one patient with C-N-S lymphoma — a group usually excluded from these trials — also reached a complete response. And the safety line is the headline: no treatment-related serious adverse events reported, with only low-grade cytokine release and neurotoxicity. That is the combination the field has been chasing — car-T-like depth of response without the car-T toxicity tax.

Why does that matter so much? Because in large B-cell lymphoma the bottleneck on car-T isn't really whether it works — the approved C-D-nineteen products work. The bottleneck is the toxicity infrastructure: the inpatient monitoring, the tocilizumab and steroids, the I-C-U capacity, the certified centers. That tax is why car-T stays concentrated at academic hubs and why a big fraction of eligible patients never get it. A construct that keeps the efficacy and genuinely drops the cytokine storm isn't just a nicer safety table — it's a drug you could imagine giving in the outpatient setting, at more sites, to older and frailer patients. That's the real prize here, and it's why the signaling-architecture angle is more interesting than any single number.

Now the skeptical read, because you need it. This is nine patients. "One hundred percent complete response" in a handful of people is a signal, not a result — small single-arm cohorts routinely look perfect and then regress to the mean as the denominator grows. Six-month durability is encouraging, but the bar that actually matters in this disease is durability at twelve, eighteen, twenty-four months, measured against approved autologous C-D-nineteen car-Ts that are fast, off-the-shelf-ish, and already have years of follow-up. And Estrella is a microcap — you'll see analyst notes floating "the next Arcellx" — so discount the framing and watch the data. The dose-expansion cohort that just opened, at the recommended phase-two dose, is the readout that will tell you whether the low-toxicity profile holds when the numbers get real.

The through-line for anyone building T-cell therapies is the mechanistic lesson, and it holds even if E-B-one-oh-three itself stumbles: you may be able to decouple T-cell potency from cytokine toxicity by changing the receptor's signaling grammar — engaging the endogenous T-cell-receptor complex rather than a fused chimeric tail — instead of only tinkering with the binder or the manufacturing. If the Artemis approach reproduces this safety separation in the expansion cohort, that's a design principle worth stealing. Watch the expansion N, watch the cytokine-release grades at the phase-two dose, and watch whether those complete responses are still complete a year out.
