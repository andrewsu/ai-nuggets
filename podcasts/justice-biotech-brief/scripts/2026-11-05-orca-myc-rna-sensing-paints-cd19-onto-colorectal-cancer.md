# ORCA — an RNA sensor that paints CD19 onto MYC-amplified colorectal cancer

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.11.02.763782v1

## Script

The thing that has kept C-A-R T cells out of solid tumors is not the T cell. It's the antigen. There is no C-D-nineteen out there — no surface protein that is on every malignant cell and on nothing you need. Twenty years of target discovery have mostly produced molecules that are enriched on tumor rather than restricted to it, and the clinic has paid for that difference in on-target off-tumor toxicity.

A preprint posted this week from Xiaojing Gao's lab in chemical engineering at Stanford, with Crystal Mackall's group, takes a different line entirely. Don't find the antigen. Install it. And gate the installation on something inside the cell that actually defines the cancer.

The platform is called ORCA, for oncogenic R-N-A-triggered cancer ablation, and the sensing half is a technology Gao's lab published a few years ago called RADAR. The trick is clean enough to describe in a sentence. You deliver a transcript that has a stop codon sitting in the middle of it, blocking translation of whatever payload comes after. The region around that stop codon is complementary to a target R-N-A — in this case, the transcript of an amplified oncogene. If the target is present in the cell, the two R-N-As base-pair, and that double-stranded stretch recruits the cell's own A-DAR enzyme, which does adenosine-to-inosine editing. The edit lands on the stop codon, destroys it, and the payload gets translated. No target R-N-A, no edit, no payload. It's a post-transcriptional A-N-D gate using host machinery, and critically, it reads an intracellular transcript and produces an extracellular consequence.

So what do you put downstream of that stop codon? Their first answer is the one that should interest you most: human C-D-nineteen. Sense amplified M-Y-C, and if it's there, express C-D-nineteen on the surface. Now the cell is a target for an approved, extremely well-characterized C-A-R T product — in a tumor that has never had a C-A-R T target.

The choice of M-Y-C is deliberate and it's the strategic core of the paper. M-Y-C is a transcription factor, it's been called undruggable for thirty years, it's overexpressed in roughly seventy percent of colorectal cancer, and in a meaningful fraction of those tumors it's amplified on extrachromosomal D-N-A — which means the transcript is not just present, it's abundant. Abundance is exactly what a sensor like this wants. The paper's argument is that you can't drug the protein, so stop trying; drug the fact that the message is there.

They found sensors for three amplified transcripts — M-Y-C in a colorectal line, F-G-F-R-two and wild-type K-RAS in gastric lines — screening ten to fifteen candidates each, which is a small screen and they say so. The specificity in the reporter assays is real but graded, not binary. The M-Y-C sensor gave strong signal in the high-M-Y-C colorectal line and essentially nothing in H-E-K two-ninety-three. The F-G-F-R-two sensor gave about thirty-six-fold over background. And the editing rates underneath all of this are low — about five percent of transcripts edited at the stop codon for the best M-Y-C sensor. Five percent is enough, which is itself the interesting observation; you're integrating over a lot of R-N-A.

Now the C-D-nineteen result. They made stable lines with the M-Y-C sensor driving C-D-nineteen and looked at surface expression across three cell types with high, intermediate, and low endogenous M-Y-C. Sixty-eight percent of cells C-D-nineteen-positive in the high line, twenty-seven percent in the intermediate colorectal line, six percent in the low line. That's a dose-response on an intracellular transcript read out as a surface antigen, which I think is genuinely new. And it kills: both C-D-nineteen C-A-R constructs, 28-zeta and 4-1-B-B-zeta, killed the M-Y-C-sensing cells at one-to-one with 28-zeta ahead, and killed the scramble-sensor controls not at all. A C-D-nineteen-by-C-D-three bispecific engager with unmodified T cells also worked, more weakly. Activation markers moved the way they should, interleukin-two and interferon gamma came out.

Here is where I want you to look hard, because the paper's framing and its evidence diverge. The C-D-nineteen arm — the arm that matters for anybody doing cell therapy — never leaves the dish. Every in vivo experiment in this paper uses the other payload: cytosine deaminase, which converts five-fluorocytosine into five-fluorouracil. They're explicit about why. The deaminase cassette is four point three kilobases, which fits inside an A-A-V, and a small-molecule prodrug is a much simpler thing to deliver than engineered T cells. That's an honest engineering decision. It's also the reason the mouse data tells you about a prodrug strategy and not about the C-A-R strategy.

And the mouse data is mixed. Intratumoral A-A-V plus prodrug cut tumor volume about sixty-one percent — but performed about the same as the constitutive control, because at those prodrug concentrations the bystander killing swamps the selectivity. Systemically, in a liver metastasis model by tail vein, the imaging differences weren't significant and median survival went from thirty-seven days to thirty-nine. Two days. I would not build a thesis on that.

The experiment that does carry weight is the toxicity comparison. The mice that got the always-on version lost significant body weight and showed organ damage; the sensor-gated version didn't. That's the real claim — not that gating makes it work better, but that gating makes it safe enough to give systemically. Which is the same claim the whole conditional-therapy field is making, and it's the right one.

Two things I'd push on. First, six percent of the low-M-Y-C cells still turned C-D-nineteen on. If you're painting a C-A-R target onto cells, leak is not a cosmetic problem — it converts a normal cell into a target for a serial killer. They built an A-N-D gate, M-Y-C plus C-D-X-two, and it did sharpen discrimination between two colorectal lines, but it cost payload expression, and they dropped it for the in vivo work to preserve efficacy. That trade-off — specificity against expression — is the whole ballgame and it is unresolved here. Second, the paper is built on cancer lines expressing the construct stably, with A-DAR overexpressed in most experiments. The delivery question is deferred to future work on A-A-V serotypes and lipid nanoparticles. They've traded a target-discovery problem for a delivery problem.

But I'd still rather have that trade. Target discovery for solid tumors has been grinding for two decades. Delivery is a problem the entire nucleic-acid industry is already pouring money into, and anything that works for an m-R-N-A vaccine works for this. The deeper idea is that oncogene addiction — the thing that made targeted therapy work in the first place — has always been read at the protein level, where most oncoproteins are terrible drug targets. Reading it at the R-N-A level means the hard part of the biology, finding something the tumor cannot turn off, is already solved. M-Y-C amplified on extrachromosomal D-N-A is about as un-losable as a cancer dependency gets. Whether you can get the sensor into enough cells is an engineering question, and engineering questions tend to move.
