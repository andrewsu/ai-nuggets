# BMS-986453 — the BCMA×GPRC5D dual-target CAR-T posts 94% response at the top doses, and the toxicity table tells you it is an OR-gate, not an avidity gate

Primary presentation: Hansen D et al. "BMS-986453, a dual-targeting BCMA × GPRC5D autologous CAR T cell therapy in patients (pts) with relapsed/refractory multiple myeloma (RRMM): initial results from a phase 1 trial." Abstract OA-10, 23rd International Myeloma Society Annual Meeting, September 23-26 2026, Glasgow.

## Script

Yesterday I spent this show on a B-C-M-A by G-P-R-C-five-D trispecific engager and the logic of the avidity gate — two weak grips that only add up to a kill when both antigens are present on the same cell. Today, from the closing day of the same meeting in Glasgow, the same antigen pair comes back in a completely different chassis, and the design logic is not just different, it is inverted. I think the contrast is the most useful thing either dataset offers, so let me put them next to each other.

The drug is B-M-S nine-eight-six-four-five-three, an autologous C-A-R T cell carrying binders against both B-C-M-A and G-P-R-C-five-D. It comes out of Juno, which is Bristol Myers Squibb's cell therapy arm. Doris Hansen from Moffitt presented the first substantial dataset from the Phase one dose-escalation and expansion study, and the numbers are the kind that get a press release.

Fifty-eight patients treated. Median age sixty-seven, a third with high-risk cytogenetics, a median of four prior lines. Across all dose levels the response rate was eighty-three percent. At the seventy-five million and one-hundred-fifty million cell doses combined — thirty-five patients — the response rate was ninety-four percent, with complete responses in seventy-one percent. Maximum tolerated dose was not reached.

Now the safety table, because this is where the episode actually lives. Grade three or four treatment-emergent adverse events in ninety-eight percent of patients — essentially everybody — driven by cytopenias: neutropenia in eighty-eight percent, thrombocytopenia in forty-eight. Cytokine release syndrome in seventy-eight percent, but only three point four percent at grade three. There was one treatment-related death from C-R-S, at the three-hundred-million cell level. And then the line I want you to sit with: on-target, off-tumor toxicity affecting skin, nails, or oral mucosa in fifty percent of patients, nearly all of it grade two or lower.

That fifty percent is the answer to a question nobody asked out loud, and it is the reason I picked this over three other datasets from the same day.

Here is the argument. When you put two tumor antigens on one molecule or one cell, you are making a choice between two very different design philosophies, and the field uses the phrase "dual-targeting" for both of them as though they were the same thing. They are not. One is an A-N-D gate — avidity. The agent needs to engage both antigens to fire, so any normal tissue carrying only one of the two is structurally spared. That is what the trispecific engager I covered yesterday is doing, and it was measurable: more than fifty-fold more potent against cells carrying both antigens than against cells carrying either alone. The other philosophy is an O-R gate — redundancy. Either antigen is sufficient to trigger killing. You get no selectivity benefit at all; what you buy instead is coverage, because when a myeloma clone deletes one antigen and relapses, the product still works.

So which one is this? I have not seen the construct diagram, and the abstract summary does not draw it. But you do not need it, because the toxicity table already answered. G-P-R-C-five-D is a genuinely beautiful myeloma target for one reason: outside plasma cells, its expression in normal human tissue is essentially confined to hard keratinized tissue — the hair follicle, the nail bed, the tongue. That tissue does not express B-C-M-A. Not a little; not at all. So if this C-A-R required both antigens to fire, skin and nails and oral mucosa would be untouched — that is precisely the tissue an avidity gate is supposed to protect. Fifty percent of patients had exactly that toxicity. The C-A-R is firing on G-P-R-C-five-D alone. It is an O-R gate. The company descriptions, for what it is worth, say the same thing in passing — cells expressing B-C-M-A and-slash-or G-P-R-C-five-D — but the patients' fingernails are the harder evidence.

And I want to be clear that this is not a criticism. It is the correct design choice for this modality, and the reason is that a C-A-R T cell is a one-time product. You infuse it once. You do not get to escalate it, you do not get to switch it, and you cannot re-dose it the way you can hold a subcutaneous engager for a month and restart. Everything you are ever going to get from that cell, you have to build in up front. So the thing you most want to insure against is the dominant failure mode, and in B-C-M-A C-A-R T the dominant failure mode is relapse — much of it antigen-driven. A published meta-analysis pooling eighteen trials and six hundred thirty-six patients put the relapse rate after B-C-M-A C-A-R T at fifty-seven percent, against twenty-six percent for G-P-R-C-five-D directed products. B-C-M-A is also shed into serum, so there is a soluble antigen sink working against you, and biallelic deletion of the B-C-M-A locus is a documented escape route. G-P-R-C-five-D is a seven-transmembrane receptor — it is not shed, and its epitope sits close to the membrane, which makes for a tighter immune synapse. The two antigens fail in different ways and are expressed independently. If you only get one shot, you want both guns.

What you pay for it is the union of two toxicity profiles rather than the intersection. That is the trade, stated plainly, and I would like to see the field stop describing dual-targeting as though it were inherently a safety feature. In an engager it can be. In this architecture it is the opposite: you have added a second on-target organ.

Now, the part that tells you what Bristol Myers actually believes. The relapsed-refractory study is a hundred-eighty-seven-patient dose-finding program — fine, expected. But there is a second trial, and it is the interesting one. In February of this year a Phase one-b opened at the University of Alabama at Birmingham, investigator-sponsored by Susan Bal with Bristol Myers as collaborator, giving this same dual-target C-A-R T to patients with newly diagnosed myeloma — after induction, in people who are either not transplant-eligible or are deferring transplant. Twenty-five patients. And the stated purpose, in the protocol's own words, is to see whether one-time treatment can control the disease for a prolonged period and, quote, challenge continuous treatment which is otherwise prescribed.

That is a company betting that this becomes a one-and-done therapy in frontline myeloma, and it opened seven months ago, before the data I just described were public. It also lands in the same week that this meeting adopted a formal written definition of cure in myeloma — five years off therapy with serial M-R-D-negative marrows. The one-shot products are now being designed against that bar explicitly.

So what would change my mind, in either direction. Phase one, fifty-eight patients, single arm, no randomization, maximum tolerated dose not reached, one death from C-R-S, and — critically — no durability data yet. Response rate is not the discriminating variable in myeloma anymore; I said that yesterday about the engagers and it is just as true here. Ninety-four percent at the active doses is table stakes. The entire thesis of paying for a second antigen in a one-shot product is that it bends the relapse curve, and a response rate cannot tell you whether it did. That takes two years of follow-up and, honestly, a randomized comparison against a good single-target B-C-M-A C-A-R T — which Bristol Myers happens to own, in Abecma. Whether they are willing to run that head-to-head is the thing I would watch, because running it is the only way to find out whether the nails were worth it.
