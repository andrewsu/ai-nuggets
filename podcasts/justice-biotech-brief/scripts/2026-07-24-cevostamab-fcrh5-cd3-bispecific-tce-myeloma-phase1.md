# Cevostamab — FcRH5×CD3 bispecific T-cell engager posts its 324-patient phase 1 in relapsed/refractory myeloma, validating a third redirector target after BCMA and GPRC5D

Paper link: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-026-04522-3

## Script

Today's pick is a bispecific T-cell engager story, and it's one that reframes how we should think about sequencing these drugs in multiple myeloma. Nature Medicine just published the full phase-one experience with cevostamab — Genentech's Fc-R-H-five by C-D-three bispecific antibody — and this is the mature dataset, 324 patients, that the field has been waiting on.

Start with the target, because that's the whole point. Myeloma T-cell engagers have so far lived at two addresses on the plasma cell: B-C-M-A first — that's teclistamab and elranatamab — and then G-P-R-C-five-D, which is talquetamab. Cevostamab goes after a third antigen entirely, Fc-R-H-five, which sits on essentially all myeloma cells. So the immediate question this paper answers is whether you can keep redirecting T cells against a fresh myeloma antigen after the first two have been used up. And the answer is basically yes.

Who were these patients? Heavily pretreated — a median of six prior lines, nearly ninety percent triple-class refractory, and almost half had already seen B-C-M-A-directed therapy. A quarter had prior car-T, and some had prior bispecifics. This is exactly the population where you've run out of the standard redirectors.

The efficacy, at the recommended dose of 160 milligrams every three weeks. Overall response rate was about 44 percent, with a quarter of patients reaching very good partial response or better, and a median duration of response of over ten months — and for the deeper responders, past twenty-two months. Now here's the nuance that matters clinically: in patients who had never seen a B-C-M-A drug, the response rate jumped to over 60 percent with a nearly twenty-month duration, while in B-C-M-A-exposed patients responses were shallower and progression-free survival was only a couple of months. So cevostamab clearly works better earlier — but, and this is the point, it still pulls a 44 percent response rate out of a population that is largely out of options, against an antigen the tumor has never been immunologically pressured on.

Two things make this genuinely differentiated, and they're both about the profile rather than the raw response number. First, dosing is fixed-duration: seventeen cycles, and then you stop. That's a real departure from the B-C-M-A and G-P-R-C-five-D bispecifics, which are treat-to-progression, indefinite therapy — the possibility of a defined treatment course and a drug holiday is a meaningful quality-of-life and infection-risk lever. Second, the safety texture is distinct. Cytokine release syndrome was common, around 74 percent, but with the triple step-up priming schedule it was essentially all grade one or two — manageable, and that step-up dosing is the mechanism that tames it. Grade three-or-higher infections came in around 16 percent, which the authors flag as notably lower than what you see with the B-C-M-A bispecifics, where infection is a leading driver of treatment-related death. And you don't get the G-P-R-C-five-D signature toxicities — no taste disturbance, no nail and skin problems that make talquetamab so hard to live on.

Now the opinionated read. Don't take this as "cevostamab beats teclistamab." The progression-free survival overall is short — under three months in the full 160-milligram group — and in the B-C-M-A-exposed patients it's barely two months. This is not a curative single agent, and the depth of response doesn't touch what car-T delivers up front. What this paper actually establishes is a principle: myeloma T-cell engaging is now a target-sequencing problem, and Fc-R-H-five is a legitimate, non-cross-resistant third target you can deploy after B-C-M-A and G-P-R-C-five-D are exhausted. The whole logic of these drugs — that resistance to one redirector doesn't have to mean resistance to the next if you change the antigen — just got its cleanest clinical validation.

Where does it go? The obvious future for cevostamab isn't monotherapy in the last line; it's moving earlier, where the response rates are much higher, and into combinations — and Genentech has been exploring exactly that. For anyone working on T-cell engagers, the read-through is the sequencing framework: the number of distinct, well-tolerated myeloma antigens you can hit with a redirector is what determines how long you can keep a patient responding, and this pushes that count to three. Watch for the fixed-duration design and the step-up-driven cytokine-release control to become the template, and watch Fc-R-H-five show up as an arm in earlier-line and combination trials.
