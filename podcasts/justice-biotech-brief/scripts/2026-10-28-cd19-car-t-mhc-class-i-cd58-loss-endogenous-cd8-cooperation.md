# CD19 CAR T — biallelic MHC class one or CD58 loss predicts refractoriness, because endogenous CD8 T cells do half the work

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.10.23.761341v1

## Script

Today's pick is a preprint posted yesterday from Moffitt, Sylvester at Miami, and Memorial Sloan Kettering, and it takes a swing at something the field has mostly treated as settled. The claim is that CD19 CAR T cells do not cure large B-cell lymphoma by themselves. They need the patient's own non-CAR CD8 T cells to finish the job, and when the tumor has permanently blinded those endogenous T cells, the CAR T product fails — even though the CAR itself still works perfectly well.

Here's why that should be surprising. The entire design premise of a CAR is that it bypasses the T cell receptor. You engineer recognition directly, so you don't care whether the tumor presents antigen, you don't care about HLA matching, you don't care about MHC class one. That independence is the selling point. So if you sequence a cohort of CAR T patients and find that the tumors which lost MHC class one are the ones that failed, you have a result that shouldn't happen.

That's exactly what they found. Sixty-one biopsies from fifty-four patients with relapsed or refractory large B-cell lymphoma, whole genomes and transcriptomes, all treated with CD19 CAR T. A third of them were durable responders, which lines up with the published experience. Ten patients had biallelic inactivation of MHC class one. Five had biallelic inactivation of CD58 — that's the ligand for CD2, the costimulatory arm of the immune synapse. Every single one of those patients was refractory. The separation on progression-free survival is as clean as these curves get, and the association survives a multivariate model that already contains genomic complexity and CD19 expression, with a hazard ratio near six.

The detail I'd hang onto is that monoallelic loss did nothing. Twenty-one patients had one hit in MHC class one, twelve had one hit in CD58, and their outcomes were unremarkable. So this isn't a dose-response relationship with antigen presentation. It's a threshold. You have to take the pathway all the way to zero before it bites, which is the signature of something being either on or off rather than something being tuned down.

Then they did the experiment that actually makes the argument. They knocked out beta-2 microglobulin in a syngeneic mouse lymphoma line, so no surface MHC class one, CD19 intact. In a dish, those cells were killed by CD19 CAR T cells just fine — slightly better than wild type at low effector-to-target ratios, which is the expected missing-self behavior. So the CAR works. Then they put the same cells into immunocompetent mice and treated them, and the response vanished completely. Same tumor, same CAR, same killing machinery, and the only thing that changed was whether the rest of the immune system could see the target.

They nailed it down from the other direction too, in CD8-knockout hosts, using CAR T cells sorted to a hundred percent CAR positive so they weren't accidentally transferring in a few normal CD8 cells with the product. Wild type hosts cleared the tumor — every surviving animal was tumor-free at necropsy on day ninety. The CD8-deficient hosts all progressed. The CAR product was identical. The host was the variable.

The single-cell work adds a wrinkle I didn't anticipate. In the MHC-class-one-deficient tumors, it's not just that the endogenous CD8 cells fail to expand — they actually decline after treatment. And the CAR T cells themselves infiltrate the tumor much less. So losing MHC class one doesn't only cost you the second wave of killing, it somehow degrades trafficking of the engineered cells, with a myeloid-heavy, inflamed microenvironment taking over and the endogenous CD4 compartment drifting toward regulatory and Th17 states. The ligand-receptor analysis points at CXCL12-CXCR4 as a plausible exclusion mechanism. That's correlative, but it's the kind of correlative that suggests an intervention.

And then the part that will matter clinically. They ran interferon-gamma ELISPOTs on patient samples from before and after infusion, depleting the CAR-positive cells out of the post-infusion samples first, and challenged them with autologous tumor. Reactivity went up after CAR T. In mice, the endogenous T cells from treated animals responded to CD19-knockout tumor — so the new reactivity is against something other than the CAR target — but they did not respond to beta-2-microglobulin-knockout tumor, which says the acquired reactivity is running through a conventional TCR–MHC synapse. And mice cured by CD19 CAR T survived significantly longer than naive controls when rechallenged three times with escalating doses of CD19-negative lymphoma. The CAR cured them of a disease, and then they were protected against a version of that disease the CAR cannot see.

So the reframe is that CAR T is partly a vaccine. You've heard a version of this here before — the glofitamab work a couple of weeks ago made the same argument for T cell engagers through dendritic cell cross-priming. What's new is the human genomics anchoring it, and the inversion it implies for resistance. We spend a lot of effort on antigen escape — CD19 loss, antigen density, dual targeting. This cohort says the escape route that actually predicted failure wasn't escape from the CAR at all. It was escape from the T cells the CAR recruits.

The honest caveats: the biallelic groups are ten patients and five patients, in a retrospective single-arm cohort of fifty-four. A clean all-or-nothing split in a group that small can be real and can also be luck, and the p-value doesn't distinguish those. The mathematical model is explicitly framed as a plausibility argument, not a predictor. And they can't name the neoantigens the endogenous cells are responding to, so epitope spreading is inferred from function, not demonstrated at the level of a specific target. But the in vivo logic is tight, and the practical reading is immediate. If this holds, MHC class one and CD58 status belong on the pre-infusion workup, and the next generation of constructs should be judged partly on how well they wake up the cells you didn't engineer.
