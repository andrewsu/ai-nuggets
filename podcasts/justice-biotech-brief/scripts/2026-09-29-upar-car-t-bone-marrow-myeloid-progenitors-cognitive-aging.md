# uPAR CAR T — an intravenous CAR T cell that never enters the brain reverses cognitive aging by resetting the bone marrow progenitors that feed it

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.09.27.754777v1

Prior senolytic CAR T work from the same lab (Nature 2020): https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-020-2403-9

uPAR PET imaging in humans, gallium-68 NOTA-AE105, Phase 2: https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT02681640

Monopar lutetium-177 uPAR radioligand, expanded access: https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06980519

## Script

Today's pick went up on bioRxiv yesterday from Corina Amor's lab at Cold Spring Harbor, with Chris Vakoc. It is a CAR T paper, and I am covering it on this show even though there is no tumor in it anywhere, because it makes an argument about where to point a CAR that I think generalizes straight back into your world.

Here is the setup. Amor's lab built the senolytic CAR years ago — a chimeric antigen receptor against you-PAR, the urokinase plasminogen activator receptor, which is a surface marker of dysfunctional, proinflammatory, senescent cells. They showed it clears senescent cells in liver fibrosis, then that a single dose given prophylactically in young mice protects against age-related metabolic dysfunction decades later in mouse time, then the same trick in intestinal regeneration. Each of those was a new organ. This paper is the brain, and it is where the logic breaks open.

Start with the straightforward half. Aged mouse brains accumulate you-PAR on immune cells. They sorted you-PAR-positive and you-PAR-negative cells out of eighteen-month-old brains — about nine thousand positive, twenty thousand negative — and sequenced them. The dominant you-PAR-positive population is not microglia. It is border-associated macrophages: the macrophages of the meninges, the choroid plexus, and the perivascular spaces. And the you-PAR-positive ones are the inflamed subset specifically. Give one intrathecal dose of a million you-PAR CAR T cells to an old mouse and, two and a half months later, that inflamed subset is depleted, the remaining border macrophages are transcriptionally calmer, brain inflammatory cytokine protein is down, and the animals outperform controls on novel object recognition, Y-maze alternation, and Morris water maze. Still true at six months. In the P-S-nineteen tauopathy model, a single dose at two and a half months of age attenuates cognitive decline, drops tau accumulation, and increases median survival by thirty-eight percent. And a prophylactic dose at two months of age leaves the animals cognitively intact at eighteen months — sixteen months after one infusion, with CAR T cells still detectable in the brain.

That alone would be a good paper. What makes it a different kind of paper is the experiment that comes next, which I think starts as a control and turns into the thesis.

Intravenous dosing does not put these CAR T cells in the brain. They show that in the first figure — intrathecal yes, intravenous essentially no. So intravenous should be the negative control. It is not. Intravenous you-PAR CAR T also improves cognition in old mice, also depletes you-PAR-positive border macrophages, also drops brain cytokines. The clean tell is the kinetics: intrathecal takes two and a half months to show a cognitive benefit, intravenous takes six. You are not treating the brain. You are treating something slower upstream, and waiting for it to wash through.

That something is the bone marrow. Aged marrow expands an inflammatory, myeloid-biased progenitor compartment — hematopoietic stem cells, the multipotent progenitor tiers, granulocyte-monocyte progenitors — and those aged progenitors are the ones carrying surface you-PAR. Intravenous CAR T cells accumulate in the marrow with an effector phenotype, delete the you-PAR-positive fraction across all of those tiers, and, in the sequencing, the progenitors that survive look transcriptionally younger — less inflammatory, less myeloid-biased.

Then they close the loop properly, and this is the experiment I would make someone present at journal club. Treat eighteen-month-old mice intravenously with either untransduced T cells or you-PAR CAR T cells. Six weeks later, pull two hundred thousand myeloid-biased progenitors out of their marrow and transplant them into a different set of aged mice, alongside a third arm getting progenitors from a young donor. Engraftment is equal across all three arms, and — importantly — the total number of donor-derived myeloid cells that end up in the recipient brain is equal across all three arms. So the progenitors are getting there either way; this is not a trafficking effect. What differs is what they become. Recipients of progenitors from CAR-T-treated old donors have fewer you-PAR-positive donor monocytes, fewer you-PAR-positive border macrophages in the brain, and better recognition and spatial memory — at the level of the mice that received young progenitors. They back that up with bulk sequencing showing the you-PAR-positive marrow progenitors are inflammatory, and that macrophages differentiated from them in a dish inherit that inflammatory program and secrete more inflammatory protein.

So the claim is: an inflammatory state is written into the aged myeloid progenitor, it is heritable through differentiation, it rides into the brain in the progeny, and you can delete the carriers from the bloodstream with a drug that never crosses into the tissue you are treating. There is even a non-cell-autonomous kicker — the transplanted CAR-T-treated progenitors reduced myeloid bias in the recipients' own endogenous marrow.

On the human side they did the honest minimum. P-L-A-U-R, the gene for you-PAR, is highest in central nervous system macrophages in the human brain atlas, rises with age and with Alzheimer's in human perivascular macrophages, and rises with age across human marrow stem cells, progenitors, and monocytes. That is expression data, not function, and they say so.

Now where I would push. Everything here is syngeneic mouse with a mouse CAR and a mouse C-D-twenty-eight costimulatory domain, and — worth noticing — no lymphodepletion in the CAR arms at all. Busulfan appears only in the transplant experiment. A therapy that works with zero preconditioning in a mouse is not telling you what it costs in a person. And the target is not a tumor antigen sitting on expendable tissue. You-PAR has a homeostatic job in young hematopoietic stem cell mobilization, and what is being proposed is to durably delete a fraction of an aging person's hematopoietic progenitor pool with a persistent, self-amplifying cytotoxic cell. In relapsed refractory myeloma, that bargain is easy. In a cognitively normal sixty-year-old, it is a completely different conversation — and the prophylactic result, which is the most scientifically beautiful thing in the paper, is exactly the indication where the risk-benefit is hardest to defend. They also flag a real alternative explanation themselves: because there are channels connecting skull marrow to the meninges, the intrathecal arm may also be hitting progenitors rather than resident macrophages.

One more piece of context for calibration. I searched the trial registry: ninety-seven studies mention you-PAR or P-L-A-U-R, and not one of them is a CAR. The human you-PAR clinical experience is a gallium-sixty-eight PET tracer and a lutetium-one-seventy-seven radioligand from Monopar. So the target has been dosed in people and imaged in people, which is more de-risking than most preclinical CAR targets get, but the receptor itself has never been in a patient.

The reason I think this belongs on your radar even though it is neuroscience is the targeting logic. Every hard CAR problem you work on is framed as a question about the diseased tissue — antigen density on the tumor cell, epitope position, how deep into the tissue the cells get. This paper says: sometimes the tissue is downstream. The pathogenic population is being manufactured somewhere accessible, and if you can find a surface marker on the factory rather than on the product, you get to treat an organ you never enter, with a single dose, from a vein. That framing has an obvious next question in autoimmunity, where the field is currently arguing about how deeply an autoimmune CAR T has to penetrate lymphoid tissue to get a durable reset. Maybe, for some diseases, the answer is that it does not have to penetrate anything.
