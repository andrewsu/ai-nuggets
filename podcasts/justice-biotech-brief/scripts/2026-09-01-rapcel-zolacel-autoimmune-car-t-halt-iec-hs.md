# Rap-cel and zola-cel — three deaths halt the two biggest autoimmune CAR-T programs, and the toxicity is welded to the efficacy

Paper link: https://www.biopharmadive.com/news/novartis-bristol-myers-autoimmune-cell-therapy-trial-halt/829218/

## Script

The two most advanced autoimmune C-A-R-T programs in the industry both stopped this week, and if you build T-cell-redirecting therapeutics for a living, this is the most consequential thing that has happened in the field this year. Here is what is actually known, and then the part I think matters more than the headline.

Novartis placed voluntary holds on eight trials of rap-cel — rapcabtagene autoleucel, its autologous C-D-nineteen C-A-R-T — on August twenty-fourth. The trigger was three serious cases of immune effector cell-associated hemophagocytic syndrome. On Sunday night the Wall Street Journal reported, citing a Novartis spokesman, that all three of those patients died. The eight studies cover essentially the entire non-oncology footprint of the asset: lupus and lupus nephritis, systemic sclerosis, A-N-C-A-associated vasculitis, inflammatory myopathies, rheumatoid arthritis and Sjogren's, generalized myasthenia gravis, and multiple sclerosis. Separately, Bristol Myers paused enrollment across the autoimmune program for zola-cel, its C-D-nineteen Nex-T C-A-R-T, citing what it called transient and reversible inflammatory events found in routine safety surveillance. Bristol's language is much softer than Novartis's, and Bristol had a systemic sclerosis study about to open that is now deferred.

Start with the toxicity itself, because "known C-A-R-T risk" is doing a lot of unearned work in the company statements. Cytokine release syndrome is the toxicity you have a playbook for — it tracks target engagement, it shows up on a predictable schedule in the first week or two, and tocilizumab plus steroids usually resolves it. The hemophagocytic syndrome is a different animal, and the consensus definition the transplantation and cellular therapy society wrote in twenty twenty-three makes the key point explicitly: it is chronologically independent of cytokine release. This is macrophage activation. T cells, N-K cells and macrophages driving each other, ferritin in the tens of thousands, cytopenias, coagulopathy, multi-organ failure — and it can arrive after the cytokine release window has closed and the patient looks recovered. There is no established rescue algorithm. So the accurate translation of "known risk" is: known to exist, badly quantified, and without a standard treatment.

Now the part I would actually spend your thinking on. Both of these products are built on rapid manufacturing — Novartis's T-Charge, Bristol's Nex-T. That is not a supply-chain convenience. Conventional C-A-R-T sits in ex vivo culture for one to two weeks, and culture differentiates the cells; you infuse a mature, partially exhausted product. Compressing culture to roughly two days deliberately preserves a less differentiated, stem-like phenotype that does its expansion inside the patient instead of inside a bioreactor. In autoimmune disease that is the whole design rationale, because what you need is deep B-cell depletion in tissue, not just in blood, from a modest infused dose in a patient who will not tolerate aggressive lymphodepletion. In vivo expansion is how you get there. Novartis's own Phase one-two data in twenty-one patients with severe refractory lupus reported exactly that: robust expansion, deep B-cell depletion, sustained disease-activity improvement out to twelve months.

William Blair's Sami Corwin wrote in a Monday note that the faster manufacturing could be driving increased cell expansion and, with it, the toxicity — while cautioning that other factors may be involved. I think that is the right first suspect, and it has an uncomfortable implication. If expansion is the toxicity driver, it is also the efficacy driver, and it is one knob. You cannot turn it down without turning both down. And note the tell: Novartis left the two oncology trials of the same product running. The company does not think the product is broken. It thinks the population is the variable — which is another way of saying the benefit-risk denominator changed. In third-line lymphoma, a low-single-digit risk of fatal hyperinflammation is a price the disease already justifies. In rheumatoid arthritis or Sjogren's, in a patient with decades of life expectancy and approved biologics on the shelf, one death rewrites the label and the eligible population. Three stops the program.

Here is the design argument I would take back to the bench. The failure mode here is not target biology — C-D-nineteen depletion resets autoimmunity, that part keeps working. The failure mode is architectural. An autologous C-A-R-T is fire-and-forget. Once it is infused, the pharmacology is whatever the expansion kinetics dictate, you cannot dose down, and you cannot stop. Every property that makes it a one-and-done cure candidate also makes it uncontrollable after the fact. Which is why the read-across you should care about is not "which C-A-R-T company is next," it is "which architectures let you titrate potency after the patient is exposed."

That is a T-cell-engager argument, and Cullinan's C-L-N-nine-seven-eight data at rheumatology's European congress in June is the cleanest version of it. Sixty-five kilodalton C-D-nineteen-by-C-D-three engager with an albumin-binding domain for half-life, given subcutaneously, no lymphodepleting chemotherapy. In eighteen lupus patients and eleven with rheumatoid arthritis, seventy-one percent in each group improved, five lupus patients hit remission, B cells went below the limit of quantification in half the lupus and two-thirds of the R-A patients at twenty micrograms and above — and critically, they showed clearance of C-D-nineteen-positive B cells in lymph node and synovial tissue, not just blood. Most cytokine release was grade one after the first ten-microgram dose, one grade three at forty-five micrograms, no neurotoxicity. The tissue-depletion result is the one that matters, because tissue penetration was supposed to be the thing only a cell therapy could do.

Do not overread it. Engagers are not a safe harbor from this syndrome — the hemophagocytic phenotype has been reported across bispecifics, it ran about one and a half percent in the blinatumomab arm of TOWER, and the F-D-A flagged it as a class signal for bispecific antibodies at the end of twenty twenty-four. And no engager has yet demonstrated the durable, drug-free remission that is the entire premise of autoimmune C-A-R-T. The honest framing is that the engager gets you a dose-response curve and a stop button, and pays for it in chronic dosing and, so far, unproven durability.

Two things to watch. First, whether these voluntary holds convert into F-D-A clinical holds — that is the difference between a quarter and a year. Second, and this is the discriminating datum: whether Novartis discloses the lymphodepletion regimens and peak expansion in the three fatal cases. If those patients sat at the top of the expansion distribution, the platform hypothesis is largely confirmed and conventionally manufactured competitors are mostly spared. If they did not, the problem is the host — an already hyperinflammatory immune system — and every C-D-nineteen-directed therapy in autoimmunity, engagers included, inherits it.
