# ORM-6151 — Bristol Myers kills the only degrader-antibody conjugate in the clinic, and the payload is the common factor

Paper link: https://www.biospace.com/drug-development/bms-scraps-orums-targeted-degrader-after-reviewing-phase-1-data

Paper link: https://www.koreabiomed.com/news/articleView.html?idxno=33237

Trial link: https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06419634

## Script

Good morning, Justice. One pick today, and it's a negative — a Phase one asset going quietly into the bin. Normally that's a two-line item. This one is worth five minutes, because the molecule going into the bin was the only clinical test anyone had of an entire modality.

Bristol Myers Squibb has discontinued development of B-M-S nine-eight-six-four-nine-seven, the compound Orum Therapeutics sold them in twenty twenty-three as O-R-M six-one-five-one. Orum disclosed it Thursday in a regulatory filing. The stated reason, in its entirety, is that Bristol decided to stop after reviewing the clinical data for the program. No numbers. No word on whether this was safety or efficacy. Orum told the Korean press it was notified unexpectedly, and that it will need further meetings with Bristol to understand the technical issue.

The deal mechanics are quick and slightly unusual. This was an asset purchase, not a license. Bristol paid a hundred million dollars up front against a hundred and eighty million total. The remaining eighty million in milestones is now extinguished, Orum keeps the up-front, and there are no reversion rights — Orum cannot take the molecule back even if it wanted it, and its chief executive says it doesn't. About a hundred and eighty million in cash on hand as of the second quarter, no layoffs planned.

Now what the molecule actually is, because that's the part that generalizes. An anti-C-D-thirty-three antibody, carrying — instead of a cytotoxic payload — a degrader of G-S-P-T-one, the translation termination factor. That's a degrader-antibody conjugate, a DAC. The thesis behind the whole category is one you already know in its complaint form: the antibody-drug conjugate field has crowded into a handful of payload classes, mostly microtubule poisons and topoisomerase-one inhibitors, resistance to those payloads is cross-class and increasingly common, and the toxicity ceilings are payload-determined rather than target-determined. So put something mechanistically new on the antibody. A catalytic protein degrader is the most interesting version of that idea, because in principle one payload molecule can chew through many copies of its target, so you need to deliver fewer of them.

The trial was a Phase one in relapsed or refractory acute myeloid leukemia and myelodysplastic syndrome, planned for about a hundred and five patients, monotherapy plus doublet and triplet combinations with azacitidine and venetoclax, sites across the United States, Canada, France and Spain. It started in May twenty twenty-four and its primary completion was pencilled in for February of next year. The registry, as of this morning, still says recruiting — it hasn't caught up.

Here's why this matters past one asset. That compound was, as far as anyone tracking the space can tell, the only degrader-antibody conjugate that had made it into patients anywhere in the world. And capital has been piling into the category on the strength of the concept alone. In June, Johnson and Johnson paid about a billion dollars to buy Firefly Bio for its degrader-conjugate platform. In April, Roche put twenty million up front to C4 Therapeutics for two DAC programs with an option on a third and milestones that could exceed a billion. Merck K-G-a-A is also partnered with C4. And Vertex signed a multi-target option deal with Orum in twenty twenty-four that the Korean coverage values at up to nine hundred and forty-five million. That's a great deal of money riding on a modality whose one clinical readout has just been abandoned by the company that owned it.

So, my read — flagged clearly as inference, because no data was released.

Look at what G-S-P-T-one has already done in this exact disease. C-C nine-zero-zero-zero-nine was Celgene's, and then Bristol's, free-standing G-S-P-T-one molecular glue degrader. It was run in relapsed and refractory A-M-L, and it was terminated. The reason posted on the registry is lack of efficacy in the short-term acute phase. A second study, the combination study, was terminated as well, that one because business objectives had changed. So Bristol has now retired G-S-P-T-one degradation in myeloid leukemia twice — once as a systemic small molecule, and once delivered on an antibody.

The second data point is Orum's own. O-R-M five-zero-two-nine was a HER2-targeted G-S-P-T-one conjugate. It was discontinued in April of last year after serious adverse events and a death in its Phase one. The working suspicion at the time was that the linker released the degrader prematurely into circulation and the liver took the hit — and that was never confirmed. Orum treated it as a problem specific to that candidate, improved the antibody and the linker, and moved on.

That's the pattern I want to put in front of you. Three programs. Three different antigens — HER2, then C-D-thirty-three, and next C-D-one-twenty-three. One payload. And every single time, the response has been to change the antibody. The implicit theory is that this is a delivery problem.

I'd put more weight on the payload. G-S-P-T-one encodes eRF3a. It is the translation termination factor. It is essential in every dividing cell, it is abundant, and it is rapidly resynthesized. The catalytic argument for degrader payloads is at its strongest when the target is a scarce oncoprotein that you only need to nudge below a threshold. Against a high-abundance housekeeping protein, you still have to clear a large pool, inside a cell your antibody reached maybe a few thousand times — and the normal cells that pick up free payload, hepatocytes and myeloid progenitors above all, need that protein for exactly the reason the tumor does. Which means the therapeutic window is being set by payload biology in normal tissue, and that does not move when you move antigens. If the free drug could not kill blasts at a tolerated systemic dose, the conjugate has to be simultaneously more selective and deliver more. Both, at once.

The fair counter is that this is precisely what a conjugate is for, and it isn't a silly bet — the entire history of antibody-drug conjugates is narrow-window payloads made usable by targeting. And Orum's preclinical package on the next one is not nothing: picomolar potency, tolerability in non-human primates on repeat dosing, activity in T-P-fifty-three-relevant models where A-M-L has almost nothing.

But the antigen choices haven't been neutral either, and this is where I'd push back hardest. C-D-thirty-three in A-M-L has a graveyard that is specifically about liver and about marrow. Gemtuzumab ozogamicin — Mylotarg — was withdrawn in twenty ten and only came back in twenty seventeen with fractionated dosing, and hepatic veno-occlusive disease is still on its label. Vadastuximab talirine's Phase three, CASCADE, put a C-D-thirty-three conjugate on top of azacitidine or decitabine in A-M-L, and it was terminated for a higher rate of deaths, including fatal infections, in the drug arm. Bristol's Phase one was combining a C-D-thirty-three conjugate with azacitidine and venetoclax — the same backbone, in patients whose marrow is already the problem. C-D-thirty-three sits on normal myeloid precursors. So does C-D-one-twenty-three, along with basophils, plasmacytoid dendritic cells and a slice of hematopoietic stem cells; tagraxofusp, the approved C-D-one-twenty-three agent, carries a boxed warning for capillary leak.

So here is what I'd watch, and how I'd read it. Orum's next conjugate, O-R-M eleven fifty-three, pairs C-D-one-twenty-three with the same G-S-P-T-one payload. Its investigational new drug application cleared last month and the first patient is expected before the end of the year. When that dose escalation starts, don't read it for responses. Read the first two or three cohorts for transaminases, for bilirubin, and for time to count recovery. If liver signal and myelosuppression show up again on a third antigen, then the payload is the answer, and what the platform needs is a different degradation target, not a different antibody.

Second thing to watch: whether any data from the Bristol study ever surfaces. Abstract deadlines for the December hematology meeting are right about now, and a program killed on Phase one data is exactly the kind that never gets presented. If Bristol posts a why-stopped line on the trial registry, that one sentence will tell you more than the press release did.

And the transferable lesson, for anyone here building conjugates with payloads that aren't cytotoxins — and I include the KRAS-inhibitor conjugate we covered two weeks ago and the whole class of platforms selling an escape from topoisomerase-one resistance — is that a novel payload buys you a novel mechanism and a novel toxicity profile in the same purchase. The catalytic pitch is a story about the tumor cell. The margin gets decided everywhere else.
