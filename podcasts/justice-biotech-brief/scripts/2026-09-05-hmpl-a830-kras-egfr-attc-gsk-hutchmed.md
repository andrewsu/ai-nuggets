# HMPL-A830 — GSK pays $110M up front for a conjugate whose payload is a KRAS inhibitor, not a cytotoxin

Paper link: https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/09/03/3355629/0/en/hutchmed-announces-licensing-agreement-with-gsk-for-kras-egfr-antibody-conjugate-cancer-therapy.html

Trial link: https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07718581

## Script

On Thursday GSK agreed to pay HUTCHMED one hundred and ten million dollars up front, with up to one point two nine five billion in total value, for a molecule that has never been given to a human being and whose preclinical data has never been shown in public. That's the number everybody ran. It isn't the interesting part. The interesting part is what the molecule is, because H-M-P-L A-eight-three-zero quietly inverts the founding assumption of the entire antibody-drug conjugate field.

Here's the assumption. Every conjugate you and I have worked with exists because the payload is something you cannot give systemically. Deruxtecan, monomethyl auristatin E, calicheamicin, exatecan — these are chemotherapies with essentially no therapeutic window on their own. The antibody is a delivery address, and the whole engineering exercise is rescuing the index of a drug that has none. Potency is free. Selectivity is the entire problem.

Now look at what HUTCHMED conjugated. The payload is a K-RAS inhibitor. K-RAS inhibitors are oral pills. Sotorasib, adagrasib, and as of August twenty-sixth daraxonrasib, which the FDA approved in metastatic pancreatic adenocarcinoma. You can simply prescribe these. So why on earth would you spend an antibody on one?

There are two answers and both of them are good.

The first is the therapeutic window, and daraxonrasib's own label makes the case better than any slide deck could. Rash in eighty-eight percent of patients. Diarrhea in forty-six. Stomatitis in forty. Warnings for gastrointestinal perforation and for dermatologic and soft tissue toxicity. None of that is off-target promiscuity — that's a multi-selective RAS-ON inhibitor doing exactly what it was designed to do, in skin and in gut, where wild-type RAS is load-bearing. The dose is capped by normal tissue rather than by tumor biology. That is a real therapeutic-index problem and antibody-directed delivery is a real answer to it.

The second answer is the one I think actually drove the deal, and it's the reason the antibody here is anti-E-G-F-R specifically. In colorectal cancer, anti-E-G-F-R is not a delivery vehicle. It's the clinically validated combination partner. Sotorasib as a single agent in K-RAS G twelve C colorectal was a disappointment while the same drug in lung was a success, and the reason is adaptive E-G-F-R reactivation: you shut down K-RAS, upstream receptor signaling floods back within days, and the pathway reopens. CodeBreaK three hundred is the fix. Sotorasib plus panitumumab gave a twenty-six percent response rate against zero percent for investigator's choice, and median progression-free survival of five point six months against two. FDA approved that combination in January twenty twenty-five. Adagrasib plus cetuximab got there first, on accelerated approval.

So what HUTCHMED has built is an approved drug combination collapsed into a single molecule. The antibody does pharmacology at the cell surface — blocking the precise resistance node — then internalizes, traffics to lysosome, and releases a payload that does pharmacology inside. The address and the second drug are the same protein. That is a genuinely different design logic from a TROP2 topoisomerase conjugate, and it earns the new name they've given it: A-T-T-C, antibody-targeted therapy conjugate. For once the acronym is doing work.

And this isn't one asset, it's a factory. Their first, A-two-five-one, was a P-I-three-K and P-I-K-K inhibitor payload on an anti-HER2 antibody through a cleavable linker; first patient dosed in December. Their second, A-five-eight-zero, is the same payload on an anti-E-G-F-R antibody, dosing since March in China and the United States, and it has a real AACR poster behind it — payload I-C fifties of one to ten nanomolar across the P-I-three-K and P-I-K-K families, clean across four hundred and eighteen kinases, active in thirty-eight cell lines and in xenografts. The backbone stays fixed; the payload rotates. That's a platform bet that the binding constraint in targeted therapy is not potency but window, and that an antibody is the fix.

Which brings me to the thing that decides whether any of this works, and it's a question HUTCHMED has not answered.

E-G-F-R's highest normal-tissue expression is in skin and gastrointestinal epithelium. That's why cetuximab and panitumumab cause acneiform rash and diarrhea and hypomagnesemia — it's on-target toxicity in the tissues that need the receptor. Now go back and look at where daraxonrasib hurts people. Rash. Diarrhea. Stomatitis. Gut perforation. Skin and gut. The delivery vector points directly at the two tissues that were already the ceiling.

If the payload is a multi-selective RAS-ON inhibitor, that's not a therapeutic index, it's a targeting system aimed at the toxicity. If the payload is mutant-selective — a G twelve D inhibitor, say — then the argument works beautifully, because normal skin and normal gut simply have no target for it, and those tissues eat the antibody's toxicity without eating the payload's. HUTCHMED's phrasing is "a highly selective K-RAS small-molecule inhibitor payload." The word "selective" is carrying an enormous load in that sentence, and it is not the same word as "mutant-selective." They have never disclosed a standalone K-RAS program, so we have no independent read on the chemistry. That single unstated fact is, to me, the whole investment case.

Two more things I'd hold against it. You lose independent dosing. With sotorasib plus panitumumab you titrate each drug separately. In a conjugate the ratio is welded in at whatever drug-to-antibody ratio the conjugation chemistry hands you, and you get one shot at guessing the right stoichiometry of receptor blockade to K-RAS inhibition. If that optimum differs between colorectal and pancreatic — and it will, because anti-E-G-F-R antibodies have never worked in pancreatic cancer, and while roughly ninety percent of pancreatic tumors are K-RAS mutant, almost none of them are G twelve C — you cannot adjust it.

And the protocol tells you what they're bracing for. It's a Phase one and two-A, a hundred and forty-seven patients across seven Chinese sites, no one dosed yet. They're measuring pharmacokinetics on three species — intact conjugate, total antibody, and free payload — which is standard. But they exclude strong C-Y-P three A four inhibitors, they exclude inhibitors of P-glycoprotein and B-C-R-P, and they exclude anyone with a history of inflammatory bowel disease. That is a protocol written by people who expect meaningful circulating free payload with real small-molecule pharmacology, and who expect gut toxicity. Which means the therapeutic-index claim here is quantitative, not categorical.

How to price it: a hundred and ten million against one point three billion is about eight and a half percent up front. That's option pricing on a preclinical asset, and GSK also took a right of first negotiation on an earlier-stage A-T-T-C, so they bought the platform thesis at least as much as the molecule. Notice which one they took, though. Not A-five-eight-zero, which has published preclinical data and is already dosing patients in the United States. They took the one with neither. GSK has no RAS asset at all, and its only real colorectal foothold is dostarlimab in mismatch-repair-deficient disease, which is a small and largely RAS-indifferent niche. Colorectal and pancreatic are where RAS lives, and that's what a hundred and ten million bought — a seat, early.

The first readout I want out of dose escalation is not response rate. It's the free-payload exposure curve and the grade of rash and diarrhea at the recommended dose, placed next to daraxonrasib's numbers. If A-eight-three-zero delivers K-RAS inhibition to tumor at exposures that would be intolerable by mouth, and the skin and gut look like cetuximab alone rather than cetuximab plus a RAS inhibitor, then the A-T-T-C thesis is real and rotating payloads onto that backbone becomes a franchise. If the rash rate looks like the sum of both drugs, then what's been built is an extremely expensive fixed-dose combination that you cannot titrate.
