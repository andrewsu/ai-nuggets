# TS-104 — a five-integrin peptide-MMAE conjugate clears IND, and its best data is rescuing tumors that escaped sigvotatug vedotin

## Script

Good morning, Justice. One pick today, and it's a small company you probably haven't been tracking, but the preclinical package underneath it does something I've been waiting for someone to do properly.

TwoStep Therapeutics, out of San Carlos, announced yesterday an oversubscribed sixty-two and a half million dollar Series A alongside F-D-A clearance of the I-N-D for their lead program, T-S one-oh-four. Insight Partners and Medical Excellence Capital led, with M Ventures — that's Merck K-G-a-A's corporate venture arm — and Pfizer Ventures joining as co-leads. Total raised is now seventy-one million. First patient expected before the end of the year. And I'll flag the Pfizer Ventures line item now, because it's going to look strange in about three minutes.

Here's the molecule. Instead of an antibody, the targeting component is a synthetic peptide they call PIP, for polyspecific integrin-binding peptide. It's an engineered knottin — a disulfide-constrained cystine-knot scaffold, four to five kilodaltons, fully synthetic, site-specific linker chemistry, and cross-reactive across human, monkey, rat and mouse. It binds five different tumor-associated integrins with high affinity: alpha-v beta one, alpha-v beta three, alpha-v beta five, alpha-v beta six, and alpha five beta one. Conjugate one M-M-A-E to it through a clinically validated linker and you have T-S one-oh-four.

The lineage is worth knowing, because this is a fifteen-year-old imaging program that finally grew a payload. Jennifer Cochran's lab at Stanford spent the late two-thousands doing directed evolution on a squash trypsin inhibitor knottin to get high-affinity binders against alpha-v beta three, alpha-v beta five and alpha five beta one, and those variants became P-E-T and near-infrared imaging probes — tumor imaging agents, not drugs. The company is co-founded with Cochran, with Ron Levy, and with Carolyn Bertozzi, whose contribution you can read off the conjugation chemistry. So the binder has been characterized in living animals for over a decade before anyone hung a cytotoxin on it.

Now the thesis, because the thesis is the interesting part and it's a direct argument against how the ADC field has been operating. Every targeted conjugate you and I have talked about this year bets on one antigen being high and uniform. And the field's own data keeps saying that's the wrong bet — expression varies across tumor types, across patients, and across cells inside a single tumor, so the addressable population shrinks and the antigen-low cells escape. TwoStep's answer is not a better antibody or a stronger payload. It's to stop depending on any single antigen. Bind five integrins, sum the receptor pool, and let heterogeneity in any one of them wash out.

They tested that in the sharpest way available, and this is the experiment that made me pick the story. They ran T-S one-oh-four head to head against sigvotatug vedotin — the integrin beta six ADC, same M-M-A-E payload — in a head and neck cell-line model chosen because it carries more than twenty-five thousand receptors for both agents. Sigvotatug vedotin induced strong initial responses and then the tumors became resistant and progressed on treatment. They harvested the resistant tumors and stained them. Alpha-v beta six went from an H-score of forty-two in vehicle tumors down to two. Total alpha-v integrin only fell from ninety-one to forty-eight. So the escape mechanism was outgrowth of an antigen-negative subclone, and the other four addresses were still on the cell surface. Then they took mice whose tumors had already become resistant, split them into two arms at matched tumor volume, kept one on sigvotatug vedotin and switched the other to T-S one-oh-four — and the switched arm regressed.

You covered sigvotatug vedotin on this show in June, when Pfizer's Phase three missed overall survival against docetaxel in the full population. The read at the time was a payload-mechanism trap — M-M-A-E stacked on taxane-experienced patients. This poster proposes a second, additive failure mode: even where the payload has a window, single-integrin targeting selects for antigen loss. Neither explanation excludes the other, and this is a mouse. But it's a mechanistic story with an intervention attached, which is more than most resistance papers manage. And that's why Pfizer Ventures on the cap table reads oddly — they've co-led a round for the molecule whose flagship data set is beating and then rescuing their own Phase three asset.

The second experiment I'd weight heavily is the cleanest control in the whole deck. They compared T-S one-oh-four against zelenectide pevedotin — Bicycle's Nectin-four bicyclic peptide conjugate, the most clinically advanced molecule in this format — in a gastric model picked because it expresses similar receptor numbers for both. Same molecular weight class, exactly one M-M-A-E per peptide each, same molar dose. That comparison isolates the binder, not the format, not the payload, not the drug-to-antibody ratio. T-S one-oh-four was substantially better, and they attribute it to faster internalization. Bicycle, for its part, converted that program from a pivotal Phase two-three to a randomized Phase two and deprioritized it, pivoting to their radioconjugates. Which is the honest frame for the whole package: both of TwoStep's benchmarks are wounded, so clearing them is a softer bar than it looks on the slide.

Four things I'd push on. First, and this is the load-bearing one — integrins are a graveyard as drug targets, and the reason is that alpha-v beta three, alpha-v beta five and alpha five beta one are all over angiogenic endothelium, fibroblasts and osteoclasts. Alpha-v beta six is the genuinely epithelial-restricted one. So broadening from one integrin to five buys tumor coverage and simultaneously buys normal-tissue footprint, and the only thing standing between those is their claim of preferential binding to active integrin conformations. That claim is stated, not quantified anywhere in the public data, and it is doing all of the specificity work. The counterweight is real, though: the S-P-E-C-T imaging with an indium-labeled construct is clean, tumor-enriched, fast renal clearance, minimal kidney retention, and PIP cross-reacts with mouse integrins — so the absence of healthy-tissue uptake isn't the artifact you get from a human-only binder. Thirty-two-day repeat-dose studies in rats and non-human primates showed no target-dependent toxicity, with the findings at high dose being the transient hematotoxicity you expect from M-M-A-E.

Second, the payload is M-M-A-E. There's no new pharmacology here, which is a deliberate de-risking choice, and it also means this molecule inherits every tubulin-inhibitor liability — including the taxane-crossresistance problem that arguably sank the comparator. Third, the ovarian comparison against Elahere is the weakest slide: the model expresses about twenty-two thousand folate-receptor-alpha copies per cell and Elahere failed to regress it, which tells you more about the model than about the molecules. And the one patient-derived xenograft out of nine that didn't respond is explained as expressing an unnamed M-M-A-E resistance protein — presumably an efflux pump, and it would have cost them nothing to say so.

Fourth, and this is the strategic problem rather than a data problem. The pitch is breadth without patient selection, so the Phase one will enroll all-comers with no enrichment biomarker. That's the point of the platform, but it means a middling response rate across a basket will be very hard to read — you won't know whether the targeting failed or you drew the wrong tumors. Every cell line they tested cleared ten thousand receptors, so their answer is that everybody is target-positive and selection is unnecessary. I'd want an integrin-quantified retrospective cut planned from day one anyway.

Net take. The interesting claim is not the peptide format — Bicycle already proved small conjugates clear fast and spare skin and nerve. The interesting claim is polyspecificity as an answer to antigen heterogeneity and antigen-loss escape, and they've supported it with the one experiment that actually tests it rather than asserting it from expression data. Nine patient-derived xenografts with regression in eight, five complete, is a strong breadth package for an I-N-D. What to watch is whether the conformation-selectivity story survives contact with a human dose-escalation, because if the tumor-selectivity margin is narrower in patients than in mice, five integrins is five times the problem rather than five times the coverage. Both links are in the show notes — take the poster, it's the substance.

That's the brief. Back tomorrow.
