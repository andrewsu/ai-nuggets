# Miv-cel — the autoimmune C-A-R T with a clean safety record, while two rivals' are paused after patient deaths

Paper link: https://www.biospace.com/drug-development/weighing-the-risks-of-car-t-in-autoimmune-disease-as-kyverna-nears-the-market

Novartis pause after three IEC-HS deaths (Sept 1 BioSpace report): https://www.biospace.com/drug-development/novartis-pauses-car-t-studies-due-to-3-deaths-bms-follows-suit-after-safety-events

Kyverna's one-year KYSA-8 durability data (Sept 24 press release coverage): https://www.biospace.com/drug-development/kyverna-steps-closer-to-fda-first-car-t-for-an-autoimmune-condition-with-durability-data

## Script

BioSpace ran a piece today that puts three C-D-nineteen-directed C-A-R T programs for autoimmune disease side by side, and the safety divergence between them is the actual story. Novartis paused its candidate, rap-cel, after three patients died of a hyperinflammatory complication called immune effector cell-associated hemophagocytic syndrome, or I-E-C-H-S. Bristol Myers Squibb paused its own candidate, zola-cel, after separate but related inflammatory events. And Kyverna, running the same basic playbook — deplete C-D-nineteen-positive B cells with a C-A-R T, let the immune system reset — just posted one-year data in twenty-six patients with stiff person syndrome with no high-grade cytokine release syndrome, no neurotoxicity, and none of the hemophagocytic syndrome that killed Novartis's patients. Kyverna is on track to file for approval by the end of this year, which would be the first C-A-R T ever cleared for an autoimmune indication.

Here's why that's worth more than a company-by-company scorecard. All three products are going after the same target with the same conceptual rationale. Ever since Georg Schett's group put eight lupus patients into drug-free remission with a C-D-nineteen C-A-R T back in twenty twenty-three — a result solid enough to land in the New England Journal a few months later — pretty much every oncology C-A-R T company looked at that result and saw a new market with vastly more patients than cancer ever offers. What today's piece documents is that the label "C-D-nineteen C-A-R T" is doing a lot of work to hide real engineering differences underneath it. Same target, three different safety profiles.

The mechanistic hypothesis floated by people in the field — and it is a hypothesis, not a demonstrated result — is manufacturing. Novartis and B-M-S have both been shortening the protocol between collecting a patient's T cells and infusing the finished product back in, to get cells out faster. The suggestion is that compressing that expansion window pushes the cells toward a more activated, more cytokine-prone state by the time they're infused, independent of anything about the construct itself. Kyverna, by contrast, is using an established expansion process paired with a fully human C-D-twenty-eight costimulatory domain that the company says was deliberately chosen for a tamer activation profile. If that holds up, it means the safety signal that sank two competitors' programs was never really about whether C-A-R T belongs in autoimmune disease at all — it was about a specific manufacturing choice that happens to be common in this generation of autoimmune constructs.

The reason this matters more here than it would in oncology is the risk-benefit math is inverted. A hyperinflammatory syndrome that kills three patients is a cost oncology has absorbed for decades, because the alternative is a fatal cancer. Stiff person syndrome, lupus, myositis — these are chronic and disabling, genuinely severe, but for the most part not fatal. Kyverna's C-E-O makes the disability case hard, noting that most stiff person syndrome patients end up needing a walker or wheelchair. That's real. But it doesn't change the fact that a treatment-related death rate tolerable in relapsed lymphoma is very plausibly disqualifying in a disease where patients survive decades without the drug. Which is exactly why Novartis and B-M-S paused rather than pushed forward, and why Kyverna's clean profile is doing so much commercial work for it right now.

Before anyone treats this as settled, two things are worth holding onto. First, twenty-six patients with zero serious events is reassuring, but it is not statistical proof of a safer construct — three deaths at a competitor is also a small-number event, and we don't yet know whether it reflects the product, the manufacturing change, or something about the specific patient populations each company enrolled. Second, nobody has actually run the controlled experiment that would isolate manufacturing protocol from construct design from patient selection. Right now this is pattern-matching across three uncontrolled programs, dressed up as a mechanistic explanation because it's the most plausible one available. It's a good hypothesis. It is not yet a confirmed one. What would confirm it is Novartis or B-M-S modifying their expansion protocol and rerunning a trial — and whether either company is willing to do that, rather than quietly deprioritizing autoimmune disease altogether, is the thing actually worth watching over the next year.
