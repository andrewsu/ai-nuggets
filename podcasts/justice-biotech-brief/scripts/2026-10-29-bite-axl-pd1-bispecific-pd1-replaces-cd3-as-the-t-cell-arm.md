# BiTE-AXL/PD-1 — a bispecific that uses PD-1, not CD3, as its T cell arm

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.10.26.761755v1

## Script

Almost every T cell engager in the clinic shares one design decision: the T cell arm binds C-D-three. That choice buys you universality — every T cell has a C-D-three complex, so you can redirect the entire repertoire — and it costs you specificity, because every T cell in the body is now a substrate, which is where cytokine release syndrome and the step-up dosing and the hospital monitoring all come from. A group at Ben-Gurion University posted a preprint yesterday that takes the other branch. Their engager binds P-D-one.

That's a real swap, not a cosmetic one. P-D-one is on tumor-infiltrating, antigen-experienced T cells — cells that have already seen antigen and been chronically stimulated. It is mostly not on the naive circulating pool. So a P-D-one-targeted engager is, in principle, self-selecting for the T cells you actually want to put next to a tumor cell, and self-sparing for the ones whose activation produces nothing but toxicity. And because the arm is an anti-P-D-one antibody, the same binding event that tethers the cell also blocks the checkpoint. One molecule, two jobs, no extra engineering.

The other arm goes to A-X-L, and the choice of A-X-L is what makes this more than a format exercise. A-X-L is a receptor tyrosine kinase that shows up on tumor cells, on cancer-associated fibroblasts, on macrophages and on dendritic cells, and in all of those places it pushes the same direction: it induces P-D-L-one and P-D-L-two, it pushes macrophages toward an immunosuppressive phenotype, and it blunts antigen presentation. High A-X-L correlates with both primary and acquired resistance to P-D-one blockade in patients. People have already tried combining A-X-L inhibitors with checkpoint antibodies, and the results are the usual lukewarm combination results. The argument here is that the two drugs circulate independently and bind stochastically, so nothing guarantees that the T cell you just unblocked ends up anywhere near the cell that's suppressing it. The bispecific makes that proximity a property of the molecule instead of a matter of luck.

Now the part I think is the most transferable finding in the paper, and it's buried in a supplementary figure. They built three tetravalent formats: two with the two single-chain fragments in tandem on the same side of the Fc, and one non-tandem, with one specificity on each side. Then they ranked them. The non-tandem version had the *weakest* measured binding of the three — and the *strongest* functional activation in their P-D-one reporter assay. They picked the worst binder and it was the right call. If you have been listening to this show for the last couple of weeks you have heard a version of this before, with the C-D-three arm and antibody valency: the thing that determines whether an engager works is the geometry it imposes on the synapse, and affinity measured on a chip is a poor proxy for that. Here's the same lesson from a different direction. The affinity data make the point again — in the bispecific the A-X-L arm binds at about sixty nanomolar against twenty for the monospecific parent, so the tumor-side arm got weaker, while the P-D-one arm stayed at roughly six nanomolar with a slow off-rate. Strong on the T cell, weak on the tumor. That is the inverse of how C-D-three engagers are normally detuned, and nobody should assume it's safe by analogy.

The bridging evidence is reasonably well controlled. In co-culture, the bispecific roughly doubled tumor-T cell doublets; adding either monospecific antibody back as a competitor collapsed the effect; knocking down A-X-L on the tumor cells reduced it. In a patient-derived system, tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes co-cultured with A-X-L-high melanoma made significantly more interferon gamma with the bispecific than with the two separate antibodies, and A-X-L-low melanoma showed no such benefit. That A-X-L-low arm is the control that matters, and it behaved.

In vivo is where I'd temper things. In humanized mice reconstituted with donor P-B-M-Cs, the bispecific significantly delayed growth of a subcutaneous head and neck tumor relative to untreated — but in the orthotopic lip model, the advantage over vehicle and over the combination was a trend that did not reach significance, and the authors say so. The mouse surrogate in fully immunocompetent syngeneic models did better, beating the combination in two different head and neck lines. Across all of it, the result is growth delay, not regression. Pharmacokinetics look workable — about a three-and-a-half-day serum half-life and tumor localization on imaging within twenty-four hours.

The spatial imaging is the most interesting biology and the thinnest statistics. Eighteen-marker multiplexed imaging, three animals per group, tumors split into concentric zones from margin to deep core. In vehicle and combination-treated tumors, dendritic cell density falls off a cliff as you move inward. In bispecific-treated tumors it doesn't — dendritic cells and C-D-eight T cells reach the mid and deep core, which the other groups simply don't. Granzyme-B-positive C-D-eight cells went up against both comparators. Two negatives are worth as much as the positives: exhausted C-D-eight cells, by T-O-X and L-A-G-three and T-I-M-three, were *not* reduced, and regulatory T cells were unchanged and stayed stuck at the margin. That second one is the obvious objection to this whole design — P-D-one is on Tregs too, so an engager using it as a handle could be recruiting exactly the wrong cells — and the data say it didn't happen here. With three mice per arm, I'd call that encouraging rather than settled.

One caveat the paper doesn't dwell on and I would. The workhorse P-D-one readout is an engineered reporter line in which P-D-one is fused to a zeta signaling chain, so engaging the receptor produces interleukin-two. That construct converts P-D-one occupancy into an activating signal by design. It is a clean way to measure whether your arm binds and clusters, and a bad way to measure whether you have relieved inhibition — and cross-linking an inhibitory receptor against a P-D-L-one-bearing cell is precisely the scenario where you'd want to know the difference. They do add a partial answer, showing the interleukin-two gain survives P-D-L-one and P-D-L-two blockade. The thing I'd want before believing the concept is the same experiment in primary human T cells with native P-D-one signaling intact.

Where this leaves me: the A-X-L biology is the weaker half of the pitch, because A-X-L has disappointed repeatedly as a target. The format is the stronger half. If you can use a checkpoint receptor as a targeting handle and get synapse-forming proximity plus blockade out of one molecule without C-D-three's toxicity tax, that generalizes to every suppressive surface protein in the microenvironment, and A-X-L is just the first one somebody tried it on.
