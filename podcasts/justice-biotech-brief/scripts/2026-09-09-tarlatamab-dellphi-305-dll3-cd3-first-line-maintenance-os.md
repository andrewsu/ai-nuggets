# Tarlatamab — DLL3xCD3 engager wins overall survival in first-line maintenance small cell lung cancer

Press release: https://www.amgen.com/newsroom/press-releases/2026/09/imdelltra-in-combination-with-imfinzi-demonstrated-landmark-improvement-in-overall-survival-in-first-line-extensive-stage-small-cell-lung-cancer

Trial record: https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06211036

## Script

Yesterday I spent the whole episode on a retrospective preprint arguing that D-L-L-three by immunohistochemistry predicts who benefits from tarlatamab. Today Amgen announced that tarlatamab won a Phase 3 in first-line maintenance small cell lung cancer, in a population where nobody measured D-L-L-three at all. So we're staying on the same molecule, because the two stories are the same argument seen from opposite ends, and because this readout is the biggest thing to happen to the T cell engager field in solid tumors since the drug was approved.

Here's the announcement. DeLLphi-305 is a global open-label Phase 3 that took five hundred sixty-three patients with extensive-stage small cell lung cancer who had completed three or four cycles of platinum, etoposide and durvalumab without progressing, and randomized them one-to-one to tarlatamab plus durvalumab or durvalumab alone as maintenance, until progression. It hit its primary endpoint of overall survival at a pre-specified interim analysis. Progression-free survival and objective response rate, both secondary, also hit. Safety was described as consistent with the known profiles of the two drugs with no new signals. Amgen used the word landmark twice and disclosed exactly zero numbers, which we'll come back to.

Why this matters more than a fifth positive trial for an approved drug normally would: this is the first time a C-D-three bispecific has been tested in solid tumors in a setting where the patient's T cells are in decent shape. Think about how engagers actually get developed. You run them in the most refractory population you can find, because that's where the regulatory path is shortest and the ethics are easiest. Which means every efficacy number the class has ever produced in solid tumors came out of patients who had been through multiple lines of cytotoxic chemotherapy, whose marrow was flattened, whose repertoire was depleted, and whose remaining T cells were the ones that had survived that. Then the field turns around and complains that engagers underperform in solid tumors relative to what the biology promises.

Maintenance inverts that. These patients finished induction, they didn't progress, their disease burden is at its minimum, they're ECOG zero or one, and their T cells have seen four cycles of carboplatin and etoposide but not the years of salvage therapy a third-line patient has. If the redirect-a-T-cell thesis is right, this is where the effect size should be largest. DeLLphi-305 is the first clean read on that hypothesis in a solid tumor, and it read out positive. That generalizes past D-L-L-three and past lung — if you're building an engager against any surface antigen, the developmental lesson is that the line of therapy you pick may matter more than the affinity you engineered.

There's also a plain arithmetic reason this trial matters, and Amgen's own release states it: only about forty percent of extensive-stage patients ever receive second-line treatment. Tarlatamab in the relapsed setting, however good, can only ever reach that forty percent. Everyone else dies before they get there. Moving the drug into maintenance means the whole post-induction population is exposed. That's a distribution argument, not a biology argument, and in a disease with a median survival of roughly one year from the start of maintenance it may be the larger contributor to population-level benefit.

Now the detail in this release I find most interesting, and it is not the efficacy claim. It's the monitoring schedule. Tarlatamab's current label requires patients to be monitored in a healthcare facility for twenty-two to twenty-four hours after the first and second doses, and to stay within an hour of a hospital, with a caregiver, for a full forty-eight hours after each. That requirement is the single biggest reason this drug lives in academic centers. DeLLphi-305 ran on one to two hours of monitoring — six to eight in Europe — and Amgen says no new or unexpected safety signals emerged. If that holds when the detailed data show up, the operational barrier that has confined C-D-three bispecifics to tertiary care just got dramatically smaller, and it got smaller in the population where it matters most, because you cannot ask an asymptomatic maintenance patient to spend two days near a hospital every cycle. Cytokine release syndrome in the pooled label population runs fifty-seven percent, neurologic toxicity sixty-five percent, I-C-A-N-S ten percent. Those are the numbers this trial is implicitly claiming you can manage in an outpatient afternoon.

Where I'd push back. The comparator is durvalumab alone, which is the right control for the question asked but is no longer the only maintenance option. IMforte, published in the Lancet last year, put lurbinectedin plus atezolizumab into this exact slot with a progression-free survival hazard ratio of point-five-four and an overall survival hazard ratio of point-seven-three. So there is already an active maintenance regimen here, and the question the field actually needs answered — tarlatamab versus lurbinectedin — is not being asked by anyone. What tarlatamab has going for it in that unrun comparison is orthogonal toxicity. IMforte bought its benefit with thirty-eight percent grade three or four adverse events versus twenty-two percent on atezolizumab alone, driven by myelosuppression, in patients whose marrow had just absorbed four cycles of carboplatin and etoposide. Tarlatamab's problems are cytokine release, neurotoxicity and infection. Those don't stack on the same organ.

The rest of the caveats are the ordinary ones and you should hold them firmly. No numbers means landmark is unpriced — an interim hazard ratio crossing a pre-specified boundary early is by construction the most optimistic estimate the trial will ever produce. Post-induction randomization selects for people who already didn't progress, so this is a favorable population by design. Anyone who had received or was planning consolidative chest radiation was excluded, which cuts against a real practice pattern. And there is no D-L-L-three criterion anywhere in the eligibility — I checked the trial record — so this is an unselected population.

Which is exactly the thread from yesterday. If baseline D-L-L-three really does separate ten months of time on treatment from three, then an unselected maintenance cohort is averaging over two quite different drugs, and the aggregate hazard ratio is diluted by the third of patients who were never going to hold. The number to demand at the congress presentation is not the overall survival curve. It's whether they banked tissue, and what the D-L-L-three-stratified analysis looks like. Amgen has the assay. AbbVie built it a decade ago for a drug that failed on its payload. Somebody should run the test.

One more open question worth flagging. DeLLphi-312 is testing tarlatamab concurrently with durvalumab, carboplatin and etoposide as induction and then maintenance. Maintenance winning tells you nothing about whether concurrent is better — it's plausible that adding an engager to active cytotoxic induction wrecks the T cells you're trying to redirect, and equally plausible that antigen release during chemotherapy is when you most want them there. That trial is the one that decides whether this class belongs in the front line or only after it.
