# IFX-101 and IFX-201 — Infinimmune's IL-22 and IL-13 antibodies raise $75M from Regeneron and Merck to chase Dupixent in eczema

Paper link: https://www.biopharmadive.com/news/infinimmune-atopic-dermatitis-series-a-financing-dupixent/827504/

## Script

Today's pick is a financing, not a data drop, and I want to be upfront about that, because the interesting part has almost nothing to do with the two drugs at the center of it. On Monday, a startup called Infinimmune came out of the gate with a seventy-five-million-dollar Series A. It's based in Alameda, California, it was founded by people who came out of ten-x Genomics — the single-cell sequencing company — and the framing everywhere was "chasing Dupixent." Another antibody company aiming at atopic dermatitis, the eczema market that Regeneron and Sanofi turned into one of the biggest drug franchises on earth. And if that were the whole story, I'd skip it. Another me-too eczema antibody is not worth your five minutes.

Here's what makes me stop: look at who wrote the checks. The round was co-led by Playground Global and by Regeneron's venture arm. Regeneron — the company that makes Dupixent. Alongside them, R-A Capital, the Merck Global Health Innovation Fund — that's Merck-and-Company's fund — and nine other backers. And Infinimmune already had prior research collaborations with both Merck and Immunome. So the incumbent whose franchise this company is explicitly targeting just helped fund it, and a second big pharma is in both the cap table and a research deal. When the incumbent backs the challenger, it is not betting that these two antibodies dethrone its own blockbuster. It is buying an option on the platform. And the platform is the actual asset here.

That's the lens I'd use. Infinimmune is a discovery-engine company wearing eczema-drug clothing. The founders come out of the single-cell genomics world, and their pitch is a discovery approach that reads out human antibody repertoires and yields molecules they claim are more durable and more potent than what conventional discovery produces. Now, "more durable and more powerful" is exactly the kind of unquantified platform promise you should hold at arm's length — there's no data behind it yet, and every antibody startup says some version of it. But the syndicate tells you sophisticated people who have seen the platform think there's something to it. Regeneron built its whole empire on antibody-discovery technology. If Regeneron's venture arm is paying to sit close to somebody else's discovery engine, that's a more interesting signal than any slide in the pitch deck.

Now the two lead molecules, because the target choice is where the strategy shows. IFX-two-oh-one is an antibody against interleukin thirteen. That is the safe, de-risked bet. Dupixent works by blocking the interleukin-four receptor alpha chain, which shuts down both interleukin four and interleukin thirteen signaling, and there are already two approved standalone interleukin-thirteen blockers — lebrikizumab and tralokinumab. So the biology is validated to the hilt. IFX-two-oh-one isn't competing on novelty; it's competing on durability and dosing — the pitch is a longer-acting, harder-hitting version of a mechanism that already prints money. The other one, IFX-one-oh-one, is the swing. It targets interleukin twenty-two, and they're calling it potentially first-and-best-in-class. Interleukin twenty-two is not a validated atopic-dermatitis driver the way interleukin thirteen is — it's more established in psoriasis, in inflammatory bowel disease, in the kidney. So IFX-one-oh-one is the platform showing off: go after a target the field hasn't de-risked, in a disease where it's a genuine hypothesis, and if the antibody is as good as advertised, you've made a first-in-class drug rather than a fourth-in-class one. And notice both targets, interleukin twenty-two and interleukin thirteen, reach well beyond eczema — into I-g-A nephropathy, into ulcerative colitis. The eczema indication is the beachhead, not the ambition.

So how should you hold this? Not as a drug story — there is no clinical data, both molecules are only slated to reach the clinic in twenty twenty-seven, and the CEO himself described atopic dermatitis as "clinically crowded, but not commercially crowded," which is a polite way of saying the science is settled and the fight is now about who makes the better molecule and sells it better. Hold it instead as a read on where antibody-engineering value is migrating. Think about the arc we've been on the last couple of weeks — Attovia taking its V-H-H multispecific platform public, sonelokimab validating the Nanobody format in phase three. Those were format stories: new molecular architectures. This is the same theme from the discovery side: a single-cell-genomics-native company claiming it can mine human repertoires for better antibodies, and getting the incumbent it's targeting plus a second pharma to pay for a front-row seat. The two eczema antibodies may or may not become drugs. The bet the smart money is actually making is on the engine that made them.
