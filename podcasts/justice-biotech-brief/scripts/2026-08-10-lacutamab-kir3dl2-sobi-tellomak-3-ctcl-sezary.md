# Lacutamab — first-in-class anti-KIR3DL2 antibody heads into a confirmatory Phase 3 in cutaneous T-cell lymphoma on a Sobi partnership

Paper link: https://www.innate-pharma.com/media/all-press-releases/innate-pharma-advance-lacutamab-towards-phase-3-following-partnership-sobi-and-appoints-new-chief-medical-officer

## Script

Today's pick is a Monday-morning corporate item from Innate Pharma, but stay with me, because the interesting part isn't the deal — it's the target, and it's a target that should appeal to your particular instincts as an immunologist. The news is that Innate signed a strategic partnership with Sobi, the Swedish rare-disease company, to license and fund lacutamab, and with that money in hand they're taking it into a confirmatory Phase 3 in cutaneous T-cell lymphoma. They also named a new chief medical officer. So on the surface it's a licensing headline. Underneath it is one of the more elegant antibody stories in oncology right now, and it's a nice counterpoint to the T-cell engagers and A-D-Cs you spend most of your time on.

Start with the molecule. Lacutamab is a first-in-class antibody against kir-three-D-L-two, which is a killer immunoglobulin-like receptor. Now here's the twist that I think you'll enjoy. In its normal biology, kir-three-D-L-two is an inhibitory receptor — it's part of the machinery that keeps N-K cells and some T cells from attacking healthy tissue, so it's functionally a self-tolerance checkpoint. You might expect a drug against it to be a checkpoint blocker. It isn't. In cutaneous T-cell lymphoma the malignant T cells overexpress kir-three-D-L-two — it's on more than eighty-five percent of Sézary syndrome patients — and lacutamab exploits that purely as a lineage marker. It's a naked depleting antibody. It kills the tumor cells by antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity and by phagocytosis — it flags the cancerous T cells and recruits N-K cells and macrophages to clear them. So the same molecule the field thinks of as a checkpoint is being used here not as a signaling node to block, but as a surface address to deliver an immune hit to. That's the lesson I'd take away: sometimes the value of a "checkpoint" receptor is just that it happens to be the cleanest thing on the surface of the cell you want dead.

The disease matters for why this is worth your attention. Sézary syndrome is the leukemic, aggressive form of cutaneous T-cell lymphoma — malignant T cells circulating in the blood, in the skin, deeply symptomatic, and after patients fail the existing options, including mogamulizumab, there is very little left. This is a genuine orphan disease with real unmet need, and it's exactly the kind of setting where a targeted depleting antibody can shine because the tumor wears such a specific marker.

Now the data that earns the Phase 3, because a partnership on its own is just money. In the TELLOMAK Phase 2 trial, in relapsed and refractory Sézary syndrome — patients who'd already been through at least two prior systemic therapies including mogamulizumab — lacutamab as a single agent produced an objective response rate of about forty-three percent, with a median duration of response of twenty-five-and-a-half months. Sit with that duration number for a second. Forty-three percent responses in triple-refractory disease is good; responses lasting north of two years, from a single-agent naked antibody, in a population with essentially nothing else, is the part that makes this a real drug and not just a signal. And it was clean — grade three or higher treatment-related events in under ten percent of the Sézary patients, which is a tolerability profile you basically never get with a T-cell engager. One more mechanistic detail worth flagging: the antitumor activity showed up whether or not the tumor scored high for kir-three-D-L-two at baseline, which suggests the depletion effect doesn't need heavy target expression to work.

The mycosis fungoides side is more sobering — there the response rate was closer to twenty percent, so this is not a uniform win across all cutaneous T-cell lymphoma. And that shapes the Phase 3 design in a smart way. TELLOMAK-3 is an open-label, randomized, multicenter trial with two cohorts: a confirmatory cohort in Sézary syndrome, which is meant to support an accelerated approval filing, and a separate registrational cohort in mycosis fungoides aimed at full approval. The primary endpoint is progression-free survival, and eligibility opens up to patients who've failed just one prior line rather than two. Lacutamab already carries F-D-A Fast Track and Breakthrough Therapy designations in refractory Sézary syndrome, plus the European PRIME designation, so the regulatory scaffolding is in place.

Now let me be opinionated about the deal itself, because it tells you something. Innate is a small company, and the accelerated path here has not been smooth — this asset has been through regulatory back-and-forth, and a confirmatory Phase 3 is expensive. Sobi is paying seventy-five million dollars up front, up to another forty million in near-term Sézary milestones, and then up to roughly four hundred sixty-five million tied to an option for full development rights plus later regulatory and commercial milestones, with tiered double-digit royalties on top. Read that structure honestly: it's an option deal. Sobi is buying a look, funding the confirmatory trial, and keeping the right to walk deeper in later. And it extends Innate's cash runway only into the third quarter of twenty twenty-seven. So this is as much a survival-and-de-risking move for Innate as it is a scientific milestone — they needed a partner with rare-disease commercial muscle and a checkbook to get the confirmatory study done, rather than going it alone.

So why put this on your radar. Most of what crosses your desk is about making the format more clever — trispecifics, masked engagers, dual-payload conjugates. Lacutamab is the opposite argument: a plain old depleting antibody, no engager, no payload, that works because somebody picked a target that is almost perfectly restricted to the malignant cell. In a field obsessed with engineering, it's a reminder that target selection is still the thing that wins. Watch the TELLOMAK-3 confirmatory readout — if the Sézary durability holds up in a randomized setting, this becomes the reference therapy in a disease that badly needs one.
