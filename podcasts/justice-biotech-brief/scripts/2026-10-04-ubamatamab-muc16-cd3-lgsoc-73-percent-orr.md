## Script

On Thursday, at a late-breaking plenary session at the International Gynecologic Cancer Society meeting, Regeneron reported the first data for ubamatamab in low-grade serous ovarian cancer. Ubamatamab is a MUC-sixteen by C-D-three bispecific T cell engager. In eleven heavily pretreated patients dosed at eight hundred milligrams as monotherapy, the objective response rate was seventy-three percent, with a confidence interval running from thirty-nine to ninety-four. Median duration of response, nine months. Median progression-free survival, eleven months. Regeneron is already enrolling a dedicated registrational cohort and says it will run a randomized Phase three.

Seventy-three percent in solid tumor with a T cell engager is a number that makes you sit up. So the useful thing to do with this readout is ask what actually changed, because it was not the molecule.

This is the same drug, in the same trial, that has been running since twenty-eighteen. The Phase one read out two years ago: seventy-eight patients, median four and a half prior lines, forty-two evaluable at doses of twenty milligrams or higher, and an overall response rate of fourteen point three percent. Disease control fifty-seven percent, median duration of response twelve months — real but narrow activity. Ninety-one percent of that population was high-grade serous. Nothing about ubamatamab was redesigned between then and now. Regeneron changed the denominator.

Why low-grade serous is the right denominator is the interesting part, and it lands squarely on the antigen-density question this show keeps coming back to. Low-grade serous is a biologically distinct disease — about ten percent of serous ovarian cancer, younger patients, slower-growing tumors, poorly responsive to chemotherapy, median overall survival around seven years rather than four. And critically, it carries uniformly high MUC-sixteen. Go back to the Phase one and there is a signal pointing right at this: in an exploratory subgroup of thirteen patients whose tumors stained above seventy-five percent for MUC-sixteen, the response rate was thirty-one percent, more than double the overall number. Low-grade serous is, in effect, the version of that subgroup where nearly everybody qualifies — Regeneron did not have to biomarker-select, because the histology does the selecting.

That is the transferable lesson. Fourteen percent in an unselected population was not evidence that the target was wrong. It was evidence that target presence and target density are different variables, and that a T cell engager is far more sensitive to the second one than a checkpoint antibody is. When your engager underperforms, the first question should be whether you ran it in a tumor where the antigen is merely present.

There is a second design point worth pulling out, because it is the part people usually get wrong about this target. MUC-sixteen is the protein whose shed ectodomain is C-A one-twenty-five, the serum marker every ovarian oncologist follows. A molecule binding the shed tandem repeat would spend itself in circulation, and these patients had median baseline C-A one-twenty-five around seven hundred units. Ubamatamab avoids that by binding the membrane-proximal region that stays on the cell after cleavage — a region whose sequences are divergent enough from the shed repeats that soluble C-A one-twenty-five does not act as a sink. So this program solved the sink problem at the bench seven years ago, and it still took an indication switch to produce a response rate. Solving shedding buys you access to the membrane; it does not create antigen that was never dense enough.

Now the reasons to hold the applause. Eleven patients, and that confidence interval genuinely runs down to thirty-nine percent. All eleven responses were partial — nothing complete. And low-grade serous already has an approved targeted option: avutometinib plus defactinib, approved in May of last year for KRAS-mutant disease, with a forty-four percent response rate in fifty-eight patients and median progression-free survival of twenty-two months. Ubamatamab's headline response rate beats that, in an all-comer population rather than the roughly thirty percent who are KRAS-mutant. Its progression-free survival, eleven months, is half of it. In an indolent disease where patients live years, progression-free survival is the endpoint that pays, and response rate is the one that gets the press release. Watch what Regeneron picks as the Phase three control arm — chemotherapy versus avutometinib-defactinib is the whole question.

Tolerability cuts the same way. Across the nineteen low-grade patients, every single one had a treatment-emergent adverse event and ninety percent had cytokine release syndrome, all grade one or two, mostly during step-up. Grade three or higher treatment-related events included neutropenia at twenty-one percent and anemia at eleven. That is a manageable profile in relapsed refractory myeloma. It is a heavier lift in a forty-five-year-old woman with slow-growing disease and a seven-year survival horizon who is weighing it against two oral drugs.

And one specific thing to keep an eye on. Of the eleven patients generating this seventy-three percent, eight were dosed weekly and three were dosed every three weeks. The registrational cohort, up to a hundred patients, is every three weeks. The convenient schedule — the one that makes this viable in an indolent outpatient population — is carrying a registrational bet on an efficacy dataset of three. For an engager, where exposure between doses is exactly what governs serial killing and T cell re-engagement, that is not a bookkeeping detail. That is the risk in the program.
