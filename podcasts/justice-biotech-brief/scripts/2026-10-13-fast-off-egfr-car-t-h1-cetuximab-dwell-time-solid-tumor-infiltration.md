# Fast-off EGFR CAR T — a cetuximab-matched binder with an 18-fold shorter dwell time migrates into the tumor parenchyma instead of stalling at the rim

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.10.10.756030v1

## Script

Here is a CAR design paper that argues the field has been screening for the wrong number. It went up on bioRxiv yesterday from Yukai He's lab at the Georgia Cancer Center at Augusta University, with Nicholas Gascoigne. The claim is that for solid tumors, the parameter that matters is not affinity. It is the off-rate — how long the receptor stays bound once it has bound — and most CARs in development are, in their phrase, kinetically over-stabilized.

The reason to take it seriously is the design. Every previous attempt to ask whether lower-affinity CARs do better in solid tumors compared different antibody clones, which confounds affinity with epitope, geometry, and everything else that differs between two antibodies. This group picked a pair that removes the problem: cetuximab, and H1, its own humanized derivative. Same epitope, and by surface plasmon resonance, essentially identical on-rates. What differs is the dissociation. Cetuximab holds on with a half-life around seven and a half minutes. H1 lets go in about twenty-five seconds. Eighteen-fold shorter dwell time, same target, same site.

They tried to do this twice, incidentally, and the second pair failed. A published antibody and its affinity-enhanced variant were supposed to differ ten-fold in dwell time; re-measured in-house they differed two-fold, and the CARs built from them were superimposable. They report that rather than quietly dropping it, which tells you something about how shaky the published kinetics in this field are.

Put both binders into the same second-generation 4-1BB construct and the first result is that the biophysics survives the trip into the membrane: the fast-off CAR saturates at a much lower antigen occupancy. Then the functional results, and the shape of them is the most important thing in the paper. In a dish, at a high effector-to-target ratio, the two kill identically. In mice with a small tumor and a generous dose of cells, both cure every animal. The difference only appears when you make the experiment hard — double the tumor burden, cut the cell dose — and then the slow-off cetuximab CAR fails to expand at all in six of eight mice, while the fast-off version expands, controls the tumor significantly better, and in a couple of animals nearly clears it.

That cuts both ways. It means the standard preclinical setup — small tumor, generous cell dose — is blind to this entire variable by construction. It also means the effect size comes from a stress model with eight mice per arm, and the most dramatic responses are two animals.

The mechanism comes in three parts and they fit together. First, trogocytosis. When a slow-off CAR engages a tumor cell, the membrane exchange is severe: the Jurkat version loses about half its surface receptor after thirty minutes of contact, and the tumor cells light up with CAR-derived fluorescence. The fast-off version loses about twenty percent. In vivo it is worse — tumor-infiltrating cetuximab CAR T cells carry about seventy percent less surface CAR than their splenic counterparts, and after an overnight rest they only partly recover, while the fast-off cells bounce straight back to baseline. A CAR that stays bound gets stripped.

Second, and this is the image that sells the paper, immunohistochemistry at days eighteen and twenty. The slow-off cells are still at the tumor edge, and there are fewer of them. The fast-off cells are in the parenchyma, in large proliferating clusters, in all three tumors sectioned. The authors propose what they call pulsatile migration — short dwell time means the cell is never adhesively locked to the first antigen-positive cell or matrix fiber it touches, so it can step-migrate inward. And here is why this is more than a nice story: these are NSG mice with no human EGFR anywhere else in the body. The standard explanation for why low-affinity CAR T cells accumulate in tumors is the peripheral antigen sink, high-affinity cells getting trapped in normal tissue. There is no sink in this model. Whatever this is, it is intrinsic.

Third, single-cell RNA sequencing of cells pulled from spleen. After acute stimulation in a dish, and even at day fourteen in vivo, the two products look alike — if anything the fast-off cells look more activated. By day twenty they have diverged completely. The fast-off cells hold a stem-like memory program and run on oxidative phosphorylation; the slow-off cells have gone terminal and switched to aerobic glycolysis, the metabolic signature of chronic overstimulation. The divergence is late. A two-day assay would have told you nothing.

Now the obvious objection, which to their credit they take on. If slow off-rates are so bad, why do the approved CD19 products use FMC63, with a dwell time of about twenty-two minutes? Their answer is that liquid tumors let you off the hook. In suspension it is one T cell against one target, and when the target dies the engagement ends by itself, so the cell gets a rest period regardless of its off-rate. In a solid mass it is one T cell surrounded by antigen on every side, with no automatic disengagement, and the off-rate becomes the only thing between your cell and permanent occupancy. That is a genuinely good argument, and it explains why the CD19 affinity literature has stayed contradictory for a decade.

Where I would push back hardest is on what this model cannot see. The usual case for dialing down CAR binding is therapeutic window — sparing the normal skin and gut that express EGFR. In a mouse with no human EGFR, that experiment does not exist, and the paper's own closing line calls the off-tumor safety projected. Second, off-rate and occupancy are not cleanly separated here: the fast-off binder also showed a lower maximum binding response, so you cannot yet tell whether the benefit comes from letting go quickly or from engaging fewer receptors in the first place, and those have different engineering implications. And the authors concede that the late re-expansion in the fast-off group may be partly graft-versus-host, since the CAR-positive and CD8 fractions fall at exactly that point.

The design brief I would take away is two sentences. If you are building a CAR for a solid tumor, measure the dissociation rate and treat it as a tunable parameter in its own right, not as a byproduct of the affinity you screened for. And if you want to know whether a binder change matters, you have to run the experiment where it can matter — a large established tumor treated with too few cells — because the comfortable version of the model gives you a cure either way and tells you nothing.
