# LIME — tumor-tropic E. coli displaying tandem scFvs become living T and NK cell engagers, and beat tarlatamab head-to-head in small cell lung cancer

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.08.18.745642v1

## Script

Today's item is a preprint out of Dana-Farber, and the one-line version is that somebody built a T cell engager that is not a molecule. It's a bacterium.

The platform is called LIME — live immune modulating engagers. Take a non-pathogenic E-coli, the K-twelve lab strain, and use an intimin-derived anchor to display two single-chain variable fragments in tandem on the outer membrane, separated by a glycine-serine linker. One fragment binds a tumor antigen. The other binds an activating receptor on an effector cell. That's it. That's the whole design. The bacterium becomes the bispecific.

And the arms swap. They built T-cell versions against C-D-three and N-K versions against C-D-sixteen, N-K-G-two-D and N-K-p-forty-six, crossed against Claudin eighteen point two, D-L-L-three, E-G-F-R, mesothelin, H-E-R-two, C-D-one-twenty-three and C-D-nineteen. Every combination they display, they show binding, effector activation, and killing across cell lines from pancreatic, glioblastoma, small cell lung, breast, myeloid leukemia and lymphoma. The Claudin eighteen point two version kills patient-derived pancreatic organoids.

Now, the reason I'm putting this in front of you rather than filing it under interesting-but-preclinical is the head-to-head. They ran D-L-L-three T-LIME against tarlatamab — the approved D-L-L-three C-D-three engager, the actual drug — in D-L-L-three-positive H-sixty-nine small cell lung cancer xenografts in N-S-G mice reconstituted with human T cells. Three intravenous doses of bacteria versus three intraperitoneal doses of tarlatamab at three milligrams per kilogram, same schedule. The bacteria won on tumor growth, p equals zero-point-zero-one-eight-five. They won on survival, p equals zero-point-zero-four-six-three. And in a separate intravenous metastasis model, they cut liver tumor burden more than tarlatamab did.

But the number that actually tells you what's happening is the biodistribution. The bacteria reached more than ten to the tenth colony-forming units per gram of tumor — five to six orders of magnitude above what they recovered from any normal organ. And that spatial separation propagates all the way through to the pharmacology. Inside the tumor, D-L-L-three T-LIME produced high interferon gamma, high interleukin six, high T-N-F. In plasma, those same cytokines stayed low. Tarlatamab did the exact inverse — higher circulating interferon gamma with lower intratumoral cytokine. Same target, same effector mechanism, opposite spatial signature.

That's the thesis. The therapeutic window problem in C-D-three engagers has always been framed as a molecular-format problem — mask it, tune the C-D-three affinity, engineer the half-life, step-dose the patient. This says: it might be a delivery problem. If you can get the engager to only exist where the tumor is, you don't have to detune it at all. And in fact they went the other direction — intratumoral T cells after LIME expressed *less* P-D-one than after tarlatamab, meaning the cells doing the killing looked less exhausted, not more.

The immunocompetent work is where it gets more interesting and also more honest. In a syngeneic A-twenty lymphoma model, C-D-nineteen T-LIME cured three of six mice outright; anti-P-D-one alone cured one of six; the combination cured six of seven. Every single cured mouse — seventeen of them — rejected a contralateral rechallenge sixty days later. So this generates real memory, not just debulking.

Then they took it to K-P-C pancreatic tumors, which is the model where immunotherapy goes to die, and here's the honest part: adding anti-P-D-one did nothing. So they combined instead with R-M-C seventy-nine seventy-seven, the multi-selective RAS-ON inhibitor. Median survival went from roughly thirty days on either monotherapy to fifty-two to fifty-six days on the combination. And that combination induced P-D-L-one across intratumoral dendritic cells, macrophages, granulocytes and monocytes — an adaptive myeloid checkpoint response that they then blocked with anti-P-D-L-one, and the three-agent regimen carried two-thirds of mice past day sixty with large established tumors. That's a clean loop: find the resistance program, name it, block it, show it mattered.

Now the caveats, because there are real ones.

First, the scaffold control does a lot of work on its own. Bare bacteria with no binders induced a broad inflammatory transcriptional program in human T cells — interferon gamma, T-N-F through N-F-kappa-B, interleukin-two-STAT-five. What the engager arms added on top was the proliferative machinery: m-TOR, glycolysis, M-Y-C, cell cycle. That's a meaningful distinction, and they make it carefully. But it means a chunk of the in vivo effect is bacterial adjuvancy, and you can't fully separate the two from these experiments.

Second, and this is the one I'd push back on hardest: they write that LIME has minimal risk of causing cytokine release syndrome. You cannot show that in these models. N-S-G mice with adoptively transferred human T cells and immunocompetent mice with murine binders are not systems that produce clinical C-R-S. What they've actually shown is low plasma cytokines at the doses tested, which is suggestive and is the right measurement — but it isn't the absence claim they're making. Same trap I flagged with masked cytokines yesterday: the efficacy claim is cheap, the safety claim is expensive, and the safety claim is the whole differentiator.

Third, they're admirably upfront about a physics problem they can't answer. A productive immune synapse wants something like thirteen nanometers of membrane apposition. An E-coli is one to two micrometers. The bacterium cannot be sitting inside the synapse. So whatever LIME is doing, it's probably clustering receptors and tethering cells into proximity so that a conventional synapse forms *next* to the bacterium. Nobody knows yet. That's not a small open question if you want to engineer the thing rationally.

And fourth, the clinical precedent for intravenous live bacteria in oncology is not encouraging. Attenuated Salmonella V-N-P-two-zero-zero-zero-nine was safe in patients and produced zero objective responses. The gap between mouse tumor colonization and human tumor colonization has swallowed this field before.

Here's what I'd take from it anyway. If you work on engagers, the interesting claim isn't the bacterium — it's the demonstration that you can decouple where the engager is active from how hot it is, and that when you do, you get better efficacy and a quieter periphery at the same time. Every masking strategy in the field is trying to buy that with chemistry. This buys it with tropism. Worth thinking about which one is actually cheaper to prove.
