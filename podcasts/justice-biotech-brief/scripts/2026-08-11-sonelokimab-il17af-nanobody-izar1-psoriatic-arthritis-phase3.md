# Sonelokimab — a trivalent IL-17A/F Nanobody clears its first Phase 3 in psoriatic arthritis, though the placebo delta stays blinded

Paper link: https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/08/10/3341679/0/en/moonlake-announces-positive-topline-results-from-the-phase-3-izar-1-trial-of-sonelokimab-in-psoriatic-arthritis-demonstrating-significant-improvements-across-all-clinical-endpoints.html

## Script

Yesterday MoonLake Immunotherapeutics reported positive topline results from IZAR-1, the first of two registrational Phase 3 trials of sonelokimab in psoriatic arthritis. In biologic-naïve adults, the drug hit its primary endpoint and every key secondary at week sixteen. I want to spend today on this one not because psoriatic arthritis is your lab's disease — it isn't — but because sonelokimab is the most clinically advanced example of a format you and I keep circling: the Nanobody, the V-H-H multivalent construct, carrying an actual registrational program across the finish line instead of just looking elegant in a preclinical deck.

So start with the molecule, because that's the whole story. Sonelokimab is a roughly forty-kilodalton humanized Nanobody — three single-domain V-H-H building blocks, the antigen-binding heavy-chain-only domains from camelids, strung together with flexible glycine-serine spacers. Two of those domains bind interleukin seventeen A and interleukin seventeen F, which means one molecule neutralizes all three flavors of the cytokine — the A-A homodimer, the A-F heterodimer, and the F-F homodimer. The third domain does something cleverer: it binds human albumin. That's not just a half-life trick, though it is that — it also concentrates the drug at sites of inflammatory edema, where albumin leaks into tissue. The pitch is that a molecule a third the size of a conventional antibody penetrates inflamed joint and skin tissue better than a full I-g-G, and that the albumin anchor pulls it exactly where the inflammation is. That's the differentiation thesis in one sentence: same cytokines everyone else is chasing, different delivery physics.

Now the numbers. The primary endpoint was A-C-R fifty at week sixteen — a fifty percent improvement in the composite of joint counts and other rheumatology measures — and forty-two percent of patients on the sixty-milligram dose with induction got there. Across the secondaries, sixty-six percent hit A-C-R twenty, forty-one percent reached minimal disease activity, and in patients who also had skin involvement, sixty-one percent achieved PASI ninety — near-total skin clearance. Physical function and patient-reported outcomes moved too — the HAQ disability index and the SF-thirty-six physical component both improved meaningfully. And the blinded safety read showed nothing new, with a low dropout rate. On its face, a clean multidomain win: joints, skin, function, quality of life, all moving together, which is exactly what you want in a disease as heterogeneous as psoriatic arthritis.

Here's the catch, and it's a big one, so hold the enthusiasm at arm's length. This topline is absolute response only. Per the unblinding protocol MoonLake agreed with the F-D-A, they disclosed the response levels for the sixty-milligram arm and nothing else — the comparison versus placebo and the full treatment-arm data stay blinded until the whole program finishes, with the complete week-fifty-two readout not expected until the first half of twenty twenty-seven. And IZAR-1 has no active comparator arm at all. So when I tell you forty-two percent reached A-C-R fifty, I can't yet tell you the delta over placebo, and I can't tell you how that stacks against the standard of care. Those are the two numbers that actually decide whether a drug is differentiated, and we don't have either one. The second trial, IZAR-2, in TNF-refractory patients, will at least run head-to-head against risankizumab — that's the one to watch for a real benchmark.

And when you do reach for a benchmark, the honest read gets more sobering. MoonLake's own C-E-O said the results are "consistent with what was previously observed in other interleukin seventeen A and F programs." Read that carefully — the other big dual interleukin-seventeen-A-and-F drug is bimekizumab, a conventional antibody already approved in psoriatic arthritis. So the pitch is not that sonelokimab beats bimekizumab on efficacy; it's that it matches it. Forty-two percent A-C-R fifty in bio-naïve patients is a good number, but it sits in the same neighborhood as the approved interleukin-seventeen and interleukin-twenty-three agents. Which means the entire investment case rests on the format — better tissue penetration, potentially less frequent dosing, easier and cheaper manufacturing of a small single-chain construct — not on a step-change in how many patients respond. That's a real bet, but it's a bet on pharmacology and commercial convenience, not on a new ceiling of efficacy.

So how should you hold this? As genuine validation of the V-H-H platform thesis, with a clear-eyed view of what it does and doesn't prove. Remember the Attovia episode from last week — the whole argument that the antibody engineering you work with in oncology is migrating into immunology, and that single-domain multivalent constructs are a big part of how it travels. Sonelokimab is the furthest-along proof point for that migration: a trivalent Nanobody with an albumin-binding domain, now with two Phase 3 wins behind it across hidradenitis suppurativa and psoriatic arthritis. The B-L-A in hidradenitis is expected by the end of September, with a P-D-U-F-A date decision around the end of November — so this platform is about to have a marketed product. That's the thing worth internalizing: V-H-H multispecifics are no longer a preclinical promise in inflammation, they're a regulatory reality. What's still unproven, and what the blinded placebo delta and the IZAR-2 head-to-head will eventually tell us, is whether the format buys you anything a well-made conventional antibody doesn't. Watch that first-half-of-twenty-twenty-seven readout.
