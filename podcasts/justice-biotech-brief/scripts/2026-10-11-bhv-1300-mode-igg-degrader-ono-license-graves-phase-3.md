## Script

On Thursday, Ono Pharmaceutical paid Biohaven eighty million dollars up front for the rights to a portfolio of extracellular IgG degraders in Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, and the ASEAN countries. Another twenty million is tied to a milestone they expect inside a year, royalties run around twenty percent on Ono's sales in the territory, and Biohaven keeps everything else — the United States, Europe, and China. The lead asset is BHV-1300, which is in a pivotal Phase 3 in Graves' disease. On its face this is a regional licensing deal and not much of a story. I want to talk about it anyway, because the mechanism is one of the more interesting things happening in antibody-directed therapy, and because the evidence base underneath a three-hundred-patient pivotal trial is thinner than almost anyone reading the headline would guess.

Start with the chemistry, because it's genuinely clever. The platform is called MoDE — molecular degraders of extracellular proteins — and it came out of David Spiegel's lab at Yale, published in Nature Chemical Biology in 2021. The idea is to take the logic of a degrader, which we normally use inside the cell with the proteasome, and run it outside the cell using the liver instead. The molecule is bifunctional and it is a small molecule, not a protein. One end binds the thing you want gone. The other end is a cluster of sugars that binds the asialoglycoprotein receptor on hepatocytes — the receptor whose day job is pulling desialylated glycoproteins out of circulation. You form a ternary complex between your target and that receptor, the hepatocyte internalizes the whole thing, and lysosomal proteases finish it. The degrader is released and goes around again. In BHV-1300, the target-binding end grabs IgG generally, so what you get is a subcutaneously injected small molecule that routes circulating immunoglobulin into the liver for destruction.

Compare that to how we currently lower IgG, which is FcRn blockade — efgartigimod, nipocalimab, batoclimab. Those drugs don't destroy anything. They occupy the neonatal Fc receptor, which is the salvage pathway that rescues endocytosed IgG from the lysosome and returns it to the surface. Block the salvage and the IgG that was going to be recycled is instead catabolized by default. It works, but three things fall out of it. It hits every IgG subclass, including IgG3, which carries a lot of the antibacterial and antiviral response. It produces the class effects we've all seen in these trials — albumin down, cholesterol up, headache. And the one that should matter most to anyone running antibody therapeutics: FcRn is also what gives every IgG-based biologic its three-week half-life, so an FcRn blocker accelerates clearance of whatever Fc-bearing drug your patient is also on.

Biohaven leans on all three of those contrasts. BHV-1300 targets IgG1, IgG2, and IgG4 and spares IgG3. It reports no clinically significant albumin, cholesterol, or liver enzyme changes. And it doesn't touch FcRn, so, they argue, it won't drag down a co-administered biologic. I'd push on that last claim. This is described as a pan-IgG degrader, and it chews through IgG1. A therapeutic monoclonal is an IgG1. The degrader doesn't need FcRn to clear your drug — it can clear your drug directly, by its own mechanism. Not interacting with the recycling receptor is not the same thing as being compatible with Fc-based therapeutics, and I'd want that question answered explicitly before anyone combines this with an engager, an ADC, or a checkpoint antibody.

Now the clinical case, which is where I think the gap between the press language and the data is widest. The Graves' numbers Biohaven has been quoting since May are these: a thousand milligrams subcutaneously, weekly, produced mean reductions in the pathogenic TSH-receptor IgG1 of more than eighty percent by week twelve, with free T4 normalizing at a median of three weeks and free T3 at a median of five weeks, in patients who were still hyperthyroid despite being on antithyroid drugs. Clean safety through twelve weeks, no discontinuations, no serious adverse events. That sounds like a drug.

Read the figure legends. Those curves are three patients. The broader biomarker and pharmacokinetic dataset they presented alongside it was eight. The registered Phase 1 study in Graves' disease enrolls fifteen people, it is open label, and its primary endpoints are safety and laboratory abnormalities. There is no control arm anywhere in this. And the company's framing — that the reduction is deeper than what the FcRn inhibitor competition has shown — deserves a comparator. Batoclimab, an FcRn blocker, ran an open-label Phase 2a in Graves' hyperthyroidism and reported mean TRAb reductions of seventy-four percent at week twelve and seventy percent at week twenty-four, with rapid normalization of thyroid function. Eighty-plus versus seventy-four, measured with different assays, in two small open-label cohorts, is not a class-separating gap. It might become one. Right now it's a claim with three patients behind it.

Having said all that, I think Graves' is the right disease to run this experiment in, and the reason is worth generalizing. The autoantibody in Graves' is not a marker of the disease — it is the disease. A stimulating IgG1 binds the TSH receptor and drives it. Remove the antibody and the pathophysiology has nowhere to go. Contrast that with what just happened to efgartigimod in Sjögren's, where a Phase 3 was stopped for futility. Autoantibodies are present in Sjögren's, but they are a readout of a process, not the engine of it, and no amount of lowering total IgG fixes that. Depth of IgG clearance is only the binding constraint when the antibody is causal. That is the question you should ask first about any program in this class, before you ask how deep it goes.

The pivotal trial is worth knowing the shape of. Three hundred adults, randomized, quadruple-blind, placebo-controlled, started in June, primary completion estimated January 2028. The primary endpoint is the proportion of patients who are off antithyroid drugs and have normal total T3, free T4, and TSH at week twenty-six. Note that TSH is in there. After prolonged thyrotoxicosis, pituitary TSH stays suppressed for months after the peripheral hormones come back — so including it is a real bar, and Biohaven appears to know it, because the identical endpoint without TSH is sitting right there as the first secondary. TRAb change at weeks six and twenty-six is also secondary.

What neither this trial nor the FcRn trials will answer is durability. Batoclimab's TRAb rebounds when you stop; a case report has total IgG back near baseline eighteen months after discontinuation. Of course it does — clearing antibody does nothing to the plasma cells making it. Both of these classes are chronic suppression dressed up as disease modification, and the honest contrast is with the B-cell-directed approach, where you delete the factory and ask whether the autoantibody comes back at all. That's the comparison I'd want in Graves' eventually. For now, what Ono bought is an elegant piece of chemistry attached to a well-chosen indication and a very small amount of human data, and eighty million dollars for four Asian markets is priced about right for that.
