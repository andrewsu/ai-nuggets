# Glofitamab and mosunetuzumab — a CD3-humanized immunocompetent mouse shows T cell engagers end up cross-priming the endogenous repertoire they were designed to bypass

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.10.06.756695v1

## Script

Today's pick went up on bioRxiv two days ago from Philippe Bousso's lab at the Institut Pasteur, and it answers a question the T cell engager field has been handing to clinical observation for a decade. Why do some patients stay in remission long after the drug has cleared? The honest answer until now has been nobody knows, because a bispecific that crosslinks CD3-epsilon to a tumor antigen is explicitly designed to skip the endogenous cancer immunity cycle — no antigen presentation, no T cell receptor specificity, no priming. This paper's claim is that the killing feeds straight back into the cycle it was built to bypass, and that the part of the response that lasts is not the engager at all. It is conventional cross-priming by dendritic cells, on antigen that the engager liberated.

Start with the model, because the model is why you can believe the rest. Mouse work on human engagers has been stuck between humanized NSG mice, which develop graft-versus-host disease and have no real immune microenvironment, and CD3-locus knock-in mice that don't fully reproduce the human drug-to-T-cell interaction. What they did instead is make bone marrow chimeras: transduce donor hematopoietic stem cells with human CD3-epsilon plus a human CD34 marker, transplant into lethally irradiated recipients, and you get a mouse with an otherwise normal immune system whose T cells can be engaged by clinical-grade human drug. Thymic development, naive-to-memory distribution, regulatory T cells — all comparable to wild type. Then they put human CD20 on two mouse B lymphoma lines and treated with research-grade anti-CD20 engager, glofitamab, or mosunetuzumab. One intravenous dose at one milligram per kilogram clears disseminated lymphoma out of marrow and spleen within three days.

The first thing intravital two-photon imaging in the bone marrow tells you is that engagement and killing are two different events separated by about a day. Before treatment, T cells touch tumor cells transiently, median contact seven minutes. Within minutes of injecting the engager, the contacts lock — more than half of the conjugates outlast the thirty-minute imaging window, and some are still going hours later. And almost nothing dies. Roughly three percent of those contacts are lytic in the first hours. By twenty-four hours, thirty percent are. So the drug builds the synapse instantly and the cell has to go get the weapons. Perforin knockouts lose the killing everywhere; interferon-gamma knockouts largely keep it; and blocking LFA-1 the day before abolishes killing without touching the synthesis of cytotoxic molecules at all. The granules get made, and without the integrin the cell can't deliver them.

Then the finding that I think should change how people read dosing schedules. The killing is a wave, not a plateau. Apoptosis peaks at day one and declines through days two, three and seven — and it declines while T cells are pouring in. There is a chemokine burst at day one, CXCL10, CCL3, CCL4, then local proliferation, Ki67 up, a threefold expansion of CD8 cells peaking at day three. So day three is the moment of maximum T cell density and minimum killing. They tested the obvious question directly, by re-injecting drug while the microscope was running. A second dose at day two or three does nothing. The tumor sits there surrounded by T cells that have the drug bridging them to it and they do not kill. A second dose at day seven triggers immediate lysis. Cytotoxic competence in this system is not a function of how many T cells you have. It is a function of what time it is.

Single-cell sequencing explains why, and this is the part worth taking back to the bench. At day three the CD8 compartment is overwhelmed — fifty-four percent — by a population that did not exist at baseline and has no lytic program: Tcf7, Slamf6, Id3, Tox, and critically CD62L. That is the progenitor-of-exhausted-cells signature, arising within three days of a single injection, in a setting with none of the weeks of chronic antigen that usually produce it. By day seven that compartment has collapsed to ten percent and a cytotoxic cluster has doubled to nearly thirty percent, transcriptionally distinct from the small pre-existing effector pool, and confirmed at protein level. So the refractory window is not the drug failing. It is a differentiation pipeline running.

Which cells feed it? They sorted naive, central memory and effector memory CD8 cells and transferred each into tumor-bearing recipients before dosing. Naive cells expand a little and make almost no effectors. Effector memory cells have the perforin and granzyme already and barely divide. Central memory does both — the best expansion, and roughly seventy-five percent of the progeny co-expressing perforin and granzyme B. The hierarchy holds in human PBMC co-cultures, where memory-derived cells out-kill naive-derived, although in a seventy-two-hour dish you lose the separation between central and effector memory because you never give central memory the proliferative advantage it wins on in vivo. The translational line they draw is the one I'd draw: the central memory compartment is both the effector reservoir and the candidate predictive biomarker, and whatever you do to a patient before the engager should be judged on whether it preserves that compartment.

Now the ending. They put ovalbumin into the tumor to track genuinely tumor-specific cells, and after a single dose there's a thirty-fold rise in tetramer-positive CD8 cells. Here the model's imperfection becomes its best control: chimerism is incomplete, so some T cells never got human CD3 and cannot be engaged by the drug at all. The tumor-specific cells are enriched in exactly that unengageable fraction, across every organ. That is de novo priming through conventional antigen presentation, not drug-mediated activation. XCR1-positive type 1 dendritic cells are carrying tumor peptide on MHC class I, and when they make chimeras lacking those dendritic cells, tumor-specific T cells are undetectable and nearly every animal dies despite identical treatment, while the wild-type chimeras achieve durable control. Cure the mice, wait, rechallenge with ovalbumin-negative tumor and no further drug, and the vast majority stay tumor-free.

The caveats are real and they list most of them. It's a transplanted, aggressive pro-B lymphoma with a strong model antigen, which almost certainly flatters cross-priming efficiency. The human CD3-epsilon is a transgene and it's overexpressed, so binding kinetics and signal strength may not be physiological. The recipients are lethally irradiated. The role of CD4 cells was not tested. The human in vitro arm is one Burkitt line. And solid tumors, where your own interest lies, are untouched.

What I'd carry out of it is three things. The exhaustion-like program is a costimulation deficit — CD3 crosslinking drives NFAT with no matching AP-1 signal — which lines up with the recent glofitamab clinical data where adding 4-1BB costimulation pushed intratumoral T cells back toward a naive-like state. Whether that Tox-positive day-three state is a productive intermediate or a bifurcation toward terminal exhaustion needs epigenetics to settle, and the answer determines whether engager plus PD-1 blockade makes sense. Second, if the durable benefit is a cross-priming cascade, then the combination partners worth testing are the ones that amplify dendritic cells — Flt3 ligand, STING agonists, vaccines — not just more engager. And third, the strongest argument yet for fixed-duration dosing: once the cascade has started, continued administration during the refractory window is not just useless, it may be interrupting the differentiation that produces the cells you actually want.
