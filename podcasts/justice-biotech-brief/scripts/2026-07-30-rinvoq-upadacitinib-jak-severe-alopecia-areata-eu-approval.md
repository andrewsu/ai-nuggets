# RINVOQ (upadacitinib) — first JAK inhibitor to claim complete hair regrowth wins EU approval in severe alopecia areata

Paper link: https://news.abbvie.com/2026-07-29-AbbVie-Announces-European-Commission-Approval-of-RINVOQ-R-upadacitinib-for-the-Treatment-of-Adults-and-Adolescents-with-Severe-Alopecia-Areata

## Script

Yesterday, July 29th, the European Commission approved AbbVie's RINVOQ — upadacitinib — for severe alopecia areata in adults and adolescents twelve and older. On paper this is a routine new indication for a drug you already know well, and I almost passed on it. I want to spend a few minutes on it anyway, because it's a clean marker of where autoimmune dermatology has settled, and it lands right on a tension you and I keep circling: how much you can ask a single kinase to do before the safety ceiling stops you.

Start with the biology, because it's the whole reason this class works. Alopecia areata is a T-cell-driven autoimmune attack on the hair follicle — C-D-8 T cells, an interferon gamma signature, and a collapse of the follicle's normal immune-privileged status. That interferon signal, and the survival cytokines that keep the attacking cells alive, run through the JAK-STAT pathway. So if you inhibit the JAK kinases, you quiet the whole cytokine circuit that sustains the attack, and the follicle finally gets a chance to recover. Upadacitinib is an oral, once-daily, selective JAK-one inhibitor, and that mechanism is exactly the point.

The approval rests on the phase 3 UP-A-A program — two replicate trials, roughly fourteen hundred patients ranging from twelve to sixty-four years old. Both the fifteen and thirty milligram doses hit the primary endpoint: a SALT score of twenty or below at week twenty-four. SALT is the Severity of Alopecia Tool, and twenty-or-below means at least eighty percent of the scalp has regrown hair. The headline AbbVie is leaning on is that this is the first JAK inhibitor to show complete scalp regrowth — a SALT score of zero, a full head of hair — versus placebo at week twenty-four, and it moved eyebrows and eyelashes too.

Here's my read. Upadacitinib is not first to this party — it's more like fourth. Eli Lilly's baricitinib, Pfizer's ritlecitinib, and Sun Pharma's deuruxolitinib are all already approved in alopecia areata, and ritlecitinib already carries the adolescent label. So upa is walking into a JAK-crowded room, and its differentiation pitch is depth of response — not just hair back, but all the way back to zero. Whether that complete-regrowth rate translates into a real edge over the incumbents is not something a placebo-controlled trial can answer; you'd need head-to-head data, and that's the honest caveat.

But the bigger point — and the reason I'd flag this to you specifically — is the safety ceiling. Every one of these drugs carries the JAK-class boxed warning: serious infections, blood clots, malignancy, major cardiovascular events, all extrapolated from the rheumatoid arthritis population. In alopecia areata you are treating otherwise-healthy people, a lot of them teenagers, for a condition that is genuinely disfiguring but not life-threatening. That risk-benefit calculus is completely different from oncology or R-A, and it is the ceiling the whole oral pan-JAK approach keeps running into.

Which is exactly why I don't think the interesting frontier is another oral JAK inhibitor at all. It's targeted JAK delivery — the JAK antibody-drug-conjugate idea we've talked about, where you tether a JAK inhibitor to an antibody so the drug concentrates where the pathology is and your systemic exposure drops. Upadacitinib winning in alopecia is the mature, fully-developed version of the JAK story. The JAK conjugate programs are the field's attempt to escape the very safety ceiling that this approval quietly reminds you is still there.

So the one-line take: JAK inhibition has effectively become the default mechanism for severe alopecia areata, and upadacitinib's contribution is a stronger complete-regrowth claim rather than a new mechanism. For a space that is simultaneously chasing JAK-free options — the interleukin-7 receptor and interleukin-15 axis antibodies we covered earlier this month — this approval is a reminder that the incumbents are not standing still, and that the real prize goes to whoever figures out how to get JAK-level efficacy without the JAK-level warning label.
