# IDP-001 — a proximity-guided EGFR bispecific ADC enters the clinic, testing InduPro's "tumor proximity antigen" platform

Paper link: https://www.biospace.com/press-releases/indupro-closes-77-million-series-b-financing-and-doses-first-patient-in-phase-1-study-of-lead-oncology-program-idp-001

## Script

Today's pick is another financing paired with a first dose, but stay with me, because the money is the least interesting part. A Seattle-and-Cambridge company called InduPro closed a seventy-seven-million-dollar Series B and, at the same time, put the first patient on a phase one trial of its lead program. What earns it the slot is the idea underneath the drug — a genuinely different way of choosing what an antibody should point at.

Here's the problem it's trying to solve. If you want to build an antibody-drug conjugate — an antibody carrying a toxic payload — you need a target that's on the tumor and not on healthy tissue. That's the whole game, and it's brutally hard, because most tumor surface proteins are also sitting on some normal cell somewhere, and the moment your payload lands there you get toxicity. E-G-F-R is the poster child for this. It's a validated cancer target, but it's also all over your skin and your gut, which is why E-G-F-R-targeted therapies come with rashes and diarrhea and a narrow therapeutic window. Conventional discovery has been grinding on this for years, hunting for a single antigen that's cleaner.

InduPro's move is to stop hunting for a cleaner single antigen and instead look at how proteins are arranged next to each other on the cell surface. Their platform uses proximity labeling — chemistry that tags whatever proteins are physically adjacent to a given anchor protein — to map the spatial neighborhoods on tumor cells at high resolution. And what they're betting is that even when no individual protein is tumor-specific, a particular pairing of two proteins sitting side by side can be. Two ordinary proteins, each present on normal tissue on its own, but only found together, in close proximity, on the cancer. They call the partner a Tumor-Associated Proximity Antigen. The lead one is named TAPA-E-one, and it's undisclosed beyond that.

So IDP-001 is a bispecific antibody-drug conjugate — one arm to E-G-F-R, one arm to TAPA-E-one — and the logic is that it only really engages and delivers its payload where both targets coexist in that tumor-specific spatial pattern. E-G-F-R gives you the tumor address and the internalization you need to drag the payload inside the cell; the proximity antigen gives you the selectivity that E-G-F-R alone can't. If it works, you've widened the therapeutic window not by finding a magic clean target, but by requiring a coincidence — two things in one place — that healthy tissue doesn't reproduce. That's a real conceptual shift for the ADC field, which has mostly competed on payloads, linkers, and single-target affinity, not on spatial co-localization.

The clinical bet is pointed and telling. The phase one is in advanced squamous and non-squamous non-small-cell lung cancer and other squamous solid tumors that have already progressed on standard of care. The chief medical officer's framing is that squamous cancers are an underserved corner of oncology — they largely lack the actionable driver mutations and the clean cell-surface targets that the approved ADCs were built around, so there's shared squamous biology here and not much competition for it. It's monotherapy, open-label, first-in-human, so this is a safety-and-early-signal study — dose escalation, pharmacokinetics, and whether the tolerability story the platform promises actually holds up in people.

Now the part I'd flag hardest, because it's the same discipline I'd apply to any platform round. There is no efficacy data. TAPA-E-one is undisclosed, so we can't independently sanity-check the proximity thesis. First-in-human ADCs fail on tolerability all the time, and a two-target requirement is a beautiful idea on a slide that has to survive contact with real tumor heterogeneity and real payload chemistry. So hold this as a platform-validation bet, not as a drug that works.

But the syndicate is the tell, and it's an unusually loud one. The round was led by The Column Group, with Vida Ventures and Merck's therapeutics venture fund already in — and the new money includes Sanofi and Eli Lilly. When three separate large pharmas are circling one antibody-engineering platform at the Series B, they are not buying a single squamous-lung asset. They're buying proximity to a discovery engine, the same pattern we keep seeing — Attovia's multispecific platform, Infinimmune's repertoire-mining engine just yesterday. And here's the piece that matters most for where this could go: InduPro says the same proximity-antigen map feeds not just ADCs but T-cell engagers. If spatial co-localization can define a tumor-selective target pair, that's exactly the selectivity problem that's been holding solid-tumor T-cell engagers back. The ADC is the first shot. The platform is the thing to watch.
