# RP1 (vusolimogene oderparepvec) — oncolytic-plus-nivolumab melanoma combo heads into a make-or-break FDA adcom on single-arm data

Paper link: https://www.statnews.com/2026/07/28/replimune-rp1-skin-cancer-fda-staff-analysis/

## Script

Tomorrow, Thursday July 30th, the FDA's cell and gene therapy advisory committee sits down to decide the fate of an oncolytic immunotherapy that has already been rejected twice — and the fight is a clean, almost textbook illustration of a question you run into constantly in immunotherapy development: when can a single-arm trial tell you a drug actually works?

The drug is Replimune's R-P-1, generic name vusolimogene oderparepvec. It's a herpes simplex virus that's been engineered into a replication-selective oncolytic — you inject it directly into a tumor, it preferentially replicates in and lyses the cancer cells, and it's armed to turn that local killing into a systemic immune response. It carries granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor, G-M-C-S-F, to recruit and mature dendritic cells, and a fusogenic viral glycoprotein that makes infected tumor cells fuse and die in a highly immunogenic way. The whole thesis is the abscopal idea: light a fire in one injected lesion and get T-cell immunity that travels to lesions you never touched. It's given on top of nivolumab — Opdivo, the P-D-1 inhibitor — and the target population is advanced cutaneous melanoma that has already progressed on anti-P-D-1 therapy. That's a genuinely underserved group with few good options.

Here's the data package, and here's the problem. The registrational evidence comes from IGNYTE, a phase one/two single-arm study — no control group. In the anti-P-D-1-failed melanoma cohort, R-P-1 plus nivolumab produced a confirmed response rate of about thirty-four percent, and the responses were strikingly durable, with a median duration north of two years. Company-reported three-year overall survival was in the high forties across the cohort — and over eighty percent among the patients who responded. On a slide, that looks like a drug doing real work in people who had run out of road.

But sit with what the FDA said in its briefing documents this week, because it's the crux. The agency called the overall-survival analysis from the single-arm study "not interpretable," and said the way responses were assessed "confounds interpretation" of the efficacy. Translate that out of regulatory language and it's an argument any immunologist should feel in their bones. In a single-arm trial you cannot separate what R-P-1 contributed from what nivolumab contributed — and these are patients defined as having progressed on a P-D-1 antibody, then put back on a P-D-1 antibody plus the virus. And the "responders lived a long time" number is close to circular: by construction, patients whose tumors shrink and stay shrunk are the ones who live longer. That's a selection effect, not a treatment effect. It doesn't tell you the responder would have done worse without the drug. The whole point of the abscopal, systemic-immunity claim is that it operates beyond the injected lesion — and that systemic benefit is exactly the thing a single-arm design is worst at proving.

What makes this genuinely frustrating, rather than just a weak package, is that the answer exists — it's just not done yet. Replimune is running IGNYTE-three, a randomized phase three of the same combination against physician's choice in advanced melanoma that's progressed on both anti-P-D-1 and anti-C-T-L-A-4. That trial, if positive, would settle the causal question the agency is raising. So the real dispute tomorrow isn't "does this drug work" in the abstract — it's whether the FDA should grant accelerated approval now, on suggestive single-arm data, for a population with real unmet need, or make patients wait for the controlled trial to read out. Replimune's counter is the ethical one: they argue you can't credibly randomize these patients to continued P-D-1 monotherapy when there's no evidence that continuing it does anything. That's not a crazy position. But "a randomized trial would be unethical" is a much stronger argument when the confirmatory randomized trial you're already running doesn't have that same control arm — and this one does.

Two things put an exclamation point on this. First, the history: this is attempt number three. R-P-1 got complete response letters in July of last year and again in April, both over trial-design and evidence-quality concerns — the same concerns, essentially, that are back on the table tomorrow. Second, the timing. This resubmission landed right after a leadership shake-up at the agency — the departures of Vinay Prasad and Marty Makary — and analysts have been openly asking whether "third time's the charm" reflects new data or a new evidentiary mood. There's basically no new efficacy data. So an adcom vote in favor would be a signal about where the bar is sitting now, as much as a verdict on this specific virus.

Why should this matter to you beyond melanoma? Because the intratumoral-oncolytic-plus-checkpoint field lives or dies on the systemic-immunity claim, and this is the highest-profile test yet of whether regulators will accept single-arm evidence for that claim. Watch the vote and — more importantly — watch the discussion. The specific objections the committee dwells on are a preview of the evidentiary hurdle every "inject locally, cure systemically" program is going to have to clear. The decision deadline is August 2nd.
