## Script

Friday, BioNTech and Genentech terminated their Phase two trial of autogene cevumeran in resected colorectal cancer. The independent data safety monitoring board recommended it, and the language is worth reading closely: the board identified a numerical imbalance in overall survival between the treatment arms, and concluded that continuing was unlikely to change the efficacy outcome. No new safety signals. BioNTech stock fell about eight percent. I want to spend the whole episode here, because this trial was the cleanest experimental test anyone has run of the individualized cancer vaccine hypothesis, and it came back negative nine days after the same class of drug won its first Phase three.

Start with what made the design clean. Patients had stage three, or high-risk stage two, colon or rectal cancer, fully resected with clear margins. They then completed at least three months of standard adjuvant chemotherapy. And then they still had circulating tumor D-N-A detectable in blood — molecular residual disease. Those patients were randomized to receive up to fifteen intravenous doses of a personalized messenger R-N-A immunotherapy encoding neoantigens computed from their own tumor, or to watchful waiting. Three hundred twenty-seven patients. Primary endpoint, disease-free survival. Two features matter. The control arm is nothing at all, so the comparison is against natural history rather than against an active drug. And the protocol excluded microsatellite-instability-high tumors, so this was microsatellite-stable colorectal cancer — the cold, checkpoint-refractory kind. The vaccine was given as monotherapy. No checkpoint inhibitor, no chemotherapy underneath it. That is the pure form of the hypothesis: teach the immune system to see the patient's own mutations, and let it clear the residual disease by itself.

It didn't. And the timeline here is unusually candid. The futility boundary was crossed in October of 2025 — ten months ago. The board looked at it then and said the data were not mature enough to draw reliable conclusions about efficacy and that follow-up was too short to evaluate the primary endpoint, so the trial kept running. When they came back with mature data, not only had futility held, there was a survival imbalance between the arms. BioNTech has not disclosed the direction of that imbalance; Fierce Biotech's reporting characterizes it as more deaths in the vaccine arm than in the control arm. I'd hold that loosely — overall survival was a secondary endpoint in an open-label adjuvant trial against watchful waiting, deaths are relatively few, and a numerical imbalance in that setting is easy to over-read. What is not easy to over-read is the futility.

Here's the part that should actually bother a translational immunologist, and it doesn't come from Friday's news. It comes from the same molecule's melanoma trial, reported at ESMO last fall. First-line advanced melanoma, autogene cevumeran plus pembrolizumab versus pembrolizumab alone: median progression-free survival eight point three months versus seven point nine, hazard ratio zero point seven eight, p-value of zero point three. Missed. Objective response rate was forty-one point seven percent on the combination versus forty-eight point eight percent on pembrolizumab alone — numerically worse with the vaccine added. Now the immunology from that same trial. Autogene cevumeran induced ex vivo detectable, neoantigen-specific T cell responses in forty-seven of fifty-six evaluable patients — about eighty-four percent. And in the subset where they sequenced the repertoire, the median frequency of neoantigen-specific C-D-eight T cell receptors among all circulating T cell receptors was nine point four percent, ranging as high as twenty-nine percent, still detectable a year later.

Sit with that number. Roughly one in ten circulating T cell receptors specific for that patient's own tumor mutations, persisting for a year — and the response rate went down. The platform is not failing at priming. As a vaccine, in the pharmacodynamic sense, it works about as well as anyone could have asked: it generates a large, durable, tumor-specific T cell population on demand, in most patients. That population simply does not convert into tumor control. The bottleneck is downstream — trafficking, the suppressive microenvironment, exhaustion on arrival, antigen presentation on the tumor side, or the plainest possibility, that the neoantigens the algorithm ranked highest are not the ones that matter.

Which is why the Moderna and Merck result nine days earlier is the informative contrast rather than a contradiction. Their Phase three, in completely resected stage two-B through stage four melanoma, hit recurrence-free survival and also distant metastasis-free survival — the first positive Phase three for an individualized neoantigen therapy of any kind. But that regimen ran pembrolizumab in both arms with the vaccine on top. The vaccine supplied cells; the checkpoint inhibitor kept them from being switched off. BMO's note Friday morning framed the question precisely — whether vaccine-induced immune responses can prevent recurrence without concurrent checkpoint inhibition or chemotherapy. The colorectal trial is the experiment that answers it, and the answer is no.

There's a second variable people will conflate with the first, and it's worth separating. Melanoma is high-mutation-burden, checkpoint-responsive disease. Microsatellite-stable colorectal cancer is the canonical cold tumor. So the two trials differ in combination partner and in disease immunogenicity at the same time, and you cannot cleanly attribute the split to either one. My read is that the population selection made things worse rather than better. Selecting patients who are still circulating-tumor-D-N-A positive after completing adjuvant chemotherapy sounds like you have found minimal disease — the ideal low-burden setting for a vaccine. But it also means you have selected for tumor biology that already survived surgery and a full course of chemotherapy. That is not an easy population. It is an enriched-for-aggressive population that happens to be small. The molecular-residual-disease framing has been sold as the way to rescue immunotherapy in cold tumors by shrinking the target, and this is the first well-powered randomized test of that framing. It did not rescue anything.

What survives from Friday is the pancreatic study, which continues — and which is a combination trial, vaccine plus atezolizumab plus chemotherapy — so BMO's call that the read-through is negative but limited is fair on its face. I would add one caution about the pancreatic data everyone cites: roughly ninety percent of immune responders alive up to six years after last treatment. Immune responders is a post-hoc subgroup, and the melanoma trial just demonstrated that you can get eighty-four percent immunogenicity, a trend toward better outcomes in the patients with the broadest responses, and still miss your primary endpoint. Responder analyses in this field have earned skepticism.

For the bench, the question has moved. Whether we can induce neoantigen-specific T cells is settled — we can, robustly, in most patients, at frequencies that would have seemed implausible ten years ago. What happens to those T cells when they arrive at a microsatellite-stable tumor is the entire problem now, and it is the same problem T cell engagers and CAR T cells hit in solid tumors. The vaccine field spent a decade solving the step that turned out not to be rate-limiting.
