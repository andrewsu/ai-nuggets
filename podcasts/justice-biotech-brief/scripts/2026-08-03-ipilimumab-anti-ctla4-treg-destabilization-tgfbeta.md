# Ipilimumab — anti-CTLA-4 destabilizes tumor Tregs by agonizing CTLA-4 to fire TGF-β signaling, not by blocking it

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.07.28.741328v1

## Script

Today's pick isn't a drug launch, it's a mechanism paper — and it goes after one of the oldest unsettled questions in the checkpoint field: how does anti-C-T-L-A-four actually work? Ipilimumab, Yervoy, was the first checkpoint inhibitor ever approved, back in twenty-eleven, and fifteen years later we still argue about its mechanism of action. There are two camps. Camp one, the original story, is that the antibody blocks C-T-L-A-four on your T cells, so C-T-L-A-four can't outcompete CD twenty-eight for B-seven, and effector T cells get to stay switched on. Camp two, which took over the last decade, says the real action is Treg depletion — the antibody's Fc tail flags C-T-L-A-four-high regulatory T cells inside the tumor for killing by Fc-receptor-bearing macrophages. This paper, from WanJun Chen's group at the NIH, a lab that has spent two decades on T-G-F-beta and Treg biology, says both camps are missing the actual switch.

Their claim is genuinely counterintuitive: on intratumoral Tregs, anti-C-T-L-A-four doesn't block C-T-L-A-four, it agonizes it. The antibody cross-links C-T-L-A-four on tumor Tregs, which carry it at very high levels, and that engagement turns up T-G-F-beta signaling through the type-one T-G-F-beta receptor. And here's the twist — that ramped-up T-G-F-beta signal doesn't stabilize these Tregs, it destabilizes them. It shuts down CD twenty-five, so they lose their I-L-two survival signal, and it downregulates their lactate metabolism. Strip a Treg of I-L-two signaling and its metabolic fuel and it becomes unstable, loses Foxp-three, stops suppressing, and dies. And this happens fast — within fifteen hours to three days of dosing.

What makes me believe it is the genetics, because they did the clean experiments. If you delete the type-one T-G-F-beta receptor only in Tregs, anti-C-T-L-A-four completely stops working — no Treg loss, no tumor control. That's a hard result: the entire therapeutic effect runs through T-G-F-beta signaling inside the Treg. Then the killer control — they deleted C-T-L-A-four itself, only in Tregs. If the drug worked by blocking C-T-L-A-four, taking C-T-L-A-four away should mimic the drug and shrink tumors. It did the opposite: tumors were unaffected, and the mice actually had more intratumoral Tregs. You can't square that with a pure blockade model. And the whole thing still needs the Fc receptor — specifically Fc-gamma-receptor-four — but not for killing. It's needed to cross-link and agonize C-T-L-A-four. That reframes why Fc matters: not just as a flag for antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity, but as the thing that clusters the receptor to fire the signal.

The elegant part is that the same antibody does opposite things in different places, and it comes down to how much C-T-L-A-four a cell is displaying. On effector CD four and CD eight T cells, and on Tregs out in the periphery — all of which carry relatively low C-T-L-A-four — the antibody does the classic thing, it blocks, and those effectors expand. Only on the C-T-L-A-four-high Tregs sitting inside the tumor does it flip to agonist and tear them down. So you get de-suppression in the tumor and unleashing of effectors, from one antibody, sorted purely by C-T-L-A-four density. And they show it isn't a mouse artifact — ipilimumab itself, on human Tregs, upregulates the T-G-F-beta receptor and knocks down CD twenty-five and lactate metabolism, and in humanized mice it suppresses tumors the same way.

Here's why I'm putting this in front of you. If this holds, it rewrites the design brief for anti-C-T-L-A-four. The field has been building Fc-enhanced anti-C-T-L-A-four antibodies — botensilimab is the poster child — on the theory that better Fc-receptor engagement means better Treg depletion by cellular cytotoxicity. This paper says the Fc requirement is real, but the reason is different: you're not just recruiting macrophages to kill Tregs, you're clustering C-T-L-A-four to agonize it and drive a self-destruct program through T-G-F-beta. That's a different optimization target — you'd tune for cross-linking geometry and agonism, not just a cytotoxicity-competent Fc. And it names T-G-F-beta signaling as the effector arm inside the Treg, which is exactly the kind of node you could imagine potentiating. It's also a caution for everyone stacking T-G-F-beta blockade onto checkpoint therapy: block T-G-F-beta systemically and, if this is right, you might blunt the very mechanism that's clearing your tumor Tregs. T-G-F-beta stabilizing Tregs in one setting and destabilizing them in another is the kind of context-dependence that quietly burns a combination trial.

Big caveats, as always: it's a preprint, not peer-reviewed, and it leans heavily on mouse models even with the human-Treg and humanized-mouse data. The Treg-depletion camp won't fold on one paper, and the honest read is probably that all three mechanisms — blockade, depletion, and this agonist-driven destabilization — coexist, and their mix depends on the tumor, the antibody's Fc, and how much C-T-L-A-four the cells are showing. But it's the most satisfying single explanation I've seen for why anti-C-T-L-A-four needs an active Fc and why Treg-specific C-T-L-A-four knockout doesn't behave like the drug. Worth reading in full — it's the kind of mechanism paper that should change how you think about the next generation of these antibodies.
