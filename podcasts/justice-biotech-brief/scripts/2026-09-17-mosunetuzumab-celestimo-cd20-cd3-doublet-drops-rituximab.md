# Mosunetuzumab — CELESTIMO wins Phase 3 in second-line follicular lymphoma by replacing rituximab instead of adding to it

Paper link: https://www.biospace.com/press-releases/genentechs-lunsumio-based-regimen-significantly-improves-progression-free-survival-in-follicular-lymphoma-in-phase-iii-celestimo-study

## Script

Genentech announced this morning that the Phase 3 CELESTIMO study hit its primary endpoint. Mosunetuzumab — the C-D-twenty by C-D-three T cell engager sold as Lunsumio — plus lenalidomide, against rituximab plus lenalidomide, in follicular lymphoma after at least one prior line. Statistically significant and clinically meaningful improvement in progression-free survival, overall survival immature, safety consistent with the known profiles, no new signals. There are no numbers in the release. Data go to health authorities and to an upcoming medical meeting.

So on the surface this is a routine positive-topline day. It isn't, and the reason is in the control arm and in the molecule, so let me take those in order.

Start with what the trial actually compares. Four hundred seventy-eight patients, progression-free survival by independent review committee on Lugano criteria, and the comparator is R-squared — rituximab plus lenalidomide, which is a real standard of care in this setting, not a strawman. Notice the architecture, though. Genentech is not adding its bispecific on top of R-squared. It is taking rituximab out and putting mosunetuzumab in. A two-drug regimen against a two-drug regimen, with the anti-C-D-twenty antibody swapped for an anti-C-D-twenty T cell engager.

Now the part that makes this interesting, which is that somebody already ran the other version of this experiment and won. EPCORE FL-1 — epcoritamab added to R-squared versus R-squared alone, AbbVie and Genmab, four hundred eighty-eight patients — published in The Lancet in December. Hazard ratio for progression-free survival of zero point two one, overall response ninety-five percent against seventy-nine, complete response eighty-three percent against fifty, and the paper's own conclusion positions that triplet as the new standard of care for second-line and later follicular lymphoma. So Genentech is arriving second, into a space a competitor has already claimed in print, with a numberless press release.

Here is the thing I want you to take away, though, because I think it explains the whole strategic picture and almost nobody frames it this way. The difference between a triplet and a doublet here is not a commercial preference. It is a property of the binder.

Epcoritamab was deliberately engineered to hit an unusual epitope on C-D-twenty, spanning the first and second extracellular loops. Rituximab and obinutuzumab bind a partially overlapping epitope in the second loop, and epcoritamab's site is distinct from both. That was a design goal, and the point of it was exactly this — so you can co-administer an anti-C-D-twenty antibody and an anti-C-D-twenty engager without them fighting over the same site. Genmab has published the combinability work: there is dose-dependent steric interference between the antibody and the engager, but in cell lines, in samples from R-CHOP-treated patients, and in an animal model, it didn't impair killing.

Mosunetuzumab's C-D-twenty arm is the two-H-seven clone. That is rituximab's epitope. Same site.

So Genentech could not straightforwardly build the triplet. Their engager and the antibody they'd be adding it to compete for the same piece of the same antigen, and rituximab would be simultaneously occupying the target and depleting the B cells the engager needs in order to work. To be fair to them, this isn't an absolute bar — mosunetuzumab retained activity alongside an effectorless rituximab variant that occupies C-D-twenty without killing anything, which says co-dosing is at least conceivable. But they did not take that into a Phase 3. They ran the doublet. And this morning's release sells that as a feature: a two-drug outpatient regimen with a simple dosing schedule. Which is a very good way to describe a constraint you didn't choose.

Except — and this is the genuinely pleasing part — the constraint looks like it might be the better molecule anyway. Genentech has a non-randomized single-arm U-S extension cohort inside CELESTIMO, fifty-four patients on the mosunetuzumab-lenalidomide doublet, reported at A-S-H last December. Overall response ninety-six percent. Complete response eighty-seven percent. Cytokine release syndrome twenty-eight percent, mostly low grade. Put that next to the epcoritamab triplet's ninety-five percent overall response, eighty-three percent complete response, cytokine release syndrome around thirty-five percent, grade three or higher infections at thirty-three percent, and grade three or higher adverse events at ninety percent of patients versus sixty-eight on R-squared. That is cross-trial, fifty-four patients against two hundred forty-three, and you should hold it loosely. But the direction is that the third drug bought no detectable additional response and did add toxicity. If that holds up, rituximab in a regimen that already contains a C-D-twenty engager is not a backbone. It's a passenger.

Now the caveat that I think matters more than either drug, and it is about the comparator.

AUGMENT is the trial that made R-squared a standard of care. Median progression-free survival on R-squared in AUGMENT: thirty-nine point four months. Median progression-free survival on R-squared in the control arm of EPCORE FL-1: eleven point seven months. Same regimen. Three-and-a-half-fold difference.

The explanation is in the eligibility criteria, and it's structural. AUGMENT required patients to be rituximab-sensitive if they'd had prior rituximab. EPCORE FL-1 and CELESTIMO both do the opposite — they require prior anti-C-D-twenty antibody exposure, and neither one excludes rituximab refractoriness. Meanwhile both trials explicitly exclude patients with documented refractoriness to lenalidomide, and both exclude anyone with lenalidomide exposure in the prior twelve months.

Read those two exclusions together. Lenalidomide is in both arms, and it is protected. Rituximab is the control arm's only differentiating drug, and it is not protected. Every patient has already seen an anti-C-D-twenty antibody, having failed it is fine, and then the comparator arm gets an anti-C-D-twenty antibody. The R-squared in these trials is not the R-squared of the AUGMENT label.

I'm not calling that rigged — you cannot ethically demand rituximab sensitivity while a fully active alternative exists, and progression-free survival by blinded independent review is a legitimate endpoint. But it does mean these hazard ratios are partly a statement about the control arm.

So here is the marker I'd set for when CELESTIMO's actual numbers land. Do not read the hazard ratio first. Read the control arm. If Genentech's R-squared arm comes in somewhere near twelve months, then CELESTIMO enrolled the same refractory, early-progressing population EPCORE FL-1 did, and the two hazard ratios are roughly comparable — and a doublet matching a triplet is a real win. If that control arm comes in north of twenty-five months, CELESTIMO enrolled a genuinely less refractory second-line population, its hazard ratio will look more modest than zero point two one, and that more modest number will be the harder-won one. The second thing to watch is whether twenty-eight percent cytokine release syndrome survives the jump from fifty-four patients to four hundred seventy-eight.

And one last oddity worth sitting with. CELESTIMO is the confirmatory study required to convert Lunsumio's accelerated approval to full approval. That accelerated approval is for mosunetuzumab as monotherapy in third-line-and-later disease. CELESTIMO tests a combination, in an earlier line, against an active comparator. It will convert the label, and it does not test the thing that got approved.
