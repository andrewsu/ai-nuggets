# CD3×BCMA bispecifics — a day-4 CXCL10 spike and a lymphocyte crash separate responders from non-responders, and CRS doesn't

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.08.17.743749v1

## Script

Today's item is a preprint out of Institut Cochin in Paris, and it's the kind of paper that changes what you'd actually measure in a clinic rather than what you'd build in a lab. They took fourteen relapsed-refractory myeloma patients going onto a C-D-three by B-C-M-A bispecific — teclistamab-class, the approved stuff — and instead of profiling them at baseline and then at month three like everybody does, they sampled them at baseline, day four, week one, and weekly through the first month. Mass cytometry on T cells, cytokines in plasma, and the routine clinical labs. Deep on few patients rather than shallow on many, which they're explicit about as a design choice.

Here's the universal finding, and it's clean. Every single patient does the same thing in the first week. Circulating lymphocytes fall off a cliff — sixty-four percent down by day four, below two hundred cells per microliter, and it's already detectable twenty-four hours after the first dose. At the same time, plasma C-X-C-L-ten goes from a baseline mean of about seventy-eight picograms per milliliter — already elevated, healthy donors sit around ten — up to roughly three hundred by day four. Gamma-delta and C-D-eight T cells light up: C-D-sixty-nine goes up, C-D-thirty-eight and H-L-A-D-R go up. And then all of it comes back. Lymphocyte counts recover from week two, and by weeks three and four most patients are back at baseline with the activation markers back down.

Now the part I think matters most, and it's a negative result buried in a discussion section. It doesn't happen again. These patients are getting dosed weekly. There is no second lymphopenia, no second C-X-C-L-ten wave, no second activation burst. The whole immunological event is first-dose-dominated. Subsequent doses appear to be maintaining target engagement, not re-inducing systemic immune activation.

Think about what that implies. The mental model a lot of people carry for a C-D-three engager is a sustained synapse-forming pressure — you keep dosing because you keep needing to redirect T cells. What this looks like instead is one acute priming event in the bone marrow, T cells activate and redistribute out of blood into tissue, and then the system settles into a new equilibrium that repeated dosing doesn't perturb. If that's right, it's a mechanistic argument for the response-adapted, de-escalated dosing schedules the field has been groping toward empirically — mostly because of the infection burden — and it says the thing you're preserving with continued dosing is exposure, not activation.

Then they went after response. Fourteen clinical variables, a hundred and ninety-three immunological features, five timepoints, fourteen patients, integrated with factorial analysis of mixed data — which is just principal components for datasets that mix continuous measurements with categorical clinical variables, so cytokine concentrations and bone-lesion counts can sit in the same space. Responders and non-responders separated. Then they built a correlation network out of that space and asked which features clustered with response, and then — this is the good methodological instinct — they went back and checked each one in the raw data without imputation, and threw out the ones that didn't survive. The K-I-R and N-K-G-two-A associations didn't survive. Then they validated the rest on an extended cohort of up to forty-seven patients.

What survived is a short, cheap list. Responders had a bigger C-X-C-L-ten rise at day four — three hundred thirty-six versus a hundred seventy-three picograms per milliliter. Responders had a deeper lymphocyte drop at day four and week one. Responders had about a two-fold increase in C-D-sixty-nine on their C-D-eight cells at day four. Lower soluble B-C-M-A, sustained through the first month. Fewer bone lesions. And at baseline, fewer exhausted C-D-eight T cells — one and a half percent versus about six.

So the punchline is that the thing that looks like toxicity is the efficacy signal. The profound early lymphopenia isn't the drug hurting the immune system, it's the drug working — T cells leaving the blood because they've been recruited somewhere. And the corollary is sharper: cytokine release syndrome did not associate with response at all. Neither did age, nor line of therapy. So the toxicity everybody grades, manages, and premedicates against tells you nothing about whether the drug is going to work — while the complete blood count on day four, which every hospital on earth already runs, apparently does.

The other useful negative: baseline T-cell differentiation state didn't predict anything. Senescent fraction, cytotoxic profile, memory versus naive — none of it separated responders. Neither did C-D-thirty-eight-positive Tregs or effector C-D-eight frequencies, both of which have been reported as baseline correlates in prior work. They flag the discrepancy honestly and offer the obvious explanations — different populations, different treatment histories, and fourteen patients is not a lot of power. But the shape of the answer here is that it isn't what the T cells are, it's how many there are and how fast they move.

Caveats, because they're real. Fourteen patients in the deep arm — the authors call it a pilot study and they're right. The extended cohort is bigger but it's baseline clinical data and plasma, not full mass cytometry, so the immunophenotype findings don't get the same validation the C-X-C-L-ten and lymphocyte findings do. The bone-lesion association is a Fisher's exact test at p equals point two-one in the extended cohort — that one is a trend and they say so. And the correlation network rests on imputed data, which they warn about themselves: imputation can manufacture structure that isn't there, which is exactly why the raw-data check matters and why the parts that didn't survive it should stay dead.

Here's why I'd put this in front of you specifically. The authors close by pointing at trispecifics, and they're pointing at the right thing. If clinical response to a C-D-three engager is largely a function of pre-existing T-cell fitness plus the ability to mount a fast first-dose redistribution, then adding a third specificity — a costimulatory arm, a checkpoint arm, whatever — doesn't obviously rescue a patient whose T cell compartment can't produce the day-four event in the first place. You'd want to know whether the trispecific changes the shape of that first week or just makes it bigger. And this paper hands you a way to ask: a chemokine, a lymphocyte count, and a sample on day four.
