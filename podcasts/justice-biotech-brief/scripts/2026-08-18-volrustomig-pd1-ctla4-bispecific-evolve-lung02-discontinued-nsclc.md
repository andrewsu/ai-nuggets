# Volrustomig — PD-1×CTLA-4 bispecific fails to beat Keytruda plus chemo in first-line lung cancer, and AstraZeneca kills the Phase 3

Paper link: https://www.cancernetwork.com/view/evolve-lung02-trial-of-volrustomig-discontinued-in-metastatic-nsclc

## Script

Today's pick is a failure, and it's the most instructive thing that happened all week. AstraZeneca has discontinued eVOLVE-Lung02, a Phase 3 trial of volrustomig — its P-D-1-by-C-T-L-A-4 bispecific antibody — in first-line metastatic non-small-cell lung cancer. Eight hundred ninety-five patients, twenty-five countries, running since late 2023. At a planned interim look, the independent data monitoring committee concluded the volrustomig arm was unlikely to hit either of the trial's two primary endpoints. No new safety signals. The trial is over.

Start with why this molecule was worth building, because that's the part that should interest you. Volrustomig is not a naive combination in one package. It's a monovalent IgG1 bispecific engineered so that the P-D-1 arm does the heavy lifting on binding, and the C-T-L-A-4 arm rides along. The design intent is to block P-D-1 completely while blocking C-T-L-A-4 preferentially on T cells that are already P-D-1 positive. That's a beautiful piece of reasoning. C-T-L-A-4 blockade works — ipilimumab proved that — but it's a systemically toxic mechanism, because C-T-L-A-4 is a brake on T cell priming everywhere in the body, including on regulatory T cells in healthy tissue. P-D-1, by contrast, goes up on T cells that have actually seen antigen and gotten activated, which means P-D-1 positivity is a rough address label for the tumor. So if you tether your C-T-L-A-4 blockade to a P-D-1 binder, you're using the P-D-1 arm as a homing signal to concentrate the dangerous mechanism where you want it and spare the periphery. Format as targeting. Same instinct as masked engagers, same instinct as the proximity antigens we talked about earlier this month.

And the population they chose was the right test. Patients with P-D-L-one expression below fifty percent, with the two primary endpoints — progression-free survival and overall survival — reserved for the P-D-L-one-negative subgroup, under one percent. That's deliberately the hardest place to work. P-D-L-one-low and negative tumors are where checkpoint blockade plus chemotherapy does worst, and it's also where the prior evidence for adding C-T-L-A-4 blockade has been most credible — AstraZeneca's own tremelimumab experience in lung cancer pointed that direction. So this wasn't a fishing expedition. It was a well-motivated hypothesis, run head to head against the actual standard of care, pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy, at the population where the hypothesis should have had its best shot. That's a good trial. It's also why the negative result carries weight.

Now the interpretation, and I want to be careful here, because there's a lazy read and a useful one. The lazy read is that dual checkpoint blockade doesn't work. It does; it's approved in melanoma, in renal cell, in mesothelioma. The useful read is about what the elegant engineering did and didn't buy. It clearly bought pharmacology — the first-in-human work showed the receptor occupancy and the pharmacodynamic biomarker changes AstraZeneca predicted. But it did not buy a therapeutic window. Roughly a third of patients in the Phase 1 came off drug for adverse events, and analysts have been asking ever since whether a workable window existed at all. And it did not buy efficacy in a tumor with almost no P-D-L-one. Which, in hindsight, tracks mechanistically: if you build a drug whose selectivity depends on finding P-D-1-high, antigen-experienced T cells inside the tumor, and then you deploy it against tumors that are immunologically cold, you have aimed very precisely at something that isn't there. Better aim doesn't help when the target population of cells is missing.

The reason I'd put this at the top of the brief, though, is the contrast sitting right next to it. Six days ago, China's regulator approved ivonescimab plus chemotherapy in first-line squamous lung cancer — the third approved indication there for Akeso and Summit's P-D-1-by-V-E-G-F bispecific. That approval rests on HARMONi-6, where ivonescimab plus chemotherapy beat tislelizumab plus chemotherapy on overall survival: median twenty-seven-point-nine months versus twenty-three-point-seven, a thirty-four percent reduction in the risk of death, and the first time any regimen has beaten a P-D-1-plus-chemotherapy standard on survival in a randomized first-line lung cancer Phase 3. So inside of a week: the dual-checkpoint bispecific can't beat pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy, and the checkpoint-plus-V-E-G-F bispecific does beat a P-D-1 plus chemotherapy. Same disease, same competitive logic, opposite outcomes.

I think the lesson is about how you choose the second arm. Adding C-T-L-A-4 to P-D-1 amplifies along one axis — it's two brakes on the same T cell, and you pay for the amplification in immune toxicity. Adding V-E-G-F blockade changes the environment the T cell has to work in: the vasculature, the myeloid suppression, the trafficking. It's an orthogonal axis, and it's a mechanism with real standalone activity. If you're designing multispecifics, that's the transferable point. Orthogonality may be worth more than amplification, and format elegance is not a substitute for target selection.

Two things to hold in reserve. First, this class is not a free lunch either — ivonescimab's benefit estimates have been genuinely sensitive to how long you follow patients, moving in both directions across data cuts, and Endpoints reported yesterday that the China numbers softened on longer follow-up. I haven't been able to read the underlying analysis, so treat that as a flag, not a finding. Second, volrustomig isn't dead. Three other Phase 3 programs continue — cervical cancer, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, and mesothelioma — and mesothelioma in particular is a setting where C-T-L-A-4 blockade already has a strong prior. What's gone is the biggest indication, from a pipeline AstraZeneca is counting on to reach eighty billion dollars in sales by 2030. Lung cancer with low P-D-L-one remains what it has been for a decade: the graveyard where good immuno-oncology hypotheses go to be disproven.
