# ATTOBODY — Attovia's biparatopic VHH multispecific platform lands a $289M IPO, from an IL-31 itch biologic to a trispecific for inflammatory bowel disease

Paper link: https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/08/05/3338994/0/en/Attovia-Therapeutics-Announces-Pricing-of-Upsized-Initial-Public-Offering.html

## Script

The headline today is an IPO, and I know that's not usually where the science is. But Attovia Therapeutics priced an upsized offering yesterday — about two hundred eighty-nine million dollars, closing today — and it's worth five minutes not because of the number but because of what the company is: a pure-play multispecific-antibody shop that has aimed the whole toolkit not at cancer, but at immunology and inflammation. That's the migration you and I keep talking about — the engineering that grew up in oncology, the bispecifics and trispecifics, moving into autoimmune and allergic disease — and Attovia is one of the clearest bets that it travels.

Start with the platform, because it's the actual differentiator. It's called ATTOBODY, in-licensed from Alamar Biosciences, and it's built on single-domain antibodies — the small V-H-H domains from camelids — strung together with a proprietary peptide linker. The trick that gives it its name is biparatopic binding: two of those single-domain antibodies latch onto two different epitopes on the same target molecule at once. Think about what that buys you — higher avidity, tighter and more durable target engagement, and a modular Lego-like system where you can swap building blocks to make a monospecific, a multispecific, or even a conditional, AND-gated bispecific that only fires when both antigens are present. It's a different construction philosophy from the I-g-G-based bispecifics and the C-D-three T-cell engagers you work with, and the pitch is tunable affinity with cleaner developability.

Now the pipeline, because this is where it gets interesting for someone in your seat. The lead is ATTO-1310, and it targets interleukin thirty-one — the so-called itch cytokine, upregulated across a whole family of pruritic diseases. It finished dosing its phase-one trial earlier this year, and the early read is the kind you want: well tolerated, low immunogenicity, and pharmacokinetics plus target-engagement data pointing toward infrequent, extended dosing. The phase-one-b in chronic pruritus and high-itch atopic dermatitis showed rapid, deep itch relief in both populations plus lesion control. And rather than chase one indication, they're pursuing a pan-pruritic strategy — treat anyone with elevated interleukin thirty-one, including chronic-kidney-disease-associated itch. Low immunogenicity is the number I'd flag hardest, because a camelid-derived multi-domain construct is exactly where you'd worry about anti-drug antibodies, and so far that worry hasn't materialized.

The second program, ATTO-2306, is where the multispecific thesis shows its teeth. It's a half-life-extended bispecific that blocks interleukin thirteen and interleukin thirty-one in one molecule — the two clinically and commercially validated pathways in atopic dermatitis, hit simultaneously. The anti-interleukin-thirteen arm is derived from lebrikizumab, and the interleukin-thirty-one arms are the same single-domain binders from their lead drug. The design argument is sharp: they claim it sidesteps the reverse dose-efficacy problem seen when you target the interleukin-thirty-one receptor directly, and the target-mediated drug disposition that bedevils drugs against the interleukin-four receptor alpha — dupilumab's target. Whether that holds up in the clinic is the open question; it's in I-N-D-enabling work with a phase one expected next year.

But the one I'd circle for you is ATTO-1091. It's a trispecific Fc-fusion that blocks three things at once in inflammatory bowel disease — T-L-one-A, interleukin twenty-three, and the integrin alpha-four-beta-seven. Stop and appreciate that. Those are three separately validated I-B-D mechanisms, each already a drug class of its own, folded into a single molecule. This is the boldest version of the multispecific thesis: not combination therapy, but combination pharmacology in one construct — the same conceptual move as the trispecific T-cell engagers you follow, pointed at gut inflammation instead of a tumor. It's still I-N-D-enabling, so it's a long way from proof, but it's the most novel thing in the portfolio.

So how should you hold this? Skeptically but with real interest. Every one of these franchises is crowded — interleukin thirty-one has nemolizumab, atopic dermatitis has dupilumab and lebrikizumab, I-B-D has a wall of biologics — so a platform play only wins if biparatopic multispecifics deliver something the incumbents can't: less frequent dosing, deeper responses, or a genuinely differentiated three-in-one that no combination regimen matches on convenience or cost. The interleukin-thirty-one data are early phase one, and the two most interesting assets are still preclinical. But the reason to watch is the platform bet itself. If Attovia shows that biparatopic V-H-H multispecifics can be built cleanly, dosed infrequently, and stacked up to three targets deep in autoimmune disease, that's a template that reaches well past dermatology and the gut — and it's the same engineering conversation you're having on the oncology side, just with the targets swapped out.
