## Script

On Friday I argued that in mismatch-repair-deficient colon cancer, the timing of checkpoint blockade relative to surgery matters more than which checkpoint you block. Over the weekend Akeso announced that the first patient has been dosed in a Phase 3 that tests the neoadjuvant version of that idea, with a twist: the drug is cadonilimab, its PD-1 by CTLA-4 bispecific, given alone, with no chemotherapy, before and after surgery. The trial is called COMPASSION-40. It is the thirteenth registrational study for cadonilimab, and the first time it has gone into potentially curable disease.

Here is the design. Three hundred and eighty-six patients, aged eighteen to seventy-five, with resectable stage two-B to stage three, MSI-high or mismatch-repair-deficient colon cancer, randomized open-label. One arm gets cadonilimab, then surgery, then more cadonilimab. The other goes straight to surgery and then gets the physician's choice of fluoropyrimidine, with or without oxaliplatin. That is the old standard, the one chemotherapy is known to do little for in this tumor type. There are two primary endpoints, pathological complete response and event-free survival. Primary completion is estimated for mid-2030.

Look at that first primary endpoint next to the control arm. Pathological complete response is defined in the registry as no residual tumor and no positive lymph nodes after neoadjuvant therapy. The control arm gets no neoadjuvant therapy. Its patients go straight to the operating room with an untreated tumor, so its complete response rate is zero more or less by construction. Cadonilimab's own Phase 2 reported complete responses in eighty-five percent of patients who went to surgery. So one of the two primary endpoints is close to a formality. It will read out early, it will be dramatic, and it will tell you almost nothing about whether the strategy beats the alternative. Event-free survival is the real experiment. When the press release about the pCR hit lands, and it will, that is the number to wait for.

And that eighty-five percent deserves a footnote of its own. It comes from a Phase 2 at the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences, presented at ESMO Immuno-Oncology in 2024. That trial enrolled colon and rectal cohorts, had a combined pathological-plus-clinical complete response as its primary endpoint, and dosed at ten milligrams per kilogram every three weeks for up to eight cycles. The figure Akeso quotes is among patients who reached surgery, not everyone enrolled. Patients who got a complete clinical response and skipped the operation, or who didn't make it to resection, sit outside that denominator.

The scientific question I actually care about is what CTLA-4 adds here. This is the most immunogenic tumor in the colon. PD-1 blockade alone already gets you a long way: in one Chinese neoadjuvant study with toripalimab, monotherapy produced complete pathological responses in eleven of seventeen patients. NICHE-2 got sixty-eight percent with one low dose of ipilimumab and two doses of nivolumab before surgery, with grade three or four immune toxicity in four percent and no recurrences at two years. The usual case for a PD-1 by CTLA-4 bispecific is toxicity. Full-dose combination checkpoint blockade in metastatic disease hurts a lot of people, and cadonilimab was built to take the edge off. It has an Fc-null design, so no Fc-receptor engagement, no antibody-dependent killing of regulatory T cells, and little interleukin-six release. And it is tetravalent, so it binds with higher avidity where PD-1 and CTLA-4 are both dense on the same cell, which is to say on tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes rather than in the periphery. In metastatic disease that is a genuine advantage. In the neoadjuvant colon setting, NICHE-2 has already shown the toxicity problem is largely solvable with a short course and a low CTLA-4 dose. So the bispecific's selling point has less headroom here than anywhere else in its pipeline.

There is a mechanistic argument in its favor, though, and it runs straight through Friday's thesis. The avidity design should work best exactly when the primary tumor and its draining nodes are still in place, full of PD-1 and CTLA-4 double-positive T cells. That condition holds only before surgery. Adjuvant use of this molecule would waste its most distinctive property.

The competitive picture is worth knowing. GSK's AZUR-2 is testing perioperative dostarlimab monotherapy against standard care in eight hundred and ninety-two patients with T4 node-negative or stage three disease. It has been enrolling since August 2023, its primary endpoint is event-free survival by blinded central review, and its estimated primary completion is about a year ahead of Akeso's. Neither trial compares against the regimen the FDA approved last Friday, atezolizumab with FOLFOX after surgery, because both were designed before ATOMIC changed the standard. So whichever wins, the next argument is already set up: short neoadjuvant blockade against a year of adjuvant chemo-immunotherapy, head to head. One smaller point. The upper age limit of seventy-five matters in a disease where sporadic mismatch-repair deficiency skews older.

My read: this is a sensible trial with a flattering co-primary bolted on. The interesting result, three or four years from now, is whether a PD-1 by CTLA-4 bispecific given around surgery beats PD-1 alone given the same way. COMPASSION-40 can't answer that, because its control arm is chemotherapy. The cross-trial comparison against AZUR-2 is where that argument will actually be fought, and cross-trial comparisons are where good arguments go to die.
