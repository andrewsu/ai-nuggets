# ZMYND8 — the chromatin reader that closes the IL-2 receptor alpha enhancer, and why it argues with the not-alpha IL-2 designs you've been reading about all month

## Script

Housekeeping first, because it explains the shape of today's episode. bioRxiv is down. Fine. It's a journal day, and Nature published something yesterday that is squarely yours.

It's out of Hongbo Chi's lab in the immunology department at St. Jude, and the one-line version is that they found the epigenetic brake that turns off C-D-twenty-five in exhausted C-D-eight T cells, and it is a chromatin reader called Z-M-Y-N-D-eight.

Here's the setup you already know. As C-D-eight T cells go down the exhaustion path under chronic antigen, they progressively lose the high-affinity interleukin-two receptor alpha chain. Low C-D-twenty-five means the cell only ever catches I-L-two intermittently, S-T-A-T-five activation stays weak, and weak S-T-A-T-five is permissive for the terminal exhaustion program. Everyone has known this for years. Nobody knew why the alpha chain goes quiet. That's the hole this paper fills, and the way it fills it is what makes it worth your time.

They did in vivo single-cell CRISPR screens in P-fourteen T-C-R-transgenic cells under chronic L-C-M-V clone thirteen, with a custom library of ninety-one epigenetic factors nominated from public expression and accessibility data, plus nine transcription factors already known to steer exhaustion as internal positive controls. Three sgRNAs per gene, thirty non-targeting controls, readout at day twenty-eight, and separately a bulk screen and a second screen against I-L-two signaling regulators. Then they asked three independent questions of the same dataset. Which perturbations expand the effector-like states — the intermediate exhausted cells and the K-L-R-expressing cells that do most of the actual killing. Which perturbations push cells into the terminal state. And which perturbations change S-T-A-T-five activity, scored against a constitutively-active-S-T-A-T-five signature.

Z-M-Y-N-D-eight is the top hit on all three. Top negative regulator of effector-like states, top positive regulator of the terminal state, top negative regulator of S-T-A-T-five activity. One gene landing at the extreme of three orthogonal rankings out of the same screen is the kind of triangulation that usually doesn't happen, and it is why I believe the rest of the paper.

The mechanism is clean and it's the part you can steal. Z-M-Y-N-D-eight is a reader — it has a plant homeodomain, a bromodomain and a PWWP domain in an N-terminal cassette. CUT-and-RUN says its binding sites overlap heavily with H-three-K-four-mono-methyl and H-three-K-twenty-seven-acetyl, which is the signature of active enhancers, and when you ask which pathway is enriched across its roughly twenty-nine hundred core binding sites, I-L-two-S-T-A-T-five signaling is number one. It sits directly on the enhancers of the genes for the alpha chain and the beta chain of the I-L-two receptor. And it shares those enhancers with p-three-hundred, the histone acetyltransferase that writes the H-three-K-twenty-seven-acetyl mark in the first place. Delete Z-M-Y-N-D-eight and p-three-hundred occupancy goes up, acetylation goes up, chromatin opens, C-D-twenty-five and C-D-one-twenty-two come back. They co-immunoprecipitate the two proteins out of C-D-eight T cells, and in a Jurkat reporter using the human I-L-two-R-A enhancer, co-expressing Z-M-Y-N-D-eight flattens p-three-hundred's transactivation of it.

Then they did the domain mapping, which matters for anyone thinking about druggability. Knock out the plant homeodomain, or the bromodomain, or the triple reader cassette, and the mutant no longer represses p-three-hundred — and it also binds p-three-hundred worse. So the reader cassette is doing double duty, and a small molecule that occupies it should, in principle, phenocopy the knockout. I'll come back to why "in principle" is carrying weight there.

The epistasis is where the paper earns its journal. Co-delete S-T-A-T-five-B along with Z-M-Y-N-D-eight and the whole phenotype reverses — no more effector-like expansion, T-O-X goes back up. Co-delete C-D-twenty-five, or C-D-one-twenty-two, largely the same. Co-delete p-three-hundred and you reverse the effector-like expansion and the receptor upregulation together. That's four separate genetic reversals all pointing the same direction. The frame they land on is nice: signal one, chronic antigen, is being used by the cell to shut off its own receptor for signal three. Exhaustion isn't just a failure to receive help. It's an actively written refusal to be helped.

Therapeutically: Z-M-Y-N-D-eight-deficient T cells control B-sixteen melanoma better on their own, better again with systemic I-L-two, and better again with P-D-L-one blockade. And there's a detail I liked — after you stop anti-P-D-L-one, control cells rebound their T-O-X expression, which is the maladaptive reprogramming that makes checkpoint responses fall apart, and Z-M-Y-N-D-eight-deficient cells largely don't.

Now the two things I actually want you to take out of this, both of which are arguments, not summaries.

The first one is about I-L-two engineering, and it runs directly against what I covered on this show yesterday. Outpace's designed I-L-two, the one encoded downstream of their activation-responsive promoter, deliberately ablates I-L-two receptor alpha binding — beta-gamma interfaces preserved, alpha thrown away, C-D-eight-alpha targeted, all to bias away from regulatory T cells. That is the dominant design philosophy in the field right now and it has good reasons behind it: the alpha chain is how you hit Tregs and it's implicated in vascular leak. But this paper says the specific lesion in an exhausted C-D-eight cell is loss of the alpha chain, and it proves the alpha chain is load-bearing by showing that co-deleting C-D-twenty-five erases most of the benefit. So the not-alpha designs are optimizing around a receptor subunit that this paper says is the thing you want back. Those two positions are not strictly incompatible — you could plausibly restore C-D-twenty-five epigenetically and then use ordinary I-L-two rather than a re-engineered one, which is arguably the cleaner play. But if you are choosing whether your armored C-A-R T's cytokine payload keeps alpha-chain engagement, there is now a mechanistic argument on the other side of that choice, and there wasn't one a week ago.

The second is a warning, and it's the most immediately actionable thing here. p-three-hundred is a drug target. Inobrodib is an oral p-three-hundred-slash-C-B-P bromodomain inhibitor from CellCentric, and it is in a pivotal trial in relapsed-refractory myeloma with pomalidomide and dexamethasone. Myeloma patients are also the population getting B-C-M-A and G-P-R-C-five-D T cell engagers and B-C-M-A C-A-R T. This paper's p-three-hundred arm shows that deleting p-three-hundred in C-D-eight T cells does the opposite of what you want: fewer effector-like cells, more progenitor and more terminal exhausted. I want to be precise about the gap — that's a genetic deletion of the whole protein, not bromodomain inhibition, and they never put a drug in an animal. But "does an oral p-three-hundred bromodomain inhibitor blunt the T cell arm of a T cell engager" is now a specific, cheap, obvious experiment, and I'd want it done before anyone designs that combination trial.

The caveats are worth listing plainly, because several of them are load-bearing. The C-A-R T data is phenotype only — mouse transgenic C-D-nineteen C-A-R cells, C-D-twenty-five and C-D-one-twenty-two and phospho-S-T-A-T-five up, T-O-X down, and that's it. There is no C-A-R T efficacy curve anywhere in the paper. Every tumor experiment is B-sixteen melanoma with T-C-R-transgenic cells. All the human evidence is signature scoring on public datasets — H-I-V, melanoma, colorectal, basal and squamous cell carcinoma — which is consistent and which is also correlation. And the druggability story is a promise: there is no Z-M-Y-N-D-eight chemical probe on the shelf the way there is for the B-E-T family, and Z-M-Y-N-D-eight's better-known day job is recruiting the NuRD complex to damaged chromatin for homologous recombination repair, so a systemic inhibitor would be doing something to tumor cells too, in a direction this paper doesn't test.

One last thing that's been bothering me since I read it. The state Z-M-Y-N-D-eight loss pushes cells into is the K-L-R-expressing, N-K-G-two-D-high, N-K-like one, and in this system that's the good state — the most cytolytic subset in the paper. Yesterday I described Outpace's promoter approvingly for suppressing the K-L-R N-K-like signature, because in the C-A-R T literature that signature reads as dysfunction. Same transcriptional vocabulary, opposite sign, depending on which model you're in. Somebody needs to work out whether those are actually the same cells.
