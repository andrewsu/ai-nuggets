# Tec-Tal — teclistamab plus talquetamab, the first dual-antigen bispecific T-cell-engager combination, posts a Phase 3 progression-free-survival hazard ratio of 0.11 in earlier-line myeloma (MonumenTAL-6)

Paper link: https://www.jnj.com/media-center/press-releases/tecvayli-talvey-reduced-the-risk-of-disease-progression-or-death-by-89-and-the-risk-of-death-by-62-in-earlier-line-relapsed-refractory-multiple-myeloma

## Script

Today's pick is a T-cell-engager story too, but flip the frame from yesterday. Yesterday we talked about cevostamab reaching for a third myeloma antigen after the first two are used up — sequencing redirectors one after another. Today's story is what happens when you stop sequencing and just fire two of them at once. Johnson and Johnson reported topline Phase 3 results from MonumenTAL-6, and the number on the page is one you almost never see in this disease.

Here's the setup. The two most established myeloma bispecifics are teclistamab, which hits B-C-M-A, and talquetamab, which hits G-P-R-C-five-D. Both are approved as single agents in the late-line setting, both redirect T cells through C-D-three, and both target antigens the plasma cell doesn't share. MonumenTAL-6 asks the obvious next question: what if you give both at the same time, and move them earlier — into patients with just one to four prior lines who've already seen a C-D-thirty-eight antibody and lenalidomide? It's a three-arm study: the double bispecific, which they're calling Tec-Tal; a lighter combination of talquetamab plus pomalidomide; and a standard-of-care pomalidomide triplet as the control.

Now the result. In the Tec-Tal arm, the hazard ratio for progression-free survival was zero-point-one-one. Let me say that plainly — an eighty-nine percent reduction in the risk of progression or death versus the control triplet. That is the lowest hazard ratio anyone has reported in a Phase 3 bispecific study in relapsed myeloma, by a wide margin. Overall survival came in at a hazard ratio of zero-point-three-eight, a sixty-two percent reduction in the risk of death. Even the lighter talquetamab-plus-pomalidomide arm cleared the bar, with a progression-free hazard ratio of zero-point-two-seven. The independent monitoring committee saw enough at the very first interim analysis to recommend unblinding.

So what's the real idea here, and why should you care beyond the headline. The thesis is that two non-cross-resistant redirectors given together aren't additive — they look multiplicative. Cevostamab's logic was that if the tumor escapes one antigen, you switch to the next. Tec-Tal's logic is that if you hit two antigens simultaneously, before the tumor has escaped either, you close the escape routes at the same time and you get depth you can't get from either drug alone. And this is the direct, real-world competitor to the trispecific programs — the single molecules like Innovent's B-C-M-A-by-G-P-R-C-five-D-by-C-D-three antibody that try to build both antigens plus the T-cell arm into one construct. MonumenTAL-6 is the brute-force version of that same bet: don't engineer one clever trispecific, just combine two proven bispecifics you already have. And right now the brute-force version has the Phase 3 data.

Here's where I'd pump the brakes, though, and it's all about the part of the release that's conspicuously thin. They told us the efficacy hazard ratios and essentially nothing quantitative about the combined safety. The language is that the profile was "consistent with the known profiles of the monotherapies" — and that is exactly the claim I don't take on faith when you stack two T-cell engagers. Each of these drugs alone drives cytokine release syndrome in the majority of patients, each carries serious infection risk, and infection is the leading cause of treatment-related death in this whole class. Talquetamab layers on its signature G-P-R-C-five-D toxicities on top — the taste loss, the dry mouth, the skin and nail damage that make it genuinely hard to live on. Run both together and the honest question isn't whether efficacy goes up; it obviously does. It's whether the overlapping immune toxicity and the infection burden stay tolerable, and whether that zero-point-one-one holds up once you're not cherry-picking the patients who could stay on both. The overall-survival number in particular is from a first interim with immature follow-up — zero-point-three-eight will move.

And one sharpening contrast, because it landed the very same day: Genmab and AbbVie disclosed that epcoritamab, the C-D-three-by-C-D-twenty bispecific, missed its overall-survival endpoint in a Phase 3 lymphoma study in the US. Same class of molecule, same week, opposite result. So the read-through for anyone building redirectors isn't that bispecifics win automatically — it's that combining two of them against complementary antigens, in earlier lines, may be the single most powerful thing you can do with this modality right now, and that the field's next real argument is combination-of-bispecifics versus one-molecule-trispecific. Watch for the full MonumenTAL-6 dataset at the next major meeting — and read the safety tables before you believe the efficacy.
