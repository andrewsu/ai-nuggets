# TEV-53408 — a single shot of anti-IL-15 protects the villi in a gluten challenge, seven years after the same experiment failed

## Script

Wednesday morning Teva put out topline from a Phase 2a in celiac disease, and I want to spend the whole episode on it, because on the surface it is a fifty-patient study from a generics company in an indication with no approved drugs, and underneath it is the cleanest causal experiment anybody in autoimmunity gets to run — and it just flipped a result that has been sitting unflipped since two thousand nineteen.

The drug is T-E-V-five-three-four-oh-eight, which Teva refers to in the press release as T-E-V four-oh-eight. It is a Teva-discovered human monoclonal antibody against interleukin fifteen, engineered for high affinity and a long half-life so it can be given subcutaneously and infrequently. Fifty adults with biopsy-confirmed celiac disease, all on a gluten-free diet for at least a year, all with essentially healed guts at baseline — the entry criterion was a villous-height-to-crypt-depth ratio of two or better. Each participant got one subcutaneous dose. Two weeks later they started eating gluten daily for six weeks under supervision, and at week eight everybody got biopsied.

That design is the reason this is worth your time. Celiac is the only common autoimmune disease where you know the antigen, you can remove it, and you can put it back. A gluten challenge is a provocation trial. You are not waiting for flares to happen on their own in a lupus cohort and arguing about whether the placebo arm got unlucky — you are administering the trigger on a schedule and reading the tissue response with a ruler. Fifty patients and eight weeks buys you a causal statement that a five-hundred-patient rheumatoid arthritis trial cannot buy at any sample size. Every autoimmune program should be jealous of this model, and almost none of them can copy it.

Now the mechanism, because the target choice is the interesting bet. Interleukin fifteen is not a helper-T-cell cytokine. It gets presented in trans, bolted to interleukin-fifteen receptor alpha on the surface of stressed enterocytes and dendritic cells, and handed directly to C-D-eight intraepithelial lymphocytes sitting in the gut lining. What it does to those cells is license them — it drives up N-K-G-two-D, it makes them cytotoxic, it makes them resistant to regulatory suppression, and it keeps them alive. In celiac the gluten-specific C-D-four T cell is the thing that recognizes the antigen, but the intraepithelial lymphocyte is the thing that actually kills the enterocyte. And you can get villous atrophy in mice from interleukin fifteen alone, with no gluten in the picture at all. So blocking interleukin fifteen is a very specific wager: that you can leave the antigen-recognition arm completely intact — these patients will still have their anti-transglutaminase antibodies, still have their gluten-reactive C-D-four cells — and simply revoke the license to kill. You are not treating the autoimmunity. You are treating the effector arm and letting the recognition happen harmlessly.

Here is why the result matters historically. Amgen ran almost exactly this experiment. A-M-G seven-fourteen, an anti-interleukin-fifteen antibody, Phase 2a, gluten challenge, Finnish sites, published in Lancet Gastroenterology and Hepatology in two thousand nineteen. Sixty-four patients, twelve weeks of dosing, and the primary endpoint was the same villous-height-to-crypt-depth ratio. It missed, and not narrowly in the interesting direction — the low dose came in at minus two-and-a-half percent versus placebo, the high dose at plus six percent, both nowhere near significant. The conclusion in print was that the drug did not prevent gluten-induced mucosal damage. What it did do was reduce the intraepithelial lymphocyte influx by about forty percent and improve diarrhea. That combination — biomarker moves, histology doesn't — is the classic profile of a mechanism that is real but underdosed, and it is also the classic profile of a mechanism that is real but not load-bearing. For seven years there was no way to tell which. Amgen handed the molecule off, it became P-R-V-oh-fifteen at Provention Bio, Sanofi bought Provention, and the field kept waiting.

Teva's numbers say underdosed. The villous-height-to-crypt-depth ratio fell by zero-point-four-three on drug versus zero-point-eight-eight on placebo — a treatment difference of zero-point-four-five, confidence interval zero-point-zero-six to zero-point-eight-four, p less than zero-point-zero-five. And the intraepithelial lymphocyte number is the one I would actually hang my hat on: placebo gained twenty-seven-point-six lymphocytes per unit, the treated arm gained zero-point-three-seven. Not attenuated — held flat. That is about as clean a target-engagement readout as histology produces, and its confidence interval is nowhere near zero.

So let me give you the honest read on those two numbers together, because they do not say the same thing. The primary endpoint is statistically fragile — the lower bound of the interval is zero-point-zero-six, which is to say the data are compatible with a benefit that is almost nothing, in fifty people, at nominal significance. The secondary endpoint is emphatic. And notice the dissociation: the intraepithelial compartment was completely prevented from expanding, while the villi still shrank about half as much as placebo. If intraepithelial lymphocyte number were the whole story, protection should have been near-total. It wasn't. That leaves you with a real biological question rather than a marketing one — either the remaining damage is C-D-four and interferon-gamma driven and interleukin fifteen was never going to touch it, or the lymphocytes that were already resident got more cytotoxic per cell without recruiting friends, or eight weeks is simply too short for the epithelium to show the difference. That question is worth more to your thinking than the p-value is.

The dosing is the part I would not skip past. One subcutaneous injection, and the effect is still there eight weeks later; the protocol follows safety out to week twenty-eight and keeps participants on study for eighty-six weeks. This is the commercial argument in its entirety. Celiac patients are young, mostly well, and managing their disease with a diet. Nobody in that population accepts a fortnightly injectable and an infection risk in exchange for being able to stop reading labels. A shot every couple of months, maybe less, changes that calculation completely — the same way etentamig's monthly schedule, which we covered Friday, is the actual argument for that drug rather than its response rate. Convenience is not a soft endpoint in a disease whose current therapy is free.

On the competitive picture — back in July we covered argenx paying two-point-two billion dollars for Forte and F-B-one-oh-two, an anti-C-D-one-twenty-two antibody, and I ended that episode saying watch the celiac Phase 2 in the second half of this year. Teva got there first with a different lever on the same axis, and the contrast is instructive. C-D-one-twenty-two is the shared receptor beta chain, so blocking it takes out interleukin fifteen and interleukin two together — broader, and it drags regulatory T cells, which live on interleukin two, into the blast radius. Teva is neutralizing the ligand only, which spares the interleukin-two arm entirely. Novartis is in the same lane, having bought Calypso for C-A-L-Y-oh-oh-two, which also showed mucosal protection in a gluten challenge. Three companies, one cytokine, and all three of them are running celiac and vitiligo — Teva's own vitiligo Phase 1b just graduated to Phase 2b. That convergence is not coincidence; it is the field agreeing that the two diseases where tissue-resident C-D-eight cells do the killing under interleukin-fifteen license are the two most winnable.

What I would watch. First, whether the fuller dataset Teva is holding for a scientific meeting shows exposure-response, because that is what converts "we out-dosed Amgen" from a story into a fact. Second, the persistent question for this whole class: interleukin fifteen keeps natural killer cells and tissue-resident memory alive for good reasons too, so infection and malignancy surveillance over years of dosing in otherwise-healthy thirty-year-olds is the real gate, not the eight-week safety table. Third, whether anyone runs the study that matters commercially, which is not a supervised gluten challenge but free-living patients eating normally, where the question is whether the drug lets you actually eat bread or merely covers you for the crumbs. Teva has not claimed the former and the trial cannot test it.
