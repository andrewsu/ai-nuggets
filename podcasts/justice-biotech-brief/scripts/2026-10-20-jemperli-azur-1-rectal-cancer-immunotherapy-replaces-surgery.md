# Jemperli — FDA approval lets dMMR rectal cancer patients skip surgery entirely

## Script

Yesterday the FDA approved GSK's dostarlimab, sold as Jemperli, as the first immunotherapy for stage two and three rectal cancer that is microsatellite instability high or mismatch repair deficient. That biomarker description is doing a lot of work, so here's the plain version: for the five to ten percent of rectal cancer patients whose tumors carry this defect, the approval lets them take an antibody infusion instead of chemotherapy, radiation, and surgery. Not in addition to. Instead of.

This traces straight back to a Memorial Sloan Kettering trial that made headlines in twenty twenty-two, when Andrea Cercek's group reported that single-agent dostarlimab produced a complete clinical response in all twelve of their mismatch-repair-deficient rectal cancer patients. No recurrence, no residual tumor on imaging or endoscopy, nothing on biopsy. It was a small, single-institution, short-follow-up result, and the standard and correct response from the field at the time was that it needed to replicate in a larger, more diverse trial before anyone changed practice. That larger trial is exactly what got approved yesterday.

It's called AZUR-1, a global single-arm study of a hundred fifty-four previously untreated patients, run by Cercek as principal investigator. The number that earned accelerated approval: sixty-eight percent of evaluable patients, ninety-eight of a hundred forty-four, had a sustained clinical complete response at twelve months by investigator assessment. Widen the lens to an exploratory analysis of who shows no evidence of cancer at a year or more, including a handful confirmed pathologically after opting for surgery anyway, and it's seventy-six percent. And this is the detail that actually matters for a therapy whose whole premise is watch-and-wait: after a median follow-up beyond two years, there have been zero recurrences among responders. The number came down a lot from Cercek's original twelve for twelve, which is exactly what should happen when a dramatic single-center result meets a large registrational trial. What held up is the part that counts — once you're a durable responder, you appear to stay one.

One number is still unsettled, and it's worth naming rather than averaging away. The independent central review, reading the same scans with no knowledge of which patient is which, currently has fifty-four patients of a hundred sixteen assessed at a twelve-month complete response — forty-seven percent, well under the investigator number. Before you read that as inflation, know that thirty-eight more patients in that cohort haven't hit their twelve-month readout yet and have already shown a complete response in earlier scans, so the independent number is very likely to climb as follow-up matures rather than settle where it sits today. But it is the honest state of the data at approval, and the gap between investigator and independent assessment of a radiologic-and-endoscopic endpoint, with no tissue confirmation for most patients, is the single most important thing to watch when GSK presents the fuller analysis at the European Society for Medical Oncology congress later this month.

The safety trade is the easy part of this story. Serious adverse reactions in twenty percent, permanent discontinuation in three percent, and the common toxicities — fatigue, rash, diarrhea — are the familiar checkpoint-inhibitor list. Against total mesorectal excision, which routinely leaves patients with bowel, bladder, or sexual dysfunction for the rest of their lives, that's not a close call for the responders.

Zoom out and this is the cleanest example you'll find of neoadjuvant logic taken to its actual endpoint, rather than its usual half-measure. The adjuvant checkpoint trial I covered on this show ten days ago, in colon cancer, resects the antigen and the draining lymph nodes before dosing the drug and gets a hazard ratio for recurrence with a flat survival curve. The perioperative trial two days after that still takes nearly everyone to the operating room regardless of response. AZUR-1 is the version of this idea that was always implied but rarely tested in a registrational trial: if a T cell needs antigen and an intact lymph node basin to work, and dosing before surgery gets both, the logical experiment is to ask whether surgery is still required at all once the tumor is gone. For this biomarker-defined slice of rectal cancer, the answer the FDA just endorsed is no.
