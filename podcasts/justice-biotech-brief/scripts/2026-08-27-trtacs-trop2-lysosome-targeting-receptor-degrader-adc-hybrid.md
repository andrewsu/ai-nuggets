# TRTACs — TROP2, the most crowded target in ADCs, gets repurposed as the shuttle instead of the target, degrading EGFR, HER2 and PD-L1

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.08.19.745708v1

## Script

Today's pick is a preprint out of East China University of Science and Technology, and the reason I want it in front of you is that it does something structurally clever with the most worked-over target in your field. TROP-two is where everyone is building antibody-drug conjugates right now — sacituzumab govitecan, datopotamab deruxtecan, sac-T-M-T, a dozen more in the clinic. This group looked at TROP-two and asked a different question. Not what payload should we hang off it, but what if TROP-two isn't the target at all. What if it's the elevator.

The background here is extracellular targeted protein degradation. The idea is old enough to have a canon: you want to get rid of a protein that sits on the cell surface or floats in the extracellular space, so you build a bifunctional molecule with one arm that grabs your protein of interest and another arm that grabs a receptor which constitutively cycles into the lysosome. The receptor drags your target down with it and the lysosome does the rest. The original version, LYTACs — say it as lie-tacks — used the cation-independent mannose-six-phosphate receptor, and the workhorse variant uses I-G-F-two as the lysosome-targeting handle. The chronic problem with all of them is the same: those receptors are on essentially every cell in the body, so you get degradation everywhere, and there's no tumor selectivity in the mechanism itself.

So this group ran a small, honest screen. They took six clinically validated ADC targets — HER-two, TROP-two, folate receptor alpha, tissue factor, claudin eighteen point two, and C-D-forty-four — and asked which of them can actually function as a lysosome-targeting receptor. They fused a nanobody against each one to a nanobody against E-G-F-R, used E-G-F-R as the model substrate, and ran all six in the same two cell lines so the comparison isn't confounded by cell background. TROP-two won, claudin eighteen point two came second, and the rest did almost nothing. And the detail that makes it interesting is that TROP-two was not the most abundant receptor on those cells. Whatever makes it good at this is not expression level, it's trafficking.

The molecules they built are small — about thirty kilodaltons, a nanobody fused to a nanobody, no Fc. They call them tur-tacks. Against the I-G-F-two benchmark, the TROP-two version degraded E-G-F-R better, and neither arm alone did anything, so the chimeric architecture is doing the work rather than either binder. Internalization goes through clathrin-mediated endocytosis, and blocking lysosomal acidification with bafilomycin rescues the protein without blocking its internalization — so it really is lysosomal degradation and not just receptor occupancy. A hundred nanomolar for twenty-four hours takes intracellular E-G-F-R down about half, dose-dependent, no hook effect in the range they tested.

Now here's the load-bearing result, and it's also the one I'd push on hardest. They tested six cancer lines and two normal ones — lung fibroblasts and human umbilical vein endothelial cells. Every cancer line degraded E-G-F-R. Neither normal line did, essentially at all. And critically, both normal lines express appreciable TROP-two. So the selectivity is not coming from a tumor-versus-normal expression gap. It's coming from something about how malignant cells internalize and sort TROP-two — the authors say as much in the discussion, invoking altered membrane dynamics or endosomal sorting, and they cite prior work for it rather than demonstrating it. That's the crux of the whole platform and it's the one thing in the paper that rests on assertion. If that differential holds up, this is a genuinely tumor-selective degradation modality. If it turns out to be a property of the specific normal cells they chose, the therapeutic window gets a lot narrower.

They then showed the scope is real: swap the substrate arm and you degrade P-D-L-one on a triple-negative breast line and HER-two on a HER-two-positive line, both beating the I-G-F-two-based controls, with HER-two degradation detectable down at four nanomolar.

And then the part I think you'll find most fun. They put a payload on it. Terminal cysteine, standard valine-citrulline linker, M-M-A-E — so now you have a molecule that degrades its target and delivers a cytotoxin through the same trip to the lysosome. The HER-two version killed half the cells at two-tenths of a nanomolar, while the plain HER-two-nanobody conjugate and the plain TROP-two-nanobody conjugate did essentially nothing at that concentration. It held up in spheroids, it held up in two xenograft models against both non-chimeric conjugates, and doxorubicin worked as a payload too.

Here's the conceptual point I'd take away. In a conventional ADC, one antigen has to do three jobs at once — it has to be the thing you bind, it has to be the thing that internalizes, and it has to be the thing that's tumor-specific. Those requirements fight each other constantly, and it's why the target list is short and everyone is crowded onto the same few. This architecture splits the jobs. The substrate arm picks what you want removed, TROP-two supplies the internalization and, they claim, the selectivity. That opens up targets that are famously terrible internalizers, which is a real constraint you've run into.

Four things to be skeptical about. First, every in vivo experiment is in nude mice. P-D-L-one is the most provocative substrate in the paper, and degrading P-D-L-one instead of blocking it is a genuinely interesting question — and it is completely untestable in an animal with no T cells. They didn't run it. Second, there is no pharmacokinetics in this paper at all. Thirty kilodaltons is well under the renal filtration cutoff, they dosed every two days for five to eight doses, and they acknowledge in the limitations that half-life extension is required — but every fix for that adds mass and starts eating the tumor-penetration advantage they're claiming. Third, the tyrosine-kinase-inhibitor resistance angle is oversold: their conjugate was less potent against the gefitinib-resistant line than the parent line at high concentrations, and better only in a narrow middle window. Fourth, all the head-to-heads are against I-G-F-two chimeras, which is the right academic benchmark, but nobody in the clinic is dosing an I-G-F-two chimera. The comparator that matters is a TROP-two ADC, and it isn't here.

Still, the strategic logic is sound in a way that should interest you specifically. TROP-two immunohistochemistry is already a routine assay because of the ADCs, so the patient-stratification infrastructure for this platform exists on day one. And if the literature is right that TROP-two goes up after chemotherapy and is enriched in drug-tolerant populations, then the shuttle gets more available exactly in the setting where you've run out of other options.

One other thing worth a minute of your morning. Akeso and Summit reported that ivonescimab plus chemotherapy beat durvalumab plus chemotherapy on overall survival in first-line advanced biliary tract cancer — a six-hundred-eighty-two-patient registrational Phase three, the first Phase three overall survival win in that disease and the first non-lung readout for a P-D-one-by-V-E-G-F bispecific. They released no numbers whatsoever, it's a China-only trial, and full data goes to a conference later. So: real event, zero data, and worth watching for when the curves actually show up.
