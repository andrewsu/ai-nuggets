# Zenbexus (iberdomide) — first CELMoD protein degrader wins FDA approval in myeloma, on a minimal-residual-disease endpoint

## Script

Today's pick is a genuine class-first. The FDA has granted accelerated approval to Zenbexus — that's iberdomide — from Bristol Myers Squibb, combined with daratumumab and dexamethasone, for multiple myeloma patients who've had at least one prior line of therapy. Here's why it leads the brief for a translational immunologist: iberdomide is the first approved CELMoD, a cereblon-modulating protein degrader, and that's not just marketing for a next-generation immunomodulatory drug. It's the successor class to thalidomide, lenalidomide, and pomalidomide, and it's the first thing from Bristol Myers Squibb's targeted protein degradation platform to actually reach patients.

Here's the mechanism that makes it interesting to you. Like the older immunomodulatory drugs, iberdomide binds cereblon and hijacks that E3 ubiquitin ligase to destroy two transcription factors, Ikaros and Aiolos — but it degrades them faster and more completely than lenalidomide does. That buys you two things at once. First, direct myeloma-cell killing, because those transcription factors sit at the top of the myeloma survival program. And second, a stronger immune kick — more T-cell and natural-killer-cell activation. That second arm is the reason CELMoDs matter beyond myeloma monotherapy. They're becoming the immune-conditioning backbone you layer underneath antibodies — here it's daratumumab, an anti-CD38 antibody — and, increasingly, underneath T-cell engagers and CAR-T. If you're building combination immunotherapy, this is the potentiator, not the passenger.

Now the data, and the part I'd actually circle. Approval came off the Phase 3 EXCALIBER trial: the iberdomide triplet versus the standard daratumumab-bortezomib-dexamethasone triplet, about four hundred twenty patients randomized. And the primary endpoint wasn't progression-free survival, and it wasn't response rate — it was minimal residual disease-negative complete response. The iberdomide arm doubled it: forty-one percent versus twenty-one percent, at sixteen months' median follow-up. And this is the first approval ever, in relapsed or refractory myeloma, based on that minimal-residual-disease endpoint. That regulatory precedent is arguably worth more than the drug itself. Deep minimal-residual-disease negativity is a surrogate regulators have been circling for years, and if it's now a registrational endpoint, the next wave of myeloma combinations — including the T-cell-engager and antibody regimens you care about — can register on a biomarker readout instead of waiting years for the survival curves to separate. That moves the whole field's clock forward.

The caveats are real, and worth saying out loud. This is accelerated approval on a surrogate, so the confirmatory progression-free and overall survival data are still owed — minimal-residual-disease negativity predicts survival, it doesn't guarantee it. And it's not a clean drug. There are boxed warnings for embryo-fetal toxicity and for blood clots, neutropenia in ninety percent and infections in nearly eighty percent of treated patients, second primary malignancies, and about a five percent fatal-adverse-event rate. So it's potent immunomodulation, with the toxicity bill that comes with potent immunomodulation.

The bottom line for your beat: don't read this as one more myeloma triplet. Read it as the first CELMoD across the line — validating a degrader platform that generates immune-potentiating backbones for exactly the antibody and T-cell-engager combinations at the center of your work — and as the approval that put minimal-residual-disease-negative complete response on the map as a registrational endpoint. Both of those outlive this one label.
