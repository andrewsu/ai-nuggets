# KRGR-zeta-1-40 — a 45-amino-acid CAR intracellular domain that beats 4-1BB-CD3-zeta on expression and durability

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.10.28.762481v1

## Script

Here's a question nobody in the CAR signaling field has really asked. Twenty years of work has gone into what else you can put into a chimeric antigen receptor's intracellular domain — pooled screens of dozens of signaling modules, shuffled costimulatory domains, receptors built from C-D-three subunits other than zeta, from DAP-twelve, from proximal kinases. All of that asks which domains to combine, exchange or reorder. A preprint posted yesterday from Generate Biomedicines asks the opposite question: how much of it can you throw away?

The answer is most of it. The clinical second-generation domain, four-one-B-B fused to C-D-three zeta, is a hundred and fifty-four amino acids. They got down to forty-five, and the short version is better on nearly every axis they measured.

The winning design holds three things. One C-D-three zeta I-TAM, not three. A segment lifted from C-D-forty that contains the TRAF-six binding site, serving as the costimulatory half. And a four-residue membrane anchor — lysine, arginine, glycine, arginine — which happens to be the first four residues of four-one-B-B, now used as a standalone anchor rather than as part of a domain. That's the whole thing. In Jurkat reporter cells it signals through the I-TAM for N-FAT and through the C-D-forty motif for N-F-kappa-B, confirmed by alanine and tyrosine-to-phenylalanine mutants, so the architecture does what it says on the tin.

Now the finding that actually matters, and it was not what they were looking for. Compact domains put dramatically more receptor on the cell surface. In a primary T cell screen of seventy-eight designs, seventy-three exceeded the clinical reference, median two-point-seven-fold. The lead design runs six-point-two-fold higher. Because the effect is shared across designs rather than attached to one sequence, it looks like a property of shortness itself — maybe shorter receptors traffic to the surface more efficiently, maybe they're pulled off it more slowly, since a shorter tail carries fewer lysines and lysine ubiquitination is what drives C-A-R downmodulation after antigen engagement. They don't distinguish those, and they say so.

The thing that should have broken is tonic signaling. Pile six times more receptor onto a T cell and you expect antigen-independent signaling to rise, which is the main route to exhaustion. It didn't. Interferon gamma without target cells stayed below the detection limit for the lead design and for every round-two variant. More receptor, no measurable leak.

There's a real mechanistic payoff buried in that, and it's the part I'd want a signaling person to argue with. To get to forty-five residues they deleted most of the basic-rich stretch of the C-D-three zeta tail. Two competing models of how that tail works both predict this should fail, in opposite directions. If the disordered tail drives condensate formation and condensates drive activation, stripping out charged residues should cost you activation. If instead the basic stretch pins I-TAM tyrosines against the inner leaflet of the membrane until antigen engagement releases them, stripping it out should unleash tonic signaling. Neither happened. What the screens did show is that a short basic segment near the membrane is genuinely required — juxtamembrane segments with no lysine or arginine lowered surface expression — and the four-residue anchor supplies it. The authors are careful here: they measured neither condensates nor basal I-TAM phosphorylation, so this argues against the basic-rich stretch being required, not against either model as such. That's the right level of claim.

Where this becomes practically interesting is messenger R-N-A delivery. At matched conditions, the compact design reaches the receptor density that the clinical domain needs its highest dose to hit, using one-eighth the R-N-A. Over a seventeen-day time course it starts twelve-fold higher on day one, and here's the honest version of the persistence story: it does not decay more slowly. Both constructs decline at the same rate. It simply starts so far above the floor that it's still functional long after the other one has fallen through it — killing more than ninety percent of myeloma targets through day thirteen, where the reference had lost most of its killing by day nine, and still releasing interferon gamma on day thirteen, where the reference went undetectable from day seven. That is a dose effect dressed as a durability effect, and it's worth being precise about which one you're buying.

Two applications follow directly. They electroporated a cocktail of four C-A-R messenger R-N-As — B-C-M-A, C-D-nineteen, HER-two and MUC-sixteen — each at half the single-C-A-R dose. With the compact domain, over eighty percent of cells were positive for each of the four. With the clinical domain, at most a third. And with C-D-three-targeted lipid nanoparticles added to whole P-B-M-C cultures from five donors, generating C-A-R T cells in situ, the compact design hit fifty percent B cell depletion at a four-point-six-fold lower messenger R-N-A dose. If you believe in vivo C-A-R generation, payload size is a first-order constraint, and this is a direct attack on it.

The caveats are real and mostly stated. Per receptor, the single-I-TAM design is slightly less antigen-sensitive — it needed about one-point-seven-fold more B-C-M-A per target cell for half-maximal response — which the eight-fold higher receptor density more than covers, but that trade is a thing to watch at low antigen density. The benefit is partly binder-contingent: an earlier fifty-four-residue version lost its expression and cytokine advantage entirely when paired with the C-D-nineteen F-M-C-sixty-three binder. There is no in vivo work at all. Few donors. And critically, nobody measured exhaustion — which is precisely the readout that tonic signaling is supposed to damage, and the repeated-challenge assays measured sustained function across a population rather than serial killing by the same cells.

One more detail worth filing away: among twenty-one synonymous codon recodes, encoding the identical protein, surface expression varied widely. So some unknown fraction of the expression story lives in the messenger R-N-A sequence rather than the peptide.

Last, the A-I framing, because the title leans on it and the data undercut it in a useful way. Current models could not predict which designs would work. Co-folding confidence and protein language model likelihoods did not separate active from inactive. Five general-purpose language models called almost everything active — specificity of fifteen to thirty-four percent. The agents were useful as literature synthesizers and proposal generators, with humans steering and wet-lab screens deciding, which is a much more modest and much more believable claim. The one genuinely interesting measurement is that feeding earlier rounds' results back into the prompt improved predictions at the level of *design ideas* more than individual sequences, and only when the models were asked which design would beat the comparator, not whether a design was active in absolute terms. Models rank better than they score. And the authors note that nobody ran the control that would settle whether agent-guided design beats expert design at matched experimental budget. Take the receptor; hold the methodology claim loosely.
