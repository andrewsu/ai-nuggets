# BATF2 — a bZIP screen builds a CAR T cell that takes over the tumor and kills no better

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.10.28.762687v1

## Script

Transcription factor engineering is the most fashionable thing you can do to a CAR T cell right now. Knock out the bad ones, overexpress the good ones, rewire the exhaustion program from the inside. The trouble is that the family doing most of the work in that program — the b-ZIP family, fifty-three transcription factors in humans — does not behave like a list of good guys and bad guys. B-ZIPs only bind DNA as dimers, mostly heterodimers, and which partner a given factor grabs depends on how much of everything else is around. So when you overexpress one and the cells get better, you have not learned that the factor is good. You have learned that shifting the stoichiometry of the whole network in that particular direction was good. Knockout has the mirror-image problem. It is why the literature has flatly contradictory results for BATF and for ATF-four depending on whose system you read.

A preprint posted this week from Glenna Foight's group at Baylor, with Will Hudson and Leonid Metelitsa, does something about that, and the methodological move is the part I'd steal.

Instead of screening overexpression constructs, or screening knockouts, they screened both in the same library, against the same cells, in the same experiment. The knockdown side is the clever bit, because it isn't a knockdown at all — it's protein. They took prior work from the b-ZIP protein engineering field and built genetically encoded dominant-negative inhibitors: designed leucine zippers with specificity for a given b-ZIP subfamily, fused to an acidic helical extension. The zipper grabs the target factor the way its normal partner would; the acidic extension pairs with the target's basic DNA-binding region and holds it off DNA. You get a factor that is still expressed, still in the room, and electrically unable to do its job. The library also contained bare leucine zippers and bare b-ZIP domains without the acidic extension, which turns out to matter — only the versions with the acidic extension moved the phenotype much. Blocking the protein-protein interaction isn't enough. You have to block the DNA.

The screen ran in G-D-two CAR T cells against a neuroblastoma line, restimulated over and over for fifteen days until the cells stopped proliferating and stopped killing and looked transcriptionally exhausted, with barcode sequencing at four time points to ask which constructs let cells persist.

Now, the result you get from putting overexpression and inhibition in the same pool is a kind of readout you cannot get any other way, and here is the clean example. Inhibitors of JUN — several different ones, built off BATF, off FOS, off BATF-three leucine zippers — all crashed out of the library. The cells carrying them disappeared. But inhibitors of BATF and of FOS themselves did almost nothing. Same cells, same assay, same chemistry of inhibitor. The asymmetry tells you something specific: JUN is limiting in these cells, and BATF and FOS are in excess. That's a statement about operating point, not about function, and overexpression alone will never give it to you.

On the enrichment side, the screen pulled up the ones you'd want as positive controls — BACH2, BATF, BATF-three — and pushed out CREB-one, NFIL-three, STAT-four, all with known pro-dysfunction roles. But the top full-length hit was BATF-two, which outperformed both of its much better-studied paralogs, and which barely appears in this literature because T cells express almost none of it at baseline. The other standout was an inhibitor they call A-E-L-CREM — the acidic-extension construct against the CREB-one family, which is CREB-one, CREM, and ATF-one together.

So far this is a nice screen with two new hits. Here is where it gets interesting, and where I'd argue the paper is more valuable for what it refuses to oversell than for what it found.

BATF-two overexpression makes CAR T cells proliferate. Substantially, reproducibly, and in every system they tried. In vitro it's the best expansion hit. In a mouse melanoma engineered to carry G-D-two, BATF-two CAR T cells reach nearly ten times the number of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes. And in the experiment that really nails it — a co-transfer where congenically marked ordinary CAR T cells and BATF-two CAR T cells go into the same mouse, same tumor, same antigen exposure — by day fifteen the BATF-two cells are up to eighty percent of the C-D-eight T cells in that tumor and the ordinary CAR T cells are barely detectable. That is about as dominant as a single-gene modification gets.

Tumor control: no better than CAR alone.

Both arms beat untreated. Neither beats the other. Eighty percent of the infiltrate, ten-fold more cells, and the tumor curves lie on top of each other. In vitro it's the same story and arguably worse — BATF-two expands the cells and mildly *reduces* killing.

I want to sit on that for a second, because expansion and persistence are the endpoints that essentially every CAR T engineering paper reports, and this is a clean demonstration that they are not a proxy for the thing you actually want. The mechanism the paper lands on is consistent: BATF-two is restraining terminal differentiation. In the mouse tumor, BATF-two cells are enriched for the progenitor-exhausted, SLAMF-six-positive state and depleted of TIM-three-positive terminal cells. In a chronic L-C-M-V infection, where they moved it into T-C-R transgenic cells to check that it isn't a CAR artifact, BATF-two drives an unusual intermediate state with low TIM-three, low TOX, and very low granzyme B. Low granzyme B is the tell. The cells are being held back from the differentiation step that makes them killers. You get more of them, and each one does less. The paper's own phrase is an uncoupling of effector functions, and they are right to call it that rather than dress it up.

The combination experiments make the same point from the other side and are the most practically useful section. Pairing BATF-two with JUN, or with A-E-L-CREM, gave no additive benefit — and on immunophenotyping, the BATF-two phenotype simply dominated whatever it was paired with. But JUN plus A-E-L-CREM, the two constructs that individually did little for expansion, together beat either alone for tumor clearance. More granzyme B, more perforin, more cells in an N-K-receptor-positive C-D-eight state, and the same result reproduced in a different CAR against a different tumor, G-P-C-three against hepatocellular carcinoma. So the axes really are separable: one pair buys you proliferation at the cost of differentiation, the other buys you cytotoxicity without the expansion. That is a design choice a person building a construct can actually make.

The mechanistic section explains why BATF-two and A-E-L-CREM look so similar despite being an overexpressed factor and an inhibitor of a different family, and it's a genuine surprise. Affinity-purification mass spec on overexpressed BATF-two pulls down CREB-one family members — CREB-one, CREM, ATF-one — which BATF-family and CREB-family proteins are not reported to do in solution. So overexpressed BATF-two is partly acting as a sponge for the same family that A-E-L-CREM blocks by design. Two routes to the same endpoint. Under chronic stimulation the BATF-two interactome also picks up BACH2, which fits the restraint-of-differentiation phenotype. And that is a caution worth generalizing past this paper: when you overexpress a dimerizing factor well above its physiological level, some of what you measure is not that factor doing its job. It is that factor titrating other people's partners away. The acidic-extension inhibitors are, in a sense, the control for exactly that artifact — which is the argument for building them.

What I'd want before believing the translational framing. The in vivo tumor work is B-sixteen-F-ten melanoma transduced to express G-D-two, which is an aggressive, poorly immunogenic model where almost nothing separates arms, so "no additional advantage" is a weaker negative than it sounds — a model with more dynamic range might have shown something. The reverse caveat matters more though: the best-case reading of BATF-two here is that it helps in a setting this model can't detect, and the honest reading is that an eighty-percent takeover of the infiltrate that moves no tumor curve is a real result and a sobering one. The human work is one CAR in one restimulation assay with a second CAR used only as a cytotoxicity check. And the pairing of JUN with A-E-L-CREM, which is the combination I'd actually be most interested in, has no in vivo data at all — it's in vitro in two models, and the in vivo program went to BATF-two, the construct that expands.

The take. The headline most people will take from this is BATF-two, a new anti-exhaustion transcription factor. I think the more durable contribution is two things that aren't the hit. First, designed protein inhibitors as a screening modality for transcription factors — they work, they're genetically encodable so they ride along in the vector you're already using, they can be made subfamily-selective, and screened against full-length overexpression in the same pool they tell you whether a factor is limiting or in surplus, which is the question that actually determines whether perturbing it will do anything. Second, the clean dissociation between proliferation, persistence, and killing. A modification can win the tumor and not treat it. If your screen's endpoint is expansion — and most of them are, because expansion is what barcode counting measures — you will find constructs like BATF-two, and you will need a separate experiment to find out whether you've built a better T cell or just a more numerous one.
