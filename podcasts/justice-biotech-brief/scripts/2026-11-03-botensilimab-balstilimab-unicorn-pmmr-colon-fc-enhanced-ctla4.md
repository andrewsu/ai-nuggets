# Botensilimab plus balstilimab — two weeks of preoperative dosing produces pathologic responses in MMR-proficient colon cancer

Paper link: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-026-04710-1

## Script

Mismatch-repair-proficient colon cancer is the control arm of immuno-oncology. It is the tumor you point at when someone claims checkpoint blockade works broadly. Low infiltration, low mutational burden, essentially no single-agent activity from first-generation P-D-one or C-T-L-A-four antibodies, and a decade of trials saying so. So when a paper lands reporting that fourteen days of preoperative antibody produces complete pathologic responses in that disease, the right reflex is to ask what exactly is doing the work.

The paper is the UNICORN trial, published yesterday in Nature Medicine by the Italian GONO group out of the National Cancer Institute in Milan, with Filippo Pietrantonio as senior author. It is a window-of-opportunity platform trial — patients with radiologically staged locally advanced colon cancer who are going to surgery anyway get a short course of something first, and you read out the pathology of the resected specimen. Fifty-six patients, four cohorts of fourteen. The agents are botensilimab, an Fc-enhanced anti-C-T-L-A-four antibody from Agenus, and balstilimab, a fairly ordinary anti-P-D-one antibody from the same company. The dosing is almost absurdly light: one milligram per kilogram of botensilimab, once, on day one. Where the combination was used, balstilimab three milligrams per kilogram on day one and day fifteen. Surgery between day thirty and day forty.

Here is the design feature that makes this worth your time. They did not run one combination cohort and report a number. They ran botensilimab alone and botensilimab plus balstilimab, separately, in both the M-M-R-proficient and the M-M-R-deficient populations. Four cells of a two-by-two.

The primary endpoint was pathologic major response, meaning ten percent or less residual viable tumor. In M-M-R-proficient disease, botensilimab alone gave a major response rate of zero. Add balstilimab and it goes to thirty-six percent, with four of fourteen patients at a complete pathologic response. In M-M-R-deficient disease, botensilimab alone gave thirty-six percent, and the combination gave one hundred percent — fourteen of fourteen, thirteen of them complete responses.

Let me handle the M-M-R-deficient result first, because it will get the headlines and it is the least interesting part. A hundred percent pathologic major response is a beautiful number, but we already knew mismatch-repair-deficient colon cancer melts under preoperative checkpoint blockade. NICHE-two established it with nivolumab and ipilimumab. Dostarlimab is already standard non-operative management in deficient rectal cancer. This show covered the dostarlimab rectal approval two weeks ago and the perioperative deficient-colon phase three programs the week before that. The one genuinely useful detail in that cohort is what happened after surgery: not a single patient in the deficient combination arm received adjuvant chemotherapy. Compare that to the proficient monotherapy cohort, where seventy-one percent went on to adjuvant chemo and the median duration was a hundred and fifty-four days. If you are building the economic and toxicity case for moving immunotherapy before surgery, that is the number, not the pathology.

The proficient result is the one that matters, and it is also where the design starts to bite back.

The clean internal reading is that botensilimab initiates and balstilimab consolidates. Botensilimab alone did not produce a single major response — but it was not inert. Forty-three percent of those patients had a pathologic partial response, meaning somewhere between ten and fifty percent residual tumor. So a single one-milligram-per-kilo dose of Fc-enhanced C-T-L-A-four blockade visibly moved tumors in a disease that is supposed to ignore immunotherapy entirely; it just did not move them past the ninety-percent bar. Adding two doses of P-D-one blockade pushed a third of patients over it. That maps onto the metastatic data, where the ACTIVATE trial randomized refractory proficient colorectal cancer to standard of care, botensilimab, or the combination, and got response rates of zero, nine, and nineteen percent. Same shape. The C-T-L-A-four arm does something on its own, and the P-D-one arm roughly doubles it.

Now the caveats, and they are not small.

These cohorts were sequential, not randomized. The proficient monotherapy cohort enrolled from June 2023 to January 2024; the combination cohort enrolled from January to May 2024. Fourteen patients each. The thirty-six percent major response rate carries a ninety-five percent confidence interval running from twelve-point-eight to sixty-four-point-nine percent. The authors are explicit that the trial was not designed to support cross-cohort comparison and that cohorts landing near the decision boundary for the primary endpoint should be read cautiously. Take them at their word on that.

And then the comparison the paper makes and most coverage will skip. In NICHE, thirty-one proficient patients got conventional ipilimumab plus nivolumab and had a twenty percent major response rate with ninety-three percent three-year disease-free survival. Twenty percent versus thirty-six percent, with a confidence interval that wide, across different trials with different staging criteria, is not evidence that Fc enhancement beats conventional C-T-L-A-four blockade. It is consistent with it. Those are different claims. And NEST-two, the American trial of the same two drugs given over eight weeks instead of two, reported forty-seven percent in proficient disease — which suggests duration may matter more here than Fc engineering, since that is the same molecule with more of it.

Where the paper earns its place is the translational work, because they took paired pre-treatment biopsies and post-treatment resections from all fifty-six patients and ran spatial transcriptomics at single-cell resolution on both.

Two findings I would carry forward. First, the P-D-L-one signal. Baseline P-D-L-one expression predicted response in proficient tumors — but only in the stroma, both adjacent and distant, and explicitly not within the tumor compartment. If that holds, it is a biomarker you would miss with a conventional tumor-cell-scored immunohistochemistry assay, which is most of what is used clinically. The odds ratio was nine-point-five with a confidence interval from zero-point-six-nine to a hundred and thirty-two, so treat it as a direction, not a number.

Second, and this is the idea I find most interesting. In proficient responders, the regulatory T cell signature was numerically higher at baseline than in non-responders, and significantly lower at surgery. An inversion. The tumors that responded were not the ones that started out immunologically clean — they were the ones whose suppressive compartment could be stripped out in two weeks. That is a different selection principle than the one the field usually works with, where we hunt for pre-existing inflammation and call everything else cold and hopeless. It also fits botensilimab's design rationale, since Fc-gamma-receptor co-engagement is supposed to deplete intratumoral regulatory T cells rather than merely block the receptor. The authors are careful to say a transcriptional signature does not demonstrate depletion, and it could equally reflect a change in regulatory T cell state or location. Fair. But as a hypothesis for why an Fc-enhanced molecule would work where ipilimumab does not, it is the right shape, and it is testable.

The supporting detail on that front: in deficient tumors, responders had higher baseline expression of the activating Fc-gamma receptors two-A and three-A specifically within intratumoral macrophages. Prior work linked those genes to botensilimab benefit without cellular resolution; this localizes them. That association did not appear in proficient tumors, which is a little awkward for a unified mechanism.

On circulating tumor DNA, the data are tidy and probably the most immediately actionable thing in the paper. All five proficient responders cleared circulating tumor DNA before surgery, and every one of them was in the combination arm. One additional patient cleared despite having thirty percent residual tumor — a partial responder by pathology who looked like a responder by blood. No patient who cleared has had a disease-free survival event. But only twenty-two percent of proficient patients cleared at all, so as a population-level readout in this disease it is still sparse. And the concordance is imperfect in both directions: one deficient patient with a complete pathologic response was circulating-tumor-DNA positive at every single pre-surgical timepoint, dropping from a hundred and seventy mean tumor molecules per milliliter to one-point-three-seven but never to zero.

The cost side deserves a sentence, because it usually gets skipped in window-of-opportunity trials. Immune-related adverse events occurred in forty-one percent of patients overall and fifty-four percent of those who got the combination. And quality-of-life scores in the proficient combination cohort fell from seventy-eight at baseline to sixty-one at end of treatment, a statistically significant drop, over fourteen days. That is a real price in a population that is curable by surgery alone and is walking into the operating room regardless. Ninety-eight percent still got to surgery on time, which is the thing that would have killed the approach, and it did not.

So where does this leave you. I would not describe this as proficient colon cancer becoming an immunotherapy-sensitive disease. I would describe it as the strongest signal yet that there is a treatable fraction inside it, that the fraction is larger in the preoperative setting than in the metastatic one, and that whatever is driving it needs both arms of the combination. The trial has already been amended to an eight-week proficient cohort of twenty-nine patients, which is the right next experiment and directly tests the duration question that NEST-two raises. Agenus is planning a randomized phase three called ROBBIN against standard of care in high-risk stage two and three proficient colon cancer. That is the trial that will actually answer this, and given that botensilimab has a history of impressive early signals that have not yet converted into a randomized win, I would hold the enthusiasm until it reads out.

One last thought, aimed at anyone building antibodies rather than running trials. The specific claim embedded in this result — that an Fc-engineered C-T-L-A-four antibody can convert a cold tumor by remodeling its suppressive compartment, rather than by finding one that was already inflamed — is a claim about the Fc, not about the target. If it is right, the implication extends well past C-T-L-A-four, and well past this tumor.
