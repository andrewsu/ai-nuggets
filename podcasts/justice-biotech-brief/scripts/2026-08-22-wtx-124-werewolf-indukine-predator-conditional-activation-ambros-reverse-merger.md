## Script

Today's item is not a readout. It's a price, and the price is the whole story.

Yesterday morning Werewolf Therapeutics signed a definitive merger agreement with a private company called Ambros Therapeutics. All stock. The combined company takes the Ambros name, moves to San Diego, and spends the next two years running a Phase 3 of neridronate — a bisphosphonate — in complex regional pain syndrome. There's a hundred and fifty million dollar private placement attached, oversubscribed, co-led by R-A Capital and Janus Henderson. Ambros goes in valued at five hundred million dollars. Werewolf goes in valued at forty-seven and a half million. Pre-merger Werewolf shareholders come out with roughly six-point-eight percent of the combined company and a contingent value right — a promise of some slice of whatever the old pipeline eventually sells for, if it sells. The stock rose a hundred and twenty percent on the announcement, which tells you plainly what the market thought Werewolf was worth the morning before.

I'm putting this in front of you because forty-seven and a half million dollars is the number the market just attached to tumor-conditional activation, and conditional activation is a design principle you work next to every day.

Here's what was actually being priced. Werewolf's platform was called PREDATOR, and the molecules were INDUKINEs. The architecture is clean enough to describe in one breath: take a cytokine you can't dose systemically, fuse it to a domain that blocks its receptor interaction with high affinity, add a half-life extender so the thing accumulates in tumor, and join the pieces with a linker that tumor proteases cut. In circulation it's an inert prodrug. In the tumor microenvironment the linker gets cleaved and you release free cytokine locally. The two clinical assets were W-T-X one-twenty-four, an interleukin-two INDUKINE, and W-T-X three-thirty, an interleukin-twelve INDUKINE. Those are not arbitrary choices. High-dose I-L-two and recombinant I-L-twelve are the two most notorious cases in immuno-oncology of a cytokine with real, curative-in-some-patients antitumor activity that the body cannot tolerate delivered systemically. If tumor-restricted activation works anywhere, it should work there. That's the experiment.

And the experiment mostly worked. As of the last data cut Werewolf disclosed, W-T-X one-twenty-four as monotherapy produced a twenty-one percent objective response rate in heavily pretreated advanced or metastatic cutaneous melanoma — which the company itself framed, correctly, as consistent with historical high-dose I-L-two activity. In the subset of those patients who had previously responded to any immunotherapy regimen, meaning they weren't primary-resistant, the response rate was thirty percent. Grade three related adverse events in about a quarter of a hundred-and-six-patient safety population, grade four in two patients, and — the line that matters — no evidence of vascular leak syndrome. Vascular leak is the thing that made high-dose I-L-two an intensive-care drug. It didn't show up. Then, at an end-of-Phase-one meeting, the F-D-A accepted eighteen milligrams as the dose to carry forward and gave initial guidance on a monotherapy registration path in post-checkpoint relapsed or refractory melanoma. That is about as clear a signal as a small company gets: the agency told them what trial to run.

W-T-X three-thirty is the more elegant demonstration, actually. Across dose levels, free I-L-twelve exposure was about one-tenth of one percent of prodrug exposure. That's the masking claim measured directly — the payload is essentially not free in the periphery — alongside a confirmed partial response in metastatic gallbladder cancer, a tumor where you expect nothing.

So this is not a molecule that failed. It's a platform that produced a drug with an agency-endorsed path to registration and then ran out of money before it could take it. Cash was twenty-two million dollars at the end of June, runway into the second quarter of next year, R-and-D already cut roughly in half year over year, a formal strategic-alternatives process running with a banker since at least July. The Phase one-b of one-twenty-four needed additional funding to go anywhere. Nobody wrote the check for the drug. Somebody wrote a hundred and fifty million for the bisphosphonate.

Here's the part I think should actually bother you. Last December, Werewolf announced its twenty-twenty-six priority was not the cytokines at all — it was a new platform called INDUCER, which is a masked T cell engager platform. Same cleavable-linker chemistry, now applied to a C-D-three bispecific, with two named candidates against S-T-E-A-P-one and C-D-H-six. The company's own language was that the preclinical package showed robust silencing, reversible masking, efficient activation in human tumor tissue, and prevention of cytokine release in non-human primates, and that these qualities could make them the leading T cell engager company. Two I-N-Ds planned for mid-twenty-twenty-seven, pending additional funding. Eight months later the company is a listing vehicle and those programs are legally described as legacy assets subject to disposition.

The lesson I'd draw isn't that masking doesn't work. The evidence here is that it does — one-tenth of one percent free I-L-twelve is masking working. The lesson is about what it costs to prove. A masked biologic makes two claims: it's active where you want, and it's inactive where you don't. The first claim is a response rate, and response rates are cheap. The second is a claim about the absence of an event, and absence claims are expensive — you cannot establish "this doesn't uncork in normal tissue" from a hundred and six patients, because the toxicity you're ruling out is exactly the kind that shows up in the tail. So conditional-activation companies get stuck in a specific trap: they generate encouraging efficacy that reads as merely comparable to the unmasked cytokine, because that's what the historical benchmark is, while the differentiating claim — the safety — needs a trial nobody will fund on Phase-one data. Werewolf's twenty-one percent looked like high-dose I-L-two's twenty-one percent. That was the point. It was also, commercially, the problem.

One counterweight worth holding onto. Jazz bought the interleukin-eighteen program outright earlier this year, a twenty-one million dollar payment, so the chemistry does have buyers — just not at a price that sustains a company. If you're designing masked engagers, watch where the INDUCER assets land. The contingent value right guarantees somebody will be shopping them, and a masked S-T-E-A-P-one engager with non-human-primate cytokine-release data is going to be cheap.
