# CR-001 — PD-1×VEGF bispecific opens chemo and ADC combination cohorts before it has reported a single response

Paper link: https://www.biospace.com/press-releases/crescent-biopharma-announces-clinical-pipeline-momentum-including-enrolling-patients-in-standard-of-care-chemotherapy-combination-cohorts-of-global-phase-1-2-ascend-trial-of-cr-001-a-pd-1-x-vegf-bispecific-antibody-and-initiation-of-additional-adc-combination-studies-with-cr-001
Paper link: https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07335497

## Script

Crescent Biopharma put out a pipeline update yesterday on C-R-oh-oh-one, their P-D-1 by V-E-G-F bispecific antibody. There is no efficacy data in it. I want to talk about it anyway, because the shape of what they announced tells you something about how this entire class is now being developed, and I think the shape is more informative than another response rate would have been.

Here is what they said. Dose escalation in the global Phase 1/2 trial, called ASCEND, is complete. More than a hundred and twenty-five patients have been dosed across the United States, Europe and South Korea, in a trial that opened in February. They are now enrolling the expansion cohorts, and those cohorts are C-R-oh-oh-one plus standard-of-care chemotherapy, in seven settings at once: first-line lung, squamous and non-squamous, on the usual platinum doublets; first-line mismatch-repair-proficient colorectal on FOLFOX; third-line colorectal on trifluridine-tipiracil; first-line biliary tract on gem-cis; first-line proficient endometrial on carbo-taxol; and first- and second-line gastric and gastroesophageal junction. Hundreds of patients. On top of that, their Greater China partner Kelun-Biotech is running combination trials of the same molecule with sacituzumab tirumotecan, their TROP2 topoisomerase-one A-D-C — more than seven hundred patients across lung and other solid tumors — with another combination planned against an integrin-beta-six A-D-C in the first half of next year. The company expects more than two thousand patients dosed across the portfolio by the middle of 2027.

And the first efficacy readout from any of this? First quarter of 2027. Overall response rate in up to twelve first-line lung cancer patients.

Sit with that ratio for a second. Twelve patients of monotherapy response data, arriving after more than a thousand patients have already been committed to combination regimens. That is a complete inversion of how a first-in-class asset gets developed. You do not normally enroll hundreds of patients on chemotherapy doublets before you have seen your own drug produce a tumor response.

I don't actually think that's reckless, and the reason it isn't reckless is the thing worth noticing. C-R-oh-oh-one is a tetravalent P-D-1 by V-E-G-F bispecific built on the same architectural logic as ivonescimab. The class question — does co-targeting P-D-1 and V-E-G-F in one molecule beat a P-D-1 antibody alone — has already been asked in thousands of patients by somebody else. Crescent is not running a biology experiment. They are running a positioning experiment. The company is behaving as though the monotherapy signal is a formality and the actual unknown is whether this molecule can serve as a combination backbone, which is where the field has quietly arrived: nobody in oncology development is building P-D-1 by V-E-G-F as a single agent. They're building it as the thing everything else gets added to. If that's the product, then the twelve-patient response rate genuinely isn't the data that matters, and spending a year waiting for it would be a year wasted.

Which means the readout to actually care about is the mid-2027 chemotherapy combination data, and the number to care about inside it is not response rate. It's dose intensity. The whole premise of a backbone is that you can give it with full-dose everything else. V-E-G-F blockade brings hypertension, proteinuria and bleeding risk. The P-D-1 arm brings immune-related toxicity, and immune-related toxicity on top of cytopenias is a miserable attribution problem in the clinic. Then the chemotherapy brings its own. If what comes back in 2027 is a good response rate achieved with forty percent of patients dose-reduced on their platinum, that is a failed backbone with a flattering headline. Watch relative dose intensity and discontinuation, not the waterfall plot.

There's a second combination problem that's more specific, and this is the part I find genuinely interesting. The A-D-C pairings. We talked about this two weeks ago with ivonescimab and Datroway — V-E-G-F blockade does two opposite things to tumor perfusion, normalizing vasculature in some regimes and pruning it in others, and an antibody-drug conjugate is a large molecule whose activity depends on getting into the tumor. Pair a V-E-G-F-blocking bispecific with a TROP2 A-D-C and you have a delivery pharmacology question that no one has answered with data, only with hope. Kelun is now asking that question in more than seven hundred patients.

But look at Crescent's own A-D-C, because it makes the entanglement sharper. C-R-oh-oh-two, which just cleared I-N-D and starts dosing this quarter, is a topoisomerase inhibitor A-D-C targeting P-D-L-1. So the plan, eventually, is a molecule that blocks P-D-1 on the T cell combined with a molecule that uses P-D-L-1 on the tumor as a delivery address. That is a clever idea and it has a real hazard in it. P-D-L-1 is not a tumor-restricted antigen. It is expressed on activated T cells, on myeloid cells, on dendritic cells — expressed, in fact, most highly on exactly the immune compartment that an interferon-rich, inflamed, responding tumor has just recruited. You are proposing to deliver a cytotoxic payload to the cells whose activation is the point of the other drug. And the more the immunotherapy arm works, the more P-D-L-1 goes up on those cells, so the targeting gets worse precisely when the therapy is succeeding. Whether the free payload, the bystander effect and the sensitivity of activated lymphocytes to a topo-one inhibitor add up to self-sabotage is an empirical question — but it's the first question I'd ask in the APOLLO trial design review, and I'd want the on-treatment peripheral T cell counts and the intratumoral C-D-8 density, not just the tolerability table.

The structural read on Crescent is worth a sentence, because it explains the pace. This is another company built on antibodies discovered at Paragon, listed on Nasdaq through a reverse merger with the remains of GlycoMimetics, and the Kelun partnership is the load-bearing element. Kelun got Greater China rights and both parties can independently run combinations with their own A-D-C pipelines. That's how a company this young gets to claim two thousand patients dosed — most of those patients are Kelun's, in China, at a trial cost and enrollment rate a U.S. sponsor can't match. Crescent's plan is to import that data to support global development. It's an efficient structure, and it's also a structure where the most important data about your lead asset is generated by someone else, in a population and a standard-of-care context that regulators here will interrogate.

One last thing to file. At the merger announcement, proof of concept for this program was guided to the second half of 2026. It's now the first quarter of 2027, in twelve patients, and the first Phase 3 is penciled in for early 2028 — a Phase 3 decision that will be made largely on somebody else's combination data. In a class where the lead asset is already in registrational trials, that compressed sequence may be the only rational move available. It's also the kind of plan that has no slack in it anywhere.
