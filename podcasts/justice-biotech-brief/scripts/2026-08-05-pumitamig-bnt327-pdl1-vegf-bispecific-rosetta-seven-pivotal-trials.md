# Pumitamig — BioNTech and Bristol Myers push their PD-L1×VEGF bispecific into a seven-trial pivotal program

Paper link: https://www.biontech.com/int/en/home/mediaroom/news/press-releases/2026/08/BioNTech-Announces-Second-Quarter-2026-Financial-Results-and-Corporate-Update.html

## Script

Today's pick isn't a data readout — it's a corporate update, and normally I'd skip one of those. But buried in BioNTech's second-quarter results, out yesterday, is the clearest picture yet of what has quietly become one of the biggest bets in oncology on a class you track more closely than almost anyone: the P-D-L-one by V-E-G-F bispecific. The asset is pumitamig, and BioNTech just told us it is now running in seven pivotal trials.

Let me set the mechanism, because the intuition is the whole story. Pumitamig is a single antibody that does two things at once: it blocks P-D-L-one, so it releases the checkpoint brake on T cells, and it neutralizes V-E-G-F-A, the growth factor tumors use to build their leaky, immunosuppressive blood supply. The bet behind the whole class is that anti-angiogenic normalization and checkpoint release aren't just additive — they're synergistic, because fixing the vasculature is part of what lets T cells actually get into the tumor. This is the class Akeso and Summit's ivonescimab put on the map when it beat Keytruda on progression-free survival in Chinese lung-cancer trials. Pumitamig is BioNTech's entry, and critically, it's partnered with Bristol Myers — the same Bristol Myers that was in this week's merger chatter. So one of the two companies rumored to be combining is already holding a large piece of this class.

Here's what makes the update worth five minutes. BioNTech didn't dip a toe in. Pumitamig is in seven pivotal trials across a program they call ROSETTA, and five of those trials launched just in the first half of this year — spanning triple-negative breast cancer, colorectal cancer, gastric cancer, and two separate non-small-cell lung cancer settings, one in unresectable stage-three disease and one in high P-D-L-one patients. On top of that there's a global phase-two-three trial in first-line lung cancer running pumitamig-plus-chemo head-to-head against pembrolizumab-plus-chemo — an actual comparison against the standard of care, not a placebo. And then there's a whole second tier of earlier trials in liver cancer, glioblastoma, pancreatic cancer, and kidney cancer.

That breadth is the opinionated part, so let me be opinionated. The V-E-G-F by P-D-L-one class is genuinely contested right now. The Chinese progression-free-survival wins were striking, but the Western overall-survival story has been murkier and slower to mature, and there's a live, serious debate about whether this is a real next-generation checkpoint backbone or a regionally flattering signal that won't fully translate. BioNTech and Bristol Myers are answering that debate not with one careful confirmatory trial but with a seven-trial blitz across five tumor types. That is a statement of conviction — they're betting the mechanism generalizes, that this becomes a platform backbone across solid tumors rather than a one-indication lung drug. If they're right, the class stops being an ivonescimab story and becomes a category. If they're wrong, this is a very expensive way to find out, and it's a lot of pivotal capital riding on a mechanism whose Western survival data is still coming in.

Now here's the piece that lands squarely in your lab's work, because it's not just the bispecific. BioNTech is pairing pumitamig with its own antibody-drug conjugates in what it calls novel-novel combinations — its own new backbone plus its own new payload, rather than layering onto someone else's approved drug. The lead example is elfetabart drozuntecan, a B-seven-H-three-targeting A-D-C. More than a thousand patients have now been dosed with that A-D-C across ten-plus tumor types, and — this is the number that jumped out at me — four hundred of them got it in combination with pumitamig. So they've already run a serious combination program pairing a P-D-L-one-by-V-E-G-F bispecific with a targeted cytotoxic A-D-C. That's exactly the checkpoint-plus-anti-angiogenic-plus-payload logic you'd sketch on a whiteboard, being tested at real scale. The thesis is that you release the brake, you normalize the vasculature, and you deliver a cytotoxic warhead to the tumor cell — three mechanisms, one regimen.

So how should you hold this? The encouraging seed is real — the first-line lung-cancer phase-two data at this year's big American oncology meeting showed high response rates across both squamous and non-squamous disease and across P-D-L-one levels, which is the kind of histology-agnostic, biomarker-agnostic signal you'd want from a true backbone. But everything that matters now is still ahead: these are pivotal phase-three trials that haven't read out, and the Western survival question hangs over the whole class. The two things I'd watch are the head-to-head lung trial against pembrolizumab-plus-chemo, because beating the actual standard of care is the real bar, and the novel-novel combination readouts, which BioNTech says are coming this year. If the bispecific-plus-A-D-C combination shows a clean additive benefit, that's the template a lot of us will be building against — and it's worth knowing now that Bristol Myers, whatever happens with the merger talk, is already deep into it.
