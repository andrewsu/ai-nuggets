# FB102 — anti-CD122 antibody anchors argenx's $2.2 billion Forte buyout in vitiligo and celiac

## Script

Today's pick is a deal, not a data drop, but the interesting part is the mechanism argenx just paid two-point-two billion dollars for. This morning argenx — the Belgian immunology company behind Vyvgart — agreed to buy Forte Biosciences for seventy-seven dollars a share in cash, an all-cash tender offer valuing Forte at roughly two-point-two billion dollars. What they're actually buying is a single early-stage antibody: F-B-one-oh-two, a first-in-class anti-C-D-one-twenty-two monoclonal antibody. If you only remember one thing, remember the target, because that's the whole thesis.

Here's why C-D-one-twenty-two is a smart place to aim. It's the beta chain shared by two receptors — the receptor for I-L-two and the receptor for I-L-fifteen. And I-L-fifteen is the survival cytokine for exactly the cells that drive a certain class of autoimmune disease: tissue-resident memory C-D-eight T cells and natural killer cells. In vitiligo, it's I-L-fifteen-dependent resident-memory T cells sitting in the skin that keep coming back to kill melanocytes — which is why vitiligo relapses right where it cleared. In celiac disease, it's I-L-fifteen driving the intraepithelial lymphocytes that chew up the gut lining. So an antibody that blocks the shared beta chain doesn't tune one cytokine — it starves both the autoreactive T-cell and the N-K-cell arm at the same node, upstream of the tissue damage. That's the "pipeline-in-a-product" pitch: one mechanism, many I-L-fifteen-driven diseases.

Now the angle that should matter most to you: this is a biologic playing in territory that's been defined by jak inhibitors. Vitiligo's marquee approved therapy is topical ruxolitinib, a jak inhibitor — and I-L-fifteen signals through jak-one and jak-three. So F-B-one-oh-two is going after the same pathway, but from the opposite direction: instead of a small molecule blocking the kinase downstream inside every cell, it's a systemic antibody blocking the receptor itself, with the selectivity and the dosing profile of a biologic rather than a chronically-dosed oral jak. That's the same "targeted biologic versus broad jak" contrast we've kept coming back to on this show, and here argenx is betting real money that the antibody route wins in these indications.

Does the clinical signal justify the check? It's early, but it's real. Forte reported positive Phase 1b data in vitiligo just this month — a statistically significant treatment benefit — and that readout is what triggered this deal; the premium is quoted as roughly eighty-six percent over Forte's average share price since that vitiligo data on July ninth. There's also positive Phase 1b data in celiac from last year, with a Phase 2 celiac readout expected in the second half of this year. So argenx is buying on a proof-of-concept in one indication and a wave of catalysts in another, not on a pivotal result.

The strategic read is the part I'd flag. argenx is flush — Vyvgart turned them into one of the genuine immunology success stories of the last few years — and they're deploying that cash the way big pharma does: paying a rich premium, entirely from cash on hand, for an early asset whose value is almost entirely a mechanism and a hypothesis. A forty-one percent premium over Friday's close, eighty-six percent over the pre-data average, for a molecule that's still in Phase 1b. This is a midsize company acting like an acquirer, and it fits the broader pattern this year of autoimmune M&A being driven by mechanistic differentiation — F-c-R-n, complement, cytokine receptors — rather than by late-stage de-risking. It slots F-B-one-oh-two alongside efgartigimod and their other antibody programs as a distinct lever: pathogenic T-cell and N-K-cell activity, a mechanism they didn't previously own.

Here's the skeptical read, because you need it. This is Phase 1b. "Statistically significant" in an early vitiligo study is a green light, not a landing — vitiligo endpoints are slow, repigmentation takes many months, and the durability and the magnitude at scale are still open questions. Blocking C-D-one-twenty-two broadly means blocking I-L-fifteen and I-L-two signaling across the board, and those cytokines aren't only pathogenic — I-L-fifteen and N-K cells do real immune-surveillance work, so the questions to watch are infection risk and whether chronic suppression of that axis is tolerable in otherwise healthy autoimmune patients. And celiac is a notoriously graveyard indication; the Phase 2 readout later this year is the real test of whether the vitiligo signal generalizes or was indication-specific.

The through-line for anyone building in autoimmunity: the money is moving toward mechanisms that sit upstream at a shared signaling node and shut off a whole cell program, rather than antibodies that neutralize one cytokine or one surface marker. C-D-one-twenty-two is attractive precisely because it's a choke point — one receptor beta chain feeding two cytokines that keep autoreactive resident T cells and N-K cells alive. If F-B-one-oh-two holds up, expect more shopping around shared-chain cytokine receptors, and expect the jak-versus-biologic fight to keep migrating into diseases, like vitiligo, that everyone assumed were small-molecule territory. Watch the celiac Phase 2 in the second half of this year — that's the readout that tells you whether argenx bought a platform or a single indication.
