# C1q — a macrophage complement protein shuts off CAR T and T cell engager killing, and dexamethasone helps induce it

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.09.15.751784v1

## Script

Posted to bioRxiv yesterday out of Joan Seoane's group at the Vall d'Hebron Institute of Oncology in Barcelona, with Sonia Guedan's C-A-R T group at Hospital Clinic: a soluble complement protein made by tumor macrophages blunts both T cell engagers and C-A-R T cells in glioblastoma, and the thing that induces it is leukemia inhibitory factor working together with dexamethasone. I want to spend the episode on this one because I think the second half of that sentence is the part that should make anyone running an engager program uncomfortable, and because the target it nominates already has a Phase 3-positive antibody sitting in somebody else's neurology pipeline.

Start with the setup, because the framing matters. When we talk about why T cell engagers and C-A-R T cells have gone nowhere in solid tumors, the usual suspects are all cell-intrinsic or logistical. Antigen density. Target heterogeneity. Trafficking and infiltration. Persistence and exhaustion. On-target off-tumor toxicity forcing you off the dose you need. Those are all real. This paper is arguing for something much more mundane and, if it holds, much more annoying: there is a soluble factor sitting in the tumor at roughly blood concentrations that turns the T cell off upstream of everything you engineered.

The protein is C-one-q. It is the recognition subunit of the classical complement pathway, encoded by three genes, and outside the liver it is made mostly by myeloid cells. C-one-q-positive tumor-associated macrophages have been showing up in single-cell atlases across tumor types for a few years now, always tagged as immunosuppressive, and nobody had worked out what makes them or what they do to cell therapy. This group had already found that cluster in glioblastoma. Here they went after the function.

The gain-of-function experiments are straightforward. Add C-one-q to T cells and they stop working. In co-culture, C-one-q repressed the killing you get from a mouse B-seven-H-three C-A-R T cell and, separately, from a mouse D-L-L-three-directed T cell engager. In orthotopic brain tumors in immunodeficient mice, an E-G-F-R-v-three C-A-R T cell and an E-G-F-R-v-three engager both controlled tumor growth, and adding C-one-q impaired both. And on the T cell side it looks like proximal receptor signaling: less C-D-twenty-five and C-D-sixty-nine, less granzyme B and interferon gamma, less phosphorylated L-C-K and E-R-K, and in Jurkat cells, repressed ZAP-seventy phosphorylation. That is not exhaustion and it is not metabolic starvation. That is a brake on the T cell receptor.

Here is the number that decides whether you should care. They used one hundred micrograms per milliliter, and they picked it because human blood runs fifty to two hundred fifty micrograms per milliliter of C-one-q. So the suppressive dose is not a pharmacological curiosity — it is what is already circulating in every patient. Which raises a genuinely uncomfortable question about potency assays. If you are characterizing an engager in a serum-lean or heat-inactivated medium, you may be reading a number that the patient will never see.

But the experiment that actually carries the paper is the loss-of-function one, and it is the cleanest thing in here. They took C-one-q-A knockout mice, put S-B-twenty-eight glioblastoma into the brain, and treated with either the B-seven-H-three C-A-R T cells or the D-L-L-three engager. In wild type animals, under what they describe as stringent conditions, neither treatment had significant anti-tumor activity. In the knockouts, the same treatments significantly reduced tumor growth. Same construct, same dose, same tumor. The only change is deleting a host complement gene, and that converts a non-responding model into a responding one. If you have ever had a promising engager die quietly in an orthotopic solid tumor model, that result should get your attention, because it says the failure may not have been in your molecule.

They also ran this in a model I think is the most transferable thing in the paper. Tumor organoids have no immune cells. Patient-derived xenografts have no human myeloid compartment. So the human tumor microenvironment has been effectively unmodelable, which is a large part of why immunotherapy preclinical work is so weakly predictive. What they do instead is take fresh surgical glioblastoma, embed it in agarose, cut three hundred fifty micron slices on a vibratome, and keep the intact tissue alive on inserts for two to four days. The myeloid compartment stays. Nineteen patients' tumors went through this. That window is short, but it is long enough to dose an antibody and read out single-cell transcriptomes, and adding C-one-q to those cultures suppressed engager- and C-A-R-driven granzyme B, interferon gamma and C-D-sixty-nine, matching the reductionist systems.

Now the induction mechanism, which is where this turns clinical. Screening against the C-P-T-A-C glioblastoma cohort, leukemia inhibitory factor came out as the cytokine best correlated with the C-one-q macrophage state. Treat bone marrow-derived macrophages with it and all three C-one-q genes go up, and that induction is abolished by a JAK inhibitor — a nice detail if you care about JAK pharmacology, because it means the axis is JAK-STAT-three, not something exotic.

Then they asked what dexamethasone does. Essentially every glioblastoma patient gets dexamethasone around surgery for edema. Dexamethasone alone is not the story — the story is that dexamethasone and leukemia inhibitory factor together induce C-one-q well beyond what the cytokine does on its own, at messenger R-N-A, at percent positive cells, and at protein. And it is genuinely cooperative in the pharmacological sense: block JAK and it collapses, block the glucocorticoid receptor with R-U-four-eighty-six or silence the receptor and it also collapses. You need both arms.

Sit with that for a second in the context of engagers rather than glioblastoma. We give corticosteroids as premedication and as rescue for cytokine release syndrome, essentially universally, across every step-up dosing schedule in the class. The standard justification is that steroids blunt the cytokine storm without much compromising the anti-tumor activity, and there is retrospective clinical data broadly supporting that for hematologic disease. This paper proposes a mechanism by which, specifically in a myeloid-rich solid tumor, steroid exposure would help manufacture the resistance factor that shuts your engager off. I want to be careful here: that cooperation was demonstrated in bone marrow-derived macrophages in a dish, not in patient tissue, and the paper does not show that steroid premedication reduces engager efficacy in anything. But the in vivo experiments were run with dexamethasone on board from day five, which at least means the effect they report survives a steroid-exposed animal. And the hypothesis is testable right now, retrospectively, in trials where steroid exposure varied — that is a dataset several companies already own.

The translational payoff runs through anti-LIF antibodies, and this is where I think the paper is quietly doing patient selection work it does not fully claim credit for. Seoane's lab is upstream of M-S-C-one, the humanized anti-leukemia-inhibitory-factor antibody that became A-Z-D-oh-one-seven-one, now falbikitug. That first-in-human study enrolled forty-one patients against a planned one hundred thirty and was terminated — monotherapy, unselected advanced solid tumors. The program moved into pancreatic cancer with durvalumab and chemotherapy, with C-D-eight infiltration as a selector. Look at what this paper shows about response heterogeneity: of nine patient slice cultures treated with the anti-LIF antibody, four showed a substantial myeloid transcriptional response and five did not. In a separate six-patient validation cohort, two tumors were signature-high, and the antibody dropped the C-one-q macrophage signature in one tumor and not in another. So you are looking at something like a third to a half of patients, enriched for mesenchymal subtype and N-F-one alterations, and the signature-high group has shorter overall survival. An unselected Phase 1 would wash exactly that out. This is a biomarker hypothesis that arrived a few years after the trial that needed it.

Two more things and then the caveats. First, the epistasis result: anti-LIF had no anti-tumor effect at all in C-one-q-A knockout mice, and knockout tumors already had the higher T cell infiltration that anti-LIF produces in wild types. Mechanistically satisfying, because it means the antibody works through C-one-q. Strategically double-edged, because it also caps what anti-LIF can buy you at whatever removing C-one-q buys you. Which invites the obvious question — why not just block C-one-q?

And you can. A-N-X-double-oh-five is a C-one-q-blocking antibody that hit its primary endpoint in a pivotal Phase 3 in Guillain-Barré syndrome with a two-point-four-fold improvement on the disability scale, with a European filing in and F-D-A discussions underway. That is a systemically dosed, clinically de-risked anti-C-one-q antibody that exists, in a neurology company, aimed at a completely different biology. If the mechanism in this paper is right, the combination experiment — engager or C-A-R T plus C-one-q blockade in a myeloid-rich solid tumor — is available immediately and does not require anybody to discover a molecule.

The caveats are real. This is a preprint, not peer reviewed. The model paper describing the slice cultures is co-submitted, meaning you cannot yet read the validation for the platform that carries most of the human data. The in vivo rescue experiments are six to eight animals per group with bioluminescence endpoints rather than survival. Exogenous C-one-q was added at a single concentration as an overnight pre-incubation, which is not how a T cell meets it in a tumor. And they never nail the receptor — the rescue was done with a LAIR-two fragment that sequesters C-one-q, which points at L-A-I-R-one on T cells without proving it, and the receptor identity is exactly what you would need to know to build a selective blocker instead of a pathway-wide one.

What I would take from this if I were building engagers. The interesting claim is not that glioblastoma is immunosuppressive; we knew that. It is that a specific, abundant, already-druggable soluble protein sits between your construct and the T cell receptor, and that two drugs we hand patients routinely — steroids, and in some settings cytokine-directed agents — sit upstream of it. The cheapest version of this for your own program is not a new molecule. It is going back and asking what complement was in the medium when you measured your potency, and what steroid your patients were on when you measured your response rate.
