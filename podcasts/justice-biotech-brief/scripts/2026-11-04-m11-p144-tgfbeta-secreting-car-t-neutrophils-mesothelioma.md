# M11-P144 — a TGF-beta-peptide-secreting CAR T cell whose benefit runs through neutrophils

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.10.28.762702v1

## Script

Armoring a CAR T cell against T-G-F-beta is one of the oldest ideas in solid tumor cell therapy, and the field has mostly converged on one way of doing it: make the T cell deaf. Dominant-negative T-G-F-beta receptor two, base-edited knockouts of the endogenous receptor, switch receptors that convert the suppressive signal into costimulation. All of those are cell-intrinsic. They protect the cells you infused and they do nothing for anybody else in the tumor.

A preprint out this week from Astero Klampatsa's group at the Institute of Cancer Research in London takes the other option — have the CAR T cell secrete a T-G-F-beta antagonist into the tissue around it — and the interesting part is not that it works. It's who does the work when it does.

The payload is a peptide called P-one-forty-four, derived from the ligand-binding domain of the type three T-G-F-beta receptor, betaglycan. It has a history as an antifibrotic. They compared it head to head against a phage-display-derived peptide, P-seventeen, and the comparison is worth noting because the two look identical on the assays most people would run. Both partly restore E-cadherin and EpCAM in T-G-F-beta-treated lung adenocarcinoma and mesothelioma lines. Both knock about a third off a reporter readout — thirty-nine percent for P-seventeen, thirty-six percent for P-one-forty-four. They only separate on phosphorylated Smad-three, the actual canonical node: P-seventeen takes it down about twenty percent, P-one-forty-four about fifty-one percent, essentially back to baseline. If you had picked on the reporter you'd have picked the weaker molecule.

The model is mesothelioma, which is a reasonable place to test this. Dense fibrotic stroma, high ambient T-G-F-beta, immune exclusion — it is the tumor where the microenvironmental version of the argument should beat the cell-intrinsic version if it is ever going to.

They ran it two ways. First, mesothelin-targeted CAR T cells plus repeated intratumoral injections of free peptide, in a syngeneic mouse mesothelioma model. CAR alone brought tumors to about ninety-two cubic millimeters by day fifteen against roughly two-sixty-five untreated. Adding the peptide essentially arrested growth, at thirty-one. Peptide alone did almost nothing — about two hundred. And here is the number that got my attention: CAR-positive C-D-eight cells inside the tumor went from zero point nine percent with CAR alone to nine point five percent with the peptide. A ten-fold change in intratumoral CAR T accumulation from blocking T-G-F-beta in the tissue, not in the T cell.

Then they built it into the construct — the peptide cassette downstream of an IRES in the mesothelin CAR — and this is where the phenotype gets specific. Transduction, unchanged, about seventy percent either way. Expansion, unchanged. Antigen-specific killing, unchanged: eighty-one percent specific lysis versus eighty at ten to one, and comparable all the way down. The only in vitro thing that moved was cytokine. Interferon gamma rose modestly at high effector-to-target ratios and then diverged sharply as targets became scarce — at one to one it went from about thirteen hundred picograms per milliliter to six thousand, roughly four and a half fold. That dissociation, more cytokine with identical cytolysis, is the tell that T-G-F-beta was gating the secretory program and not the killing machinery. In vivo the armored construct halved tumor volume relative to the parental CAR.

So far this is a competent armored-CAR paper. The part that makes it worth your time is the immunophenotyping, because the adaptive compartment barely moves. Endogenous C-D-eight frequency: a non-significant bump. Ki-sixty-seven and interferon gamma in those cells: numerically higher, that's all. What moves is myeloid. Neutrophils — C-D-forty-five positive, C-D-eleven-b positive, Ly-six-G positive — sit at two to three and a half percent of live cells in untreated, non-transduced, and parental CAR-treated tumors, and rise to about ten percent with the peptide-secreting construct. Three-fold, and specifically absent from the parental CAR arm, so it is the peptide doing it and not the T cell therapy.

Tumor-associated neutrophils are normally the cells you are complaining about. In most solid tumor immunology they are the suppressive, N2-polarized, arginase-secreting problem. Here they pulled them out of the tumors and ran a killing assay: neutrophils from untreated tumors did basically nothing, one point seven percent specific lysis at twenty to one; from parental CAR-treated tumors, about eleven percent; from peptide-secreting CAR-treated tumors, twenty-four percent, with the advantage holding down to low ratios. I'll flag the caveat the authors flag themselves, in the figure legend, which I appreciate: that is three replicate wells from a single experiment. One n.

The experiment that actually carries the claim is the depletion. Antibody-deplete neutrophils, then treat. Parental CAR T cells are completely unaffected — about two-seventy cubic millimeters with or without neutrophils. The armored construct loses most of its advantage, going from roughly a hundred twenty to two-twenty. So neutrophils are dispensable for baseline CAR T function and become a load-bearing effector arm precisely when you block T-G-F-beta locally. The benefit is attenuated, not abolished, which the authors say plainly — some of the effect is still the T cells.

And then they generalized it in the way you'd want: same cassette, different CAR, this time targeting fibroblast activation protein — the stroma rather than the tumor cell. Same result. Better tumor control than the parental F-A-P CAR, one-sixty-eight versus four-forty-four cubic millimeters at day twenty, and the same significant neutrophil enrichment. Two different antigens, one on the malignant cell and one on the fibroblast, both recruit the same innate arm. That's what makes it a modular claim rather than a single-construct curiosity.

What I'd push on. This is all murine CAR T cells in syngeneic mice — necessary, since you cannot see an endogenous innate response in an immunodeficient xenograft, but it means no human construct has been tested. Nothing is cured; these are growth-delay curves at fixed endpoints, not survival. There is no direct evidence for the mechanism the discussion proposes, which is that the extra interferon gamma recruits and repolarizes the neutrophils — the authors say outright that this needs an interferon gamma neutralization or knockout experiment, and it hasn't been done. And mouse neutrophils are not human neutrophils, a gap that has embarrassed this particular cell type before.

The reason I picked it anyway. The whole clinical history of T-G-F-beta targeting is a history of trying to get the spatial restriction right — bintrafusp alfa, a P-D-L-one antibody fused to a T-G-F-beta trap, failed to beat pembrolizumab in a randomized phase three, and the live approaches now are about restricting where and which form of the ligand you hit. A CAR T cell is already the most spatially selective delivery device anybody has; it goes where the antigen is and makes protein there. This paper is an argument that if you use it that way instead of just hardening the T cell, you recruit a second effector population you weren't paying for, and that population is one the field usually writes off.

There's a broader design point underneath it. A cell-intrinsic armor caps your benefit at the number of cells you infused. A secreted armor doesn't. If you are choosing between dominant-negative receptor two and a secreted antagonist, this says those are not two routes to the same place — they recruit different biology, and they're probably complementary rather than redundant. That is a hypothesis somebody should test directly, in the same construct, which nobody here did.
