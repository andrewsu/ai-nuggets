# TIGIT-CD28 switch receptor — one amino acid in the costimulatory tail separates one-of-five from five-of-five tumor-free

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.10.02.755760v1

## Script

A preprint went up on bioRxiv yesterday from Hakim Echchannaoui's group at University Medical Center Mainz, with the German Cancer Consortium, and it is the cleanest demonstration I have seen lately of a problem we all know about and mostly hand-wave past: tonic costimulation inside an engineered receptor can quietly destroy the cell you built.

The construct is a chimeric switch receptor. You take the ectodomain of an inhibitory checkpoint, bolt it onto an activating signaling tail, and the tumor's suppressive ligand now pushes the T cell forward instead of holding it back. The version here uses TIGIT on the outside and CD28 on the inside — TIGIT's signal peptide and ectodomain fused to CD28's transmembrane and cytoplasmic domains. The logic is appealing because of who the ligand is. CD155 is broadly expressed across human tumors, and TIGIT climbs on T cells exactly as they differentiate and exhaust, so the suppressive signal is loudest precisely when you want a rescue. They put this into primary human T cells alongside a p53-specific single-chain T cell receptor and tested it against a CD155-high osteosarcoma line.

In vitro it looked like a win. Over three consecutive rounds of twenty-four-hour co-culture — the serial-killing format, not a five-hour chromium release — control T cells left about fifty-nine percent of the tumor alive. The switch-receptor cells left about twenty-two percent. Degranulation was up, interferon gamma was up. If you stopped there you would write a nice paper.

They went into mice and the whole thing nearly collapsed. One of five animals cleared its tumor. Four grew out. T cells were detectable at two weeks and essentially gone by day twenty-seven. And then they did the thing that makes this paper worth your time: they went looking for why, and the answer was the receptor itself. Switch-receptor T cells were doubling fewer times in culture than controls, and their differentiation markers had shifted — fewer naive-phenotype cells, more differentiated. Apoptosis was unchanged. This was not killing; it was burning down. The CD28 tail was signaling without waiting to be told to, and the cells were spending themselves before they reached the tumor.

The fix is a single amino acid, and it is borrowed rather than invented. CD28's cytoplasmic domain carries a four-residue motif — tyrosine, methionine, asparagine, methionine — that drives the NFAT output linked to exhaustion in CD28-based CARs. ICOS has the same motif with phenylalanine in place of that asparagine. Earlier CAR work from Guedan and colleagues showed that swapping that one residue preserves CD28's cytolytic punch while removing much of the exhaustion liability. Mainz ran site-directed mutagenesis at position one eighty-two in their switch receptor and made the same swap.

The in vivo result is not subtle. Five of five mice tumor-free, durable through seventy days. Median survival sixty-four days, against twenty-nine for the control receptor-less cells, thirty-two for the original switch receptor, and thirty-four for untargeted cells. Four of five animals showed real CD8 expansion between days twenty-two and forty-four, up into the hundreds of cells per microliter of blood, and the animals whose T cells expanded were the animals whose tumors disappeared. Meanwhile in vitro killing was unchanged from the original design. They did not buy potency with the mutation. They bought persistence, and persistence was the whole deficit.

Two mechanistic pieces are worth carrying. First, the blocking experiment. Add an anti-TIGIT antibody and the switch-receptor cells lose their advantage, and they lose it in oxygen consumption as well as in killing — but control cells, which only carry endogenous inhibitory TIGIT, get better with blockade. That dissociates the two hypotheses properly. The benefit is not relief from inhibition. It is costimulation delivered through the chimeric receptor. Second, the metabolic readout. On Seahorse, the tuned cells roughly double basal oxygen consumption on antigen-specific stimulation, with no change in glycolysis, and the mitochondrial-dysfunction population — low membrane potential per unit mass, the phenotype that tracks terminal exhaustion in tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes — sits at well under one percent versus four and a half percent in controls. Mitochondrial respiration, less-differentiated phenotype, longer persistence: the same axis the CAR costimulation field has been circling for a decade.

Now the caveats, because this is a mouse paper with five animals a group. The entire in vivo story is one xenograft model in NSG mice with exogenous interleukin-2, so there is no host immune system and no normal-tissue compartment to speak of. That matters more than usual here, because CD155 is not tumor-restricted — it is on endothelium, on myeloid cells, in plenty of healthy tissue. Their safety argument is that the switch receptor alone, without the antigen receptor, does not lyse targets, which is necessary but nowhere near sufficient. I would also flag a detail they report honestly and then set aside: several cured animals became sick after clearance, with weight loss, hair loss and apathy, which they attribute to T cell persistence. In an NSG mouse carrying human T cells that is a reasonable description of xenogeneic graft-versus-host disease, and it sits on the same survival curve as the efficacy claim. Also, the tuned cells upregulate LAG-3 and TIM-3 more than controls — more costimulation, more brakes engaged — and the CAR data, using an NKp30 CAR against B7-H6 in a leukemia line, are in vitro only, with a 1.6-fold interferon gamma bump.

Two things to take away. The narrow one: if you are building any synthetic receptor with a CD28 tail — switch receptor, second-generation CAR, a costimulatory module on a T cell engager platform — the asparagine-to-phenylalanine swap is a nearly free experiment, and in this system it was the difference between a failed construct and complete eradication. The broader one is about TIGIT. The antibody blockade programs have had a brutal few years. This work points at the other use of the same biology: not blocking the checkpoint, but using it as a docking domain, where the tumor's own suppressive ligand becomes the costimulatory input. The ectodomain was never the hard part. The tail was.
