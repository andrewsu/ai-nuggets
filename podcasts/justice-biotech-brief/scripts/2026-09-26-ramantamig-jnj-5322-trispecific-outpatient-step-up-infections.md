## Script

Eleven days ago, when GSK bought a preclinical myeloma trispecific from Chimagen, I argued that the tolerability question everybody leads with had already been answered by somebody else, and that the genuinely open question was different — what a two-antigen avidity gate structurally cannot fix. On Wednesday in Glasgow, at the twenty-third International Myeloma Society meeting, the molecule I used as that comparator came back with more patients and longer follow-up. Both halves of the argument now have numbers, and the interesting one is not the response rate.

The drug is ramantamig, which you may still think of as J-N-J-five-three-two-two. Jeffrey Matous from the Colorado Blood Cancer Institute presented the expanded recommended-dose cohort out of the first-in-human Phase one.

Hold the architecture in your head for a second, because the design logic is unusually clean. It is one molecule carrying an anti-B-C-M-A binder, an anti-C-D-three binder, and an anti-G-P-R-C-five-D binder — the first two grafted onto the front of a silenced antibody scaffold, the third hung off the back. Two tumor antigens, one T cell. The preclinical paper that came out in Blood in February quantifies exactly what the second tumor antigen buys, and it is the number I would want you to take away. They CRISPR'd B-C-M-A out of a myeloma line, then G-P-R-C-five-D out of the same line, and compared killing against the double-positive parent. Ramantamig was more than fifty-fold more potent against cells carrying both antigens than against cells carrying either one alone. That is avidity doing its job — two weak grips beat one. But then in mice, single-antigen tumors were cleared at essentially the same dose as double-antigen tumors. So the design is doing two different things at once: the avidity gives you potency and a therapeutic window where both antigens are present, and the redundancy still covers you when a clone drops one. Given that biallelic B-C-M-A deletion after teclistamab and G-P-R-C-five-D loss after talquetamab are both documented escape routes, that redundancy is the whole thesis.

Now the clinic. As of an April cutoff, fifty-six patients had received the recommended dose — median age sixty-eight, a median of three prior lines running as high as eleven, and forty-two percent with high-risk cytogenetics. Forty-seven of them had never seen a T-cell redirector. In that group the response rate was ninety-three point six percent, with complete response or better in seventy-seven percent, and every patient who was evaluable for minimal residual disease was negative at one in a hundred thousand by sequencing. At eighteen months, ninety-one percent of responses were holding and eighty-five percent of patients were progression-free. The recommended-dose cohort was thirty-six patients with an eighty-six percent response rate at the last public update. It is bigger now, and it got better, not worse.

But that is not the news, and I want to be blunt about why. Ninety-three percent in T-cell-redirector-naive myeloma is a ceiling number in a single-arm Phase one, and the field is stacked with ceiling numbers right now — the mezigdomide plus elranatamab cohort presented on day one of this same meeting hit a hundred percent in thirteen patients. Efficacy has stopped discriminating between these molecules.

What discriminates is the schedule, and that is the actual headline. The recommended dose is one five-milligram step-up, and then a hundred milligrams under the skin once a month. One step-up. Monthly maintenance. Compare that to teclistamab, which needs two step-ups and then weekly dosing, or talquetamab, which is weekly or every other week. And in a thirty-patient cohort run specifically to test this, ninety-three percent of patients got the step-up dose as outpatients and seventy-nine percent got their first full dose as outpatients, with prophylactic tocilizumab in essentially all of them. Cytokine release syndrome in that setting was ten percent at the step-up and under seven percent at the first full dose, all grade one. Four of the thirty still ended up in hospital within two days, which is the honest asterisk, but the direction is unambiguous: this is a community-oncology drug in a class that has so far been an academic-center drug.

Here is the tell that I think says more than any of the efficacy numbers. Johnson and Johnson is running a two-hundred-thirty-patient randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled Phase two whose entire purpose is to test whether a single dose of tocilizumab before ramantamig prevents cytokine release syndrome. It started at the end of June. The primary endpoint is the fraction of patients alive and free of grade two or worse C-R-S by day twenty-eight without needing rescue. Companies do not usually randomize their premedication against placebo. Doing it means the sponsor has concluded that the commercial question for this molecule is not whether it works — it is whether somebody outside a transplant center can give it safely. They are generating regulatory-grade evidence for the supportive care rather than leaving it to institutional habit, and I think that is the right read of where the class is.

Which brings me back to what I said eleven days ago. Across all fifty-six patients at the recommended dose, infections occurred in eighty percent, and a quarter of the cohort had grade three or four infections. That is the structural cost, and it is not fixable by cleverness with the C-D-three arm. An avidity gate spares normal tissue carrying only one of the two antigens. Normal plasma cells carry both. You built a better plasma cell killer, and plasma cells are where immunoglobulin comes from. The cytokine problem yields to a tocilizumab dose and a step-up schedule; the hypogammaglobulinemia problem does not, and the more effective the molecule is, the worse it gets. Watch what the immunoglobulin replacement rate looks like — the tocilizumab study is actually collecting it as a secondary endpoint, which is a good sign somebody is paying attention.

And the strategic picture is worth a sentence, because it is unusual. There are now two Phase three trials running ramantamig head to head against teclistamab — four hundred patients at three or more prior lines, seven hundred patients at one to three — plus a thousand-patient frontline study with daratumumab that started on the ninth of this month. Teclistamab is Johnson and Johnson's own approved drug. They are running a controlled experiment to cannibalize themselves, which means they believe the trispecific wins on something a bispecific cannot match. My bet is that the thing it wins on is the calendar, not the immunology. If ramantamig moves into newly diagnosed patients, though, the infection number stops being a manageable toxicity and starts being the whole risk-benefit argument, and nothing presented this week tells you how that comes out.
