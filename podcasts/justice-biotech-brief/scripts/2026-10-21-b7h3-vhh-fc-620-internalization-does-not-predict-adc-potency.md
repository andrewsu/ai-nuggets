# VHH-Fc 620 — a B7-H3 nanobody that out-internalizes ifinatamab and still loses badly as an ADC

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.10.16.759583v1

## Script

Good morning, Justice. One pick today, from the Princess Máxima Center for Pediatric Oncology in Utrecht, posted to bioRxiv on Sunday. It's a B7-H3 binder-engineering paper, and on the surface it reads like a routine panel characterization — six llama nanobodies, some surface plasmon resonance, an epitope map, a conjugate. It isn't routine. Buried in the last two figures is the most useful negative result I've seen on this target all year, and it's aimed squarely at the screening cascade that most ADC discovery groups, probably including yours, are running right now.

Start with what makes B7-H3 structurally weird, because the whole paper hangs off it. B7-H3 comes in two flavors. There's a short form with a single immunoglobulin V-plus-C domain pair, and there's the dominant long form that carries two of those pairs in tandem — and the two pairs are near-identical in sequence. That duplication has a practical consequence nobody has really confronted: when you characterize an anti-B7-H3 antibody, you cannot tell which of the two copies it's actually sitting on, because standard epitope mapping can't distinguish them. And there's a soluble version of the short form circulating in patients, elevated in osteosarcoma, at concentrations in the same range as the affinities of the antibodies we're dosing.

So the Utrecht group did the thing that resolves it. They displayed the membrane-proximal domain pair and the membrane-distal domain pair separately on yeast and ran deep mutational scanning on each one independently. That's the methodological move worth stealing — by splitting the duplicated domains apart before you mutagenize, you convert an unanswerable question into a residue-level map. They ran it on six of their own nanobody-Fc fusions and, critically, on three clinically advanced antibodies as benchmarks: ifinatamab, mirzotamab, and vobramitamab.

The first result is a clean one. Four of their six binders strongly prefer the membrane-proximal domain, and bind the long isoform ten- to a hundred-fold more tightly than the short one. The three clinical antibodies show no discrimination whatsoever — proximal, distal, long, short, all the same. And the reason comes down to a single position. Threonine two ninety-eight sits in the shared footprint of five of the nanobodies, and nearly any substitution there kills binding. In the membrane-distal domain, and in the short isoform, that position is an alanine. That's it. That's the whole molecular basis of isoform selectivity on this target, and it's one of a very small number of surface residues that actually differ between the two copies.

Why you'd want that selectivity is the soluble-sink argument: a binder blind to the short form doesn't get consumed by circulating antigen before it reaches tumor, which matters most for modalities you dose at low protein mass — radioligands, T-cell engagers, ADCs. The authors are honest about the limit, and so should we be: shed ectodomain from the long isoform still carries the proximal domain, so selectivity narrows the sink, it doesn't close it.

Now the part that actually changes how you should work. They took three properties the field treats as a predictive chain — affinity, epitope, internalization — and showed the chain is broken at every link. Two of their binders, six-twenty and six-thirty-six, have essentially the same footprint and the same affinity, and differ several-fold in how much antibody they drag into the endolysosome. So epitope and affinity don't predict uptake. Fine — that's an argument for screening on internalization directly, which is exactly what everyone does. Six-twenty was the best internalizer in the panel, better than ifinatamab in both cell lines at both concentrations.

Then they conjugated six-twenty and ifinatamab to the same payload, exatecan, through the same protease-cleavable linker, with near-identical binding after conjugation and only a slight difference in drug-to-antibody ratio. Ifinatamab-exatecan was three- to twenty-nine-fold more potent. Across all four overexpressing cell lines. The binder that delivered more antibody into the cell killed fewer cells. Not affinity, not epitope, not isoform selectivity — none of them explain it, and the authors say plainly they don't know what does. Everything downstream of the endosome — trafficking route, lysosomal processing, how much free drug actually gets out — is the unmeasured variable, and it dominated.

Take that seriously, because the internalization assay is load-bearing in almost every ADC discovery funnel I know of. This paper says it will reliably tell you a binder gets inside, and will not reliably rank your binders on the thing you care about. If you pick leads that way, you're optimizing a proxy with a measured inversion.

Two caveats so you read this correctly. First, neither conjugate killed anything at endogenous B7-H3 levels — no measurable potency up to two hundred nanomolar in wild-type lines, activity only in overexpressing derivatives. That's a property of this reagent pair, not of the target: both were drug-to-antibody ratio around two with no bystander payload, against a clinical molecule running deruxtecan at roughly double the loading with bystander killing. This is not a verdict on ifinatamab deruxtecan in the clinic. Second, there's a format confound the authors flag themselves — a nanobody-Fc has only four interchain cysteines to conjugate to, against eight in an IgG1, and nobody knows whether that scaffold routes differently after it's internalized. Answering that requires site-specific conjugation, which is the obvious next experiment and isn't in this paper.

What I'd carry out of it: the duplicated-domain display trick is immediately reusable on any target with internal repeats, threonine two ninety-eight is now a named, mappable handle for isoform-selective B7-H3 targeting that three clinical antibodies all walked past, and internalization is necessary but demonstrably not sufficient. If your ADC campaign ranks binders on uptake and stops there, this is the paper that says go one step further, to payload release, before you commit.
