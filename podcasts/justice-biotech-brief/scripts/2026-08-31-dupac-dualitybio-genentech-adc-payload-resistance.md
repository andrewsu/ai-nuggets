# DUPAC — Genentech pays $45M up front for a payload platform whose whole reason to exist is that topoisomerase-1 ADCs will stop working

Paper link: https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/dualitybio-enters-a-global-collaboration-and-license-agreement-with-genentech-to-develop-next-generation-adcs-built-on-dualitybios-dupac-novel-payload-platform-302862514.html

## Script

Today's pick is a licensing deal, which normally I'd skip, because a deal with no data in it usually tells you more about a balance sheet than about biology. This one is different, and the reason is what the buyer is implicitly conceding.

On Friday, DualityBio in Shanghai announced a global collaboration and license with Genentech built on a platform they call DUPAC — say it doo-pack, it stands for DualityBio Unique Payload Antibody Conjugate. Forty-five million dollars up front, more than a billion dollars in aggregate development, regulatory and commercial milestones across all programs, plus tiered royalties. DualityBio generates and develops antibody-drug conjugates against Genentech-defined oncology targets, DualityBio leads discovery and early global clinical development, and Genentech takes over sole responsibility after Phase one-A. For Genentech this is the second Asia-Pacific deal in about a week — they'd just put up to two point three billion behind Hanmi in Korea on a non-incretin obesity peptide.

Here's why the ADC half of that matters to you. The antibody-drug conjugate field has quietly become a payload monoculture. Enhertu, datopotamab deruxtecan, patritumab deruxtecan, Trodelvy, sac-T-M-T, and most of the Chinese-origin conjugates now flooding into licensing deals — essentially all of them carry a topoisomerase-one inhibitor. D-X-d is an exatecan derivative. S-N thirty-eight is the active metabolite of irinotecan. Different antibodies, different antigens, different linkers, one chemical class doing the killing. And the industry has spent five years differentiating on the antibody: novel antigens, bispecific ADCs, the proximity and masking tricks we've talked about on this show. Almost nobody differentiated on the warhead.

The problem with that is the direction of travel. Topoisomerase-one ADCs are moving into earlier and earlier lines across the big solid tumors. Which means the population that has already progressed on a topoisomerase-one payload stops being a niche and becomes the default second-line patient. And resistance to a payload is a property of the payload class, not of the target. If a tumor has upregulated its efflux pumps, lost the lysosomal processing that liberates the drug, or fixed its topoisomerase-one biology, then swapping HER-two for TROP-two and re-conjugating the same exatecan derivative buys you very little. That's the wall the field is walking toward, and it's the wall DUPAC is explicitly sold against. DualityBio's own framing says the platform is designed to retain activity in patients refractory to that payload class and in tumors that were never very sensitive to it in the first place.

Now the part I find genuinely delicious. DualityBio is one of the companies that built the monoculture. Their flagship platform is DITAC — die-tack — and the products off it are third-generation topoisomerase-one ADCs: B-N-T three-two-three, trastuzumab pamirtecan, the HER-two conjugate that hit its progression-free survival primary endpoint in a Chinese Phase three; B-N-T three-two-five against TROP-two, with FDA Fast Track in platinum-resistant ovarian; B-N-T three-two-four against B-seven-H-three, Fast Track in prostate. So the company selling Genentech the antidote is the same company that helped create the disease. Which, if you think about it, is exactly who you'd want to buy it from — they've been running these payloads in over three and a half thousand patients across twenty countries, so they have the resistance data before anyone else does.

What's actually in DUPAC is thinner than the headline. It's a family of payloads with several members disclosed only as doop five, doop nine and doop ten, each said to work through a distinct antitumor mechanism, each paired with a matched linker tuned for systemic stability and tumor-specific release. The mechanisms are not named. What is disclosed is the design specification, and that's the part worth thinking about, because it tells you where the engineering tension sits. Four criteria: high potency, broad-spectrum activity across tumor types, a short systemic half-life so free payload clears fast, and a bystander effect suited to heterogeneous solid tumors.

Sit with the last two, because they pull against each other. A bystander effect requires that payload released inside an antigen-positive cell be membrane-permeable enough to diffuse out and kill the antigen-negative neighbor — which is the only reason ADCs work at all in tumors with patchy target expression, and it's why Enhertu works in HER-two-low. But membrane permeability is also exactly what lets deconjugated payload get into your bone marrow and your gut epithelium. So you want the molecule to be permeable locally and gone systemically. The way you square that is a short free half-life — kill the neighbors, then get cleared before you reach the marrow. That's the actual reason the exatecan derivatives won: D-X-d threads that needle better than the maytansinoids and the auristatins did. Any replacement payload has to thread the same needle with a novel mechanism, and that's a much harder problem than picking a new antigen.

So how should you price this? Skeptically, and the deal structure tells you Genentech does too. The entire public evidence base is three conference abstracts — AACR twenty twenty-five, AACR-N-C-I-E-O-R-T-C late that year, and AACR twenty twenty-six — reporting activity in tumor models relatively insensitive to topoisomerase-one payloads plus non-human-primate tolerability. That is preclinical and animal safety. There is no human data on any DUPAC payload. The targets are undisclosed, the number of programs is undisclosed, and forty-five million against more than a billion in milestones is roughly a four percent up-front ratio, which is option pricing, not conviction pricing. And look at where the risk sits: DualityBio pays for discovery and carries the molecules through Phase one-A. Genentech is not buying a program. It's buying the right to a first look at whatever survives first-in-human, and it's paying accordingly.

The reason I'd still put it in front of you is the reframing, not the money. For the last few years "next-generation ADC" has meant a cleverer front end — a bispecific, a masked binder, a proximity-guided antigen, a degrader-conjugate hybrid. Roche just spent real money betting the next differentiation is at the back end, and every large-cap oncology group is going to have to make the same call about payload diversity as a portfolio hedge, because a payload monoculture means correlated failure. That generalizes past ADCs, and it's the version of this I'd actually apply at your bench: when a whole field converges on one mechanism for the killing step, every asset built on it shares a single failure mode. It's worth asking the same question of the T cell engager field, where the killing step is C-D-three agonism in essentially every molecule in the clinic.

One more thing worth noting, and it's a small line in the release with real implications: DualityBio says its forward pipeline includes bispecific ADCs, novel-payload ADCs, and autoimmune ADCs. Conjugates aimed at autoimmune disease is a genuinely different risk-benefit problem than oncology, and it's a space to watch.

And the number that would change my read: if any DUPAC payload shows a response in patients who progressed on a deruxtecan- or govitecan-based conjugate — even a handful of them in a Phase one — the whole payload-diversification thesis stops being a hedge and becomes the main event. Until then, this is Genentech buying cheap insurance against a problem the field has known was coming and mostly declined to work on.
