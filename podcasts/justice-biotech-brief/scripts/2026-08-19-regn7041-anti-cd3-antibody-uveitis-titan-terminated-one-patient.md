# REGN7041 — first-in-human anti-CD3 antibody for uveitis terminated after a single patient, cause unidentified

Paper link: https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07218770

## Script

Today's pick is a very small trial with a fairly large lesson. Regeneron has terminated the only clinical trial of REGN seven-oh-four-one, its anti-C-D-3 monoclonal antibody, in active non-infectious uveitis involving the back of the eye. The reason entered in the federal trials registry is an unfavorable benefit-risk assessment. A company spokesperson says the decision followed a thorough review of a safety event observed in the trial, and that the cause of that event has not been identified. But the number that stopped me is the enrollment field. Actual enrollment, one. One patient. The study opened in mid-December of last year, ran until late July, and closed with a single participant dosed.

Start with why you would build this molecule at all, because the target choice is the interesting part. C-D-3 is the same receptor complex that every T cell engager in your world grabs onto — the invariant signaling module sitting next to the T cell receptor. In the engager setting you crosslink it to a tumor antigen and you want maximal activation and killing. But there is an entirely different pharmacology available at the same receptor, and it is much older. Bind C-D-3 without crosslinking it to a second target, with an Fc that doesn't recruit effector function, and instead of full activation you get partial, unproductive signaling — receptor downmodulation, anergy, a shift toward regulatory T cells. That is tolerance induction, and it genuinely works. Teplizumab is approved on exactly that mechanism to delay type 1 diabetes. Foralumab and otelixizumab are the same idea. So an anti-C-D-3 in an autoimmune indication is not a strange thing to try. It is one of the oldest disease-modifying ideas in clinical immunology.

The problem has always been the window. The same engagement that eventually tolerizes a T cell first activates it. You get first-dose cytokine release, you get lymphopenia, you get viral reactivation at the depleting end of the dose range. Otelixizumab didn't fail in type 1 diabetes because the target was wrong. It failed because the dose that was tolerable wasn't the dose that worked. The entire history of this target is a dose-finding history.

Which is why putting it in the eye is a clever idea, at least on paper. Non-infectious uveitis is a T cell-driven disease in a small, semi-enclosed compartment. If you can get an anti-C-D-3 to act locally, you get the tolerizing pharmacology where the pathology actually is, at a total dose measured in micrograms rather than milligrams, and you never approach the systemic exposure that produces cytokine release. The compartment does the same job that masking or conditional activation does in an engager — it restricts where the drug is active. Regeneron hasn't disclosed the route of administration and I'm not going to assert it. But look at what the protocol was measuring: drug concentrations in serum and in the fluid of the eye at multiple timepoints, plus the incidence and the magnitude of anti-drug antibodies, with treatment-emergent adverse events as the primary endpoint out to twenty-four weeks. Every site was a retina practice — Sacramento, Oak Park, Nashville, and two in Texas. This was a design built around the question of how much drug stays where you put it. Single ascending dose cohorts first, then multiple ascending dose. Textbook cautious.

It stopped at patient one.

I want to be precise about what we don't know here, because the gap is the story. We don't know what the event was. We don't know its severity. We don't know whether it was ocular or systemic. And Regeneron, having investigated it, says it does not know the cause. That last part is worse than it sounds. A dose-dependent cytokine release event in the first cohort is bad news you can work with — you lower the dose, you premedicate, you step-dose, which is precisely what the engager field spent a decade learning to do. An unexplained event at the starting dose gives you nothing to titrate against. You cannot engineer around a mechanism you haven't identified, and if you're the person who has to write the next protocol, you have no defensible starting dose to write down. That is how a single patient kills a program.

The transferable point for anyone building C-D-3-directed biologics is about restriction as a safety strategy. Our field's answer to engager toxicity has been to constrain where the molecule is active — masked engagers cleaved by tumor proteases, costimulation split across separate arms so that full activation requires two addresses at once, deliberately low-affinity C-D-3 arms that need high target density to do anything. Compartmental delivery is a cousin of all of those. It's restriction by geography instead of by biochemistry. And this is a datapoint that geography is not a firewall. Antibody leaves the vitreous. T cells traffic in and out. A tolerizing signal delivered into a compartment can have consequences that aren't confined to it — and the compartment being small cuts both ways, because there is very little room for an inflammatory response you didn't intend.

Two closing notes. This lands on a company that has been absorbing hits. Itepekimab, the interleukin-33 blocker that a lot of people wanted to be the Dupixent follow-up, missed one of its two Phase 3 trials in C-O-P-D. A LAG-3-containing melanoma combination lost head-to-head against Keytruda in Phase 3. There is eighteen billion dollars of cash on the balance sheet and a steady drumbeat of analysts asking for acquisitions, which the chief executive keeps answering with the word disciplined. Against that backdrop, killing an early asset over an unexplained safety event is both the right call and an expensive one.

And notice how we found out. Not a press release, not an investor update — a registry record quietly updated to terminated, with an enrollment count of one, noticed by a reporter who then went and asked. That is the disclosure floor for a first-in-human failure right now. If you want to know which early programs are dying, the trials registry is where it surfaces first, and the field would be better off if that weren't true.
