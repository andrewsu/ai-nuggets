## Script

Good morning, Justice. One pick today, and it's a post-mortem rather than a readout — which is usually the less interesting genre, except this one names a cause, and the cause is a sugar.

Immutep put out a development update on Friday for eftilagimod alfa, which everyone including the company calls efti. The headline is a strategy pivot, but the load-bearing paragraph is the root cause analysis for why their Phase three died in March.

Start with what the molecule is, because it's not what the name suggests. Efti is a soluble Lag-three fusion protein — the four extracellular domains of human Lag-three stuck onto a human I-g-G-one F-c. It is not a checkpoint blocker. It runs in the opposite direction from relatlimab. Lag-three's natural ligand is M-H-C class two, so a soluble dimeric Lag-three binds and clusters class two molecules on antigen-presenting cells — dendritic cells, monocytes — and pushes them to mature and secrete chemokines, which then primes C-D eight T cells downstream. It's an A-P-C agonist masquerading as a checkpoint asset. That distinction is about to matter a great deal.

Tacti oh-oh-four was the registrational bet: randomized, double-blind, efti plus pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy versus placebo plus pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy, first line non-small cell lung cancer, dual primary endpoints of progression-free survival and overall survival, around seven hundred fifty-six patients planned across more than a hundred fifty sites. In March the independent data monitoring committee looked at the planned interim analysis with three hundred seventy-eight patients enrolled and recommended stopping for futility.

Friday's explanation is that the drug in the Phase three was not the same drug that generated the Phase one and two data. Immutep says its analysis has identified structural differences between the efti used in Tacti oh-oh-four and the efti used in the earlier successful studies — and the example they give is a subtle difference in N-glycan structure. The correlate is manufacturing scale. Ten G-M-P batches were made at two hundred liters and fed the trials that worked. Tacti oh-oh-four was run exclusively on material made at two thousand liters. They have now contracted a fresh two-hundred-liter run. They also say they've ruled out the boring explanations — protocol design, arm imbalance, safety, randomization, trial conduct.

Now, my instinct with this genre is deep skepticism, because "the manufacturing changed" is the single most convenient thing a company can say after a futility stop. But there are two reasons to take it more seriously here than usual.

The first is mechanistic. If your molecule is an antagonist, potency is mostly a binding constant, and comparability panels are pretty good at catching binding changes. If your molecule is an agonist that works by clustering a receptor, potency is about avidity and higher-order assembly — how many class two molecules you crosslink, in what geometry, in a lipid raft on an immature dendritic cell. Glycans sit right on top of that. They change hydrodynamic radius, they change aggregation and self-association behavior, and on the F-c side they change every effector interaction. You can pass size-exclusion chromatography, pass charge variant analysis, pass a binding E-L-I-S-A, and still have shifted the thing that actually drives receptor clustering. So the failure mode they're describing is real and it's specific to the class of molecule they have.

The second reason is the one sentence in the release worth more than the rest of it. They say the glycan difference is considered potentially relevant given the markedly different immune activation profile observed in Tacti oh-oh-four compared to previous trials. That is a pharmacodynamic claim, not an analytical one. Efti has a well-characterized on-treatment signature — monocyte and dendritic cell activation, interferon-gamma, C-D eight expansion. If they measured that in Phase three patients and it was blunted relative to the earlier trials, that is genuinely strong evidence, because it's the drug failing to do its job in humans rather than a peak shifting on a chromatogram. And it is exactly the number they have not published. Everything hinges on it, and we got an adjective.

Here's the counter-hypothesis, and it's the one I'd bet on. Ask what actually generated the confidence to run a seven-hundred-patient Phase three. It was Insight oh-oh-three — an investigator-initiated, single-arm Phase one, twenty-one patients at the mature cut, median overall survival of thirty-two point nine months, held up against twenty-two months for pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy from Keynote one-eighty-nine. That's a cross-trial comparison against a historical control in a tiny selected population. It is the most reliably misleading kind of evidence in oncology, and the entire field knows it. Tacti oh-oh-four was the first randomized, controlled test of that regimen in that setting, and it failed.

And it's not the first randomized test of efti. Aipac was randomized, double-blind and placebo-controlled — paclitaxel plus efti versus paclitaxel plus placebo in hormone-receptor-positive metastatic breast cancer. It missed its primary endpoint of progression-free survival. Overall survival was about a three-month numerical difference and not significant globally, with the positive signals living in exploratory biomarker subgroups. That trial ran years before the two-thousand-liter scale-up and it is not on the list of studies Immutep attributes to the good two-hundred-liter material. So efti's record in randomized, placebo-controlled trials is zero for two, and its wins are all single-arm. The N-glycan story has to explain Tacti oh-oh-four without explaining Aipac, and it doesn't.

There's also a process problem in the fix. Going back to two hundred liters is a reasonable way to reproduce the material that worked. It is not a plan. You cannot register and launch a solid-tumor immunotherapy on two-hundred-liter batches — you will have to scale again, and you'll be repeating the same comparability exercise that just cost you a Phase three, using a characterization panel you now know was blind to the attribute that mattered. The version of this plan I'd find convincing is: run the next study on small-scale material, and in parallel build a functional potency assay that actually discriminates the two scales — an A-P-C activation or receptor-clustering bioassay, not another binding assay — and make it a release specification. Nothing in the release says they're doing that. If the completed root cause analysis lands without a cell-based potency assay that separates the two batch families, then what they have is a correlation between a peak and a trial result.

Where they're going next is head and neck squamous cell carcinoma in patients with a combined positive score below one, and the neoadjuvant setting in soft tissue sarcoma. The head and neck choice is more defensible than it looks, on mechanism: C-P-S below one is precisely where P-D one blockade has the least to offer, and an agent that acts upstream — licensing antigen-presenting cells to prime T cells in the first place — has a coherent story for why it might add something in a tumor that isn't inflamed. It also carries Fast Track. But it is still a retreat from the largest indication in oncology into a P-D-L-one-negative subgroup, justified by mature overall survival data from non-randomized cohorts. That's the same structure of evidence that produced Tacti oh-oh-four. And the next study isn't targeted to start until the second half of two thousand twenty-seven, which is roughly eighteen months of nothing while a partner is sought.

Two things worth carrying into your own work. First, if you're building F-c fusions or multispecifics that work by agonism or by clustering — and a lot of what's in your building does — comparability for an agonist is not comparability for an antagonist, and your release panel should include a functional assay that can actually see the mechanism. That lesson is transferable no matter how this particular story resolves. Second, watch for the completed analysis. If Immutep publishes the Phase three pharmacodynamic comparison with numbers, and a potency assay that splits the two scales, this becomes a case study the whole field should read. If it stays qualitative, the parsimonious reading is that a single-arm trial oversold a drug and a glycan is taking the blame.

One footnote that may end up mattering more than the headline: Immutep says I-M-P seven-sixty-one, the agonist anti-Lag-three antibody for autoimmune disease, continues on plan. An agonist that dials T cell responses down, rather than a soluble ligand that dials antigen presentation up, is a cleaner pharmacological proposition — and after Friday it may be the more interesting half of that portfolio.
