## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Saturday, September twelfth. An eye gene therapy delivered clinically meaningful benefit at one and a half billion vector genomes — four to five logs below a systemic dose — and walked out of a Type C meeting with an agreed pivotal endpoint. Both halves of that sentence matter to Aletira, and the second half matters more.

Opus Genetics reported three- and six-month results from the low-dose cohort of BIRD-1, its Phase one-two trial of a subretinal A-A-V therapy for BEST-one-related retinal disease. Five participants — three with Best vitelliform macular dystrophy at three months, two with autosomal recessive bestrophinopathy at six — all dosed at one and a half billion vector genomes per eye. All five showed clinically meaningful improvement in visual function. Retinal sensitivity on microperimetry improved in three of four evaluable participants; best-corrected visual acuity in three of five. Structurally, vitelliform material fell in two of the three dystrophy patients and intraretinal fluid cleared in both bestrophinopathy patients. No serious adverse events, no dose-limiting toxicities, no intraocular inflammation. The higher cohort, at four and a half billion, over-enrolled to eight.

Geoff, Hugh, Avi — this is the third answer in three days to the question of where selectivity comes from, and this one says the compartment. Opus does not need a liver-detargeted capsid, because it injected under the retina. Nothing systemic ever happens. That is precisely the argument we made on Friday about intracochlear injection, and here it is again with a dose number attached that is almost embarrassing next to a systemic program. So the honest version of our differentiation sentence keeps narrowing, and it is now quite narrow: in a compartmentalized sensory organ, the route already buys the organ and the dose, and what is left to buy is the cell type inside that organ. Retinal pigment epithelium versus photoreceptor. Hair cell versus supporting cell versus spiral ganglion neuron. That strip of real estate is genuinely ours. It is also the whole of it, and the deck should say so before someone else says it for us.

The more valuable half of this release is regulatory, and it is the part I want Matt and Geoff to read twice. The F-D-A agreed on a pivotal endpoint: an improvement of three decibels or better on microperimetry at five or more prespecified loci, combined with a patient-reported outcome, in a randomized controlled trial — with acuity, low-luminance acuity and contrast sensitivity also acceptable. The agency accepted a functional measure at named locations plus how the patient says they are doing, in an ultra-rare inherited sensory disease, off a five-patient open-label cohort. That is a template. In April the same agency approved the first otoferlin gene therapy on a single-arm package of twenty-four children. The endpoint terrain for inherited sensory disease is being surveyed right now, by other people, and our lead indication sits on it. The action is to find out what the otoferlin programs have agreed with the agency, and to know whether there is a Type C-able endpoint for a hearing-loss indication before we are choosing one under time pressure.

The candid limit: five patients, open label, no untreated control, clinically meaningful defined by the sponsor, and three of four on the measure that actually counts. And it is an eye, which is the most forgiving organ in gene therapy. Nobody should put this in a deck as proof of a general principle.

Second item is Winnow, and it is a competitor choosing a different route to the same promise. On Thursday, Adagene announced that Third Arc Bio selected its first lead candidate under an amended collaboration to develop masked C-D-three T cell engagers using Adagene's SAFEbody technology. SAFEbody shields the binding domain and unmasks it in the tumor microenvironment, so the engager is inert until it arrives. Target and economics are undisclosed; the release says total potential development and commercial milestones payable to Adagene have increased over the original agreement from last November. Third Arc is worth knowing: founded in twenty twenty-two, run by Peter Lebowitz, who was global head of oncology R and D at Johnson and Johnson, a hundred and sixty-five million dollar Series A from Vida, Cormorant and Hillhouse plus a fifty-two million dollar extension led by Andreessen Horowitz, lead asset a claudin-six engager in Phase one, and a stated remit across solid tumors and immunology and inflammation.

Jon, Hemaka — the competitive read is that a company with more than two hundred million dollars behind it, led by someone who has shipped engagers before, working in both oncology and autoimmunity, has decided to buy conditional activation rather than build it. That is a second route to the safety story Winnow sells. You can make a T cell engager safe by restricting which antigen it sees, or by restricting where it switches on. Our answer to that is real and it is specific: a mask keyed to the tumor microenvironment has nothing to unmask it in an inflamed joint or a sacroiliac joint. Protease-triggered masking is a tumor trick, and it does not obviously port to autoimmunity. That sentence needs to exist in writing, because the moment someone does port it, antigen selectivity stops being the only way to get there.

Eddie, Emily — there is a second read, and it is Friday's lesson with numbers on it. On Wednesday Adagene collected a two million dollar milestone from Exelixis for a masked R-O-R one and two antibody-drug conjugate. On Thursday it collected an increase in potential milestones from Third Arc. Same technology, two partners, two days. That is what the bolt-on selectivity-layer business actually looks like: paid in milestones rather than upfront, paid repeatedly across partners, and modest per deal. It is a real business. It is not a large one per transaction, and it is the model our own layer most resembles.

Third and fourth items go together, because two companies placed opposite bets on disease-modifying diabetes this week, and the structures are more instructive than the biology. Yesterday we covered twenty societies endorsing islet-autoantibody screening in toddlers — the machine that finds stage one and stage two patients. Here is what two teams propose to do with them.

Kura Oncology carved out Caspian Therapeutics around K-O seventy-two forty-six, a next-generation selective menin inhibitor purpose-designed for chronic metabolic disease rather than cancer. Fifty million dollars led by BVF Partners, with the T-one-D Fund, Invus, Montanova, Eli Lilly, Kura itself and Kura's own leadership. Kura keeps roughly half on a fully diluted basis and board representation; CEO Troy Wilson becomes executive chairman. The biology is clean to state: menin is a brake on beta-cell proliferation, so inhibiting it expands functional beta-cell mass and raises endogenous insulin. Preclinically the effect persisted after dosing stopped, in both type one and type two models and in human islet systems. Preclinical data land at E-A-S-D on the twenty-ninth.

Matt, Eddie, Emily — look at the structure rather than the target. A parent contributes the asset and the chemistry, keeps about fifty percent and the chair, and brings in a crossover lead, a strategic, and a disease foundation in the same round. We do the same de-risking work and typically end up with a licence and an early check instead of half the company. That asymmetry is worth an hour of Emily's time, because the thing Kura has that we usually do not is ownership of the IP-contributing entity. The other transferable piece is the T-one-D Fund itself — venture philanthropy that exists to sit beside exactly this kind of round. For every therapeutic theme on our list there is an equivalent fund, and none of them are in our rounds today.

The candid note on Caspian: menin inhibitors carry an oncology safety history, and this is a chronic indication in people who are otherwise well. Purpose-designed for metabolic disease is the entire bet. The twenty-ninth is when we find out whether the preclinical package speaks to that.

The other bet is in Maryland. On Tuesday, Sernova and Seraxis signed a definitive merger agreement to form BetaNova Biotherapeutics, headquartered in Germantown, with each side's existing shareholders holding roughly half. Sernova brings the Cell Pouch, an implantable device that vascularizes and houses insulin-producing cells, tested for years at the University of Chicago with most first-cohort patients reaching insulin independence and cells surviving as long as five years. Seraxis brings stem-cell-derived islet cells manufactured at commercial scale under G-M-P. Lead program is allogeneic, with Phase one-two dosing expected early twenty twenty-seven and data by mid-year; the follow-on adds immune-evasion gene edits with an I-N-D targeted for the second half of twenty twenty-seven. They have lined up ten million dollars from existing investors, intend a Nasdaq listing in early twenty twenty-seven, and expect to close in November.

Hemaka, Jon — the merger is the lesson. Two companies each owned half of one product and neither could sell half. A housing device with no scalable cell source, and a cell source with nowhere durable to live. The resolution was a fifty-fifty merger and a ten million dollar bridge, which tells you how much leverage either side had by the time they got there. The standing question for us: wherever a Blackbird program needs a component we do not own, decide early whether we license it, buy it, or merge into it — the price of that decision rises every round, and it is cheapest before anyone has a clinical asset to protect.

And Eddie, one geographic note. BetaNova will sit twenty-five miles from the BioHub working on disease-modifying type one diabetes, in the same week we put Islex on your first-read list — a Baltimore Hopkins spinout with a humanized islet-homing lead and two hundred and seventy-five thousand dollars of N-S-F money as its only disclosed capital. Neighbor, comparator, and possibly partner, and worth a call before a Nasdaq listing changes their posture.

Fifth item is a short sourcing note, held loosely. Technical dot l-y's Maryland innovators list named Christopher Adrian Johnston, who turned his Johns Hopkins doctoral thesis into DUA, a cancer biotech he founded this year around what he calls velocity receptors — engineered proteins meant to help CAR-T cells physically reach solid tumors that have resisted them. Esther, Maisha, Eddie — a list entry is not diligence and I am not calling this a lead. The signal is the pattern. A Hopkins thesis became a Baltimore company in twenty twenty-six, in solid-tumor cell therapy, adjacent to Clasp and to our oncology surface-target theme, and it formed without us. The question is not whether DUA is good. It is whether the pre-formation Hopkins founder pool reaches us before it incorporates. It is worth remembering that our own founding funder is the named sponsor of the top prizes in Johns Hopkins Technology Ventures' student-venture program. That is the same channel, and this is one more reason to work it on purpose rather than opportunistically.

Last, the capital weather. Frazier Life Sciences closed more than one point one billion dollars in new commitments for its public fund, taking it to about two point eight billion since twenty twenty-one — public companies, with flexibility to cross over into late-stage privates. The accompanying read called it a selective recovery, with money concentrating in later-stage, de-risked assets carrying near-term clinical catalysts, and away from early-stage companies with less tested science.

Emily, Eddie — two of Frazier's listed exits are ours to think about. Cerevel went to AbbVie for eight point seven billion on the muscarinic thesis that created the market our G-P-R fifty-two program is aimed at. Avidity went to Novartis for twelve billion, and its lead asset failed Phase three last week. Both got the exit anyway. The phrase to design toward is near-term clinical catalyst. The crossover money is real and it is available, and it is available on the far side of a de-risking event — which is the specific product Blackbird Laboratories manufactures. That is an argument for being explicit, in every program plan, about which single event converts the asset from early-stage to de-risked, and how fast we can buy it.

Quiet where it matters. Nothing on G-P-R fifty-two or Nxera, and that out-license window has under four months of the year left. Nothing new from Sensorion or the otoferlin programs in hearing loss. Nothing on Clasp's monotherapy readout, nothing in Artemyx's space, and nothing on NuGate.

To close: the week's through-line is that selectivity keeps getting bought somewhere other than the transcript — at the capsid on Friday, at the compartment today in the eye, and at the mask in Adagene's two deals — and in every one of those cases the layer was licensed rather than built. Our layer is the only one aimed at a cell type inside an organ, which is the narrowest and most defensible strip on the map, and also the smallest. Say the narrow thing confidently; do not let the deck say the broad one.

That is the Portfolio Watch for Saturday, September twelfth. Tomorrow is Sunday, which means both a Sourcing Radar and a Portfolio Watch.
