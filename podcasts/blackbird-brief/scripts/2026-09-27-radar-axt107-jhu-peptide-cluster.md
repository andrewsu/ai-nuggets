## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Sourcing Radar for Sunday, September twenty-seventh. Three leads. The first is a first-in-human dataset on a Johns Hopkins peptide that went up quietly as a preprint on Thursday, and the most actionable sentence in it sits in the financial disclosures rather than the results.

That paper is DISCOVER, a Phase one/two-a trial of a single suprachoroidal injection of A-X-T one-oh-seven, generic name gersizangitide, in neovascular age-related macular degeneration. The authors include Niranjan Pandey, Peter Campochiaro, Aleksander Popel and Jordan Green, out of Biomedical Engineering and the Wilmer Eye Institute, with AsclepiX Therapeutics of Bethesda. Popel, Green and Pandey are already in our file as a serial-founder cluster. This is what that cluster looks like at the far end of a cycle.

The mechanism is genuinely differentiated. A-X-T one-oh-seven is a twenty-amino-acid peptide from type four collagen. It binds two integrins, and from there it disrupts integrin-receptor complexes and shuts down V-E-G-F receptor two signalling — that half is familiar. The other half is not. It clusters Tie2 at endothelial cell junctions, and in doing so it converts angiopoietin-two from an antagonist of Tie2 into an agonist of it. Faricimab, the approved bispecific, works by neutralising angiopoietin-two. This peptide takes the same disease-elevated pool and turns it into the drug's own stabilising ligand.

The second differentiated thing is the formulation, and it is the more licensable of the two. The peptide is soluble at low pH and low ionic strength and precipitates at the pH of the eye, so it forms its own depot. No polymer, no encapsulating excipient — the drug is the particle. Half-life in animals is about a hundred and eighty days.

The history is why this is a lead rather than a press release. The first clinical formulation was an intravitreal gel, and on dose escalation it produced sustained intraocular pressure elevations that drops could not control — most likely gel fragments obstructing outflow at the trabecular meshwork. In ophthalmology that is a program-killing signal. They reformulated the same peptide as self-assembled microparticles and moved the route to the suprachoroidal space, away from the front of the eye.

DISCOVER tested whether the route fixed it. Fifteen subjects at four U-S sites, open label, three dose cohorts, one injection, forty weeks. Eleven of the fifteen were treatment-experienced partial responders to anti-V-E-G-F; four were naive.

On safety, the reformulation worked. No drug-related serious adverse events, no endophthalmitis, no retinal detachment, no suprachoroidal haemorrhage, no clinically significant inflammation, no meaningful pressure rise at any dose, and nobody on chronic pressure-lowering therapy. That is the result that matters, because it converts a dead program into a live one.

On activity, be careful. Two treatment-experienced eyes completed forty weeks with no protocol-specified rescue and a third reached week thirty-six. In the top-dose treatment-experienced group median durability was thirty-six weeks. Across the fourteen evaluable eyes, visual acuity ranged from a gain of fifteen letters to a loss of thirty-one. Fifteen patients, no control arm, rescue permitted from week twelve — this is a safety result with a durability signal in a subgroup, and the authors say so.

Now the disclosure, which is where the real lead is. Green, Popel and Pandey are co-founders of OptaNova Pharma, which acquired AsclepiX's intellectual property. And Pandey is co-founder and chief executive of Terebra Therapeutics, with Popel a co-founder there too.

Put the timeline together. Enrolment finished in May of twenty twenty-four, topline was guided for the second quarter of twenty twenty-five, and the forty-week dataset surfaces as a preprint in September of twenty twenty-six — no financing announcement, and the intellectual property now in a different entity. That usually means one thing: the asset needed a new balance sheet and the founders built one.

A peptide that is its own sustained-release formulation, with a six-month half-life and no polymer to qualify, is a chassis for any target needing durable local exposure in a small enclosed compartment. For Aletira that compartment is the inner ear. The question for Hopkins Tech Ventures is whether composition of matter on the self-assembly behaviour is separable from composition of matter on this particular peptide.

Second lead. Zhaozhu Qiu's lab, in Physiology, Pharmacology and Therapeutics at Johns Hopkins, posted on Tuesday. They name the export route for the oncometabolite that defines I-D-H-mutant glioma, and the route is a druggable channel.

Mutant I-D-H makes D-two-hydroxyglutarate, which piles up to millimolar levels, drives the hypermethylation phenotype, and once it leaves the cell suppresses tumour-infiltrating T cells. That last part has been known and unexploited because nobody knew how the metabolite gets out. Their answer is the volume-regulated anion channel, SWELL1 paired with L-R-R-C-eight-C. Delete SWELL1 and release collapses, and in an immunocompetent mouse model the immunosuppression reverses.

Then comes the finding that turns a mechanism into a drug. Blocking the exit does not only protect the immune system outside the cell — it poisons the tumour cell from inside. The metabolite accumulates intracellularly and limits proliferation through epigenetic remodelling and mitochondrial stress. One intervention, two independent anti-tumour effects.

The pharmacology uses dicumarol, an old anticoagulant that turns out to block this channel. Median survival was twenty-four days on vehicle, twenty-nine on dicumarol, thirty on an anti-P-D-one antibody, and forty-five on the combination. It raised intratumoral C-D-eight T cells and their effector markers — and did none of that in mice whose tumours lacked SWELL1, which is a genuine on-target control that rules out the anticoagulant activity doing the work. In human lower-grade I-D-H-mutant gliomas, high expression of the partner subunit tracks with hypermethylation and worse survival.

The limits are the whole investment case. Dicumarol does not cross the blood-brain barrier, so it went in through an implanted intracerebral cannula. That is a tool, not a therapy, and no selective brain-penetrant blocker of this channel exists. And the competitive question is vorasidenib, which is approved in grade two disease and blocks production of the metabolite upstream — if you can stop making it with an oral drug, why block its exit. The paper's implicit answer is the intracellular accumulation effect, which an I-D-H inhibitor cannot produce. Build the diligence call around that one question.

Competing interests: none declared. On work that names a target, runs a genetically controlled in vivo experiment and reports checkpoint synergy, that is worth moving on. Anthony and Matt Lawler — ask Hopkins Tech Ventures whether the target and the combination use are filed separately from any chemistry. The chemistry does not exist yet, which is exactly the stage we fund.

Third lead, shorter. David Benavides in Neurology at the University of Maryland School of Medicine, with Maureen Kane's group at the School of Pharmacy, posted on Monday.

Anti-N-M-D-A-receptor encephalitis is the most common autoimmune encephalitis, and the syndrome overall leaves up to thirty-five percent of patients dead or poorly recovered. The textbook mechanism is that patient antibodies crosslink the receptor and drive it inside the cell. Everything we do therapeutically follows from that premise — immunosuppression, B-cell depletion, cell therapy in refractory cases — all of which remove the antibody, and none of which helps the patient already in the intensive care unit.

They took a human monoclonal antibody cloned from a patient and put it on primary cortical neurons. At two hours it did not reduce synaptic receptor levels and it did not reduce surface receptor by biotinylation, with positive controls that moved — so a real negative. What it did do was remodel the synaptic phosphoproteome, in a direction correlating positively with receptor activation and negatively with inhibition. At seventy-two hours the picture inverts, with eleven of twelve regulated kinases suppressed.

If that holds, the first event in this disease is a signalling event rather than a receptor-loss event, which opens a window none of the antibody-removal strategies address: an acute neuroprotective adjunct at a kinase node, given while immunotherapy takes weeks to work.

Be honest about how early it is. Primary neurons only, one antibody, two timepoints, no animal model, and the kinases the antibody regulates do not overlap with those regulated by a known receptor modulator despite shared substrates — so "it behaves like activation" is an inference from a substrate signature, not a demonstration. This is career-award-stage work.

No competing interests declared, so the position may still be open. The right instrument is a translational grant that buys exactly one thing: name the node. A phosphoproteome is not a target. Jon and Esther — find out whether this group would nominate a candidate kinase or only has a dataset. And note the adjacency for the Winnow file: autoimmune encephalitis is a lead indication for the autoimmune cell-therapy wave, so an acute mechanism-based adjunct in the same disease is either a companion or a competitor for attention, and it is being generated at a partner institution.

That is the radar. Two from Johns Hopkins, one from U-M-B, and the through-line is that the licensable thing is not always the molecule. This week it is a delivery chassis, an unowned target class, and a kinase node that does not have a name yet. Portfolio Watch is next.
