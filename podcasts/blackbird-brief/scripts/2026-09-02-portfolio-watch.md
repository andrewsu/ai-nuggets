## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Wednesday, September second. The most important development for the portfolio is a safety event. Novartis has halted eight trials of its C-D nineteen CAR T therapy in autoimmune disease after three patients died of a hyperinflammatory syndrome, and Bristol Myers paused enrollment in its own autoimmune CAR T program the same week. Yesterday we told you the in-vivo version of that lane had just filled up. Twenty-four hours later the ex-vivo version stopped.

Novartis paused on August twenty-fourth and it became public yesterday. The drug is rapcabtagene autoleucel — rap-cel — an autologous C-D nineteen CAR T. Three patients died of immune effector cell-associated hemophagocytic syndrome, a rare and potentially fatal hyperinflammatory reaction distinct from cytokine release syndrome, the toxicity cell therapy already knows how to manage. The pause covers the Phase 2 AUTOGRAPH studies in lupus, systemic sclerosis and the inflammatory myopathies, plus Phase 1/2 work in rheumatoid arthritis, generalized myasthenia gravis and multiple sclerosis. Eight trials. The cancer studies are untouched and still enrolling. Bristol Myers separately and voluntarily paused enrollment in the autoimmune trials of its own C-D nineteen product, zolacabtagene autoleucel, over what it called transient and reversible inflammatory events — no deaths, though a February publication of its Phase 1 had already documented one case of the same syndrome.

The analyst read is the interesting part. Sami Corwin at William Blair points at what the two products have in common: both are built on next-generation rapid-manufacturing platforms designed to yield a less differentiated cell that expands harder once it is back in the patient. If that is right, toxicity and efficacy sit on the same knob — you cannot dial down the expansion causing the hyperinflammation without dialling down the B-cell depletion causing the remission.

Jon, Eddie — for Winnow that is the argument, and it is now a clinical event rather than a slide. A bispecific engager is a dosed drug. You titrate it, you interrupt it, you stop it, and you do not lymphodeplete the patient to make room for it. A living, expanding cell product gives you none of those controls. That belongs as its own row in the Winnow target product profile, next to the boxed-warning row we added for Lisraya on the twenty-ninth. The word is not safer. The word is controllable.

Two limits, in the deck before an investor finds them. Engagers are not exempt from this biology — blinatumomab has produced hemophagocytic syndrome, and cytokine release is the class toxicity of C-D three engagement, so what an engager buys is dose control, not exemption. And hold the discipline we have used all week: these are C-D nineteen products depleting B cells, while Winnow is selective for T-cell-mediated disease. The read is the control architecture, not the target.

Virginia, one more. ArsenalBio declared in-vivo CAR T for autoimmunity its entire strategy on Monday, and generating the cells inside the patient gives you less control over expansion than the ex-vivo version that just stopped, not more. That does not invalidate the sourcing note on Jonathan Schneck's Hopkins nanoparticle project — it changes the first question, from what the nanoparticle does that ArsenalBio cannot, to what it does about expansion kinetics you cannot observe until the patient is already inflamed.

Second item, and it lands on the same target product profile from the other direction. Alumis reported topline data yesterday from LUMUS, a Phase 2b of envudeucitinib — a next-generation oral T-Y-K two inhibitor — in moderate to severe systemic lupus. Four hundred and eight autoantibody-positive patients, three doses against placebo, forty-eight weeks. The primary endpoint was the BICLA composite at week forty-eight. It missed in the overall population, and so did every key secondary: the cutaneous index, the lupus responder index, low disease activity state, and steroid reduction. The drug was well tolerated with no new safety signals, and the top dose showed maximal target engagement. Alumis disclosed no response rate at all, in any arm, which tells you something by itself.

The explanation is the part worth your attention. Patients with a high type-one interferon gene signature showed what Alumis calls robust responses on the primary and the key secondaries — and those patients were, in the company's own word, unexpectedly under-represented in the trial, which dragged the overall numbers down. Chief executive Martin Babler is taking that to the agency as the basis for a Phase 3 conversation, and the psoriasis filing is still on for the fourth quarter.

Two reads. For the Winnow profile, T-Y-K two is the same broad-kinase column that produced the Lisraya dermatomyositis approval nine days ago. That column just failed in lupus with a molecule cleaner and better engaged than the approved one. Broad kinase inhibition wins where a single pathway dominates the disease and loses where the population is heterogeneous — which is the structural argument for a clone-selective agent, and it is far more persuasive than any tolerability comparison.

The second read is harder, and Hemaka, it applies to G-P-R fifty-two more than to Winnow. On the twenty-ninth we drew a rule from the BioNTech colorectal termination: selecting patients buys you enrichment, not efficacy. Today is the mirror image — failing to select can bury a real effect inside an unselected population. Same rule stated twice. The enrichment hypothesis has to be pre-specified, powered and agreed before the trial runs, not discovered in the subgroup table afterwards. Ken Rhodes at Bristol Myers said the buyer's half of this out loud two days ago: genetics picks the target, biomarkers pick the patients. Alumis is the invoice for having the first and not the second — four hundred and eight patients and a failed readout, on a drug that may well work.

Third item is short and carries two things. Roche signed a licensing deal with Simcere Zaiming, a subsidiary of the Chinese group Simcere, for a molecule called SIM zero-six-six-zero — a trispecific antibody binding C-D seventy-nine A and C-D nineteen on the B cell and C-D three on the T cell. Seventy-five million dollars upfront, up to one and a half billion in milestones, plus royalties, on an asset in early clinical trials. Roche takes the malignancy indications, newly diagnosed and relapsed refractory B-cell lymphoma, and the release names lupus and arthritis among potential applications.

Read it two ways. First, a coincidence worth refusing to call one: in the same week the cell-based route to deep B-cell depletion in autoimmunity stopped over uncontrolled expansion, a large pharma paid for the engager route to the same biology — and the molecule's stated design intent is to kill B cells while limiting cytokine release. Nobody is drawing that line in public yet. Eddie, we can draw it in a partnering conversation.

Second, a number that moves. On Monday we logged forty-five million dollars upfront to DualityBio as what buys a large pharma a discovery-through-Phase-one-A partnership with an experienced Asian platform company. That buyer was Genentech, which is Roche — and the same company, days later, paid seventy-five million for an early-clinical trispecific. Three deals in eight days: two point three billion to Hanmi, up to a billion to DualityBio, now Simcere. One acquirer, all ex-U-S platforms. The benchmark moved up; the lesson did not. The differentiator has to be something the buyer cannot source in Shanghai or Seoul — human data, or a genuinely unencumbered composition position. A well-run preclinical package is still not it.

Fourth. Hemaka, this hardens a call we made softly on Monday. Bristol Myers sits on our shortlist of plausible buyers for Nxera's N-X-E one forty-nine, the only other Phase-2-ready G-P-R fifty-two agonist, and on Monday we moved them down that list on soft evidence — an interview, and a Truist note saying business development did not look like a priority. Today there is a hard reason. BioSpace laid out what the collapsed AstraZeneca merger talks cost them: roughly eighty percent of Bristol Myers revenue faces generic or biosimilar competition by twenty thirty-one, and the merger would have been the buffer. What is left is milvexian, an oral factor eleven-A inhibitor, with Phase 3 atrial fibrillation data due in twenty twenty-seven. Raghuram Selvaraju at H.C. Wainwright says a failure takes the stock down forty to fifty percent and leaves the company, in his words, hanging by a thread — and that if Bristol Myers blows up with milvexian, there may be a feeding frenzy once the carnage is over. Seamus Fernandez at Guggenheim carries that trial at a sixty percent probability of success.

A company whose next three years rest on one Phase 3 that its friendlier analysts handicap at sixty-forty is not writing a check this half for a Phase-2-ready novel-mechanism C-N-S asset — and if milvexian misses, Bristol Myers becomes a target rather than a buyer. Take them off the near-term bidder set for Nxera's stated second-half out-licensing window. Otsuka, Neurocrine, AbbVie through Cerevel, Novartis and S-K Biopharmaceuticals are the list.

The same piece carries a Cobenfy number worth logging. First half of this year: one hundred and nineteen million dollars, fifty-six in the first quarter and sixty-three in the second, up eighty-one percent year on year. Real growth. But full-year twenty twenty-five was a hundred and fifty-five million against a forecast above three hundred and ten, and this pace does not close that gap. Selvaraju also flags the Alzheimer's agitation expansion running straight into the side-effect profile in elderly patients. Third consecutive datapoint that mechanism was never the constraint — keep the channel and access plan beside the three-symptom-domain efficacy story, not behind it.

One thirty-second footnote to that biomarker gap before we leave it. The Feinstein Institutes published in the Journal of Clinical Investigation a blood immune-cell network analysis in eighty-four treatment-naive, recently diagnosed schizophrenia patients against ninety-seven controls, finding that transposable elements act as high-connectivity hubs in controls and lose those connections in patients. Not a target and not a validated biomarker at that sample size. But we have been framing the G-P-R fifty-two biomarker question purely as central target engagement, as in whether there is a P-E-T tracer for the receptor, and a first-episode state readable in blood is a different and far cheaper instrument. Hemaka, Yixuan — decide which instrument the plan is buying, because what Phase 1 collects only gets decided once.

Last item, for Emily and Eddie. Leerink counts twenty biotech I-P-Os and nineteen reverse mergers so far this year — but seventeen of those nineteen reverse mergers landed in the third quarter alone, against one in the second, while I-P-O activity stayed flat. Maha Katabi at Sofinnova explains the preference plainly: in a reverse merger you see who is in the syndicate and how the deal plays out before the stock trades. Jack Bannister at Leerink names the cost — four to six months to close instead of weeks, so investors wear six months of market risk, which filters out the fast money.

On the twenty-second we logged Werewolf Therapeutics ending as a forty-seven-and-a-half-million-dollar Nasdaq listing vehicle with legacy holders under seven percent, and we filed the reverse merger as a failure ending. One quarter later it is the most-used door to the public market and sophisticated investors are choosing it deliberately. So: three public-market paths for a Blackbird spinout, not two, and the third one shows you the syndicate. It also implies a growing inventory of cash-carrying shells with dead pipelines, which makes counterparty selection matter more than the mechanism. Underneath all of it is the number that actually matters — more than seventy biopharma M and A deals in the first half of this year against thirty in the first half of last. This is a liquidity year, and Clasp's post-readout financing, Aletira's Series A and the development-candidate-versus-first-in-human call on G-P-R fifty-two are all being decided into a window that has been open long enough that someone should be asking when it closes.

That is the watch. The through-line, if you want one: two ways of treating autoimmune disease broadly both stumbled inside forty-eight hours — one on control of an expanding cell product, one on the absence of a patient-selection hypothesis — and in the same window a large pharma paid seventy-five million dollars for the selective, dose-controlled version of the first. Winnow's thesis got three independent confirmations on a Tuesday. Have a good day.
