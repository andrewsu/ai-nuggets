## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Portfolio Watch for Friday, August twenty-first, and it is a good day for the flagship. Two things landed: a human dataset that answers the one argument Aletira did not have a prepared answer to, and — from reading the only approved label in our own lead indication — the answer to the delivery question we left open Wednesday.

Start with the paper, because it is the most useful citation this thesis has ever had. Tuesday, in Human Gene Therapy, Robert MacLaren's group at Oxford with the university eye hospital in Tübingen published a follow-on review of twenty-five consecutive choroideremia patients treated in their own dose-escalation trials with a subretinal adeno-associated virus carrying the REP1 gene under CAG — a ubiquitous promoter. The bottom rung of the ladder we have been building all week.

Twelve of the twenty-five treated eyes, forty-eight percent, developed perivascular astrocytic gliosis. Every affected eye was in the higher-dose group: twelve of nineteen. Zero of the six low-dose patients.

Be candid about what this is. It is benign — clinically silent, quiet examinations, no uveitic symptoms, no peripheral cellular immune response in the patients who had the assays. Nobody was harmed. The value is in the mechanism they propose: local overexpression of REP1 in perivascular astrocytes, a cell type nobody was aiming at, suppressing FOXO3-mediated cell death and engaging EGFR and STAT3 signaling, producing an astrocytic accumulation that resembles the hamartomas seen in the phacomatoses. Wrong cell, wrong pathway, real change in tissue architecture. Their closing sentence, verbatim: gene therapy involving ubiquitous promoters may have unexpected effects, and while benign, may not be predicted.

That is the field's founding retinal gene therapy group, in the field's own journal, saying it about their own product, with a dose-response curve attached.

And here is the part that closes a hole I flagged Wednesday. Skylark's investor pitches their program on giving less virus — a targeted micro-dose, minimal systemic exposure — which is a partial substitute for restricting expression. I said we needed a prepared answer and did not have one. Now we do, in humans. Yes, the low dose avoided the gliosis entirely. But the paper describes the high dose as the one generally accepted as therapeutic for reaching photoreceptors. So dose reduction did not solve the problem, it traded the problem for less drug — and at the dose that actually works, sixty-three percent of eyes developed the phenotype. Expression control is how you hold the therapeutic dose without paying the off-target-cell tax. That is the sentence, it is now evidenced in patients, and Geoff should have it this week.

Supporting material, offered as background rather than news because it is a preprint from the fifth of August. Ben Deverman's vector group at the Broad, with Beth Stevens and Paola Arlotta, benchmarked engineered and natural capsids for delivery to microglia. Every capsid worked, specifically and efficiently, when it carried a genome built with a cell-type promoter plus microRNA-124 target sites. CAG did not. Their conclusion is that genome design, not capsid identity, is the principal determinant of specific targeting — close to a thesis statement for us. One honest note: the control they reached for was a promoter plus microRNA sites in the untranslated region, which is post-transcriptional control in Skylark's neighborhood, not ours. Take the endorsement of cargo design over capsid engineering, and notice who it points at.

Now the delivery question. Wednesday I called Skylark's branded catheter a competitive axis we had no answer to, and asked Geoff and Hugh what ours is. So I read Otarmeni — Regeneron's otoferlin therapy, approved April twenty-third, the only approved gene therapy for genetic hearing loss in the world.

The label requires an administration kit, and the kit is a B-D needle, two B-D syringes, a tip cap, and one catheter: a Premicath, from the French device company Vygon. One French, twenty-eight gauge. That is a commercially available neonatal line designed for venous access in premature infants under a kilogram, and you can order it from a medical supply distributor. The procedure in the label is a standard mastoidectomy, an opening of the facial recess, and the catheter tip through the round window membrane using electrode insertion forceps — cochlear implant surgery, with cochlear implant instruments. The kit is required and regulated, but every part in it is a catalog part. Regeneron did not build a device.

Then I pulled all four programs off the trial registry. Skylark registers a combination product with its device inside the primary safety endpoint and again as a secondary. Akouos, now Lilly, registers two device configurations in one trial plus a device performance secondary. Sensorion registers three device secondaries. Regeneron registered a plain genetic intervention — twenty-nine secondary endpoints, not one of them about a device.

Three of the four programs took on device development risk. The one that reached approval declined it. So Skylark's catheter is not a moat we have to match; it is a regulatory surface they chose to add, and in a combination product a device deficiency can hold up your drug. Our delivery answer already exists on an approved label.

Two limits, and the second is the work. A catalog catheter is available to everyone, so this neutralizes their advantage rather than creating ours. And two well-funded groups engineered a device anyway, which is evidence, not vanity — most likely because of the target. Regeneron needed inner hair cells; Skylark's target and our harder ones need broad, even coverage of supporting cells along the length of the cochlear duct. So the sharper question for Avi and Hugh: is an engineered catheter elective for an otoferlin-shaped target and necessary for a connexin-shaped one? If it is the latter, we need a delivery partner rather than a delivery program — and there are three designs already in the clinic to partner with instead of build.

The same label carries two more things. The construct drives otoferlin off, in the label's own words, an engineered hair-cell-specific promoter derived from regulatory elements of myosin fifteen. So restricting expression with a regulatory element taken from a native gene is already on an approved label in the cochlea, four months before yesterday's Ultragenyx approval — same tissue, same modality, same agency. Better slide for Geoff than the liver drug, with the uncomfortable half attached: the principle is settled, and a promoter settled it. A promoter is an address. It says which door. Connexin twenty-six has to arrive in supporting cells at roughly the right level, because gap junctions are dose-sensitive, and an address does not give you a level. Move the claim from address to level.

And it is a dual vector, because otoferlin does not fit inside one capsid — split across two, relying on the halves reassembling. That is the approved workaround for running out of cassette space, and it costs two vectors' worth of capsid load. When the Series A asks what a selective exon costs us in kilobases, the answer we do not want to give is that we would go dual.

Second item, on 1104health, in two parts. Merck signed a collaboration with Sarah Cannon Research Institute to run oncology trials at community sites through Sarah Cannon's delivery model — about fifteen hundred oncologists, two hundred locations, twenty-plus states. The number that matters is enrollment at nineteen percent against a seven percent national average, with site activation fifty percent faster.

The validation is real, and it is from the largest oncology sponsor there is: community enrollment is the bottleneck and it is worth paying to fix. That is Rose's thesis with Merck's signature on it. The candid half is how Merck bought it — not a marketplace, but one vertically integrated network that owns its sites and sells a single operating model. And look at the mechanism they credit: Sarah Cannon removed the coordinator's work, and we pay for the coordinator's work. Same bottleneck, opposite solution, and Merck bought the other one. Nineteen against seven is now a published bar we have to approach in a network we do not own. The question for Eddie and Rose is whether our buyer is the sponsor who cannot build a captive network — everyone smaller than Merck — and whether the pitch leads with the economics of not owning the sites.

The second part is the Inspector General docket, and it starts with a correction. I have been describing that rulemaking as being about paying physicians for trial coordination work. It is not. All fourteen questions concern remuneration to trial participants — patients. Cost-sharing, travel, lodging, childcare, meals, stipends, payment for time, completion incentives. Nothing about paying a practice for coordination burden. The line to our mechanism is weaker than I have been implying, and I would rather say so than keep an item here on a wrong premise.

What is worth something is who is on the docket, which I pulled this morning rather than just counting. Eight comments. I have been telling you none are from a trade association or a major sponsor, and that remains true — but Massive Bio filed on July thirtieth, and Massive Bio is on our own competitive watch list for this company. Mural Health, a participant-payment platform, filed. Florence Healthcare, which does site software, filed. Then the Oncology Nursing Society, the STARR Coalition, and one more. That is not sponsors and not trade groups; it is precisely 1104health's category, and 1104health is absent. Two questions bear directly on a platform: one asks who should be permitted to provide participant remuneration and enumerates sponsors, investigators, other providers and suppliers; another asks what arrangements necessary to deliver it should be protected. Those are where an intermediary asks not to be written out before the safe harbor gets drafted. Comments close Monday at five p.m. Eastern. Today is the last business day, and a competitor filed three weeks ago.

The rest is short. Yesterday the F-D-A finalized its frequently-asked-questions guidance on developing cell and gene therapy products, covering manufacturing, toxicology and clinical pharmacology — Avi and Hugh should read it before the next preclinical planning cycle rather than during it. On G-P-R fifty-two there is still no news and Nxera still has no named buyer, but one out-of-window item is worth having: a fibromyalgia genetic study across two and a half million people, in Nature Medicine at the end of July, prioritizes our target through its regulation of huntingtin. A large new human-genetic validation outside psychiatry, and one more reason a buyer would move fast. Hemaka should have it.

The Merck and Moderna follow-on is market cap and Keytruda-breadth speculation, no new data, and it still argues the off-the-shelf version is worth more for Clasp and Adventris. Boehringer put a Phase 1 of an unnamed molecule on hold yesterday over an unnamed safety event; Boehringer dropped the option on Nxera's G-P-R fifty-two agonist in December, so I flag it and decline to connect them. Amgen exited its Crohn's collaboration with TScan, which changes nothing for the gut program. Ultragenyx's Sanfilippo decision is confirmed for September nineteenth and CAMP4's analyst day for September twenty-eighth. No fresh mutant-K-R-A-S catalyst for aSKY, no Clasp or Adventris update, nothing from Skylark or Sensorion since Wednesday — and no new Skylark patent publication; I re-ran the assignee and inventor searches this morning and their untranslated-region filing is still the only one, so a connexin filing remains presumably pending. Zero mentions of Blackbird anywhere.

One last correction. Wednesday I told you the splicing layer was untaken. Clinically it holds; on the intellectual property it does not. There is a Broad preprint from last November out of Fei Chen's lab describing a generative model that designs cell-type-specific splicing elements from scratch, and a patent application has been filed. It belongs in the same envelope as the freedom-to-operate work on Skylark's claims, and Fei Chen has founded two companies already, so assume it becomes one.

To close. A human, dose-dependent demonstration that a ubiquitous promoter produces consequences nobody designed — which finally answers the micro-dose argument, because you cannot dose your way out of expressing in the wrong cell, only down to a subtherapeutic level. The approved label in our own indication says restriction works and that a promoter was enough to get there, so our claim moves from address to level. Skylark's catheter turns out to be a bet, not a barrier. And for 1104health, today is the last business day to get onto a record our competitors are already on. I'll see you tomorrow.
