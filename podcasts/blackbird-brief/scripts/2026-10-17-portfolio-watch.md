## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Saturday, October seventeenth. The most important development for the portfolio is that China has drafted a rule that would stop its companies from selling ownership of their cell and gene therapy and s-i-R-N-A platforms abroad, while leaving asset licensing alone. That shifts the value of the two platform types we sit on, SELEXON and NuGate's oligonucleotide chemistry.

On Friday afternoon Beijing time, China's Ministry of Commerce and Ministry of Science and Technology published Announcement number forty-eight. It's a draft revision of the national catalogue of technologies that are prohibited or restricted from export. It covers forty technologies across artificial intelligence, solar and biomedicine. Five of them are biomedical, and the details matter. Three would be prohibited outright. The first is cell and gene therapy process platforms with independent intellectual property. The second is small nucleic acid, meaning s-i-R-N-A, process platforms with independent intellectual property. The third is gain-of-function work on pathogens. The other two would be restricted, meaning they need an export licence: discovery and validation of completely novel targets, for cell and gene therapy and for s-i-R-N-A. The draft defines a completely novel target as one that hasn't been publicly disclosed anywhere in the world.

Three carve-outs keep this from being a blanket ban. First, the platform prohibition applies only to transferring ownership. Out-licensing, outsourced research and contract manufacturing are explicitly outside its scope. Second, licensing deals that don't transfer ownership get facilitation measures, including automatic licensing. Third, single-drug development processes are excluded. So the Chinese asset-licensing machine, the one that has fed so many Western new companies this year, keeps running. Two harder edges cut the other way. The public comment window runs until November fifteenth, but a pre-export registration requirement started the day the draft was published, and it applies to deals that have already started but haven't closed.

Here's what it means for us. This week we said the field is paying for delivery platforms, not delivery routes. Roche's licence of Rona's extrahepatic R-N-A-i asset was one example, and Rona is a Chinese company. Under this draft, that kind of asset deal still goes through. What a Western acquirer can no longer do is buy the Chinese platform itself. Anyone who wants to own an extrahepatic s-i-R-N-A engine or a cell and gene therapy process platform, rather than rent one asset at a time, now has a shorter list of sellers, and a U-S academic platform with clean title moves up that list. Hemaka and Eddie, that's a sentence for the NuGate and Aletira partnering decks: platform ownership is becoming a jurisdiction question, and ours sits in Baltimore. Eddie, there's a practical point too. If any company in the BioVentures pipeline is mid-negotiation with a Chinese counterparty on target discovery or a platform, ask whether the registration has been filed, because pending deals are in scope as of yesterday. One candid caveat. This is a draft, the policy explanations come from a ministry spokesperson rather than the annex, and Beijing has calibrated these lists before. The asset-licensing flow is protected, so don't expect the China discount on in-licensed molecules to disappear. What changes is the price of owning the platform.

Second item, and it's the cleanest template for Aletira we've seen in a while. Estuary Biotherapeutics launched on Friday with one hundred and three million dollars to build A-A-V gene therapies for autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease. That's a ninety-three million dollar Series A led by RA Capital, on top of a ten million dollar seed co-led by 4BIO Capital and G-V. Biogen New Ventures, Deep Track and the National Kidney Foundation's fund are also in. The science comes from Stefan Somlo and Michael Caplan at Yale. Somlo's lab showed in mice that restoring polycystin expression in kidneys that already have cysts reverses the disease, structurally and functionally. Caplan showed that a compact piece of polycystin-one is enough to suppress cysts. That matters because the full gene is far too big to package in an adeno-associated virus. The lead program, E-S-T two-oh-one, delivers full-length P-K-D-two and is meant to enter the clinic in twenty twenty-seven. The second, E-S-T one-oh-one, delivers that polycystin-one fragment and follows in twenty twenty-eight.

Three things in this one map straight onto Aletira. First, the pitch RA Capital bought is local delivery. In their words, a known gene therapy modality paired with focal, kidney-directed delivery, and the company says confinement to the organ is designed to avoid the problems that have hurt systemic A-A-V. That's the same argument the cochlea makes, and the cochlea makes it better. Anthony and Jon, investors are paying nine figures for that argument right now, before any human data. Second, Estuary solved its packaging problem by shrinking the payload to a functional domain. Avi and Hugh, that's the same payload-budget question that sits open in our file on SELEXON's cassette size. The question a partner will ask is how many kilobases the selectivity costs, and what you give up to fit it. Third, there's the timeline. The company was formed in twenty twenty-two, so it's about four years from formation to Series A and about five to first patient, with a management team largely out of Biogen. That's a realistic calibration for an academic gene therapy spinout. One candid caveat: all the efficacy behind this round is in mice, and the release doesn't say how the virus actually gets into the kidney. One sourcing note as well. This was Yale biology built into a California company. It's exactly the kind of out-of-state program our model says we can license and build in Baltimore, and someone else's seed investors found it first.

Third item is for the Clasp file. On Wednesday, Gilead licensed three dual-masked T-cell engagers for solid tumors from Vir Biotechnology. Gilead is paying sixty-five million dollars in cash and buying twenty-five million dollars of Vir stock, with another forty-five million in near-term preclinical milestones and up to one point seven four billion after that. Vir runs discovery on its PRO-XTEN masking technology and then hands development to Gilead. It's Vir's second oncology platform deal this year, after Astellas took its prostate cancer engager. The analyst view from H.C. Wainwright is the honest one: no new clinical evidence and no targets named, so this is strategic and financial validation, not a reason to raise the odds of clinical success. Vir's HER2 masked engager reads out in Phase one before year end.

Here's the read for Clasp. Masking and T-C-R mimicry are two answers to the same problem, which is a T-cell engager that also kills healthy tissue carrying the same target. Masking keeps the engager switched off until it reaches the tumor, so it can go after common surface antigens. Clasp goes after mutant peptides that only exist inside tumor cells, presented on a specific H-L-A type. The fact that ninety million dollars upfront went to a masking platform with no disclosed targets tells Eddie what a specificity platform for T-cell engagers is worth to a large pharma before any data. The uncomfortable half is what masking sells against Clasp. It doesn't need an H-L-A match, so its addressable population isn't cut down by the two-gate problem we've flagged on both Clasp programs. The p53 R one-seventy-five H monotherapy readout is still the moment that decides which side of that trade Clasp sits on. Note the deal shape as well. Vir keeps discovery and sells development, one program at a time. That's the same structure we liked in the Wave and G-S-K deal two weeks ago.

Last item, short, and it's for Yixuan and Avi. The F-D-A sent a warning letter to Eurofins about its contract testing lab in Horsham, Pennsylvania. Eurofins admitted that some analysts had been selectively recording microbiology pour-plate results. A retrospective review traced the inconsistent reporting back to February twenty twenty-five. Inspectors also found cracked and dried-out plates. The agency rejected Eurofins' argument that the results were still valid, which rested on analyst interviews and records the company itself had called unreliable. The F-D-A wrote that a result from a compromised plate, even one within specification, can't be considered valid. The read is simple: the sponsor owns its vendor's data. For any program with release or bioburden testing running through a contract lab, and especially an A-A-V program heading toward an I-N-D, ask the vendor in writing whether any of our samples went through Horsham since February twenty twenty-five. It's a cheap question now and an expensive one in a chemistry and manufacturing review.

That's the watch. Next week, Kyverna presents its full stiff person syndrome dataset at the joint ACTRIMS–ECTRIMS meeting in Toronto from Wednesday, which is the next real data point for Winnow. A-S-H-G runs in Montreal at the same time. Then L-B Pharmaceuticals has its R and D day on the twenty-seventh, the F-D-A rare disease public meeting is on the twenty-eighth, and comments on China's export draft are due November fifteenth. Have a good weekend.
