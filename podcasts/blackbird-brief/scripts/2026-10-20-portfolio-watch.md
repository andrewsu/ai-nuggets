## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Tuesday, October twentieth. The most important development for the portfolio is a single sentence in an analyst note about yesterday's Merck takeout of Silence Therapeutics — a sentence that says, out loud and from the buyer's side, that an oligonucleotide delivery platform is worth approximately nothing once there is a Phase 3 asset sitting on top of it.

Here are the terms. Germany's Merck KGaA agreed Monday to buy Silence Therapeutics, the UK short-interfering-RNA company, at twenty dollars a share. That is an eighty-two percent premium to Silence's thirty-day average of ten dollars and ninety-nine cents. Merck puts the enterprise value at one point one billion dollars and the equity value at roughly one point three billion, with closing expected in the first quarter of twenty twenty-seven. Danny Bar-Zohar, who runs Merck's healthcare business, called it an entry point into hematology.

What they are buying is divesiran, a Phase 3 asset for polycythemia vera. The pathway logic is unusually clean: divesiran silences a gene called T-M-P-R-S-S-six, which raises hepcidin, which starves red-cell production of iron. In the Phase 2 SANRECO trial it produced an eighty-eight percent clinical response rate against nineteen percent on placebo, and it cut therapeutic blood draws from two point one per patient to zero point two through week thirty-six. William Blair had already called that best-in-field. Silence also comes with four cardiometabolic programs, an AstraZeneca partnership, and a company that has been running without a permanent chief executive since December of last year.

Now the sentence. William Blair wrote that the valuation does appear inexpensive considering Merck also gets the broader siRNA platform as well — and then immediately explained the logic, which is that the deal takes execution risk off the table in a competitive space. Read that as an operator. A strategic buyer paid a full, generous price for one de-risked asset, and treated an entire delivery platform as a free option attached to it. Not as a discount, not as a separate line — as change.

That is the Sangamo ratio problem, stated for once by the acquirer rather than by us, and it is the most useful thing on the wire this week for how we frame both NuGate's Series A and Aletira's. We have been carrying this as a caution. It is now a priced transaction. The practical implication is uncomfortable but clear: if the platform is only ever valued as a rounding error on the lead asset, then the lead asset has to carry the round, and every dollar and month we spend broadening a platform before the lead asset is de-risked is being spent on something a strategic buyer has just told us they will not pay for.

I want to connect that to the Arrowhead lead we ran on the fifteenth, because the two together make a shape. That episode was about route — a subcutaneous oligonucleotide reaching the brain, and what it does to the comparator our intrathecal program will be measured against. This one is about structure: who pays for the delivery technology that makes the route possible. The answer, at least from this buyer, is nobody, once there is an asset. Public markets will fund delivery platforms early. Acquirers will not fund them late. For NuGate that argues for spending the next raise narrowing to the lead indication and the pre-IND package rather than broadening the platform story — and for Aletira, whose SELEXON cassette is explicitly a vector-agnostic bolt-on, it argues that the cassette's value has to be demonstrated through the hearing program, not alongside it.

Second item, and it is a hard one to read if you work on Aletira. EyePoint reported Monday that duravyu, its intravitreal tyrosine kinase inhibitor, failed a second pivotal trial in wet age-related macular degeneration. In the Phase 3 Lucia study it missed statistical non-inferiority to Eylea on mean change in best corrected visual acuity — the same primary endpoint it missed in the Lugano trial back in August. EyePoint is ending both pivotal trials early without completing the second year, cutting about fifty-three percent of its workforce, which on a base of two hundred and fourteen people is roughly a hundred and thirteen jobs, and taking about nine million dollars in separation charges. The stock opened down more than eighty percent, at fifty-five cents. The company held a hundred and eighty and a half million dollars at the end of June, down from two hundred and twenty-two and a half million three months earlier, and is pivoting to diabetic macular edema, where two pivotal readouts land this quarter.

Here is the detail that matters to us. Duravyu did hit key secondary endpoints, including a superior reduction in treatment burden. It genuinely reduced the number of injections a patient needs. The market valued that at negative eighty percent.

We already carry the retinal efficacy bar from the Merck remigromig read — build the case against eleven point eight letters, not against natural history. This is the same lesson delivered more brutally, and it generalizes past the retina. In a sensory organ, durability is a secondary endpoint, and a secondary endpoint is worth nothing at all until the primary is matched. For Aletira, whose entire proposition in any sensory-organ indication is a single administration, that is the specific trap to avoid walking into. Dosing convenience is not the product. It is what we get to charge for after the function endpoint clears. The pre-IND conversation we are already having about a validated functional hearing scale is exactly the right conversation, and this week is the clearest argument yet for why it comes first.

Third, and it is the regulatory companion to that point. Disc Medicine reported Monday that its Phase 3 APOLLO trial of bitopertin in erythropoietic protoporphyria hit all primary and secondary endpoints — the primary being time spent in sunlight, which is a direct, observable clinical measure. Safety was clean: dizziness in half the treatment arm, mild to moderate and transient, and no discontinuations at all over six months, which chief executive John Quisel noted is remarkable for a study that long. Disc will resubmit by the end of this year, this time for traditional approval, with a decision expected around the middle of twenty twenty-seven. We flagged this one as a watch item yesterday with no readout attached; the readout has now landed.

The history is the useful part. The FDA rejected bitopertin in February. The complete response letter did not say the drug does not work. It said the surrogate the company had used — percent change in whole-blood metal-free protoporphyrin nine — was relatively modest, and that it is unknown whether such a magnitude of biomarker effect is reasonably likely to predict clinical benefit. And bitopertin was among the very first recipients of the Commissioner's National Priority Voucher, which compresses review from ten or twelve months down to one or two. So: a national-priority voucher, a mechanistically obvious biomarker on a disease where the biology is not in dispute, and a rejection anyway. The remedy, agreed with the agency in June, was a trial with an endpoint a reviewer can see without being persuaded.

The read for Avi and the Aletira pre-IND team is direct. Acceleration instruments buy review time. They do not buy an endpoint. If the agency will not accept the biomarker, no designation rescues it, and the voucher is spent. Our standing ask — a natural-history external control with a version lock, a pre-specified missing-data rule, and a functional scale somebody has actually validated — is this identical lesson taken one stage earlier and several hundred million dollars more cheaply.

Fourth, and this one is for the Clasp diligence file. Genmab laid out its readout slate this quarter, and inside it are two transferable frames. The context is that Genmab is facing what its own chief medical officer calls arguably the largest patent cliff any company in this industry has faced — Darzalex royalties are ninety percent of sales, the US patents expire in twenty twenty-nine, and H.C. Wainwright models those revenues falling from about three point three billion dollars in twenty twenty-eight to roughly zero by twenty thirty-two.

The first frame is about eligible fraction. Genmab's folate-receptor-alpha-directed antibody-drug conjugate showed activity in Phase 1/2 in patients with low or even absent receptor expression. The analyst read is explicit: securing a label across all expression levels would double the addressable population relative to AbbVie's competing conjugate, and the labeling terms are the single variable that determines the slope of the sales ramp. That is Clasp's central commercial question stated by somebody else's analyst. CLSP-1025 does not have one eligibility gate, it has two — the mutation and the HLA allele — and the eligible fraction those two gates leave is the number that sets the ramp. Worth making sure that number, and the plan to widen it, is in the GUARDIAN-101 narrative and not just in the appendix.

The second frame is a diligence chain worth stealing verbatim. On their head-and-neck bispecific, where Phase 2 showed a sixty-three percent response rate against nineteen percent for Keytruda in the comparable frontline setting, the chief medical officer laid out why response rate should predict the endpoints that matter: if the response-rate difference is dramatic and the durability is good, it almost has to produce a progression-free survival difference, and that in turn has to produce separation in the overall survival curves — because if it does not, what you are looking at is a safety liability. That is a clean, falsifiable way to interrogate any early efficacy signal, including ours. And there is a third thing worth noting from the same interview, on the oncology-surface-target theme: once a patient has progressed on one topoisomerase-one conjugate they are unlikely to benefit from another with the same payload, which is why Genmab has stopped pre-announcing studies and now discloses them only when the first patient is dosed. Payload class is becoming a one-shot resource, and that changes how a surface target should be valued.

Last, briefly, and this one is for Avi and for Biolinco. There was a feature yesterday on multispecific drug design in which the field named its own bottleneck out loud. Adimab's Eric Krauland put the combinatorics plainly: hundreds of format and affinity combinations to consider for a bispecific, potentially thousands for a trispecific, and his group now steers partners toward full-Fab architectures rather than single-chain variable fragments specifically to keep manufacturing tractable. Invenra's Roland Green said trispecifics today sit roughly where bispecifics were five years ago, and that the industry needs some success stories before large-scale investment follows. Every constraint anyone named was a developability constraint — heavy-chain mispairing and the heterogeneity it creates, aggregation, hydrophobicity, stickiness, expressability.

Two reads. For Biolinco, the field has just published, in public and in its own words, the problem statement that company exists to solve: the bottleneck has moved from candidate discovery to candidate selection, and the selection criterion is manufacturability. That is the reframing for the next Hopkins tech-ventures conversation and for any pharma partner call — not "barcoded antibodies," but "the trispecific selection problem." For Clasp, note the detail that Adimab maintains a panel of CD3 antibodies spanning a wide range of affinities. The CD3 arm is a dial these groups fully expect to tune, so our diligence on any engager should ask what position on that dial was chosen and on what evidence. Candid limit on the whole item: this is a vendor roundtable, not data. Keep the vocabulary of the bottleneck, not any particular claim inside it.

That is the watch. Looking ahead, ESMO opens in Madrid on Friday and runs through the twenty-seventh, and the FDA's rare disease framework public meeting is on the twenty-eighth — relevant to the third item, and worth someone from the Aletira team dialing in. Thanks, team. Back tomorrow.
