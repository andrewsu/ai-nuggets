## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Sourcing Radar for Sunday, August second, and this week's lead is a genuinely fresh, full-text-readable oncology preprint out of U-M-B's Greenebaum Cancer Center — a biomarker-defined immunotherapy strategy for the single worst subtype of acute myeloid leukemia. It comes with a real commercial catch that I want to be honest about up front, because it changes what kind of deal this is.

Let me set the week first. The corresponding-author sweep across Johns Hopkins, U-M-B, and Lieber for the past seven days was, once again, mostly thin — a lot of methods and tool papers, basic neuroscience, and clinical epidemiology, which I'll come back to at the end. But one preprint stood out, I could read it cover to cover, and it lands squarely in an area Blackbird already cares about: epigenetic and immune reprogramming of cancer.

The paper is from Feyruz Rassool's group at the University of Maryland School of Medicine, and here's the science in plain terms. Roughly one in ten new acute myeloid leukemias, and a quarter of therapy-related ones, carry a mutation in p53 — and those patients do terribly. Chemotherapy fails, transplant fails, and even the venetoclax-plus-decitabine combination that helped the rest of A-M-L barely moves the needle in the p53-mutant group. There is no established immunotherapy for them. So this is a real, well-defined unmet need with a clean molecular label on it.

The insight is a vulnerability hiding inside the mutation. STING is the cell's innate alarm system — it senses stray DNA in the cytoplasm and fires an interferon response that recruits T cells. Rassool's team found that when you lose p53, leukemia cells become more sensitive to a STING agonist, not less. They used a next-generation STING agonist called C-92 — and the drug matters here, so remember it — and in p53-mutant cells it drove much stronger STING activation and cytokine release than in p53-normal cells. Then they layered on decitabine, the generic DNA-methyltransferase inhibitor already approved in A-M-L and myelodysplastic syndrome. The combination was synergistic: decitabine unsilences repetitive elements in the genome, the cell reads that as a viral infection, and STING is already primed to respond. And the killing mechanism is the interesting part — it isn't ordinary apoptosis. In the p53-mutant setting the cells die by necroptosis, a violently inflammatory form of cell death that spills danger signals and turns a cold tumor hot. In humanized mice carrying a human immune system, the combination cut leukemia burden and pulled cytotoxic T cells into the tumor, with no weight loss. So the through-line is: a p53 mutation, which everywhere else is bad news, becomes the biomarker that says this immune-activating combination will work.

Now the honest "so what for Blackbird," because the IP story is not the clean composition-of-matter setup we look for. Two of the three moving parts are not ownable by the university. Decitabine is a generic. And C-92, the star STING agonist, is not a Maryland molecule — it belongs to Curadev Pharma, and the academic team acknowledges Curadev donated the compound. The competing-interest statement declares no conflicts, which tells me U-M-B has not disclosed a filed patent on this. So what is actually ownable here is the use and the biomarker: the method of treating p53-mutant A-M-L, and other p53-mutant cancers, with a STING agonist plus a hypomethylating agent, and the p53-loss-plus-necroptosis mechanism as a patient-selection story. That is a real, licensable asset class — method-of-use and companion-biomarker IP — but it is a partnering and co-development play with whoever owns the STING agonist, not a founding-a-company-around-a-new-molecule play. Think of it the way we thought about the CoREST program a couple weeks back: the differentiating biology sits at Hopkins or Maryland, but the composition of matter sits somewhere else.

The competitive context actually strengthens the science even as it complicates the deal. STING agonists have a rough clinical history — the first generation needed injection directly into the tumor and largely targeted mouse STING, and they underwhelmed. The whole field is now betting on next-generation, human-specific, intravenously dosed agonists like Curadev's, and the missing ingredient has been patient selection — knowing who will respond. A p53-mutant biomarker in a leukemia with no immunotherapy option is exactly the kind of stratification that could rescue the class in one indication. And note the co-authors: Stephen Baylin and Michael Topper, the Johns Hopkins epigenetics-and-viral-mimicry school, are on this paper alongside the Maryland leukemia group. That is precisely the cross-town Hopkins-plus-Maryland axis, funded here through a National Cancer Institute research-excellence grant and Maryland's own cigarette-restitution fund, that our co-investment surface is built to catch.

So my read is: watch-and-partner, not found-and-lead — but worth a real conversation. For Eddie: the question for Rassool and Maria Baer is whether U-M Ventures intends to file on the method-and-biomarker, and whether the natural home is a co-development deal with Curadev rather than a standalone newco — is Curadev already running C-92 into A-M-L, or is this an open lane? For Avi and Yixuan: pressure-test what is genuinely ownable and defensible around a use-and-biomarker claim when you don't control the molecule, and map freedom-to-operate against Curadev's composition estate. Lower dealability than the Suk conjugate we led with last week, but higher scientific conviction on the biology, and a strong relationship to have inside the epigenetic-immunotherapy space.

Quickly, what else crossed the wire and why none of it is a lead. Chulan Kwon's lab at Hopkins posted a nice mechanistic preprint on a splicing factor, S-F-3-B-2, controlling calcium handling in heart muscle cells — real target biology, but no asset and no near-term company. The rest of the Hopkins output this week was infrastructure: a very-high-channel Neuropixels brain-recording probe, a pangenome genome-assembly patching tool, a real-time A-I system for M-R-I quality — all genuinely good, none of them a therapeutic we can build around. And Bryan Krantz at U-M-B put out a perspective on the metabolic gating of human cognition — thought-provoking, but a viewpoint, not a program.

That's the radar. One biomarker-defined immunotherapy lead out of Maryland that's a partnering conversation more than a spin-out, anchored to the Hopkins epigenetics group we want to be close to — and an otherwise quiet preprint week that I'm not going to inflate.
