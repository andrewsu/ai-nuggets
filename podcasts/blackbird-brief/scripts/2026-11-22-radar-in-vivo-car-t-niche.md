## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Sourcing Radar for Sunday, November twenty-second. Two picks this week, both read in full. The lead is a Hopkins platform with a Hopkins Technology Ventures patent application already filed, and it is the best-timed piece of academic I-P I have seen all quarter: an injectable gel that makes C-A-R-T cells inside the patient, with no cells taken out, posted three days after Pfizer paid three hundred and thirty million dollars in cash for preclinical in vivo C-A-R-T programs. The second pick is a target that does nothing to a leukemia cell in a dish and everything to it in front of a T cell, which makes it a different kind of asset than it first appears.

Start with the lead.

It went up on bioRxiv on Wednesday from Hai-Quan Mao's lab at the Institute for NanoBioTechnology, with Kailei Goodier and Yining Zhu as joint first authors, and the co-author list is the thing to read first. Jonathan Schneck from the Institute for Cell Engineering and Pathology. Scott Wilson and Joshua Doloff from biomedical engineering. And Maximilian Konig, from rheumatology and from the Hopkins Center for Autoimmunity and Immuno-Oncology — the center we funded. Mao is also Vice Provost for Research at Hopkins and directs the Translational Therapeutic and Regenerative Engineering Center. This is not a one-lab paper.

Here is what they built. They call it VINCIT, a virus-incorporated niche for cellular immunotherapy. Physically it is a hyaluronic acid hydrogel with electrospun polycaprolactone nanofibers covalently integrated into it — a material Mao's group has been developing for years as a soft-tissue matrix mimic, with a storage modulus around three hundred pascals and pores around fifty microns. You mix viral vector into it and inject it under the skin. That is the entire procedure.

What happens next is the interesting part, and the paper is careful to show it happens in two separate steps that both have to work.

Step one is confinement. Give the same labeled A-A-V intravenously and it goes to liver and kidney, with about seventy percent of the transduction signal in the liver. Inject the free virus subcutaneously and about sixty-eight percent stays at the injection site with real spillover — twelve percent liver, nine percent kidney. Put it in the composite and more than eighty-five percent of the transduction signal stays at the injection site, with over sixty percent of the physical virus still there at both fifteen and thirty days, and the virus is still functionally active that whole time.

Step two is the one that matters, and it is where the paper earns its keep. Confinement alone does not get you a C-A-R-T cell. Free subcutaneous virus transduces local tissue perfectly well — in mice mostly a skin muscle layer — and only a tiny fraction of what it transduces is a T cell. So localizing exposure is necessary and not remotely sufficient. The composite does the second thing: it recruits host immune cells into itself. Compared against the same virus in plain hyaluronic acid gel without the nanofibers, the full composite pulled in about four times as many cells by day thirty, produced about four times as many transduced cells overall, and — this is the number — roughly fifteen times as many transduced C-D-three positive T cells. Same virus, same dose, same route. The difference is the material.

And those cells leave. By day thirty, transduced T cells were about one and a half percent of the spleen in treated animals. Loaded with an anti-human C-D-nineteen C-A-R, the resulting cells showed antigen-specific T-N-F alpha and granzyme B production and roughly fifty times more interferon-gamma-secreting cells than control, about four and a half times more than the same virus given intravenously. With a mouse C-D-nineteen C-A-R in a lentivirus, they drove B cells in peripheral blood down to about eight percent by day twenty — a sixty-five percent reduction — while the same lentivirus given intravenously did essentially nothing, sitting at twenty-two percent, indistinguishable from controls.

Then the efficacy experiment. Humanized mice, C-D-nineteen-positive Daudi lymphoma, one subcutaneous dose on day seven. Median survival: thirty-two days untreated, thirty-five days with the empty material, twenty-five days with the C-A-R virus given intravenously, and one hundred and ten days with the composite. No detectable elevation in I-L six, T-N-F alpha, I-L ten, I-L twelve or interferon gamma three days after dosing. No overt histopathology at four weeks.

The mechanism section is what turns this from a device into a thesis. They asked whether the gel is just a virus depot that happens to sit near T cells. It is not. When they reconstituted humanized mice with T cells only, or T cells plus B cells, or T cells plus monocytes, instead of whole P-B-M-C-s, total cell recruitment into the scaffold was the same — but human T cell recruitment collapsed, and C-A-R-T generation collapsed with it. And in immunocompetent mice, depleting neutrophils — the first responders to any injected biomaterial — reduced infiltration of macrophages, dendritic cells, B cells and T cells alike, and profoundly impaired C-A-R-T generation. So the material is not a reservoir. It is a recruitment organ, and it needs an innate wave to build the environment the T cells come into.

Three reads.

The first is for Eddie, and it is about timing. On Wednesday Pfizer agreed to pay Starna three hundred and thirty million dollars upfront for three preclinical in vivo C-A-R-T programs, and we read that here as a platform deal in an asset deal's suit. On Monday Seawolf took a hundred million plus a one-point-two-billion Sanofi pact for redosable non-viral delivery. Every one of those vehicles makes an enrichment claim about where the virus or particle goes. This paper makes a categorically different claim: it does not try to make the vector smarter, it changes where the T cells *are*. And it demonstrates, in its own control arm, that making the vector land in the right place is insufficient without the recruitment. That is a genuinely differentiated position in the most actively financed space in the sector, and the patent application is already filed and managed through Hopkins Technology Ventures with Mao, Goodier and Zhu as co-inventors. Esther, this is the call to make this week — what the claims cover, whether they are drawn on the composite, the method, or the combination, and whether anyone has approached J-H-T-V.

The second read is for Avi and Virginia, and it is the harder-nosed one. The vectors here were not engineered for T cell selectivity at all. The authors say so and list it first among their limitations: multiple immune populations enter the niche and all of them see the vector, which is why the transduced-cell composition tracks the recruited-cell composition. So the specificity story is spatial, not cellular — and spatial specificity is exactly the kind that gets harder as you scale from a mouse flank to a human. Note what that implies for us, though: a composite that confines exposure and a vector that targets T cells are complementary, not competing. Our own file already carries Mao's anionic lipid-nanoparticle chemistry for splenic T cell delivery from July. A localized niche plus a T-cell-tropic vector is a combination that one lab at Hopkins can assemble from parts it already owns, and that is a more defensible platform story than either piece alone.

The third read is the candid one. The humanized mouse model is a weak immunological approximation and the authors are unusually honest about it — N-S-G mice have impaired macrophage and dendritic cell function, so biomaterial responses in that background are blunted, there is no human blood-cell production to replenish short-lived populations, and the reconstitution experiments establish that non-T cells are required without quantifying what each one contributes. C-A-R expression was never tracked over time, only measured at the end, which for an integrating lentivirus leaves both persistence and genomic safety open. And a subcutaneous depot that recruits immune cells and holds integrating virus for a month is a regulatory conversation, not just a scientific one. The honest framing is that this is an excellent platform claim with filed I-P, a real differentiated mechanism and no durability data — which is the stage where we are supposed to be in the room, not the stage where we are supposed to be convinced.

Second pick. Shorter, and it is about what kind of asset a target actually is.

Posted Friday from Petya Apostolova in Basel with Erika Pearce at the Bloomberg-Kimmel Institute for Cancer Immunotherapy as co-corresponding author. The question is what happens metabolically to the leukemia cells that survive chemotherapy — the reservoir that produces relapse. Across mouse and human acute myeloid leukemia models and multiple different chemotherapy agents, exactly two metabolites were universally elevated in the persisting cells: glutathione, which everybody already understands as an antioxidant and detox story, and creatine, which nobody had explained. Isotope tracing and C-R-I-S-P-R knockouts show the creatine is coming in through a single transporter, S-L-C-six-A-eight, rather than being made inside the cell.

Now the result that makes it interesting. Knock out that transporter and the leukemia proliferates normally, resists chemotherapy normally, migrates normally. In a dish it is a nothing. Put the same cells under allogeneic T cell pressure in three independent models and the knockout gets eradicated. The transporter is not keeping the cancer alive. It is keeping the cancer invisible.

The mechanism is specific and it is not the one you would guess. Creatine's textbook job is energy buffering through phosphocreatine, so they deleted creatine kinase B. Phosphocreatine production stopped, and immune clearance did not change at all. So this is not about energy. What losing the transporter actually does is release the brake on interferon-gamma signaling: more S-T-A-T-one phosphorylation downstream of the receptor, more chromatin accessibility at the C-D-forty and C-I-I-T-A loci, and induction of antigen-presentation and co-stimulatory machinery — M-H-C class two, C-D-forty, C-D-eighty. The leukemia cell imports creatine and becomes less responsive to the cytokine that would otherwise force it to show itself.

Hemaka, Avi — three things to take from this.

The first is a general lesson about target classification that applies to our own pipeline. S-L-C-six-A-eight would be scored as a weak target by any conventional in vitro cascade — knock it out, nothing happens. It is only a target in the presence of a functioning immune system, and only in cells that have already survived chemotherapy. If our early-stage oncology screens run proliferation and viability readouts without an immune-pressure arm, we will systematically throw away the entire class of targets whose only phenotype is immune evasion. That is a cheap change to a diligence checklist and it would have caught this one.

The second is about the asset. There is already an S-L-C-six-A-eight inhibitor in clinical development, R-G-X two-oh-two, built and taken into trials for colorectal cancer on an anti-proliferative premise. This paper says that in leukemia the premise is wrong and the benefit would come from immune sensitization — which means the right trial is a combination after allogeneic transplant or alongside donor lymphocyte infusion, not a monotherapy. Eddie, that is a shape our divested-asset screen should recognize by now: a clinical-stage molecule whose owner may be running the wrong hypothesis, where the new hypothesis arrives from academia with no patent attached to it. The compound is somebody else's. The use is not necessarily spoken for.

The third connects to the portfolio. We have an open question in the file from September about whether modulating a degradation pathway changes how much of a mutant peptide gets presented on the surface, because Clasp's potency scales with peptide-H-L-A copy number. This is a second instance of the same structure — a metabolic lever that moves antigen-presentation machinery. The class is worth naming explicitly: any therapeutic whose potency depends on how much target is displayed has a combination partner problem, and the partners are turning up in metabolism papers rather than in oncology pipelines.

Be clear about the limits, because they are not small. The molecular link between the transporter and S-T-A-T-one phosphorylation is undefined — they show the consequence, not the connection. Everything immunological was done in the allogeneic transplant setting; checkpoint blockade, bispecifics and C-A-R-T were not tested. There is no in vivo pharmacology at all — the inhibitor was never dosed in these models. And the obvious liability is named by the authors: effector T cells themselves need creatine uptake to kill, and dietary creatine supplementation made survival worse in two models, so the therapeutic window between disarming the leukemia and disarming the T cell is unmeasured. On chain of title, the lead is Basel and Max Planck with Hopkins as the senior co-corresponding lab, funded through Swiss and German agencies alongside the Bloomberg-Kimmel Institute and a Hopkins Bloomberg Distinguished Professorship, and there is no patent declared. This is landscape and diligence intelligence, not something to license.

That is the radar. A Hopkins injectable composite that generates C-A-R-T cells inside the body by recruiting the T cells to the virus instead of sending the virus to the T cells, with filed J-H-T-V I-P, a hundred-and-ten-day median survival against thirty-two, and an explicit demonstration that confinement without recruitment does nothing — in the single hottest financing lane in the sector. And a transporter in leukemia that is invisible to every in vitro assay we would normally run and decisive the moment a T cell is in the room. Today's Portfolio Watch is in your feed alongside this, and its lead is a randomized lupus cell-therapy trial where the gap between a missed endpoint and a sixty-eight percent response rate turned out to be the steroid rule rather than the drug. Have a good Sunday.
