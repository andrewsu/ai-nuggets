Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Friday, August twenty-eighth. Yesterday the trade press picked a fight with Eli Lilly over something the company did quietly months ago — it stopped publishing its Phase 1 pipeline. That sounds like a transparency story. It is not. It is a direct hit on the diligence discipline we adopted twenty-four hours ago, and the program it hides belongs to Aletira's competitor.

Go look at the page yourself. Phase 3 is there, with names. Phase 2 is there, with names. Then you reach the Phase 1 heading, and underneath it is one sentence: Phase 1 projects are no longer disclosed. The page is marked current as of August fifth. Now hold that against Wednesday, when the Aurora post-mortem produced one instruction for every venture partner here — for each active program, name the specific well-capitalized group most likely to preempt you, and put a date on when they'd have to move. One day later, the largest single source of that information for one of our programs went dark.

Here is why it lands on Aletira. Lilly is the large-cap in inner-ear genetic medicine. It bought Akouos in October of twenty-twenty-two for four hundred eighty-seven million dollars, which brought in the otoferlin gene therapy. In January it signed a deal worth more than one point one two billion dollars with Seamless Therapeutics for programmed recombinases — enzymes that cut and paste whole stretches of DNA — aimed at hearing-loss mutations. Coverage of that deal described a portfolio spanning otoferlin, Usher syndrome type three-A, an anti-V-E-G-F program in vestibular schwannoma, and G-J-B-two. That last one is Aletira's indication. It is Skylark's indication. It is the most common genetic cause of deafness.

Now look at what Lilly publishes today. Exactly one hearing entry on the whole page: otoferlin gene therapy, Phase 2. G-J-B-two is not there. Usher three-A is not there. Vestibular schwannoma is not there. Nothing from the Seamless collaboration is there. Not because any of it was killed — because all of it sits below Phase 2, and below Phase 2 no longer exists in public.

The workaround is the actionable part, and it works. The trial registry still shows what the pipeline chart hides. Clinicaltrials-dot-gov lists a recruiting Phase 1/2 anti-V-E-G-F gene therapy trial in vestibular schwannoma that Lilly has run since January of twenty-twenty-five, plus a long-term follow-up study on that same vector opened this past March. Neither appears on the pipeline page. And there is no registered G-J-B-two or Usher trial at all, which tells you something genuinely useful: as of today, Lilly's G-J-B-two work is still preclinical. When a program does start, it will surface in the registry — trial registration being a legal obligation rather than a communications choice — well before it appears anywhere a competitor's marketing team controls.

So, Geoff, Hugh, Avi, Esther: move this watch off pipeline pages and onto what companies are compelled to file. Scheduled sponsor-level registry queries on Lilly and the inner ear, patent application publications out of the Seamless collaboration, otolaryngology abstract lists. And note the asymmetry underneath it, because it runs against us permanently. Lilly can go dark; a university program cannot. Everything we source from Hopkins or U-M-B or the Lieber Institute arrives through a published paper, so the moment a target becomes interesting to us it is visible to everyone. The only defense is the clock — how fast a paper becomes a filed provisional and a signed option.

Second item, and it belongs to the Hopkins gut program. AstraZeneca and Amgen reported the Phase 3 CROSSING trial of Tezspire in eosinophilic esophagitis. Tezspire is an antibody against T-S-L-P, the alarm signal epithelial tissue sends at the top of several inflammatory cascades rather than a downstream mediator. Three hundred sixty-eight patients, randomized across two doses and placebo, injected every four weeks on top of existing therapy. Both co-primary endpoints at week twenty-four — tissue remission on biopsy, and a patient-reported swallowing-difficulty score — hit, held through week fifty-two, and every key secondary hit too. That would be a third indication in a disease affecting more than four hundred seventy thousand Americans, where the only approved biologic today is Dupixent.

Now pair that with Tuesday. On Tuesday an anti-T-L-one-A antibody cleared statistical significance in rheumatoid arthritis and still failed its sponsor's own bar. On Thursday an anti-T-S-L-P antibody wins cleanly in the esophagus. The pattern is that in epithelial and gut-compartment disease, systemic injected biologics are winning, and winning on raw potency. That is the honest read for Barbara Slusher's gut-restricted G-C-P-two inhibitor, and it sharpens Wednesday's repositioning rather than contradicting it. An oral gut-restricted small molecule does not win a potency contest against a monoclonal antibody. It wins where a monthly injection is the wrong instrument — earlier disease, step therapy ahead of a biologic, adolescent maintenance, and layering on top. And CROSSING deliberately allowed patients to stay on proton pump inhibitors and swallowed steroids, so the field already assumes stacking. Matt, Avi: write that target product profile against Dupixent and Tezspire by name, with route, tissue restriction and systemic immune sparing as the differentiating column. Not effect size.

Third, day two on the pancreatic cancer approval, because the sell-side changed an answer I gave you Wednesday. Truist modeled two point three billion dollars by twenty-twenty-eight if the drug moves into first line and called Revolution the next oncology titan. But the useful sentence came from Leerink: no black box warning, and yet the dermatologic, gastrointestinal and pulmonary warnings, they wrote, leave room for differentiated pan-RAS and pan-K-RAS approaches. On Wednesday I said the bar for a novel immunologic modality was now "what does it add that the pill cannot do." The analysts answered in the negative space. The opening is not potency, it is tolerability. Eighty-six percent rash, sixty-three percent diarrhea, fifty-seven percent stomatitis is what a hazard ratio of zero point four costs, and an engager or a vaccine with real activity and no standing dermatology referral has a story. That also softens Wednesday's eligible-fraction problem: a two-gate biomarker funnel is survivable if what sits behind it is a materially cleaner drug. Hemaka, Anthony, Virginia — keep building the eligible-fraction row, and add a tolerability row beside it. And notice that both companies Leerink named as beneficiaries are small molecules. Nobody is pricing the immunologic column at all, which is either the whitespace or the warning, and it is the room Clasp, Adventris and aSKY are pitching into.

Fourth, a live calendar item I don't think anyone in our position is watching. The F-D-A's Expedited I-N-D pilot came into focus this week; comments closed Monday. It doesn't speed up the thirty-day review — it rebuilds what happens before. A sponsor pairs with what the agency calls a Qualified Research Institution, meaning an academic medical center, a contract research organization, or something comparable, which guides the manufacturing, nonclinical, clinical and regulatory work and lets those pieces be reviewed on a rolling basis before the application is ever filed. The bigger saving is at the back end: an institution that owns its own ethics board and trial sites can run ethics review, contracting and site activation in parallel with the agency's review, so clearance to proceed and an activated site arrive the same day instead of six months apart. The agency says it wants a deliberately diverse set of sponsors, with specific interest in rare disease, oncology, and cell and gene therapy. Applications for pairs open next month; selection and launch in the fourth quarter.

Read that preferred sponsor list again and then look around this room. Small companies, rare disease, cell and gene therapy is a description of the BioHub. A Qualified Research Institution is, almost word for word, a description of Johns Hopkins. We sit between the two, which is a position no competing seed fund can buy. Matt, Maisha, Esther: find out this week whether Hopkins Technology Ventures or the clinical research office is preparing an application, and if not, whether we raise it. One caution — the unresolved issue in the public comments is conflicts of interest, and an institution advising a sponsor in which it also holds equity through its own tech transfer office is exactly the structure we create every time we spin something out. If we want in, have that answer written before someone asks for it.

Last, briefly, with my skepticism attached. Redesign Health, the largest venture studio in healthcare with more than sixty companies launched, publicly rebranded yesterday from "studio" to "firm," arguing that artificial intelligence has made founder-led building fast enough that doing the building for people is worth less than it was. That is a health-tech conclusion and I would not import it here — what A-I collapsed is the cost of forming a software company, not a tox package, a G-M-P batch, or the twelve months it takes to negotiate a university license. But one thing transfers. Faced with a shrinking build-moat, the sector's largest studio moved up the stack toward capital and relationships, and we already hold that hedge in BioVentures. Worth knowing our own answer before someone asks for it.

CAMP4 unchanged since the August thirteenth quarter. Move the Aletira watch onto the trial registry, and find out this week where Hopkins stands on the Qualified Research Institution pilot. I'll see you tomorrow.
