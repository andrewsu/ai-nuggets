Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Sunday, November fifteenth. Today's most important development for the portfolio: the European regulator has backed an anti-CD20 antibody as an add-on in systemic lupus, and buried in Roche's own announcement is the sentence that should worry us more than the approval does — Roche is already running a CD20-by-CD3 T-cell engager in the same disease. Behind that, an allogeneic dual-target C-A-R T cell reports twelve-month drug-free remission in refractory lupus. A new preprint beats DELFI's method on DELFI's own test set, with DELFI's co-founder as an author. Two cardiac fibrosis results land in the same week, and between them they tell you what the program needs. And Gallaudet University has formally stated its institutional position on gene therapy for deafness, naming our target.

Start with lupus, because two of today's five items are in it and they bracket the same question.

On Thursday the European Medicines Agency's human medicines committee recommended approval of Gazyva — obinutuzumab — as an add-on therapy for adults with moderate-to-severe, autoantibody-positive systemic lupus, on top of standard therapy. The F-D-A accepted Roche's supplemental filing earlier this year. The data come from the ALLEGORY trial, about three hundred patients randomized one-to-one for fifty-two weeks. At one year, seventy-six point seven percent of treated patients hit a four-point improvement on the lupus responder index against fifty-three point five percent on placebo. Remission by the DORIS definition more than doubled, thirty-three point eight percent against thirteen point eight. Low disease activity, fifty-seven point six against twenty-five. Superiority on every key secondary including sustained steroid reduction and time to first flare. Safety consistent with a drug that has been on the market in oncology for years.

Eddie, Jon — here is the read, and it is not the obvious one. The obvious read is that an old, cheap, glyco-engineered anti-CD20 just took a chunk of the lupus market and raised the bar for everything behind it. That is true. The sharper read is in Roche's own pipeline sentence: they say Lunsumio — mosunetuzumab, their first-in-class CD20-by-CD3 bispecific T-cell engager — is being investigated in lupus patients with and without nephritis. So Roche's strategy is explicit. Take the antibody to approval, establish the depletion thesis in the disease, then move the engager in behind it with the commercial infrastructure already built. That is the same sequencing logic every large company will now copy, and it means by the time a Winnow engager reaches a partnering conversation, the person across the table may already own both a marketed depleting antibody and a clinical engager in the same indication. Our answer cannot be "we deplete too." It has to be the selectivity claim, in one sentence, with data — which is exactly where we landed after A-C-R last weekend.

Which brings me to item two, and it sharpens the same point from the other side. At A-C-R Convergence last weekend ImvivaBio presented twelve-month follow-up on CTA313, an allogeneic C-A-R T cell targeting both CD19 and B-C-M-A — the second target is there to clear long-lived plasma cells, which CD19-only products leave behind making autoantibodies.

The numbers, and they are small but they are deep. In a pooled analysis of eight evaluable patients at month twelve, seven were in DORIS remission — eighty-eight percent. In the broader pooled phase one analysis of fourteen heavily pretreated refractory lupus patients with a median of five prior therapies, B cells were gone by day seven, anti-double-stranded-DNA antibodies were undetectable by month three, and at month six ninety-one percent had a responder-index response, seventy-three percent were in DORIS remission, and thirteen of fourteen were off immunosuppressants. A separate sixteen-patient lupus nephritis study reported ninety-four percent responder-index response and forty-four percent complete renal response. The company also showed a cross-trial comparison — not head-to-head, interpret accordingly — where eighty percent of dual-target patients were in remission at twelve months against twenty percent for their single-target CD19 product.

The safety, which is where the argument actually lives. Cytokine release syndrome in thirty-six percent, all grade one. No neurotoxicity, no graft-versus-host disease. But grade three or higher neutropenia in seventy-one percent, thrombocytopenia in twenty-one, infections in twenty-one. And hypogammaglobulinemia in eighty-five percent of dual-target patients against twelve percent with CD19 alone — that is the cost of killing the plasma cells, stated plainly in their own data.

Virginia, Avi — this is the clearest version yet of the trade we have been circling. Depth of remission is bought with depth of depletion, and depth of depletion is paid for in infections and immunoglobulin replacement. An off-the-shelf product with no apheresis takes the access argument away from autologous C-A-R T, which is the argument Kyverna just told us they cannot fund. What it does not take away is the immune-collateral argument. If Winnow's selective T-cell-directed approach can show a comparable remission depth without flattening humoral immunity, that is the differentiation, and the comparator to beat is now an eighty-five percent hypogammaglobulinemia rate.

Item three is for DELFI, and it is an uncomfortable one.

A preprint posted Monday from Wei Sun's group at Fred Hutch introduces a method called mFrag. The idea is simple and, annoyingly, free. Fragmentomics — DELFI's approach — reads cell-free DNA fragment lengths and end motifs from shallow whole-genome sequencing. Methylation assays read something related but need deep targeted sequencing. mFrag does not generate any new data. It takes publicly available whole-genome bisulfite methylation references — one atlas from non-cancer donors, one from lung tumors — and uses them to decide which five-megabase windows of the genome are worth weighting, then feeds that into either a weighted regression or a neural network and averages the two.

They tested it on the exact independent test set from the published DELFI lung work — three hundred eighty-five non-cancer controls, forty-six lung cancers. Against their own faithful implementation of the DELFI method, precision-recall area under the curve went from zero point four five five to zero point seven two. Sensitivity at ninety-nine percent specificity went from four percent to forty-eight percent. For stage one disease, precision-recall went from zero point three one to zero point five six. The R-O-C gains were modest and did not survive multiplicity correction — they are honest about that — but the precision-recall gains did, and precision-recall is the metric that matters when your positives are rare, which is every screening population.

And the competing interests line reads: Rob Scharpf is a co-founder of DELFI Diagnostics and owns DELFI stock; Johns Hopkins owns equity in DELFI.

Eddie, Emily — three implications. One, this is not an attack on DELFI, it is DELFI's own scientific founder participating in showing that the classifier on top of the fragmentomic features has real headroom, obtainable with no additional assay cost. That is the cheapest performance upgrade available to a diagnostics company and it should be in a FirstLook roadmap conversation. Two, it is a reminder of what we said when Freenome's lung test launched last month — the market prices what a screening test misses, and a jump from four percent to forty-eight percent sensitivity at ninety-nine percent specificity is precisely the axis the market punished DELFI's competitors on. Three, and this is the diligence habit: the method is public, the atlases are public, and the code is on GitHub. Anyone can do this. The defensibility question for any fragmentomics position is now explicitly about data and distribution, not about the classifier.

Item four, the cardiac fibrosis theme, where two results landed in the same week and only make sense together.

First, Vasa Therapeutics reported positive topline results from a randomized, placebo-controlled phase one-c trial of V-S-oh-four-one, an oral narrow-spectrum matrix metalloproteinase inhibitor, in heart failure with preserved ejection fraction. Forty-two participants on background guideline-directed therapy. N-T-pro-B-N-P — the wall-stress biomarker — came down within twenty-eight days at two doses, safe and well tolerated. The drug has F-D-A fast track from last November. But the part the chief executive led with was not the efficacy. It was that they used serum endotrophin, measured with a collagen-six fragment assay, prospectively, to select a more homogeneous subpopulation whose diastolic dysfunction is actually driven by fibro-inflammation — patients you cannot identify from clinical data alone. His words: a novel enrichment strategy that should translate to smaller and faster development.

Second, a University of Utah group published in Nature Communications on A-E-B-P-one, a protein that drives fibroblast activation. Fibroblast-specific knockout or knockdown significantly improved cardiac function and prevented adverse remodeling after ischemic and pressure-overload injury in mice, and knocking it down in cultured failing human myocardium made the tissue architecture look healthier with less scarring. Two limits the authors put up front: when they lowered the protein after heart failure had already progressed, there was no improvement — it prevents, it does not reverse. And the senior author is explicit that turning off all fibrosis is the wrong strategy, because early scarring after a heart attack is structurally necessary. The goal is to let healing finish and then stop the overactive fibroblasts.

Matt, Hemaka — put those two next to each other and you have the whole design brief for anything we do in cardiac fibrosis. The biology gives you a target class with a therapeutic window that is defined by timing, not by dose. The clinical trial gives you the enrollment instrument — a circulating collagen-fragment biomarker that tells you which heart failure patient actually has a fibrotic problem. A fibrosis program without both is a program that will enroll the wrong patients and dose them at the wrong moment, and it will fail for reasons that have nothing to do with whether the mechanism was right.

Last item, and it is for Aletira. We deferred this twice waiting for the right place to put it; this is it.

On Monday, Gallaudet University's president Roberta Cordano released the university's formal institutional position statement on gene editing, gene therapy and human diversity, developed by a newly created Gene Editing Think Tank of Deaf scholars spanning genetics, biology, philosophy, history, policy and the history of eugenics. The Washington Post covered it Wednesday. The statement rests on two stated beliefs: that Deaf lives are an essential and irreplaceable part of human diversity, and that Deaf people must help shape and lead decisions that affect their future. It does not oppose the science or the medical benefit; it says individuals and families will make different choices and those choices must be informed, respected, and shaped in conversations where Deaf people are leaders rather than consultees. The framing question they put at the center is, who gets to decide. They are also launching a nationwide survey on attitudes toward gene editing and gene therapy.

Here is why it is on the portfolio list and not in the ethics folder. The university's own molecular geneticist, Derek Braun, is quoted explaining the timing, and his two markers are these: the first gene therapy for a hereditary form of deafness was approved by the F-D-A earlier this year, and in August the first child was dosed in a clinical trial targeting GJB2, connexin twenty-six, the most common genetic cause of hereditary deafness in North America and Europe.

That is our target. Named, dated, and now the subject of a formal position from the single most influential institution in the Deaf community.

Matt, Maisha — the action is not messaging. Two weeks ago we read a Korean otology meeting telling us the specialists who write the prescription want ten years of safety data against a cochlear implant comparator, and last week we read that already-implanted children may be an addressable population rather than a closed door. This is the third leg of the same structure: the adoption bar for a hearing gene therapy is set by clinicians, by families, and by the community the therapy is aimed at, and the community has just organized itself to have a formal voice. Programs that treat that as a public relations problem to be handled at launch will discover the problem during recruitment. The concrete items are, one, Deaf community advisors on the Aletira clinical advisory board now, before any trial design is locked, not after; two, track that nationwide survey, because it will become the most-cited data set on acceptance for this category and it will be quoted at us in diligence; and three, our informed-consent and family-counselling design is a competitive asset in this indication, not a compliance cost.

That is the watch. The European regulator approves the depleting antibody in lupus while its maker runs the engager in behind it, which is the sequencing our engager will be measured against. And Gallaudet has formally entered the conversation about our hearing program's target. Today's Sourcing Radar is in your feed alongside this. Have a good Sunday.
