## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Monday, November second. The most important item for us this morning comes from a company already named in the Aletira file as our clinical precedent, and it arrives carrying a design argument we should steal: Atsena Therapeutics reported non-human-primate and six-month toxicology data for a dual-vector gene therapy aimed at Usher syndrome type one-B — a disease whose patients are born profoundly deaf and go blind in the first decade — and the entire program is pointed at the eye.

Here is what they reported. The gene is M-Y-O-seven-A. Its coding sequence runs about six point seven kilobases, well past the roughly four point eight a single A-A-V can carry, so it has to be split across two vectors. Atsena's candidate, A-T-S-N three-oh-one, pairs their dual-vector approach with the laterally spreading capsid they call A-A-V dot S-P-R — the same capsid underneath their retinoschisis program in Phase Three and their Stargardt program behind it. In cynomolgus macaques, an injection placed outside the fovea produced full-length M-Y-O-seven-A at supraphysiological levels, and the capsid's lateral spread carried it into the macula without surgically detaching the fovea. A six-month good-laboratory-practice toxicology study set the no-observed-adverse-effect level at the highest dose tested, with no electroretinogram changes, clean histopathology, no meaningful immunogenicity, and biodistribution confined almost entirely to the injected retina. Truncated protein was minimal and did not affect retinal safety. I-N-D planned for twenty twenty-seven, with a manuscript on bioRxiv behind it.

Three reads. The first is the one I want Avi and Hugh carrying into every oversized-gene diligence from here. Atsena spent a paragraph of their own release on how you split a gene, and the argument is about safety, not efficiency. There are four ways the field reconstitutes a big protein from two vectors — joining at the D-N-A level by recombination, at the R-N-A level by trans-splicing or end-joining, or at the protein level with split inteins. Three of those four deliberately produce an extra species, a half-protein or a half-transcript, that can carry a toxic gain-of-function risk. Theirs relies only on the cell's own recombination and splicing machinery, so nothing extra is expressed on purpose. Then they go finer: they split upstream of the tail domain, so the front-half vector encodes none of the tail — because the tail is what binds other cellular proteins, and a front half carrying part of it is the piece most likely to do something. They note, pointedly, that other dual-vector approaches split further downstream. That is a shot at the Milan group we logged on October fifth, whose Stargardt program uses intein-mediated trans-splicing. I am not refereeing that fight. I am saying the question — where exactly is the split, and what does the half-protein bind — is now a named, answerable diligence question for any program needing more capacity than a capsid has.

Second read, and this is the second free gift in four weeks. A competitor has again printed supraphysiological expression as a success metric — on October fifth the Milan group, above one hundred percent of endogenous levels in pig retina, as a selling point; today Atsena. That is the exact failure mode native-promoter expression control is sold against, and when two credible groups in the same modality treat overshooting as the headline, our framing for SELEXON gets easier. But match the discipline they brought: they measured the truncated species and reported it rather than asserting it away. If controlling the level is our claim, we should hold the cleanest numbers on what the construct makes.

Third read, for Hemaka and Matt, and it is a question rather than a conclusion. M-Y-O-seven-A is a dual-sensory gene — these children are born profoundly deaf, and the retinitis pigmentosa arrives later. Atsena's pipeline is retina across the board; the Milan program is retina. Nobody in this week's releases is treating the cochlea in Usher one-B, and the reason is not mystery: six point seven kilobases is a brutal payload in an organ you get one shot at. But that is the same payload problem we have already decided to live with. Worth an hour of Avi's time to find out who, if anyone, holds cochlear M-Y-O-seven-A, before we conclude the ear is open because it is hard.

Second item, and it puts a number on something we have been talking about since early September without standing it up. Alligator Bioscience, a Swedish company that has exited tumor-directed immuno-oncology, sold worldwide rights to mitazalimab — a C-D-forty agonist antibody with what the company itself calls promising Phase Two data in pancreatic cancer — for seven hundred and fifty thousand dollars in cash. That is the whole upfront. Everything else is contingent: up to three million on first approval in the U-S and E-U, up to a hundred and five million tied to net sales, and a mid-single-digit royalty, all capped at two hundred and fifty million. The package transfers patents, clinical and regulatory data, know-how, manufacturing materials and drug inventory, and the buyer picks up all further development, manufacturing and patent costs. The buyer is Biossil, a Toronto and Boston company that describes itself as an A-I-native drug developer advancing a pipeline of late-stage, first-in-class candidates. Alligator's stated rationale is that the sale ends the cost of maintaining the program and its I-P portfolio.

Two reads for Eddie. The first is the price, and I want it written into the reference. Our comparator ladder for in-licensing an ex-U-S clinical asset runs from forty-five million for discovery-through-early-clinical to a hundred and ten million for a Phase One molecule. A clinical-stage antibody with positive Phase Two data in the hardest solid tumor there is just cleared seven hundred and fifty thousand dollars of certain money. The stage did not change. What changed is who was standing on which side of the table — a platform company negotiating from strength, versus a small-cap that had already announced it was leaving the field and was paying to keep patents alive. That spread cuts both ways. It is the price at which a shelved asset becomes buyable, and it is the price a Blackbird spinout's program fetches if its company ever has to exit under duress. The seller's own phrase for a seven-hundred-and-fifty-thousand-dollar cheque was an attractive balance between near-term value realization and future upside. Remember that sentence.

The second read is competitive. We flagged a standing screen for divested and shelved assets on September fifth, doubled down on September eleventh, and it still is not running. Biossil's entire stated business model is that screen with A-I as the sorting mechanism, and they just closed one. The window on cheap shelved assets does not stay open because nobody noticed; it stays open until somebody industrializes it, and somebody has. The action is small and overdue — a named owner, and a weekly pass over discontinuation announcements from small-caps under going-concern pressure.

Third item, on the C-O-P-D theme, and it is the counterweight to Friday's failure. Boehringer Ingelheim registered a Phase Three trial called AIRWARD for verducatib, an oral inhibitor of dipeptidyl peptidase one — the enzyme that activates neutrophil serine proteases. Roughly eighteen hundred patients, four hundred and fifty sites, more than twenty-five countries, at least fifty-two weeks, with the primary endpoint the annualized rate of moderate or severe exacerbations. The enrollment criterion is the part to notice: symptomatic patients still exacerbating on usual therapy, with blood eosinophil counts below three hundred cells per microliter. Boehringer's framing is that in sixty to ninety percent of people with C-O-P-D the inflammation is predominantly neutrophil-driven. This is not a new molecule for them — verducatib is already in Phase Three in bronchiectasis, where the same target has an approved first-in-class drug from Insmed. So this is indication expansion onto a de-risked mechanism, by the oral route, into the eosinophil-low population.

The read for whoever holds the C-O-P-D file. On Friday we said the fexlamose failure left the mucus hypothesis untested rather than refuted, because the maximally downstream bet missed on the function endpoint and the imaging biomarker both. Today the middle of the airway shows up with real money behind it — upstream of the mucus, downstream of the alarmin layer, enzymatic, oral, and aimed squarely at the non-type-two population we logged in September as a lane already taken. It is now taken twice, by two mechanisms, one injectable and one a pill. The discipline that imposes is simple: any Blackbird C-O-P-D program has to be able to say in one sentence what it does for an eosinophil-low exacerbating patient that an anti-I-L-thirty-three and an oral protease-pathway inhibitor do not. Absent that sentence it is a mechanism in search of a lane.

Last item, short, for Hemaka and the NuGate design team. Darwin Biotech presented a randomized Phase One in A-L-S at the NEALS meeting in Clearwater — a cell-free protein complex derived from the secretome of human umbilical-cord mesenchymal stem cells, thirty-six patients in China, randomized three to one across three doses, thirty-one days. No treatment-related serious adverse events at any dose. At day thirty-one the functional rating scale rose from baseline in all three treated groups while placebo declined, and plasma neurofilament light fell about seventeen percent in both higher-dose groups against two percent on placebo.

Treat those two results very differently. A functional rating scale rising over thirty-one days in nine people a group is the scale's noise floor, not disease modification, and the company says as much — efficacy was secondary and preliminary. The neurofilament move is the interesting half, because it is the within-patient pharmacodynamic readout the Emory panel work left standing when it bounded neurofilament as a classifier. But here is the catch, and it clarifies our own position: a secretome has no defined molecular target, so there is nothing to anchor the drop to, and the biomarker is being asked to carry the entire argument. For an antisense program the chain from target engagement to knockdown to neurofilament is the argument, and every link needs its own evidence. Landscape datapoint; watch the U-S Phase One-two-a.

Quiet elsewhere. Nothing new on Aletira's own G-J-B-two program, on the G-P-R fifty-two NewCo, on Winnow, Clasp, Biolinco, Artemyx, D-E-L-F-I, Georgiamune or 1104health, and nothing dated inside the window from Johns Hopkins, U-M-B or the Lieber Institute. One source note for the record: Fierce Biotech is still blocking us outright, so anything that ran only there this morning is not in this briefing. Have a good Monday.
