## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Portfolio Watch for Tuesday, August fourth. I'll give you the honest topline first: on direct catalysts, this is now the ninth quiet day in a row — no readout, no filing, no deal landed on a Blackbird asset over the past few days. But there is one genuinely fresh thing on the tape this morning, and it's a big one. It doesn't hit any of our programs directly, so I want to be careful with it — but it reshapes the landscape every one of our companies will eventually exit into, and it lands squarely on the competitive backdrop for one of them. So we lead there, candidly, as a landscape signal rather than a catalyst, and then I'll run the standing board fast.

The event is the megamerger rumor. On Saturday the Financial Times reported that AstraZeneca is in talks to merge with Bristol Myers Squibb — a combination that would be valued at roughly four hundred billion dollars and rank among the largest deals in the history of this industry. The sourcing says the two have been talking on and off for a few months; the companies aren't commenting; and it is very much unclear whether it comes together — some accounts already say it may not. AstraZeneca's stock dropped on the report, and the analysts flagged the obvious problem, which is antitrust: both companies are oncology heavyweights, and AstraZeneca's cancer franchise alone did about twenty-five billion dollars last year, nearly half its sales. So keep the caveat front of mind — this is a reported early-stage talk, not a signed deal. But even as a rumor, it moves how we should be thinking, and here's why.

The sharpest thread runs to our schizophrenia NewCo — the Lieber Institute and Third Rock G-P-R fifty-two program. Bristol Myers owns Cobenfy, the muscarinic antipsychotic they picked up in the Karuna acquisition — the first non-dopaminergic drug approved in schizophrenia, and the single most important competitive reference point our G-P-R fifty-two asset has to position against. Bristol Myers is also exactly the kind of strategic buyer or partner a differentiated G-P-R fifty-two program would one day be sold into; it's on the short list of natural acquirers we've been tracking for the Nxera rival, alongside Otsuka, Neurocrine, and AbbVie. Now fold Bristol Myers into AstraZeneca — a company that is essentially all oncology and rare disease and has no real psychiatry franchise — and the CNS priorities of the combined entity get genuinely uncertain. Cobenfy could become a non-core asset inside an oncology machine, or the merger could go the other way and give it a deeper-pocketed owner. Either way, one of the handful of independent big-pharma neuro-psych buyers just got absorbed. Fewer independent bidders means thinner exit optionality down the road for our NewCo. There's a second-order effect too, and it actually cuts our way in the near term: an acquirer staring down a multi-year integration is a slower, more distracted buyer — so the reprieve we've been enjoying on the Nxera G-P-R fifty-two rival, which is still unsold and unpartnered, may well extend rather than close. Net for Hemaka and the NewCo: the buyer map for this space just changed, and it's worth redrawing.

The second thread runs to aSKY. The antitrust concern the analysts named is oncology overlap — which means a combined AstraZeneca and Bristol Myers would be a twenty-five-billion-dollar-plus cancer business, the single largest potential partner, acquirer, and competitor in the solid-tumor space aSKY plays in with its KRAS-directed antibody. Megamergers of this size reliably do two things: they freeze business development for a year or more while the two organizations digest each other, and they prune and shed deprioritized pipeline. Near term, that concentrates the eventual-buyer pool for an oncology asset. Longer term, it spills out programs and scientific talent — which, for a venture studio that builds companies around de-risked IP, is a sourcing surface, not just a threat. That's an Eddie point more than an aSKY-team point: consolidation at the very top of pharma concentrates the set of acquirers every Blackbird spinout eventually sells into, which makes each exit a little more binary — and it's also the reliable moment when good assets and good people come loose. Both things are true at once.

So the read on the merger is deliberately measured: it's a rumor, it may not happen, and nobody should reorganize a program around it. But we should update the mental model of who the buyers are, because that model just got one player shorter and one player much bigger.
