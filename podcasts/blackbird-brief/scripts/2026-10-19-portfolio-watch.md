## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Monday, October nineteenth. The most important development for the portfolio landed on the wire this morning: a European company just raised a hundred and thirty-five million dollars to run a single registration-enabling trial of a cancer vaccine in people who do not have cancer — which makes cancer interception, the board Adventris plays on downstairs in the BioHub, a funded clinical category rather than an argument we have been making in landscape decks.

Start there. Nouscom, out of Basel, closed an oversubscribed one hundred and twenty million euro Series D this morning — about a hundred and thirty-five million dollars. M Ventures and Revelation Partners co-led it. The new names matter more than the number: Angelini Industries came in through its investment arm, the European Investment Bank came in through a co-investment facility it runs with Angelini Ventures, and Neva, which is the venture arm of Intesa Sanpaolo, came in as well. Every existing investor participated, and the existing list is long and serious — 5AM, Versant, EQT Life Sciences, Andera, Bpifrance, CDP Venture Capital.

The asset is NOUS-two-oh-nine, an off-the-shelf vaccine built on two viral vectors that together deliver two hundred and nine shared frameshift peptide neoantigens. The biology is the whole reason this can be off-the-shelf rather than personalized. Tumors with mismatch repair deficiency accumulate insertions and deletions at repeat sequences, those indels shift the reading frame, and the result is novel peptides downstream of the shift. Because the repeat sequences are the same across patients, the frameshift peptides are largely shared. So instead of sequencing each person's tumor and building them their own vaccine, you build one vaccine against the recurring mistakes the defect makes. In Lynch syndrome carriers, where that repair defect is inherited, you can give it before there is a tumor at all.

The money funds one thing: a single, global, registration-enabling trial for cancer interception in Lynch carriers, with FDA Fast Track already granted. The chief executive, Marina Udier, frames the unmet need as two million carriers facing up to an eighty percent lifetime cancer risk with nothing on offer but surveillance and surgery.

The financing was timed to data. On Sunday the twenty-fifth at ESMO in Madrid, Eduardo Vilar-Sanchez of MD Anderson presents long-term interception data from the Phase one-b-two. Per the published abstract, every evaluable carrier dosed with the vaccine remained free of advanced adenomas and colorectal cancer on colonoscopy at year one and at year two — forty-three carriers at the one-year mark, seventeen at two years. The T-cell responses were broad and durable, and annual re-vaccination made them stronger rather than exhausting them. The earlier Nature Medicine cohort gives the immunogenicity baseline: all thirty-seven evaluable participants mounted neoantigen-specific responses, eighty-five percent still durable at a year, with no treatment-related serious adverse events.

Three reads, and the first is for Eddie and the Adventris relationship. We have been carrying a thesis in this brief for two months — that cancer vaccines are not failing as a modality, they are failing in metastatic disease, and every clean positive signal in the field sits in resected, adjuvant, or pre-cancerous settings. That was a pattern argument assembled from other people's readouts. It is now a financing event. A syndicate including a sovereign-scale lender wrote a check specifically to take an interception vaccine into a registration trial, in a population with no tumor, on an endpoint built around precancerous events. Adventris's differentiated Phase one — twenty people with hereditary pancreatic cancer predisposition and a radiographic lesion but no cancer, vaccine alone with no checkpoint inhibitor, ninety percent mutant-K-RAS T-cell response and cyst reduction in roughly thirty-eight percent against seven percent unvaccinated — is the same shape of study on a different inherited risk. The read-across is not scientific, it is commercial: interception now has a precedent for how it gets financed, who finances it, and what the regulators will accept as a trial design.

Second read, for Hemaka and anyone who has to design a prevention trial. Look closely at the endpoint problem these people solved, because it is the thing that has killed interception as an investable category for twenty years. Preventing cancer takes a long time to prove and an enormous number of patients. Nouscom's answer is to run a single registration-enabling trial on an event-free survival endpoint that counts precancerous events alongside cancers — and, critically, to have agreed that design with both the FDA and the EMA before spending the money. That sequencing is the lesson. In an interception program the regulatory conversation is not a milestone that follows the data; it is the thing that determines whether the data can ever be affordable. If we build an interception program out of a partner institution, the first diligence question is not potency, it is what the agency will count as an event.

Third read, candid, and it belongs in the same deck. The clinical signal here is uncontrolled. Every evaluable carrier staying free of advanced adenomas sounds like a hundred percent, but the comparator is expected incidence in Lynch carriers, not a randomized control arm, and the two-year number rests on seventeen people. The company says so in its own abstract language — an encouraging signal consistent with a treatment effect. Interception is the setting where single-arm data is most seductive and least conclusive, because most of the people in the trial were never going to develop cancer in the window you observed them. The registration trial is being run precisely because this one cannot settle it.

Item two, and this one is for Jon and the Winnow team. On Friday the sixteenth the FDA approved Roche's Enspryng — satralizumab — for thyroid eye disease. It is an interleukin-six receptor antibody, already approved since twenty-twenty in neuromyelitis optica, and it becomes the first and only subcutaneous option in thyroid eye disease, self-administered at home every four weeks after training, against a field where the incumbents are infusions.

The data underneath the approval is the part I want you to sit with. Two identically designed Phase three trials, SatraGO-one and SatraGO-two, two hundred and fifty-eight patients across nineteen countries. SatraGO-one did not meet its primary endpoint at week twenty-four. SatraGO-two did: proptosis response fifty-two point nine percent versus twenty-three point four on placebo, and diplopia reduction sixty point seven versus twenty-five point eight. One hit, one miss, approved on the totality.

We have been building an argument in this brief since the middle of September — that in autoimmune disease the delivery burden is part of the product, and that this field pays for convenience at roughly a twenty-five point efficacy discount. Sling's oral thyroid-eye-disease drug at fifty-two percent proptosis response got a hundred and twenty-three million dollars while subcutaneous Tepezza sits at seventy-six point seven. Now the FDA has approved a subcutaneous agent whose own pivotal program split, with approval language that leans on the logistical burden of infusion. Thyroid eye disease has become the cleanest natural experiment available on how route beats effect size in a chronic autoimmune indication.

For Winnow the implication is the one we have been sharpening rather than a new one, and it cuts both ways. A T-cell engager is the most burdensome route on this board — infused, monitored, with a cytokine release profile to explain to a community rheumatologist. In a field where regulators and payers reward convenience, Winnow cannot win on convenience and should stop trying. What it has instead is the one claim a monthly injection structurally cannot make: one course, then off drug. That is depth and durability, and it is sharper than "antigen-specific" because it is directly testable in a clinical trial. Note also the second-order point for anyone modeling a pivotal program — Roche ran two identical trials and got one win, and that was enough. Redundancy in the pivotal design is itself a risk-management strategy, and it is expensive, and the time to decide whether you can afford it is before you commit to a single registrational study.

Item three stays on the autoimmune axis and tightens a question we opened three weeks ago. On Saturday, Vivacta Bio in Shanghai and its parent Grit Bio announced that the FDA granted orphan drug designation to GT-eight-oh-one for autoimmune hemolytic anemia. GT-eight-oh-one is an in vivo CAR-T. There is no cell manufacturing: the construct is messenger RNA encoding an anti-CD-nineteen chimeric antigen receptor, packaged in a lipid nanoparticle targeted to T cells by a single-domain antibody. You infuse the particle and the CAR-T cells are made inside the patient. No apheresis, no ex vivo expansion, no lymphodepleting chemotherapy.

The human data is two patients, presented as a poster, and both reached complete response — one at week four holding through month three, the second at week ten — with sustained B-cell depletion, hemoglobin up, and the hemolysis markers down. Two patients is an anecdote. The designation is a regulatory courtesy, not evidence.

So why is it an item. Because it is the third independent data point in seven weeks on the same vector, and the direction is consistent. In late September we covered IASO's in vivo CAR-T with ten myeloma patients, ninety percent response, no apheresis and no lymphodepletion. We noted at the time that the dataset both attacks and defends Winnow's control-architecture argument. Part of our row has been that a cell product is not titratable and that manufacturing plus lymphodepletion is a burden an engager does not carry. Every month that in vivo CAR-T works, the manufacturing half of that argument gets weaker, and now it is moving from oncology into autoimmune indications specifically. The half that still holds is control: a redosable protein you can stop is a different safety instrument than a self-amplifying cell population, and in a chronic non-fatal disease — which is exactly the distinction an operator put on the record for us two weeks ago — that difference is the product. Keep making the control argument. Retire the manufacturing argument before a partner retires it for you.

There is also a sourcing note attached, for Esther. The Johns Hopkins translational fund list we worked up in late August included Jonathan Schneck in Pathology, generating CAR-T in vivo with targeted polymeric messenger RNA nanoparticles for autoimmune disease. That is the same destination two funded companies are now walking toward from the clinic. The academic lead is real, and the window where it is differentiated is closing.

Item four is short, and it is for the Parkinson's project. On Sunday XellSmart presented Phase one results for XS-four-eleven, an off-the-shelf allogeneic cell therapy — dopaminergic neural progenitors derived from induced pluripotent stem cells, transplanted into the putamen. Nine patients, three dose cohorts, about ten years of disease duration on average. At six months, motor scores in the off state improved by more than thirty points on average and good on-time went up by more than five hours a day, with imaging showing elevated dopamine-tracer binding in the putamen consistent with the injection sites. No dose-limiting toxicities, no graft-induced dyskinesia, no overgrowth. The Phase two in China is fully enrolled at thirty patients, randomized, with blinded endpoint assessment.

Put that next to the Aspen Neuroscience S-one we covered on the tenth. Aspen's approach is autologous — the patient's own cells, no immunosuppression — and after fifteen patients their motor improvement runs about fifteen points with roughly two to three hours of added on-time, open-label, and the FDA has told them they need a controlled Phase three. XellSmart is reporting double that motor effect from an off-the-shelf product, on nine patients, on standing immunosuppression, open-label, from a company press release with no named investigator attached to the numbers. I would not believe the comparison. A thirty-point change on that scale in an unblinded trial of nine people with advanced disease is the size of effect that blinded designs exist to deflate — which, to be fair, is what their Phase two was built to test.

What is worth taking seriously is the structural question for our own program. The autologous-versus-allogeneic choice in cell replacement looks like a manufacturing decision and is actually an immunosuppression decision, and immunosuppression is what determines whether the product can ever be given to the earlier, healthier patients where cell replacement should work best. Aspen is paying manufacturing complexity to avoid it. XellSmart is paying immunosuppression to avoid the complexity. Nobody has shown they can avoid both, and whichever side a Blackbird Parkinson's program lands on, that is the trade being made, and it should be made explicitly rather than inherited from whatever the founding lab happens to have in the freezer.

That is the watch. The week ahead: ESMO opens Friday in Madrid and runs through the twenty-seventh, with the Nouscom interception data on Sunday afternoon. The joint ACTRIMS–ECTRIMS meeting is in Toronto Wednesday through Friday, where Kyverna presents its full stiff person syndrome dataset — that is the one to read for Winnow. ASHG is in Montreal through Saturday. Pearl Diagnostics presents at IDWeek on Thursday. LB Pharmaceuticals holds its R and D day on the twenty-seventh, the FDA's rare disease framework public meeting is on the twenty-eighth, and the comment window on China's export-control draft closes November fifteenth. Thanks, team. Back tomorrow.
