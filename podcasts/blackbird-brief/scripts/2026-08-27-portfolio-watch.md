## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Thursday, August twenty-seventh. Yesterday the F-D-A approved the first broadly RAS-targeted medicine in pancreatic cancer, and it did it with a label that requires no genetic test at all. That single design choice simultaneously validates and complicates all three of our mutant-K-RAS positions, and it is the lead.

Revolution Medicines got approval for daraxonrasib, branded Rasonque, in metastatic pancreatic adenocarcinoma after at least one prior systemic therapy, or for patients who are not candidates for multi-agent chemotherapy. Oral pill, once a day. Mechanistically it is a molecular glue: it bridges RAS to the chaperone protein cyclophilin A, and the resulting complex physically blocks RAS from touching its downstream effectors. Because it works on the active, on-state protein rather than a single mutant residue, it hits wild-type RAS and most of the G-twelve variants at once.

The data are the best anyone has produced in this disease. Five hundred patients against chemotherapy: median overall survival thirteen point two months versus six point seven, a hazard ratio of zero point four zero. Progression-free survival seven point two months against three point six. The safety profile is rough — rash in eighty-six percent, diarrhea in sixty-three, fatal cases of both bowel perforation and interstitial lung disease — and yet under three percent discontinued permanently, which tells you what people tolerate when the alternative is six months. The price is thirty-nine thousand eight hundred dollars for thirty days.

Now the three reads that matter for us, and the first one is the important one.

The label has no biomarker gate. No companion diagnostic, and the drug is approved for patients with or without an identified RAS mutation — Revolution ran co-primary analyses in the G-twelve-mutant population and in the whole population, the results were consistent, and the agency wrote the broad label. So the new commercial default in second-line pancreatic cancer is an oral pill with zero patient-selection steps.

Hold that against what we own. Clasp's K-RAS T-cell engager requires the patient to carry the G-twelve-V mutation and a specific H-L-A type — two independent gates, and multiplying two fractions gets small fast. Adventris needs a defined mutant-K-RAS peptide. aSKY needs its particular K-RAS frame. Every one now has to justify a two-gate eligibility funnel against a competitor with none. Hemaka, Anthony, Virginia — the K-RAS map we have been building since the fourteenth has a mechanism row and a disease-stage row. It needs an eligible-fraction row, before the next partnering conversation, because that is the number a corporate development team computes in their head while we are still talking about specificity.

The second read is the encouraging one, and it now has a date attached. When Clasp unveiled its K-RAS engager in April, the preclinical pitch was explicitly that it kills harder against tumors resistant to small-molecule K-RAS inhibitors and gets better in combination with them. Yesterday that stopped being a slide and became an addressable market with a start date. Progression-free survival on daraxonrasib is seven point two months, so the first commercial cohort of post-RAS-inhibitor pancreatic cancer patients in this country arrives around spring, and keeps arriving, against a drug analysts think peaks above eleven billion dollars a year. Eddie: the ask to Clasp is concrete. Does that Phase 1 have a post-daraxonrasib stratum written into the protocol, and if not, why not. A resistance-positioned asset with no resistance cohort in its own trial is leaving the strategic story on the table.

The third read is the candid one. Notice which column of the K-RAS board just won. I have described that field as five columns — antibody, small molecule, degrader, vaccine, T-cell engager — with Blackbird holding a stake in three. The small-molecule column just took the largest indication outright, with chemistry, not immunology. When the standard of care in the biggest RAS tumor is a pill with a hazard ratio of zero point four, the bar for a novel immunologic modality is no longer does it work — it is does it add something the pill cannot do. Resistance and combination are good answers, and they are now the only answers.

Second item, and it touches the Hopkins gut program. Spyre Therapeutics reported the rheumatoid arthritis arm of its Phase 2 basket trial for its anti-T-L-one-A antibody. A hundred forty-three patients, twelve weeks. The lower dose beat placebo on the primary disease-activity score, statistically significant; the higher dose missed. Spyre said the effect size did not clear its internal bar for monotherapy development and dropped the indication. The stock fell thirteen percent and erased more than a billion dollars of market value. They are keeping psoriatic arthritis and axial spondyloarthritis, with data in the fourth quarter, and the ulcerative colitis programs run through 2027 and 2028. The sell-side line was that the core inflammatory-bowel-disease combination thesis is intact.

That last clause is the whole item for us. T-L-one-A is the most expensive target in inflammatory bowel disease — Merck bought Prometheus for it, Sanofi and Teva partnered on duvakitug — and yesterday the class told the market, in public, that outside the gut it may not carry a monotherapy label. The field's answer is combinations. So ask what the ideal partner for a systemic anti-T-L-one-A antibody looks like. Non-overlapping mechanism. Adds no systemic immunosuppression on top of the antibody's. Oral, so no second injection. And confined to the tissue where the disease actually is. That is a precise description of Barbara Slusher's gut-restricted G-C-P-two inhibitor. Matt, Avi: that program's positioning document is written as a monotherapy story and should be rewritten as a combination-partner story. The buyers are the companies that just spent ten billion dollars on T-L-one-A and now need something safe to put beside it.

Third item, for Aletira. The Commissioner's National Priority Voucher now has two headline approvals. Regeneron's otoferlin gene therapy in April, sixty-one days from filing. Daraxonrasib yesterday, submitted July twenty-second and approved August twenty-sixth — about five weeks, and six and a half months ahead of the agency's own goal date. Two therapeutic areas, two different modalities, an A-A-V gene therapy and an oral pill. This is no longer a one-off, and one of its two proof points landed in Aletira's exact indication space.

Now look at what those two vouchers priced at. Daraxonrasib: thirty-nine thousand eight hundred dollars a month. Regeneron's hearing-loss gene therapy: free — they are providing it at no charge in the United States. Geoff, Hugh, take both halves.
