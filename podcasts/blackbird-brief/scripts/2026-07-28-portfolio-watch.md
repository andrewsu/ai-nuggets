# Portfolio Watch — 2026-07-28

## Script

This is Portfolio Watch for Tuesday, July 28th. The one development worth your time today sits right on top of one of our own platforms: the leading company in RNA-upregulation therapeutics just got the green light to put its drug into humans for the first time — and that's a direct read-across to the translation-activator platform in our portfolio.

Here's what happened. CAMP4 Therapeutics — a Massachusetts company whose whole thesis is turning genes up rather than shutting them down — announced yesterday that Australia's Therapeutic Goods Administration and the local ethics committee cleared it to start a Phase one-two first-in-human trial. The drug is an antisense oligonucleotide, but it works in the opposite direction from the antisense drugs you already know. Instead of degrading a message to lower a protein, it targets what CAMP4 calls regulatory RNA — a control element sitting upstream of a gene — and releases the brake, so the cell makes more of the protein it's supposed to make. The target here is SYNGAP-one, and the indication is SYNGAP-one-related disorder: a rare neurodevelopmental condition affecting more than ten thousand people in the U.S., driven by having only one working copy of the gene — universal intellectual disability, epilepsy in most patients, and no approved disease-modifying therapy. The logic is clean. If the disease is caused by a missing half of the normal protein, a drug that dials the good copy back up toward normal should modify the disease itself, not just the symptoms. Preclinically they showed dose-dependent protein increases in patient-derived neurons, phenotype reversal in humanized mice, seizure reduction in a seizure model, and broad brain distribution in primates. It's dosed intrathecally.

Two things make this more than a science-news item for us. First, the money. This clearance triggered the second closing of a financing they lined up last September — it unlocks up to fifty million dollars, from a syndicate that includes Janus Henderson, Vivo Capital, Five A-M Ventures, Balyasny, and the patient foundation CURE SYNGAP-one. So the market is putting real capital behind the upregulation modality at exactly the moment it enters the clinic.

Second — and this is the so-what for Blackbird — this is the clearest validation to date of the modality our own RNA translation-activator platform lives in. We've been building in the turn-the-protein-up lane: the answer for haploinsufficiency and loss-of-function disease, where knockdown drugs simply don't apply. CAMP4 has been the name to watch in that lane, and they've now done two things that matter to us. They've shown a regulator will let an RNA-upregulation drug into humans, and they've framed the commercial prize the way we should be framing ours — more than twelve hundred haploinsufficient or partial-loss-of-function disorders where even a modest bump in protein is clinically meaningful. That's the addressable-market story for our platform, now with an external company putting numbers and investor dollars on it.

I'll be candid about the read, because it cuts both ways. This is validation, not a threat to a specific asset — we don't have a competing SYNGAP-one program. But it is a pace-setter. The leading independent RNA-upregulation company is now first-in-human and freshly funded, which means the window to establish a differentiated wedge in this modality is a live clock, not an open-ended one. The question for our platform team isn't whether upregulation works — CAMP4 is answering that for the whole field. It's what our defensible angle is: a different targeting mechanism than regulatory-RNA antisense, a different delivery route than intrathecal, a different disease set. That's the conversation this milestone should trigger, and it's worth having now, while the lane is still forming, rather than after CAMP4 reads out.
