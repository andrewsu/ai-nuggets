## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Sourcing Radar for Sunday, September twentieth. Three leads, and they line up on one axis — every one of them is about engineering what a cell splices, and about who owns the piece of that machinery worth licensing. The lead is a pair of Johns Hopkins preprints that, read together, put a question mark over a forty-five-million-dollar upfront somebody else already paid.

Start with Philip Wong's lab in the Departments of Pathology and Neuroscience at Johns Hopkins. Wong is already on our sourcing list — his group's work is the origin of the whole T-D-P forty-three cryptic-splicing field. This week he posted two companion papers three days apart, one Tuesday and one Thursday, and the reason they matter is that they disagree with each other in exactly the way that decides a development plan.

The biology in one breath. T-D-P forty-three keeps junk intronic sequence out of mature messenger R-N-A. When it stops doing that — which happens early in A-L-S and frontotemporal dementia, a decade before the aggregates anyone stains for — dozens of transcripts pick up cryptic exons and get destroyed. One of them is U-N-C thirteen A, and a common risk variant makes its cryptic exon worse. That single gene became the field's favorite target: Lilly paid QurAlis forty-five million dollars upfront, plus equity, against up to five hundred seventy-seven million in milestones, for a splice-switching antisense drug whose entire proposition is restoring U-N-C thirteen A function in A-L-S and frontotemporal dementia. Nobody has ever shown that fixing U-N-C thirteen A by itself is enough. That is the question these two papers were built to answer.

Paper one takes the dementia compartment — T-D-P forty-three deleted from forebrain excitatory neurons. They used CRISPR to delete the U-N-C thirteen A cryptic exon out of the genome entirely, so that transcript can never be corrupted, and crossed it into the knockout. Behavior came back: novel-object recognition restored, social novelty preference restored, in animals that had lost both. The headline is real. Preventing this one splicing event is sufficient to preserve memory.

And then the same paper counts neurons, and the neurons are still dying. Hippocampal counts in animals lacking the cryptic exon were indistinguishable from untreated knockouts and well below controls, with caspase-3 activation unchanged. Fixing U-N-C thirteen A bought function and bought no survival. In drug-development language: symptomatic, not disease-modifying.

Paper two takes the A-L-S compartment — T-D-P forty-three deleted from spinal motor neurons — and runs the identical genetic experiment. There, deleting the U-N-C thirteen A cryptic exon bought nothing at all. Grip strength, body weight, hindlimb paralysis at six to eight months, moribund at six to nine months, motor neuron loss — every one of them indistinguishable from the untreated knockout.

They then explain why, and the explanation is the most portable thing in either paper. U-N-C thirteen A is barely expressed in the spinal cord. They show it in mouse protein, then in human tissue from the G-Tex project — cervical cord against three brain regions, hundreds of donors each. After correcting every sample for how much of it is actually neurons, cortex still carries three times the U-N-C thirteen A of spinal cord. Run the same correction on S-T-M-N two, the other fashionable cryptic target, and the apparent spinal deficit mostly evaporates. The honest reading: U-N-C thirteen A is a brain target that the field has been aiming at a spinal disease, and S-T-M-N two is the better candidate for the cord.

Now the part that makes this a sourcing lead rather than a competitive note. Wong's lab has its own molecule, and it is not an oligonucleotide. They call it C-T-R — a chimeric splicing repressor built by taking the R-N-A-recognition domain of T-D-P forty-three, throwing away its aggregation-prone low-complexity domain, and fusing on a different splicing regulator. It does the whole job, not one exon. And they built a safety feature into it that I have not seen done this cleanly: they included T-D-P forty-three's own three-prime autoregulatory element in the cassette, so the construct silences itself in any cell that still has normal T-D-P forty-three and only accumulates where the protein is missing. Vector copy number stays flat at two to three per cell; expression is conditional on the disease state. That is a self-limiting gene therapy, and it is patentable subject matter in its own right.

The efficacy numbers are the kind you read twice. Delivered intravenously with a blood-brain-barrier-crossing A-A-V, at symptom onset — the animals already had tremors — it transduced about seventy-nine percent of lumbar motor neurons. Grip strength stayed normal. Muscle atrophy attenuated, ventral-root large fibers preserved. And survival: every control-vector animal was moribund at about forty-seven weeks; every C-T-R animal was alive past a hundred and ten weeks, none with limb paralysis. In the brain paper, the same construct into the ventricle rescued hippocampal neuron number roughly in proportion to how many neurons it reached.

Be candid about what is wrong with it, because there is one thing and it is big. The capsid they used does not cross the human blood-brain barrier. The authors say so themselves. So the biology is de-risked and the delivery is not, which means the gating item on this program is a capsid deal or a direct-to-C-S-F route, not another mouse. Second limit: mouse and human cryptic exons mostly differ, and U-N-C thirteen A is a lucky overlap that sits in a different intron in each species, so the sufficiency result travels better than the specific exon does.

The verdict. Hemaka and Jon should take this meeting, and the agenda is not "is the science good." The agenda is three questions. First, what is the license status of the chimeric repressor — Wong's foundational estate dates to twenty sixteen and his most recent mechanism paper declared no competing interests, which is the kind of negative signal that says a provisional may still be open. Second, what would it cost to get this into a human-B-B-B capsid, and is that an in-license or a partnership. Third, and this one is internal: NuGate is our antisense company in this exact indication, and these two papers just set a sufficiency bar for single-target splicing correction. We should know, this week, whether NuGate's target sits above or below that bar. That question is worth more to us than the license.

Second lead, and it is early but the shape of the asset is unusually clean.

Seunghyun Lee and Chulan Kwon, in the Division of Cardiology at Johns Hopkins, with collaborators at Yonsei, posted a platform paper Thursday. They call it SEME — S-E-M-E, for spliceable editable microexon element. The problem they are solving is that every way we currently fix a broken transcript costs you something. Exon skipping restores the reading frame by deleting coding sequence. Editing inside a coding exon risks leaving small insertions and deletions right in the protein.

Their move is to stop touching exons. SEME is a modular cassette — a three-prime splice acceptor, a programmable microexon of twenty bases or less, and a five-prime splice donor — knocked into an intron. The cell's own splicing machinery then splices your microexon into the mature message, and any editing scar at the junction gets spliced out with the intron rather than carried into the protein.

The demonstration is a dilated-cardiomyopathy variant in F-L-N-C, the gene for filamin C, where a single base change costs the transcript five nucleotides out of exon twelve, shifts the frame and destroys the message. They put SEME into F-L-N-C intron twelve — only ninety bases long, a genuinely tight space — supplied the five missing nucleotides as the microexon, and recovered filamin C protein, sarcomere organization and calcium handling in iPS-cell-derived cardiomyocytes. They used AlphaGenome to tune the splice-site flanks, which is the most sensible use of a genomic foundation model I have seen in a therapeutics paper this year.

The limits are the authors' own and they are honest: in vitro only, one variant, engineered isogenic cells rather than patient cells, it still needs a nuclease to get in, and synthetic splice sites could perturb splicing elsewhere.

So what is actually here for us, honestly. Not a program — there is no delivery, no second gene, no in vivo. What is here is a cassette architecture, which is composition of matter on an engineered element rather than a biology position. That is the rarer and more defensible thing, and it is the same category as Aletira's platform, which also buys control over expression by writing splicing grammar into a construct. The instrument is a translational grant, not a license, and it buys exactly three experiments: a second gene in a different intronic environment, transcriptome-wide splicing quality control, and a delivery answer. Virginia and Avi — and note this already carries N-I-H heart-institute money and two Maryland Stem Cell Research Fund awards, so the state is in it and Johns Hopkins owns it. Worth a call to Hopkins Tech Ventures on whether the cassette itself has been filed, separately from the F-L-N-C application.

Third, briefly, and this is continuity rather than discovery.

Two weeks ago we led the Radar with Achuth Padmanabhan's lab at U-M-B-C and the Greenebaum cancer center, on U-S-P fifteen as the first identified regulator of Z-N-F two seventeen stability in ovarian cancer. On Monday the same lab posted a second paper, and it changes the commercial framing of that lead. Z-N-F two seventeen overexpression strongly raises the HER2 receptor — which sounds like a patient-selection story for HER2 drugs — and then those same cells turn out to be resistant to multiple HER2 inhibitors. The mechanism is that Z-N-F two seventeen also raises a set of other receptor kinases, most notably A-X-L, and raises E-R-K itself, producing a signaling state that survives HER2 blockade. Targeting the A-X-L and E-R-K axis cut viability and metastatic behavior in culture, and reduced tumor burden and extended survival in xenografts.

One caveat: the body of that paper has not rendered, so this is read off the abstract and I am not characterizing effect sizes. Two reads. Receptor abundance is not a drug-response biomarker, and this lab just demonstrated that in its own system — carry that discipline into any target pitched to us on expression data alone. And it sharpens the question Esther should put to U-M Ventures: is the licensable object the U-S-P fifteen chemistry, or a negative predictive biomarker for HER2-directed therapy in ovarian cancer? Those are two different companies and two different filings. A-X-L is also a crowded and historically disappointing target class, which is worth saying before anyone gets excited about the combination.

That is the radar. One lead worth a real meeting, one platform worth a grant, and one existing lead that got more interesting and more complicated in the same week. Have a good Sunday.
