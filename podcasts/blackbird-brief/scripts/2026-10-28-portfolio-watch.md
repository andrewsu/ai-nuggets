## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Wednesday, October twenty-eighth. The most important development for the portfolio is that a competitor in autoimmune cell therapy shut down its lead program yesterday with three hundred and nineteen million dollars still in the bank — and what matters for Winnow is not the two deaths. It is the one sentence underneath them.

Artiva Biotherapeutics, San Diego, announced yesterday morning that it is discontinuing Allo-N-K, its allogeneic natural killer cell therapy, which it was running in combination with rituximab in rheumatoid arthritis and other B-cell-driven autoimmune diseases. It is cancelling the planned Phase Three. It is cutting roughly ninety-four percent of its workforce — a hundred and fifteen employees as of June thirtieth, so somewhere around a hundred and eight people, leaving fewer than ten. Leerink is engaged, and the stated options run from a merger or an asset sale all the way to dissolution and liquidation.

Two Grade Five events. The first was a fatal neurological event — leukoencephalopathy — in the Phase Two-a basket trial, assessed by the investigator and an independent safety review as likely related to fludarabine toxicity. Note what that is. Fludarabine is not the product. It is the protocol-specified lymphodepleting conditioning, given with cyclophosphamide before the cells. The FDA asked Artiva to pause enrollment and dosing after that report, and Artiva paused the Phase Three as well. The second death was a sudden death about two months after the start of treatment, assessed as unrelated to any component of the regimen, cause undetermined.

Now the sentence underneath. Updated data showed deep B-cell depletion and clinical activity consistent with everything previously reported. But the longer-term follow-up did not meet the durability threshold the company required to continue. The depth worked. The duration did not.

So what for Winnow, and this is for Jon and Anthony. Three things, in order of how much they should change what we say.

First, the attribution row. On October fifth we closed the open item on whether the autoimmune cell therapy deaths were a manufacturing problem or an inherent liability, and the answer then was manufacturing — accelerated protocols over-stimulating T cells. We reopened that yesterday on the Bristol Myers day-forty-four death. Today a third variable arrives, and it is cleaner than either. Allo-N-K is not genetically modified. It is not a C-A-R. There is no rapid-manufacturing story available here, and the fatal event is attributed to the conditioning chemotherapy. Fludarabine-containing lymphodepletion is used across essentially every cell therapy program in autoimmunity, and Artiva says it intends to publish the case for exactly that reason. This is the first structural argument we have had rather than a rhetorical one: an engager does not require lymphodepletion. That is not a claim about our molecule being gentler. It is a claim about a whole toxicity category that our route does not carry.

Second, and more important commercially, the durability failure. We have spent two months defending selectivity on the safety axis. Artiva just failed on the other one — it achieved deep depletion, confirmed the clinical activity, and still could not hold the response long enough to justify a Phase Three. On October nineteenth we wrote that Winnow has to differentiate on depth plus durability, one course, durable drug-free remission. Half of that statement just got harder to make, because a one-shot cell product that depletes deeply did not deliver it. The honest version of our pitch is now the redosable one: an engager you can give again is a different answer to a durability failure than a cell product you cannot. That is a real advantage and we were not leading with it.

Third, for Maisha and Emily, the governance read. Three hundred and nineteen million dollars in cash, and the board stopped anyway. That is the Sionna template from September at a larger cash balance — named decision owner, risk-benefit review, replacement plan or liquidation, said out loud. Add it to the memo.

Second item, and it lands on two of our early-stage themes in the same week. Two biotechs set terms on Monday for Nasdaq offerings pricing this week — both raising a hundred and fifty million dollars, both nine point four million shares at fifteen to seventeen dollars. Aspen Neuroscience is Parkinson's: an autologous therapy derived from induced pluripotent stem cells made from the patient's own skin biopsy, a single dose, designed to replace lost dopaminergic neurons. Fifteen patients dosed as of mid-August, regenerative medicine advanced therapy and fast track designations, Phase Three dosing anticipated in the second half of twenty twenty-seven. Founded in 2018. The other is Bambusa Therapeutics, which we logged on October tenth off its S-One — long-acting bispecific antibodies for immunology and inflammation, including one against interleukin-four receptor alpha and interleukin-five in C-O-P-D. Founded in 2024, lead assets licensed from BioNTech's Chinese subsidiary.

Here is the number worth carrying. At the midpoint, Aspen would be valued at four hundred and ninety-four million dollars fully diluted. Bambusa would be valued at eight hundred and thirty-seven million. The in-licensed, two-year-old, fast-follower immunology company is worth about seventy percent more than the eight-year-old company with its own platform, a de novo cell therapy, two FDA designations and a pivotal in sight.

For Hemaka and Eddie, that is a pricing fact about what public investors reward, and it is uncomfortable for a studio model that creates assets rather than acquiring them. The market is paying for modality familiarity and addressable population, not for origination or for years of work. It does not mean we should buy assets instead of building them. It does mean that when we model a spinout's next round, the comparable is not the company that looks like us scientifically — it is the company that looks like us commercially. One more thing in the Bambusa prospectus for Jon: their fourth program is a B-cell-depleting bispecific for autoimmune disease, preclinical. Another entrant walking into the same room.

Third, for Avi and the Aletira team, and this is the most directly useful regulatory read of the week. BioSpace ran a feature on Monday on the two-horse race to the first gene therapy for Rett syndrome. Taysha has completed dosing of seventeen girls in its pivotal, with a submission possible in the first half of twenty twenty-seven. Neurogene has completed dosing of twenty-five patients aged three and older in its registrational trial, data in the second half of twenty twenty-seven. Both report one hundred percent response rates in their earlier trials — every patient meeting more than one developmental milestone.

Two things to take. The first is the design. Neurogene's pivotal, which the FDA signed off on, uses baseline controls — the patient as her own comparator — in a rare neurodevelopmental disease with no randomized arm. That is precisely the instrument the Ultragenyx comment letter asked the agency to name in the substantial-evidence redraft, which we logged on September twenty-sixth, and it is the ask standing in the Aletira pre-I-N-D. It is no longer a theoretical request. There is a registrational trial running on it, in the central nervous system, with far messier endpoints than ours. Cite it by name.

The second is the warning. Rett already has an approved drug, Acadia's Daybue, cleared in 2023 on a significant Phase Three. The Rett Syndrome Research Trust's own fact sheet says sixty-one percent of patients taking it did not improve, and only thirteen percent were rated much improved. A trial can win on its instrument and lose in the clinic. Our advantage is that auditory brainstem response thresholds are machine-measured, per ear, with an untreated ear available as its own control — we are not exposed the way a behavioral composite is. Say that in the pre-I-N-D narrative rather than assuming the reviewer infers it.

Fourth, briefly, an update on a thread rather than a new one. Novartis reported third-quarter results yesterday and took a one point nine billion dollar partial impairment on the Avidity acquisition after del-desiran missed its Phase Three primary in myotonic dystrophy. Vas Narasimhan called it "the mathematical shift of our assumption," said they remain very happy with the twelve-billion-dollar deal, and is putting the asset into another pivotal program. For anyone modeling a platform acquisition: a failed lead asset produced a sixteen percent write-down, not a reversal.

The line that is ours came later on the same call. The rapcabtagene autoleucel trials — the cell therapy studies paused in immunology and neurology after three deaths — remain on hold in the United States and Europe, with no further safety events, and Narasimhan expects to identify the root cause "in the coming months." So the sponsor best positioned to settle the manufacturing-attribution question we reopened on Monday has gone more than two months without an answer and is guiding to months more. Jon, stop waiting for that adjudication. Build the argument out of what Artiva gave us yesterday, which is a mechanism and a forthcoming publication rather than an inference.

And a closer for Eddie and Matt, on the exit environment our spinouts are walking into. Leerink's latest analysis puts large-cap pharma acquisition firepower at about a hundred and eighty-eight billion dollars of cash on hand. Johnson and Johnson leads with twenty-one billion and is the single most likely buyer on cash, market cap and management commentary combined. Pharma has spent ninety-seven billion dollars across twenty-eight deals so far this year, against a 2009-to-2025 average of nineteen deals and sixty-nine billion. The active therapeutic areas named are immunology and inflammation, oncology, rare disease and central nervous system — four for four against our portfolio — while cardiovascular and metabolic deal-making is declining. Pair that with issuance: through three quarters, biopharma raised seven and a half billion dollars across twenty-two offerings against eight offerings in all of last year, and the biotech index is up nearly thirty percent for the year but was flat in the third quarter. The window is open and has stopped improving.

The read is specific, Eddie. J and J's chief executive said publicly in September that the company can grow double digits this decade without acquisitions, and that the point of doing them is to fortify the next decade. That is a named buyer with twenty-one billion dollars saying he wants assets at the stage Blackbird produces them, in the four areas we work in. That is a business-development conversation, not a market-conditions slide.

Quiet today on Aletira's own program, on the GPR52 NewCo, on Clasp, NuGate, Artemyx, Biolinco, D-E-L-F-I and 1104health, and nothing dated from Johns Hopkins, U-M-B or the Lieber Institute inside the window. Have a good Wednesday.
