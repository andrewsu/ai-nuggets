## Script

This is the Blackbird Brief Sourcing Radar for Saturday, July twenty-fifth, and today's lead is the cleanest company-shaped paper we've seen out of U-M-B in weeks: a Maryland-funded, patent-filed cancer immunotherapy conjugate from a lab that has just set up shop inside the University of Maryland's new neuroscience institute.

Let me give you the honest context first, because it frames the day. This is now the fifth straight thin week across the Johns Hopkins, U-M-B, and Lieber preprint servers — the four-day corresponding-author sweep turned up almost nothing with a fresh, ownable asset attached, and the peer-reviewed journals keep landing clinical trials and reviews rather than founding-shaped biology. But this Saturday we caught a real break: one preprint whose full text I could actually read cover to cover, and it's a good one.

The lead comes out of Jung Soo Suk's lab. They've built a new immune-stimulating antibody conjugate — the field calls these I-SACs — and here's the science in plain terms. C-D forty-seven is the "don't eat me" flag that tumors wave at macrophages; block it with an antibody and macrophages start eating the tumor. The whole field has chased exactly this — Gilead's magrolimab, ALX Oncology, Pfizer's Trillium program — and the recurring disappointment is that eating the tumor turns out to be immunologically silent. The macrophage engulfs the cancer cell and nothing durable happens downstream — you don't get lasting T-cell immunity, so the tumor comes back. Suk's move is to hand the macrophage a second instruction at the exact moment it's digesting the tumor. They chemically bolt a T-L-R-nine agonist — a short C-p-G DNA fragment — onto the anti-C-D-forty-seven antibody. And the elegant part is the "where." T-L-R-nine only lives inside the endosome, the same internal compartment where the engulfed tumor gets broken down. So the conjugate guarantees that the identical macrophage that just ate the tumor also receives the danger signal, right where the antigens are being processed. You cannot get that spatial coordination by simply co-dosing two separate drugs — and that's the whole thesis.

The data back it up. The conjugate flips macrophages into an inflammatory, anti-tumor state, drives them to cross-present tumor antigen and prime C-D-eight killer T cells — which the naked antibody flatly does not do. In mice, systemic dosing cleared a human lymphoma graft, and when they wiped out the macrophages the entire effect vanished, so it's genuinely macrophage-driven. In fully immune-competent lymphoma and in triple-negative breast cancer — including a lung-metastasis model — they got deep regressions, near-complete blockade of metastasis, and, most important, durable memory: mice cured of lymphoma rejected a re-challenge more than three months later.

Now the "so what for Blackbird." Three things make this a real sourcing lead and not just a nice paper. First, the differentiation. Every other I-SAC in the clinic — Bolt Biotherapeutics and that cohort — hangs its payload off a tumor-specific antibody like HER2 or C-D-twenty-two, which locks you into one antigen and needs Fc-receptor engagement to work. Suk's conjugate rides C-D forty-seven, a checkpoint that sits on essentially every tumor, and the authors show it works without Fc engagement — meaning you can build it on an Fc-silent backbone and dial down the systemic toxicity that has dogged this class. Antigen-agnostic plus Fc-independent is a genuinely differentiated platform position. Second, and this is the part that should get Eddie on the phone: the competing-interest statement says Suk and his co-author are inventors on a patent application on this exact conjugate technology, and the work was funded by the Maryland Innovation Initiative through TEDCO, alongside an N-I-H R-01. So there's already state translational money in it and a live filing — which means the U-M Ventures licensing window is open right now. Third, the ecosystem read: Suk came up through the Johns Hopkins Center for Nanomedicine cluster and is now running this program out of U-M-B's new Medicine Institute for Neuroscience Discovery. That's a serious nanomedicine builder planting a flag on the U-M Ventures side of the fence — exactly the co-investment surface we've been mapping with the Chao and Martin programs. My action for Avi and Yixuan: pressure-test freedom-to-operate against the existing anti-C-D-forty-seven antibody estate and the I-SAC linker patents, because the conjugate chemistry is where the value and the risk both sit. This is the strongest U-M-B lead of the month.

Second pick, quicker, and I'll flag up front that I'm going off the abstract here — it's a just-published Cancer Letters paper behind the Elsevier wall, so treat this as a lead to chase, not a finished read. It's from Christina Ferrer's group at U-M-B's Greenebaum Cancer Center, with Elana Fertig — who's already on our radar — as a co-author. They've been tracking a slow-cycling, highly metastatic subpopulation in pancreatic cancer marked by an enzyme called G-S-T-T-one, and this paper ties that population to a stem-like state through C-D one-thirty-three and shows it's uniquely sensitive to F-G-F-R inhibitors. In patient-derived organoids, co-expression of G-S-T-T-one and C-D one-thirty-three predicted response to nintedanib, an already-approved multi-kinase inhibitor. Here's why that's interesting for us: G-S-T-T-one is famously polymorphic — a large fraction of people carry a whole-gene deletion — so it's a ready-made stratification biomarker. The commercial shape isn't a new molecule; it's a companion-biomarker-plus-repurposed-drug play in pancreatic cancer, sitting on the same U-M Ventures co-investment surface. Worth a diligence look at whether Greenebaum has filed on the biomarker, but lower conviction than the Suk program until we can read the full paper.

I'll leave you with one watch item on the Hopkins side. Shigeki Watanabe and David Bergles posted a striking neuroscience preprint describing a brand-new way microglia clean up neurons — they pinch off and extract oversized waste organelles from neuronal processes, a process the authors name skoupocytosis, and they pin the trigger on a specific lipid enzyme, A-B-H-D-sixteen-A. There's no asset and no I-P disclosed, and the body text hasn't rendered yet so I couldn't read it in full — but a defined, druggable enzyme controlling neuronal proteostasis is exactly the kind of target biology that seeds a neurodegeneration company, so I've flagged it to re-read the moment the full text posts.
