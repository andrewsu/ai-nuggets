Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Friday, September eleventh. Yesterday the market priced Aletira's layer at two hundred seventy-five million dollars. Today a competing answer to the same problem arrived from a different direction — the capsid — wrapped in a license agreement that also tells us what a bolt-on layer is worth.

Modalis Therapeutics, a Tokyo-listed company with research operations in Massachusetts, licensed an engineered A-A-V capsid from Exegenesis Bio for its Duchenne program. Their payload is epigenome editing — a CRISPR system that turns gene expression up without cutting D-N-A. It activates utrophin, a close cousin of dystrophin that broadly covers muscle fibers early in development, and because utrophin sits on a different gene, activating it works regardless of which dystrophin mutation the patient carries.

The delivery half is the one for us. That capsid is highly tropic for muscle and, critically, liver-detargeting — engineered to reduce exposure to off-target organs. Geoff, Hugh, Avi: yesterday, off the Encoded round, we wrote that expression control governs neither the capsid nor the dose. Today the counter-argument shows up as a transaction. This program buys its selectivity at the capsid, and by that route it reaches exactly the two things SELEXON does not touch — which organ sees the vector, and how much of it that organ has to see. Two selectivity layers now compete for the same slide in the same deck, and ours is not the one that speaks to dose.

So our differentiation sentence has to be the thing a capsid cannot do, and there is a clean one. A capsid picks an organ; SELEXON picks a cell type inside an organ. Nobody has an engineered capsid that distinguishes cochlear supporting cells from the hair cells beside them, or inhibitory interneurons from the excitatory neuron next door. That is the entire residual problem once you have arrived in the right tissue — and it makes hearing loss the right place to be standing for a reason beyond the biology. An intracochlear injection already solves organ targeting geographically: local delivery, small total dose, liver largely out of the picture. In the ear, tropism and liver-detargeting buy very little, because the anatomy has already bought them. What is left is cell-type discrimination — the open Skylark question from the eighteenth of August, where connexin twenty-six has to land in supporting cells rather than hair cells and at roughly the right level. So our lead indication is the setting where the competing layer is worth least and ours is worth most. Put it that way in the Series A deck.

Eddie, Emily — the deal structure is the less comfortable read. A bolt-on delivery layer got licensed into somebody else's program as a research collaboration, and Modalis said in the same release that the financial impact on its current fiscal year is immaterial. That is the public comparable for "bolt-on to any vector" as a business model, and the comparable is small — the same lesson the twelve-million-dollar Pfizer conjugate taught us on the fifth, arriving now from the licensee's side. Aletira has to be a product company with SELEXON inside it; out-licensing the layer is a second revenue line, not the valuation. And one pattern worth naming: Encoded turns up the patient's own sodium channel gene, Modalis turns up the patient's own utrophin. Neither delivers a gene; both deliver a switch. Two independent programs chose to carry almost nothing inside the vector, which makes our cassette-length question a pattern rather than one competitor's choice.

Second item is the chronic-infections theme, and it is the third distinct instrument in eleven days built by somebody other than us. The F-D-A granted Breakthrough Device Designation to Nutromics for a wearable vancomycin monitoring patch — D-N-A-based sensors on microneedles, reading drug concentration continuously and in real time instead of by periodic blood draw, with first-in-human data in Nature Biotechnology in February and launch targeted for next year.

Yixuan, Eddie, Avi — take the three instruments together, because each answers a different question. Astek, four miles from us at the University of Maryland BioPark, answers which drug, in under an hour, in a static assay. The micro-bladder work we covered on the seventh answers whether a drug works in a physiologically realistic organ — under flow, against intracellular reservoirs. Nutromics answers how much drug is in this patient right now. Our live lead on this theme, the beta-lactam tolerance work in the Chatterjee lab at U-M-B, needs the middle question: a tolerance phenotype read under conditions that resemble an actual infection. And that is the only one of the three with no commercial owner, no designation and no launch date. The instrumentation around our theme is filling in from both ends and leaving the middle open — either the gap we build in, or the reason this theme stays a research program. One addendum from the Maryland innovator list published yesterday: Astek's chief science officer was named, and the platform is expanding past urine into spinal fluid, effluent and blood. The overlap with us is widening, so have the conversation we flagged on the first while it is still a collaboration and not somebody else's differentiator.

Third item is a first-read item for Eddie. Breakthrough T1D convened nearly thirty international experts and published, yesterday in Diabetologia, the first international consensus guidance on general-population screening for islet autoantibodies to find early-stage type one diabetes — endorsed by twenty global diabetes societies, recommending a first screen at two to four years old during routine preventive visits and rescreening of the negatives twice more through adolescence. Two or more persistent autoantibodies means stage one or two disease, and progression to clinical disease is then highly likely.

This lands on Islex Therapeutics, the Baltimore Hopkins spinout out of Dax Fu's lab that we put on the first-read list in July, and it cuts two ways. The first is a coincidence of biology: zinc transporter eight is one of the autoantibodies this guidance tells pediatricians to look for, and it is also the beta-cell surface address the Fu lab's antibody chassis uses to home to the islet. The autoantibody that finds the patient and the epitope that delivers the drug are the same epitope.

The second is the candid one, and it is a positioning question rather than a science objection. The guidance builds a machine for finding people in stage one and two — pre-symptomatic, beta cells still present. Islex's stated indications are stage three, late-onset autoimmune diabetes in adults, and severe insulin-dependent type two, and its mechanism is beta-cell preservation. Preservation is worth the most precisely where this screening pathway delivers patients, and the company is currently pointed downstream of it. Much cheaper to ask on a first call than after a Series A.

Anthony, Jon — the read generalizes. On the eighth we wrote, off Parkinson's, that the absent finding mechanism is why that field has a graveyard. Type one diabetes just showed the other outcome: twenty societies endorsed the finding mechanism, at named ages, inside routine pediatric care, ahead of the drugs. Put that to any program claiming an early-intervention indication, and ask who is building their version of it.

Fourth item touches Clasp and Adventris. On Monday, BridgeBio Oncology reported new data for an oral KRAS G-twelve-C inhibitor that binds both the active and inactive states, and simultaneously narrowed its strategy. Its monotherapy numbers in patients who had never had a KRAS inhibitor were strong — sixty-three percent responded, disease control was one hundred percent. Yet what it chose to prioritize is the Keytruda combination in patients who had already failed a KRAS inhibitor, where the response rate was seventy-five percent at the top dose in seventeen patients. With three hundred forty-four million dollars and runway into twenty twenty-eight, that is a choice, not a constraint, and trade coverage Wednesday reported the stock sliding on the narrowed scope.

Jon, Avi — a well-capitalized company with a differentiated binder and a sixty-three percent response rate in inhibitor-naive patients declined to compete in the naive setting, and went to the post-inhibitor patient in combination with a checkpoint inhibitor. That is exactly the position Clasp chose for its KRAS G-twelve-V engager, which preclinically kills harder against inhibitor-resistant tumors and is enhanced by combination with the small molecules. So the positioning is validated by a competitor's capital allocation — and at the same time, everyone is converging on that population, and a once-daily pill plus Keytruda will get there first and set the bar. Carry the eligible-fraction row with it: the pill needs one gate, the mutation; our engager needs two multiplied gates, the mutation and the H-L-A type. And the market marked that stock down anyway for narrowing, which tells you how it currently prices "we will take the refractory population" as a strategy. For Adventris the read runs friendlier — if the small molecules hold the naive setting and then converge on the resistant one, the interception and adjuvant window a peptide vaccine plays in is the one stretch of that timeline nothing in this announcement contests.

Fifth item is governance, and it is the consequence of something we covered two days ago. Eight Novartis shareholders told Reuters that the Phase three failure of the myotonic dystrophy drug — the second major setback in days — raised questions about last year's twelve-billion-dollar Avidity purchase and other deals. The shares fell eleven percent in a day, roughly thirty billion dollars of market value, erasing the year's gains. Artisan Partners' managing director said publicly that the party is over and the chairman needs to act, calling for a board shakeup and an overhaul of dealmaking. And the reason this cannot resolve quietly is the patent cliff: three of their biggest products come off patent between twenty twenty-nine and twenty thirty-one.

Eddie, Emily, Hemaka — this is the buy side of our own exit. Novartis cannot stop buying; the replacement revenue has to come from somewhere. What changes under board pressure is not the number of deals but the terms and the evidence bar — more weight on a human proof-of-concept, less on a platform narrative, more of the money moved out of upfront and into milestones. That is the Pfizer-conjugate lesson from the fifth arriving from the acquirer's governance rather than from the asset, and it doubles the case for the shelf screen we said out loud we do not run: a pressured acquirer pays less for what we sell and divests more of what we could buy. And Emily — on the seventh we asked who here has authority to kill a running program. This is the other half: who is accountable for the deals that were done.

Sixth and last, a Hopkins item with a transferable lesson. CraniUS Therapeutics, a Baltimore neurotechnology company founded in twenty twenty-one as a Johns Hopkins spinout, won F-D-A clearance yesterday for a patient-specific cranioplasty plate, individually designed to each patient's anatomy. The founder and chief medical officer is Chad Gordon, the Hopkins surgeon who essentially created the field of neuroplastic surgery. Their longer-term platform is a skull-embedded implant with refillable reservoirs, a miniature pump and wireless charging, intended to deliver drugs into the brain and bypass the blood-brain barrier; they closed a twenty-million-dollar Series B in January.

Esther, Virginia, Avi — the transferable thing is the regulatory sequencing, and their own language gives it away. They call this a cleared, manufacturable base, and describe the architecture as configurable by intent, so brain access, diagnostics and drug delivery each attach to that base as each is authorized on its own. They found the one component of a moonshot that could clear by itself, cleared it, and now everything ambitious bolts onto something that already carries a clearance, a manufacturing process and a quality system. Any Blackbird program with a device or combination-product element should be asked which of its pieces can clear first and what that clearance buys the rest. Hold the second read loosely: what they are building is a route, and its customers are central-nervous-system assets that die on brain exposure rather than on mechanism. Nothing in our portfolio needs it today, but a locally implanted, refillable, programmable reservoir being built four miles from the BioHub is worth knowing about before a program does. The honest limits are one clearance on the simplest component and every interesting module still investigational.

Quiet where it matters. Nothing on G-P-R fifty-two or Nxera, and that out-license window has under four months left. Nothing from Skylark or Sensorion in hearing loss. Nothing direct on Aletira, Clasp, Winnow, NuGate, Biolinco, eleven-oh-four health or Artemyx — today's reads on those are all inferential.

To close, the selectivity question got a second answer today, and it came from the capsid rather than the transcript. The competing layer buys an organ and a dose; ours buys a cell type within an organ. That is a narrower claim than the one in our deck, it is a true one, and it is strongest exactly where we chose to start — in an ear you inject directly, where the anatomy has already paid for everything a capsid could sell you. Narrower and true beats broad and falsifiable, which is the same lesson yesterday's safety data taught us, arriving today through the business model.

That is the Portfolio Watch for Friday, September eleventh. Have a good weekend — Sunday brings a Sourcing Radar.
