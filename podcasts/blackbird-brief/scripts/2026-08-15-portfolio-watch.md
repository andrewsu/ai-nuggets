Now the science, because this is the part that changes the K-RAS picture I've been drawing for you. Clasp's platform builds what are essentially T-cell-receptor-mimic bispecifics — they call them precise H-L-A redirecting engagers. In plain terms: most T-cell engagers grab a protein sitting on the tumor's surface, which is a problem because those proteins usually sit on healthy cells too, and you get toxicity. Clasp instead targets the little peptide fragments of mutant driver proteins that the tumor displays on its H-L-A — the fragments that are genuinely tumor-specific because they come from the cancer's own driver mutations. One arm of the molecule clamps onto that mutant-peptide-plus-H-L-A complex, the other arm grabs a T cell, and the T cell kills only the cell showing the mutation. It's off-the-shelf, antibody-like, and the whole selling point is absolute specificity for the mutation.

And here's why it belongs on our K-RAS board specifically. Clasp's lead program in the clinic targets the most common p-fifty-three mutation, the R-one-seventy-five-H variant, and it's in a first-in-human Phase 1 — the GUARDIAN-101 study — in advanced solid tumors, with the first patient dosed back in the spring of 2025. That means a Phase 1 monotherapy readout on this whole platform is a genuine near-term catalyst — expected this year — and it's the first real-world test of whether targeting an intracellular driver through H-L-A actually produces clean, potent killing in patients. But the second program is the one that ties directly to aSKY: Clasp unveiled a K-RAS T-cell engager this spring at the cancer research meeting, aimed at the K-RAS G-twelve-V mutation presented on a common H-L-A type, and that program has now moved into its own Phase 1. The preclinical story on it is the interesting part — they showed it kills harder against tumors that have grown resistant to the small-molecule K-RAS inhibitors, and it gets better when you combine it with those inhibitors. In other words, it's positioned as something you layer on top of, or after, the daraxonrasib-style drugs, not something that competes head-to-head with them.
