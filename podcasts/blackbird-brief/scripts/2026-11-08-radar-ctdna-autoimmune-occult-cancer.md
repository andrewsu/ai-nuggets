## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Sourcing Radar for Sunday, November eighth. Three picks, all read in full, all Hopkins or Hopkins-affiliated. The lead is a blood test that finds cancer in autoimmune patients a year before any scan does, out of a lab we are already in business with three times over. The second is a result that should change how we read every methylation pitch that comes through the door. The third is a small study whose dropout rate is more useful to us than its finding.

Start with the lead. It posted Friday on medRxiv from the Hopkins School of Medicine, and the author list tells you most of what you need to know before you read a word of it: Christopher Mecoli and Ami Shah from rheumatology, Antony Rosen and Livia Casciola-Rosen, and then Chetan Bettegowda, Nickolas Papadopoulos, Kenneth Kinzler and Bert Vogelstein.

The biology is an elegant idea. Systemic sclerosis and dermatomyositis have long been suspected of being, in some patients, a response to a cancer — the immune reaction to a nascent tumor strong enough to hold it in check or destroy it, leaving an autoimmune disease behind. If that is right, then at the moment the rheumatic disease declares itself, some of these patients are carrying a cancer too small for anyone to see. The paper asks whether you can find it in blood.

They enrolled a hundred and thirty-four patients with new-onset disease, ran a targeted sequencing panel over commonly mutated cancer genes on plasma, and followed them for eighteen to twenty-four months. They were careful about the obvious confounder: sixty-eight mutations attributable to clonal hematopoiesis were excluded before anything was scored.

Six patients developed cancer. Circulating tumor DNA was present in four of the six, at a median of thirteen months before the clinical diagnosis — the range runs from six weeks to seventeen months — and in three of those four, the mutation found in plasma was the same mutation later found in the patient's own tumor. In two of the four, a diagnostic PET-CT performed around the time of the positive blood test was unremarkable. A positive test carried an eleven-fold higher risk of malignancy.

Now the honest half, because it is the whole commercial question. Sixteen patients with no cancer diagnosis also tested positive. So about fifteen percent of this population is positive, and only one in five of those positives has yet produced a cancer. The authors are straightforward: a twenty percent positive predictive value beats mammography's and spiral CT's and is still, in their words, lower than optimal from an interventional standpoint. They offer both explanations — that follow-up was only seventeen months and some of these patients may be in exactly the immune equilibrium the hypothesis predicts, or that these are biological false positives — and do not pretend to know which is right.

Three reads, and the first is the one I would act on.

The hardest problem in blood-based early cancer detection has never been the assay. It is the population. Screen everybody and a twenty percent positive predictive value is a catastrophe — a river of scans and biopsies in healthy people. This population is already defined by a clinician, already enrolled in intensive cancer surveillance because the guidelines say so, and already enriched for occult malignancy. That is the structural trick, and it generalizes. Virginia, Esther — the sourcing question is not who else is doing cell-free DNA. It is which other clinician-identified populations sit at that same intersection of elevated baseline risk and existing surveillance burden, because there a test with this performance is not a screening test at all. It is a triage test telling the clinician where to point imaging they were going to order anyway.

The second read is about who owns it, and it is unusually clean to state. The declaration of interests says the work may be the subject of patent applications filed by Johns Hopkins, and that related technologies are already licensed out. Kinzler, Vogelstein and Papadopoulos are founders of Thrive, now part of Exact Sciences, and of Haystack Oncology, now part of Quest. The realistic read is that the panel technology underneath this is spoken for. But look further down the same disclosure, because this is the part for Matt and Eddie: the same three are founders of, consultants to, or equity holders in Clasp, in CagePharma, and in Winnow. Those are three positions on our own cap tables. We are not prospecting a new relationship here. We are reading the newest output of a lab that has already produced three of our investments, and the useful question is not whether to license this paper. It is whether anybody on our side has asked that group what they think is coming next, before it is announced.

The third read is for Artemyx, and it is a framing gift. Our thesis there is cell-free DNA for chronic disease, and the standing objection is that chronic disease does not give you the clean signal cancer does. This paper inverts the relationship in a way worth stealing. Here the chronic disease is not the thing being measured — it is the enrollment criterion. The autoimmune diagnosis identifies the patient; the cancer provides the signal. That is a cleaner commercial story than measuring the chronic disease directly, and if a version of it exists in the Artemyx indication set, it is worth half a day of thinking.

Second pick, and it is a diligence instrument more than a company. From the Kimmel Cancer Center at Hopkins, with Hariharan Easwaran and Stephen Baylin as senior authors and Thomas Pisanic and Tza-Huei Wang from the engineering side on the assay: a study of whether an oncogene actually causes the DNA methylation changes we all associate with cancer.

The received picture is that cancer-associated methylation gains at CpG-island promoters, especially the ones marked by Polycomb, are driven by oncogenic signaling and are early events in transformation. That belief is load-bearing for a whole category of diagnostic product. To test it they built a low-input, genome-wide bisulfite sequencing method — the technically licensable piece — and applied it to individual clones of mouse colon organoids carrying a switchable BRAF mutation, against uninduced sister clones, monthly for five months.

The result is a clean negative. Turning the oncogene on did not acutely or globally increase methylation. Mutant and control lineages accumulated methylation at Polycomb-marked island promoters at essentially the same rate, on a shared, time-dependent trajectory. What the oncogene did do was reshape where change happened — amplifying the replication-sensitive loss of methylation outside the islands, and silencing intestinal identity genes. They then checked it in an independent human system, the classic fibroblast transformation series with telomerase, SV40 and mutant RAS, where the Polycomb gains again failed to track proliferation.

So the gains at those promoters are tracking biological aging, independent of replication, and the oncogene is riding on top of a process it did not start.

Avi, Virginia — here is why this goes in the kit. When somebody pitches a methylation-based cancer test whose signature sits at Polycomb-marked island promoters, this paper says they are substantially measuring age, not transformation. That does not make the test useless, but the specificity claim now has to survive an age-matched comparison, and that demand has a published basis rather than being a hunch. It is the companion to the brain-and-blood methylation catalog we covered last Sunday; together they give us two independent ways to interrogate an epigenetic diagnostics pitch in the first meeting.

Third pick, short, and I want the team to notice the part the authors do not emphasize. A group at Kennedy Krieger, with Jacqueline Harris as corresponding author, measured cortical auditory evoked potentials — the brain's electrical response to a simple tone — in two genetic intellectual-disability syndromes, Kabuki and Rubinstein-Taybi, both caused by broken epigenetic machinery and both with mouse models that respond to epigenetic treatment. The explicit goal is a response biomarker for trials. The later components of the response differ from controls in both syndromes, more so in Rubinstein-Taybi, and correlate with language and executive function.

The finding is reasonable. The number that matters more is in the participant flow. Thirty-five people with Kabuki were recruited and twenty-six yielded usable data. Twenty-three with Rubinstein-Taybi were recruited and eleven did. Some could not tolerate the cap; others were excluded for artifact or no valid response at the recording site. Fewer than half of that second cohort produced a measurement — and this is a passive paradigm, deliberately chosen so it would work regardless of cognitive impairment.

Anthony, Jon — we have spent weeks arguing that machine-measured endpoints beat rater-scored ones, and I still believe that. This is the qualification. An objective instrument is only better if the patients can actually produce it, and those who cannot are disproportionately the most severely affected — often the ones a trial most needs to show benefit in. For the NuGate package, or any CNS endpoint, the number to demand is not the effect size. It is the fraction of enrolled patients who yielded an analyzable measurement, and whether that fraction tracks severity. If it does, the endpoint has quietly selected a milder population.

That is the radar. A blood test whose real innovation is the choice of population rather than the assay, from a lab that already built three of our companies; a negative result that gives us a published way to cross-examine epigenetic diagnostics; and a reminder that objective does not mean obtainable. Today's Portfolio Watch is in your feed alongside this, and its lead is a fifty-million-dollar university-and-pharma fund that just did our job in a matter of months. Have a good Sunday.

**PICK 2 — a published way to cross-examine an epigenetic diagnostics pitch. RECOMMENDATION: add to the diligence kit; the low-input assay itself is the only licensable object here.**
"Polycomb Marked Promoter Methylation Gains in Aging and Cancer are Independent of Replication" — bioRxiv **10.64898/2026.10.30.763235** v1, posted 2026-11-03. CC-BY-NC. Full text read via `.full.pdf` (jina, 116 KB, 48 pp).
https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.10.30.763235v1
- **Groups:** Sara-Jayne Thursby + Nibedita Patel (joint first), **Hariharan Easwaran** (corresponding) and **Stephen B. Baylin** — Dept of Oncology, Sidney Kimmel Comprehensive Cancer Center at JHU; with **Thomas R. Pisanic II** and **Tza-Huei Wang** (JHU Mechanical Engineering) on the assay.
- **Method (the licensable piece):** low-input, **genome-wide enzymatic WGBS** applied to **single stem-cell-derived organoid clones**, longitudinally — resolves methylation at clonal resolution over months, which is what makes the question answerable.
- **Model:** Cre-activatable **BRAF V600E** mouse colon organoids + Rosa26-tdTomato, vs isogenic uninduced control clones, tracked monthly over **5 months** (the interval over which this model transforms); paired scRNA-seq at 1, 2 and 5 months.
- **THE LOAD-BEARING NEGATIVE:** BRAF V600E **did not acutely or globally increase CGI methylation**. Both lineages accumulated methylation at **Polycomb-marked CpG-island promoters at the same rate on a shared, time-dependent trajectory**. What the oncogene *did* do: selectively amplify **replication-sensitive non-CGI hypomethylation**, and progressively silence intestinal-identity TFs (Cdx1/Cdx2; Sox9/stemness motifs enriched). Differentially methylated **sites** barely overlap between lineages; the genomic **categories** do.
- **Orthogonal validation:** independent human **BJ fibroblast hTERT → SV40 → H-RAS V12** transformation series (GSE91069) — Polycomb-CGI gains again fail to track proliferative expansion, while non-CGI loss does.
- **⇒ PLAIN VERSION: methylation gains at Polycomb promoters track biological aging independent of replication; the oncogene rides on a process it did not start.**
- **⇒ READ (Avi Khanna + Virginia Burger) — THE DILIGENCE QUESTION.** If a methylation-based cancer test's signature sits at Polycomb-marked CGI promoters, **it is substantially measuring age, not transformation**. Not disqualifying — an age-correlated marker can still separate cases from controls — but the specificity claim must **survive an age-matched comparison**, and that demand now has a published basis. **Companion to the 11-01 BrESS brain/blood catalog**: together they give two independent first-meeting tests for an epigenetic-diagnostics pitch.
- **⇒ FRIENDLY NOTE ON EPOCH (BioHub resident):** a **host-response** epigenetic signature is on firmer ground than a **tumour-derived methylation** signature precisely because it does not claim those loci mean cancer.
