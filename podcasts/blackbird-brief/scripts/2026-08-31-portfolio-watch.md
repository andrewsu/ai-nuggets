## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Monday, August thirty-first. It was a genuine weekend on the tape, so the most important item today is not a data drop — it is an interview. Bristol Myers Squibb sat down and explained how it is actually building its neuroscience franchise. Bristol Myers is on our shortlist of plausible buyers for the only other Phase-two-ready G-P-R fifty-two agonist in the industry. What they said reads like a polite no, and it also tells us exactly what they would say yes to.

BioSpace published the piece this morning, with Ken Rhodes, the vice president of neuroscience, and Laura Gault, who heads neuroscience development. Their framing is that the neuro comeback was not built on the fourteen-billion-dollar Karuna purchase that brought in Cobenfy. It was built on the seventy-four-billion-dollar Celgene merger in 2019, which handed them ozanimod, now Zeposia, in multiple sclerosis, and with it a neuroimmunology organization they have been extending ever since. Three things in there matter to us.

The first is that everything they are proud of is organic and adjacent, not acquired. A small molecule that raises endocannabinoid tone, in Phase 2 for multiple sclerosis spasticity and for agitation in Alzheimer's. A tau antibody partnered with Prothena. A blood-brain-barrier shuttle antibody licensed from BioArctic. And Cobenfy itself being pushed outward into bipolar mania, Alzheimer's agitation, Alzheimer's cognition and pediatric autism irritability. The Alzheimer's psychosis readout everybody watches has slipped again, now expected in 2027. And the Truist line quoted in the piece is that Bristol Myers deals may be done to expand core therapeutic areas but business development does not appear to be a primary focus.

Hemaka, that is a real update to the Nxera bidder set. We have been carrying Bristol Myers, through the Karuna integration, as one of five or six plausible homes for N-X-E one-four-nine. This says the company is filling its schizophrenia-adjacent white space by expanding the label of an asset it already owns, and its own analysts do not read acquisition as the priority. It does not strike them off the list. It moves them down it — and it argues that the deal clock we logged three weeks ago, with Nxera guiding to at least one major out-license this half, is more likely to resolve with Otsuka, Neurocrine, AbbVie or S-K Biopharmaceuticals than with Bristol Myers.

The second thing is more useful than the first. Rhodes gave a very clean statement of what he thinks changed the probability of success in neuroscience. His words: the genetics taught us what the most valuable targets are likely to be, and the biomarkers taught us how to make sure we have the right patients in the trial and how to monitor the response to therapy. Then he was candid — in psychiatric disease the causal biology is not as mature, and the lessons from Alzheimer's may take a while to travel. That passage is simultaneously the pitch for our program and the specification for it. G-P-R fifty-two is a schizophrenia-risk-gene-linked orphan receptor, precisely the genetics-first target he is describing. But if the buyer's stated recipe is genetics plus biomarkers, then a development candidate declared with no biomarker and no patient-selection story is half of what he says he needs. That is the same conclusion we reached on the twenty-seventh from the deal comparables — that first-in-human receptor occupancy, not the development candidate, is this program's value inflection — and the same one Keri Martinowich reached from the biology when she warned that a purely neuronal readout may not capture a multicellular disease state. Three roads, one destination. Take it to Third Rock before the candidate is declared.

A third note, for Jon and for NuGate. Rhodes spent real time on brain shuttle technology, and said explicitly that there is now clinical support for shuttles carrying antisense oligonucleotides and small interfering R-N-A into the nervous system. If systemically dosed central-nervous-system oligonucleotides arrive during NuGate's development window, the comparator for an intrathecally delivered antisense drug stops being no treatment and becomes a subcutaneous injection. That is Friday's comparator-drift rule, pulled off the eplontersen miss, applied to route of administration instead of efficacy.

Second item, and it is the clearest commercial template we have seen for Artemyx. Last Monday the F-D-A cleared the Elecsys p-tau two-seventeen plasma test from Roche and Eli Lilly — a single-biomarker blood test that can both rule in and rule out amyloid pathology in people fifty-five and older with cognitive symptoms, with validated cutoffs for primary care as well as specialty care. Labcorp says nationwide availability within months; Quest announced the same day that it will offer it under its A-D Detect brand in the fourth quarter. That is the third blood test for Alzheimer's pathology to clear the agency in about fifteen months.

Three reads for Artemyx, which is doing machine learning on cell-free D-N-A fragmentation for liver fibrosis, lupus, rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease and endometriosis. First, the regulator is now clearing blood tests for a chronic, non-oncology disease on the strength of comparability to imaging and spinal fluid. That is the clearance precedent our file has been waiting for, and it sits outside oncology, which is where every liquid-biopsy precedent used to live.

Second, the commercial channel is not a sales force. It is Quest and Labcorp, and both moved within a day of clearance. Business development for Victor Velculescu's company should be building reference-lab relationships now, while the validation studies are still running.

Third, the uncomfortable one. Quest did not merely agree to distribute Roche's test. On the same day it launched its own competing laboratory-developed panel, stacking p-tau two-seventeen with amyloid-beta forty-two over forty and APO-E genotype, and claimed the multi-marker version cuts the indeterminate rate to ten percent against a benchmark of fifteen to twenty. So the reference lab is channel and competitor at once, and its counter-move was to add analytes. Artemyx's defensibility argument has to survive a Quest scientist reading the paper.

Third item, from the European Society of Cardiology meeting in Munich on Friday. AskBio, Bayer's gene therapy subsidiary, presented baseline characteristics from GenePHIT — more than a hundred and seventy patients randomized across sixty-four sites in twelve countries, one of the largest randomized gene therapy trials ever run in heart failure. The therapy is a one-time adeno-associated virus given by intracoronary infusion through a standard catheter, with no immune suppression, encoding a constitutively active form of inhibitor-one, which blocks protein phosphatase one and is meant to restore calcium handling and contractility in non-ischemic heart failure with reduced ejection fraction. Efficacy and safety come in the first half of 2027.

Baseline characteristics are not a result and I am not going to dress them up as one. But two things transfer today. For Aletira, and for the Series A narrative Geoff Lynn is building: the operational ceiling for a gene therapy company just went up in public. A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial, sixty-four sites, twelve countries, adeno-associated virus, no immunosuppression, a standard interventional catheter — that is a demonstrated thing rather than an aspiration, and it is a useful counterweight to the run of clinical holds this brief has covered all month. Investors will price what a gene therapy company should be able to execute against this, not against a ten-patient open-label study.

And for the cardiac fibrosis theme, look at what is being measured. Ejection fraction, New York Heart Association class, six-minute walk distance, cardiovascular death — contractility and function. Yesterday I told you that every relaxin-receptor trial ever run has measured haemodynamics and never once fibrosis. Here is the largest gene therapy trial in cardiology doing the same thing with a different mechanism. Anthony, Jon — that is not one company's blind spot, it is the field's default, which makes designing a trial that can actually register scar available to us as differentiation rather than as a burden.

Fourth, two stories that only mean something when you put them together. This morning BioSpace ran a long piece on the National Security Commission on Emerging Biotechnology, the congressional body that winds up at the end of this year. Its numbers: China passed the United States on clinical trials six years ago, then on natural science publications, and now runs three times as many Phase 1 trials as we do. Seventy-two percent of senior American industry and academic leaders surveyed think China's biomedical sector is improving faster than ours; eighty-five percent expect the United States to lose its lead within a decade.

Now set that beside what Genentech did last week — two Asia deals inside a week. Up to two point three billion dollars committed to Hanmi in Korea for a non-incretin weight-loss peptide. Then forty-five million dollars up front, with up to a billion in milestones, to DualityBio in China for antibody-drug conjugates, with DualityBio running discovery through the first Phase 1 before Genentech takes over.

Eddie, Matt — that second number is what our academic assets are quietly being priced against. Forty-five million dollars up front buys a large pharma a discovery-through-early-clinical partnership with an experienced platform company. When a Blackbird program walks into that same corporate development group, that is the alternative sitting in the comparison column. The policy story about a shrinking innovation edge and the deal tape are one story seen from two ends, and what it argues for is the thing this model is structurally good at: pushing a program to a package the buyer cannot source abroad, which in practice means human data or a genuinely unencumbered composition-of-matter position, not a very well run mouse study.

Fifth, briefly, and it is for Clasp. Nine days after the melanoma vaccine result that added roughly forty-five billion dollars to its market value, Moderna raised two point six billion dollars in upsized convertible notes due 2032, and the stock gave back about ten percent on the announcement. Clasp's near-term catalyst is the monotherapy readout in the p53 R-one-seventy-five-H program. This is what the sequence looks like when a company is ready: the data event is not the deliverable, the financing you execute within days of it is, and you accept a discount to get it done while the window is open. Hemaka, Eddie — worth having a view on what Clasp does in the two weeks after that readout, formed before the readout.

Three lines to carry into the week. A prospective buyer told us in public what he buys, and it is genetics plus a biomarker, not novelty — so build the G-P-R fifty-two package to that spec. A diagnostic's distributor is also its competitor, and it can ship a rival panel on the same day it agrees to carry yours. And the biggest gene therapy trial in cardiology is measuring contraction rather than scar, which leaves the fibrosis endpoint open to whoever is willing to design for it.

Everything else was quiet. Nothing on G-P-R fifty-two. Nothing from Skylark, Sensorion or Lilly in the ear since the first SONIX patient three weeks ago. Nothing from Clasp, Adventris, NuGate, Winnow, Biolinco or 1104health. No new items from Johns Hopkins Technology Ventures since the two we shipped last week, and BioBuzz has not posted since Wednesday. A real weekend, not a failed sweep.

That's the Portfolio Watch for Monday, August thirty-first. Talk tomorrow.
