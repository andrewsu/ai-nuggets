Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Friday, October thirtieth. The most important development for the portfolio is that the company that already sold the antibody version of deep B-cell depletion to Sanofi for six hundred million dollars in cash just raised three hundred and fifty million more — and the asset it raised against depletes the exact T-cell population Winnow is built around.

Dren Bio announced the round yesterday. Three hundred and fifty million dollars, oversubscribed, co-led by Lightspeed Venture Partners and by Juvia Group, the newly launched firm we logged on October fourteenth. The company did not name a series, which is itself informative. Their last raise was sixty-five million dollars in twenty twenty-two. This is one of the five or six largest private financings in biotech this year.

The money goes to late-stage development and regulatory and commercial preparation for dibotatug, which you will also see as D-R zero one. It is a non-fucosylated monoclonal antibody against C-D ninety-four. C-D ninety-four sits on terminal effector C-D eight T cells, on gamma-delta T cells, and on natural killer cells, and the antibody depletes them through enhanced antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity. The elegant part of the mechanism, and the part worth a diligence hour, is that the target cells are themselves the effector cells — they kill each other. It is in a Phase One-Two study in T-cell lymphoma and in large granular lymphocytic leukemia, the company calls that program registrational, and the registry has a primary readout scheduled for this December. Six weeks.

Now the three reads, and the first one is about price rather than science.

Dren's platform credential is D-R zero two zero one, a C-D twenty-directed bispecific that engages a phagocytic receptor found selectively on myeloid cells and switched on only in the presence of the target antigen — so myeloid cells eat B cells, deeply, and in primate work without observed cytokine release syndrome or neurotoxicity. Sanofi acquired that program in March of last year for six hundred million dollars cash up front, plus up to one point three billion in milestones, at a point when it was in Phase One in B-cell lymphoma and had not yet been tested in an autoimmune patient. Eddie, that is the cleanest comparable on the board for what a large pharma will pay for an antibody route to deep immune cell depletion, before autoimmune data. Six hundred million, in cash, pre-proof-of-concept in the indication the buyer actually wanted.

The second read is the one for Jon and Anthony, and it is a sequencing lesson rather than a threat. Dren says explicitly that it is expanding dibotatug beyond the blood cancers into aplastic anemia, bone marrow failure syndromes, and autoimmune disease. So a depleting antibody against terminal effector C-D eight cells is going to arrive in chronic autoimmunity having been paid for by a lethal hematologic indication, where a single-arm registrational path and a hard endpoint were available. That is the same structure we logged on September seventeenth with Electra taking a broad myeloid and T-cell depleter into secondary hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis before widening out. It is now a pattern with two instances and a lot of capital behind it: pick the indication where the trial economics work, then expand into the chronic disease you actually want. We should be asking that question of our own autoimmune sequencing rather than treating indication choice as a downstream decision.

The third read is where our differentiation still holds. Depletion by antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity is a fixed action — you dose it, the compartment goes away, and there is no dial. A T-cell engager is titratable and interruptible, which is the control axis we moved to on October twenty-seventh, and it is redosable, which is the claim we moved to on October twenty-eighth after Artiva failed on durability rather than on depth. Both survive contact with Dren. What does not survive is any version of our pitch that treats an antibody as the less serious competitor. The antibody route to deep depletion is funded, de-risked in humans, owned by Sanofi in B cells, and now walking toward the C-D eight compartment with three hundred and fifty million dollars behind it.

Second item, and it is the most useful failure we have seen on the non-opioid pain theme.

Ono Pharma reported results from five Phase Two studies of a single endocannabinoid modulator, O-N-O eleven ten. Two of them were in depression and social anxiety disorder. Those showed no efficacy signal at all and the company has discontinued both indications. The other three were in pain — postherpetic neuralgia, fibromyalgia, and Hunner-type interstitial cystitis. All three missed their primary endpoints. Ono is continuing in pain anyway, citing improvements in pain scores, and plans additional trials.

Here is why that matters to us rather than to Ono's shareholders. Avi, Matt Lawler — this is five mid-stage studies of one mechanism, zero primary endpoints met, and the company's response is to design more trials in the same indications. The binding constraint in non-opioid pain is not the mechanism. It is the endpoint and the placebo response, and a well-capitalized Japanese pharma with a real target just demonstrated that at a cost of five Phase Twos. Note which indication they picked: postherpetic neuralgia and fibromyalgia are the two canonical high-placebo-response settings in the field. Hunner-type interstitial cystitis is the one of the three with a visible lesion you can score objectively, and it missed as well. That is the data point I would actually carry into our own pain diligence.

The discipline this argues for is the one we adopted for Winnow indications in September — build the study around a provocation or an objective readout, and price the placebo response before committing capital, not after the first miss. For the non-opioid pain program and for our read on SereNeuro, the question to ask any pain founder is not whether the mechanism is novel. It is what their Phase Two primary is, and what the placebo arm did the last three times anyone ran that endpoint.

Third item, and it is a studio-structure item that sits directly against the one we led with yesterday.

Flagship Pioneering is collapsing three of its own portfolio companies — Etiome, Cellarity, and Empress Therapeutics — into a single new venture called Etcelera, built around A-I plus perturbation data to predict which molecules will survive clinical development. Adrian Rawcliffe, formerly chief executive of Adaptimmune, runs it. The three predecessors had raised three hundred and seventy-four million dollars between them, and no new money was announced. Focus is hematology, immunology, and metabolic-fibrotic disease, starting with myelofibrosis and essential thrombocythemia.

Now the detail that makes this an item rather than a headline. At the same time Flagship merged the three platforms together, it took the one clinically interesting asset out — Cellarity's oral sickle cell disease program is being moved into its own separate standalone company.

Matt, Maisha — put that next to yesterday's Formation Bio item. Yesterday we covered a six-hundred-million-dollar fund whose organizing principle is one corporate entity per drug program with a shared development organization underneath. Today the largest and best-resourced venture studio in the business did exactly that with its one real asset, and did the precise opposite with its three platforms, folding them into one entity with one management team. Taken together those are not contradictory, they are the same rule stated twice: entities should follow assets, not platforms. Platforms are shared infrastructure and they consolidate. Assets are financeable units and they separate. That is the BioHub argument with two independent third-party confirmations inside forty-eight hours, and it is worth putting in the deck in exactly those terms. The sobering half, for Hemaka: three companies that each launched with its own thesis and its own capital could not individually carry a platform to a drug, and the studio that funded all three is the one that called it.

Fourth item, briefly, and it is a deal structure for Eddie.

Astellas took an exclusive option to license D-A-X zero forty-four from Audax Biosciences, a Melbourne company. The asset is an antibody-cytokine fusion that concentrates interleukin-twelve activity in the neighborhood of the tumor, which is the whole game with that cytokine — the biology has been compelling and the systemic toxicity has been disqualifying for thirty years. Headline value is up to one point three four billion dollars. The number that is real is two hundred and eighty-five million, which is an undisclosed upfront plus the option exercise fee. Up to one point zero five billion sits in development and commercial milestones, with tiered double-digit royalties.

The structure is the point. Audax funds the asset itself into Phase One, which is expected to start in twenty twenty-seven. Astellas then decides whether to exercise and take over global development, manufacturing, and commercialization. Audax keeps an option to co-fund and co-promote in Australia and New Zealand. So a large pharma has pre-committed nine figures against work the small company has not done yet, and the inflection being paid for is the investigational new drug package — not clinical data. Eddie, that is a precise description of what Blackbird manufactures. It is also the right shape for a program that is too early to sell outright and too expensive for a seed-stage company to carry alone, and the regional co-promote is a detail worth copying: it keeps a strip of the asset for the originating entity without complicating the global deal.
