## Script

Good morning, Blackbird Team. This is your Portfolio Watch for Thursday, August sixth.

The direct-catalyst tape is still quiet on our own companies, but two threads we've been tracking all week finally have real content behind them — and one of them is the single most decision-relevant read of the month for a-SKY. So we lead there.

Revolution Medicines reported second-quarter results after the close yesterday. I've flagged this call every day for a week as a calendar event, not news — well, now it's news, and the update is a lot to digest. Here's the version that matters for us. On daraxonrasib, their multi-selective RAS-on inhibitor, the FDA has accepted the pancreatic-cancer application and slotted it into the Commissioner's national priority-voucher pilot — the accelerated-review lane. Europe has started a phased review of its own. And the piece that should get a-SKY's attention isn't the regulatory paperwork, it's the commercial machine already running behind it: the expanded-access program they opened in May has, within about three weeks, put drug in the hands of physicians representing more than two thousand pancreatic-cancer patients across nearly every state. They're telling the market they've achieved U.S. commercial launch readiness. This is no longer a clinical story; it's a company standing up a launch.

And it isn't confined to pancreatic cancer. The FDA granted daraxonrasib breakthrough-therapy designation in previously-treated RAS-mutant lung cancer — the non-G-twelve-C population. They completed enrollment guidance for the pivotal lung study with a readout expected in 2027. And they showed first-line lung-cancer combination data across their two mutation-selective molecules that, frankly, is the kind of number that reorders a field: elironrasib, the G-twelve-C drug, at an eighty-five percent confirmed response rate in combination; zoldonrasib, the G-twelve-D drug, at eighty-two percent — both with what they characterize as minimal added toxicity and a clean liver-safety profile. They're initiating two new first-line Phase 3s off the back of that. Add three point nine billion dollars in cash, and you have a company that is fully funded to march the entire RAS-on franchise — every major mutation, every line of therapy — into the clinic and onto the market simultaneously.

So the so-what for a-SKY, candidly. This is not a competitor's bad day, and I don't want it read that way. It's the opposite — it's the reference-setter having an exceptionally good quarter. And that's precisely why it matters. Remember what a-SKY is: a KRAS-directed antibody, the mechanism still undisclosed, built on the AVS Bio partnership. It is a genuinely differentiated modality — antibodies engaging RAS is a fundamentally different bet than the small-molecule RAS-on chemistry Revolution owns. But every quarter Revolution de-risks and commercializes the small-molecule lane across G-twelve-C, G-twelve-D, and now G-twelve-V, the bar that a-SKY's eventual disclosure has to clear goes up, and the cost of the mechanism staying undisclosed goes up with it. A response rate in the low-eighties in first-line lung cancer is now the ambient reference a differentiated modality gets measured against. So the action for Eddie and the a-SKY team is the one we flagged: treat this quarter's transcript as a competitive-map input, and press internally on when and how a-SKY articulates what its antibody does that a small molecule can't — because "another way at RAS" is a weaker story every quarter Revolution owns the category, and "the way at RAS the small molecules can't reach" is the only story that holds up against this backdrop.

Second thread, and this one resolves cleanly in our favor for now. The AstraZeneca–Bristol Myers merger rumor that anchored Monday's and Tuesday's briefs has effectively been knocked down. A senior source told Reuters yesterday there are no discussions between the companies and, in their words, there never was a deal to be done. AstraZeneca shares, which had slid roughly nine percent on the reports, recovered. Now, a source-level denial isn't a press release, and the fact that the talks were reported at all tells you the strategic logic of an oncology mega-combination is out there in the ether — so I wouldn't file this away permanently. But for the read we care about, it's a positive. Recall the sharpest thread from Tuesday: if Bristol Myers folded into an oncology-heavy AstraZeneca, our G-P-R fifty-two neuropsychiatric company loses an independent neuro-psychiatric buyer, because Bristol Myers owns Cobenfy — the marquee non-dopaminergic antipsychotic and exactly the kind of strategic acquirer we'd want on the map. With the deal denied, Bristol Myers stays independent, Cobenfy stays with a standalone owner, and that buyer stays on our board. The exit optionality we worried was thinning is, for now, intact. And the oncology-consolidation overhang for a-SKY's eventual partnering conversation lifts in the near term. Net: a threat we were watching just got smaller. Hemaka can leave the buyer map as drawn.

The rest of the board, briefly, and no re-coverage — most of it we walked in full earlier this week. CAMP4's fifty-million-dollar second close was yesterday's lead; the only thing to add is that the clock it started is now running, so the differentiation wedge Avi and the platform leads own — translation-step mechanism, delivery beyond intrathecal, and a disease set where CAMP4 is absent — stays this week's assignment. On 1104health, the one concrete forward marker is unchanged and worth repeating because the date is close: the HHS Inspector General's request for comment on anti-kickback safe harbors for paying clinical-trial participants closes August twenty-fourth — that's the exact policy surface that governs paying physicians for coordination, and getting a position in before the deadline is cheap insurance for Rose Wang's team. Aletira — no fresh news; the gene-therapy FDA calendar, next up Ultragenyx on August twenty-third, keeps precision and cell-selectivity topical, with the standing caveat that SELEXON governs where a transgene expresses, not where the capsid goes. And the gut-restricted GCPII program — the TL1A field stays crowded, so the differentiation lives on the enteric-pain axis.

That's the watch for August sixth — a genuinely important RevMed quarter to read as a-SKY competitive intelligence, a merger rumor knocked down in our favor on the neuropsychiatric buyer map, and a quiet board otherwise. Talk tomorrow.
