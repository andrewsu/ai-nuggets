## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Wednesday, October fourteenth. The most important development for the portfolio is that the most advanced genetic medicine in non-opioid pain is now for sale at auction out of bankruptcy, and the rest of that same estate has already cleared, so for the first time we have real prices against our pain theme.

Sangamo announced last night that it has opened an auction for S-T-five-oh-three, its chronic neuropathic pain program, with the process expected to close in the fourth quarter. Here's what's on the block. S-T-five-oh-three uses an A-A-V vector to deliver an engineered zinc-finger repressor that turns down expression of S-C-N-nine-A, the gene encoding the NAV one point seven sodium channel. It's given once, intrathecally. The indication is intractable pain from small fiber neuropathy, which Sangamo puts at a hundred eighty thousand patients in the US, sitting inside a peripheral neuropathy population of roughly forty million. It has Fast Track. The Phase one-two study, called STAND, has seven sites activated, and the registry lists it as recruiting with an estimated enrollment of twenty-seven patients. In non-human primates, a single dose cut NAV one point seven substantially with no off-target effects reported.

Now the prices, because that's the part that matters to us. The earlier court-supervised sales have closed and Sangamo collected a hundred sixty-three and a half million dollars in gross cash. P-T-C Therapeutics paid a hundred eleven million dollars in cash for the Fabry disease program, plus up to a hundred million in future regulatory milestones. Lilly paid fifty million dollars in cash — and for that fifty million it got the entire capsid delivery library, the zinc-finger platform, the MINT integrase platform, and the prion disease program.

Anthony and Virginia, two reads here, and they cut in different directions.

The first is for the non-opioid pain map. We added a gene-therapy column to that map on September tenth, when Encoded's NAV one point seven program showed up for post-amputation neuroma pain, funded inside a two hundred seventy-five million dollar Series F, with an I-N-D planned for twenty twenty-seven. Sangamo's asset is more than a year ahead of that. It's dosing patients, it has Fast Track, and it is going to auction anyway. Whatever it clears at becomes the number a banker quotes back at any genetic-medicine pain program for the next two years, and if it clears low, that's a comparable we inherit without having done anything wrong.

The second read is for Eddie, and it's the sharper one. One clinical asset — a single Fabry program — fetched more than twice as much cash as an entire platform estate. The capsid library, the zinc fingers and the integrase platform went for fifty million dollars combined. Back in June we read Lilly's purchase as Lilly systematically assembling an in-house gene-therapy capability at a fire-sale price, and that's still the right read. What's newly visible is the ratio. Under distress, clinical assets price and platforms don't. Any pitch that leads with the platform and treats the lead program as an illustration of it is arguing itself into the column that cleared for fifty million.

The candid limit on all of this: a section three-sixty-three sale under Chapter eleven is a floor, not a fair-market comparable. A seller with a debtor-in-possession facility to repay and a court calendar is not a seller in a negotiation. If one of these numbers comes back at us in a board meeting as the market price for a clinical-stage gene therapy, that's the answer. Eddie, it's still worth having someone watch the auction docket, because if the clearing price for S-T-five-oh-three becomes public in the fourth quarter, that's a free data point on what an early clinical pain program is worth with a real clinical package attached.

Second item, and it answers a question we left open yesterday. Agomab dosed the first patient in a global Phase two-b in fibrostenosing Crohn's disease. The drug is ontunisertib, an oral small molecule that inhibits A-L-K-five, the T-G-F-beta type one receptor. Three hundred twenty patients, four arms, three dose levels against placebo, fifty-two weeks of treatment.

Virginia, two design choices are worth your time. The first is the endpoint. The primary is the proportion of patients whose index ileal stricture becomes endoscopically passable at week twenty-four, confirmed by central reading, and patients have to enter the study with a stricture that is not passable. That is a binary, anatomical outcome, directly observed, on a named lesion. Compare it with where we were on Monday, when Bristol needed roughly twelve hundred fifty-five patients and fifty-two weeks to register a change in the slope of lung function. Related fibrotic biology, an order of magnitude apart in cost, and the difference is almost entirely that one team picked a fibrotic lesion you can look at and the other picked a function you have to measure over a year.

The second choice is the molecule. A-L-K-five inhibitors have a long history of systemic toxicity, which is a large part of why the class stalled. Agomab's answer is restriction by pharmacokinetics. Rapid first-pass metabolism in the liver means the drug works in the gut wall and never reaches clinically relevant systemic exposure. They aren't selling a new target. They're selling a confinement strategy on an old one, which is structurally the same move as Navion selling selectivity across a channel family rather than selling a channel.

So our October fifth open item now has an answer in two parts. Find the fibrotic lesion that is directly observable and binary, and buy the safety margin with tissue restriction rather than with potency. For a cardiac fibrosis program the uncomfortable half is the first part, because the heart doesn't hand you a stricture to look at. That's the question to put in front of the team, not a reason to put the theme down.

One structural note before I leave Agomab. Dosing that first patient triggered a seven million euro milestone to the former shareholders of Origo Biopharma, the company Agomab acquired in twenty twenty-one. Ten million euros of a possible twenty have now become payable. Maisha, that's a clean worked example of a small acquisition priced largely on development milestones rather than up front, and it's close to the structure we keep circling for asset carve-outs.

Third item, and the shortest. Soufflé Therapeutics closed an oversubscribed hundred thirty-five million dollar Series B, led by a new firm called Juvia Group, bringing in T-C-G Crossover, Fidelity, Balyasny and B-B Biotech alongside existing investors including Amgen Ventures, ARCH, Bessemer and Leaps by Bayer. The platform is called REMEDY. They systematically screen for internalizing receptors and then build antibodies or other ligands against them to carry oligonucleotides into specific cell types outside the liver. Three programs are headed to the clinic: facioscapulohumeral muscular dystrophy through skeletal muscle, phospholamban in cardiomyocytes for heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, and an undisclosed target in muscle and metabolic disease.

Eddie, this is the third extrahepatic delivery financing in two days, after Rona's Roche license and Undecimal's launch. Yesterday I said partners are paying for a tissue-access story as much as for a target. A hundred thirty-five million dollars into a company with no clinical data yet is the private-market version of the same sentence, and that syndicate is crossover and sovereign money, not seed money. So the NuGate question stands and gets more pointed. Intrathecal delivery to motor neurons is a route, not a platform, and the field is now explicitly paying for platforms. Hemaka, note the second program while you're at it — receptor-mediated delivery into cardiomyocytes is somebody building the delivery half of our cardiac fibrosis theme while we're still arguing about the endpoint.

That's the watch. Freenome presents its next-generation colorectal screening data at the gastroenterology meeting this morning, so that's tomorrow's specificity-definition check for DELFI. Kyverna's full stiff person syndrome dataset lands in Toronto on the twenty-first. Talk soon.
