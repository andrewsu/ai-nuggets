## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Wednesday, October twenty-first. The most important development for the portfolio is a psychiatry readout that tests the same bet our Neuropsych NewCo is making. Rapport Therapeutics took a glutamate drug, aimed it at a protein expressed only in selected brain regions, and won a Phase 2 in bipolar mania. The efficacy was barely enough. The case for buying it is tolerability.

Here is what was reported Tuesday. RAP-219 is a negative allosteric modulator of the AMPA glutamate receptor. It doesn't touch AMPA receptors everywhere. It binds only where a helper protein called TARP gamma-eight is present, and that protein is expressed in the hippocampus and neocortex but barely at all in the hindbrain, which is where conventional neuroscience drugs pick up a lot of their intolerable side effects. The trial put two hundred and fifty-three adults with bipolar one disorder in the hospital during an acute manic episode, took them off every bipolar medication and anxiolytic, and gave them zero point seven five milligrams once a day or placebo for three weeks.

On the Young Mania Rating Scale, patients on drug improved by fifteen point four points against twelve point three on placebo. That's a three point one point difference with a p-value of zero point zero four five. The secondary endpoint, the clinician's global severity rating, also hit, at p equals zero point zero two four. The separation grew every week, from under one point at week one to two point two at week two and three point one at week three. All eleven items on the scale moved in the right direction. The ones that reached nominal significance were irritability, thought content, disruptive and aggressive behavior, and sexual interest, which are the symptoms that put manic patients in the hospital.

Analysts were candid. Stifel's Paul Matteis said it "just hits," and noted the effect size sits at the low end of the three-to-four-point range considered competitive with existing drugs. Jefferies put it inside the two-to-six-point band of approved agents. Stifel also called it a clear positive against low expectations, and the stock spiked about twenty-five percent premarket before giving it all back at the open.

The tolerability data are why anyone cares. Adverse events occurred at the same rate on drug and placebo, fifty-one point six versus fifty-two point eight percent. Nothing was severe, there were no serious adverse events, and no patient was reported with akathisia, extrapyramidal symptoms or orthostatic hypotension. Somnolence was actually lower on drug than on placebo. Agitation was the most common event, at sixteen point seven percent against nine point four, mostly in week one and lasting a median of two days in both arms. Discontinuation for adverse events was three point two percent against zero point eight. Raymond James summed up the commercial thesis: in bipolar disorder, the drug that wins is the one patients stay on. Rapport plans an FDA meeting in the first quarter of next year and two Phase 3 trials in the second half of twenty twenty-seven, and it has about four hundred and thirty-six million dollars in cash.

So what for Blackbird? This touches the Neuropsych NewCo in three ways.

First, the argument is the same one we're making. Rapport's pitch is that a brain-wide neurotransmitter system becomes tolerable when you hit it only through a regionally restricted partner protein. GPR fifty-two's selectivity argument is that its expression is concentrated in the striatum. A Phase 2 has now shown that the expression-restriction logic can buy a clean side-effect table in a psychiatric population. That makes it a precedent to put in the development-candidate package and the Series A deck. It is the best public evidence so far that investors will pay for this kind of thesis in psychiatry.

Second, look at what investors paid for, and what they didn't. A three-point effect at p equals zero point zero four five on a population with a strong placebo response was enough for a Phase 3 decision, because the side-effect profile carried it. That is the bargain GPR fifty-two will be offered. If the efficacy is merely adequate, then tolerability has to be measured as hard as efficacy, from the first patient study. Which events, on which scale, and against which incumbent? Hemaka, that belongs in the development-candidate package now.

Third, the caveat we already wrote down, and it fits this case closely. Last month Xenon, whose potassium-channel opener azetukalner had more than fifteen hundred patient-years of epilepsy safety behind it, paused its depression and bipolar depression Phase 3s over neuropsychiatric adverse events its own Phase 2 hadn't flagged. That's the same path Rapport is on, an epilepsy drug moving into mood, and the lesson was that a psychiatry Phase 2 powered for efficacy says little about tolerability at scale. The signal to watch here is the agitation imbalance. Rapport's clean table comes from about a hundred and twenty-six patients per arm, for three weeks, in hospital, with lorazepam rescue allowed, and the eight-week follow-up safety data aren't out yet. Use RAP-219 as precedent for the thesis, not as proof that the thesis holds in chronic outpatient dosing.

One structural point for Eddie and Matt. Rapport is a Third Rock company. It was founded in twenty twenty-two with Third Rock and Johnson and Johnson's venture arm and built on a licensed Janssen molecule, and the founding scientist ran neuroscience discovery at Johnson and Johnson. Our NewCo partner just watched its own regional-selectivity psychiatry bet produce a Phase 2 win. It is a good week to have the GPR fifty-two conversation with them.

And a footnote for Aletira. Rapport lists hearing and vestibular disorders among its discovery programs, using the same receptor-associated-protein platform. It's a small-molecule company with four hundred million dollars, and it has just shown that its platform works in a second indication. Put it on the watch list for the inner-ear competitive map.

The second item is about deal arithmetic, and it follows directly from yesterday's lead. On Monday, Eli Lilly signed with METiS TechBio, a Hong Kong–listed company, to use its AI-driven lipid nanoparticle platform. METiS uses machine learning to discover and optimize the ionizable lipids that make up the particle, aiming for tissue-specific delivery. The headline number is up to one point one billion dollars. The upfront is, in METiS's own words, "in the low double-digit millions." Neither company named a drug, a disease, or a tissue.

The same morning, BioSpace ran an interview with Geoff Meyerson, the chief executive of the bank Locust Walk, on its year-to-date numbers. China's share of global licensing deal value is now fifty-eight percent, while the U.S. share has fallen from fifty-six percent in twenty twenty-four to twenty-six percent. U.S. biotech mergers and acquisitions have already reached one hundred and seventy-three billion dollars, against one hundred and fifty-one billion for all of last year. The ratio is the part that matters for us. Upfronts are about ten percent of total deal value for U.S. sellers and about six percent for Chinese sellers. In Meyerson's words, "In the vast majority of deals, you never receive all the milestones. That number gets the attention. My judgment is the upfront number is actually what matters."

So what for Blackbird? Put the METiS terms next to yesterday's. Merck KGaA took Silence's whole siRNA delivery platform as change on a Phase 3 asset, and now a pharma has rented an AI-built tissue-targeting LNP platform for low double-digit millions upfront with no named target. These two data points say the same thing. Delivery platforms without a lead asset are licensed, not bought, and they're priced on the upfront. For NuGate, any delivery story in the Series A narrative should be priced as an option, not as an asset. For Aletira, the SELEXON cassette becomes worth real money once the hearing program shows it working in an ear. Eddie, for BioVentures term sheets and any out-licensing conversation we start, Meyerson's rule is a good house rule: we model the upfront and treat the rest as upside.

The third item is a pricing benchmark that touches two early-stage themes. ICER, the Institute for Clinical and Economic Review, published an analysis of twenty-six drugs approved between twenty twenty-two and twenty twenty-five that it had previously reviewed. Net launch prices rose significantly over that period, and most landed well above ICER's thresholds of one hundred thousand to one hundred and fifty thousand dollars per quality-adjusted life year. Insmed's bronchiectasis drug Brinsupri and Merck's pulmonary arterial hypertension drug Winrevair showed the biggest gaps; ICER says each would need roughly a ninety percent discount. Carvykti would need about a third off its four hundred and sixty-five thousand dollar net price.

Two results are useful to us. Vertex's Journavx, the first non-opioid pain drug in its class, is priced inside ICER's value thresholds. And Casgevy, the CRISPR therapy for sickle cell disease at two point two million dollars net, would need only about a seven percent cut to be judged good value.

So what for Blackbird? For the non-opioid pain theme, the commercial comparator we'll be measured against is, by ICER's own math, already priced at value. A new non-opioid pain asset can't count on a price premium over Journavx unless it adds clinical benefit. The pitch has to be efficacy or a population Journavx doesn't serve, not price. For Aletira, the Casgevy number is a quiet tailwind. The independent cost-effectiveness watchdog in this country is saying a one-time, multi-million-dollar genetic medicine can come close to value when the benefit is durable. That's the frame a hearing gene therapy wants in its health-economics slide, with the caveat that sickle cell carries lifetime hospitalization costs that hearing loss doesn't.

The last item is a framing piece, but it is the most useful sentence I read this week for how a venture studio should price its own bets. A BioPharma Dive analysis looked at three areas where pharma is paying ahead of the evidence: PD-L1 by VEGF bispecifics, B7-H3 antibody–drug conjugates, and in vivo CAR-T. Mike Rea, who runs an R&D decision-making venture called Then, If, put it this way: companies are "paying promise prices on premise evidence." His test for a rational early bet is to name the premise that has to hold for the price to make sense, say how and when it will be tested, and define the result that would make the buyer walk away. And the line worth keeping: "Hype is when the premise is inferred from the fact that other people have bought it."

The piece applies the test well. Two independently developed B7-H3 drugs, GSK's and Roche partner MediLink's, have each cut the risk of death by fifty-four percent in Phase 3 in relapsed small cell lung cancer. That's strong target-level evidence. But both drugs carry topoisomerase payloads, and both pivotal trials ran in China, so the confound hasn't been removed. On in vivo CAR-T, Lilly paid three point two five billion dollars upfront for Kelonia on a six-patient, uncontrolled myeloma dataset with months of follow-up.

So what for Blackbird? This is the discipline our grant gates already try to apply, and it's worth stating in Rea's terms. For Clasp, the premise is that a TCR-mimic engager can kill on a mutant-p53 peptide at the copy numbers real tumors present. The walk-away result should be written down before the next data cut, not after. For the oncology surface-target theme, the B7-H3 story is a reminder that "the target is validated" and "the payload class works" are separate claims, and a new entrant should know which of the two it's relying on. And for everyone on the deal side, the hype test is a good filter for inbound decks. If the main evidence is that a big pharma paid for something similar, nothing new has been shown.

That's the watch for Wednesday. Rapport bought a Phase 3 with tolerability rather than efficacy, which is the trade GPR fifty-two will be offered. Delivery platforms rent for low eight figures upfront. ICER says Journavx is already priced at value. And the best question to ask of any early bet is what result makes you walk away. Talk to you tomorrow.
