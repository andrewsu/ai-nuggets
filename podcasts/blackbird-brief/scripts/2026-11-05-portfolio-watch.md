## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Thursday, November fifth. The most important development for the portfolio landed this morning out of Cambridge, England: T-Therapeutics, a company building T cell receptor engagers for both cancer and autoimmune disease, closed a hundred and thirty-three million dollar Series B. Four pharma venture arms are now on its cap table, and it is still preclinical. That one round touches Winnow and Clasp at once.

Here is what was announced. The round was co-led by Advent Life Sciences and the British Business Bank. New money came from Johnson and Johnson's venture arm, Samsara BioCapital, M-Ventures, which is Merck K-G-a-A's fund, and Eli Lilly. Every existing major holder came back in, including Sofinnova, F-Prime, Digitalis, Cambridge Innovation Capital, Tencent, and Sanofi Ventures. Count the history and this company raised fifty-nine million in its Series A in twenty twenty-three, extended it to ninety-one million a year ago, and now adds a hundred and thirty-three. That is roughly two hundred and twenty-five million dollars before a single patient has been dosed. The proceeds are meant to take two lead programs, one in oncology and one in immunology, into the clinic and through clinical proof of concept.

The technology is a soluble, engineered T cell receptor fused to a C-D-three arm on an Fc backbone. The receptor reads short peptides presented on the cell surface, which is what lets it reach intracellular proteins that an antibody cannot see. The company says its C-D-three binders are tuned to kill while limiting cytokine release, and the Fc gives it a half-life compatible with ordinary dosing schedules. Neither lead target is disclosed.

Two reads, and the first is for Jon and Anthony on Winnow. On Tuesday we said the money in autoimmune cell therapy had rotated to in-vivo CAR-T and not to engagers. This round partly corrects that, and the correction cuts both ways. The company describes its immunology program as a precision immune reset that selectively depletes the pathogenic cells driving chronic tissue inflammation. That is not B-cell depletion. It is the selective-depletion claim, made with a T cell engager, and it just drew a hundred and thirty-three million dollars and Johnson and Johnson, Lilly, Merck K-G-a-A and Sanofi as shareholders. That is a tailwind for the category: pharma will fund an engager in autoimmunity when the pitch is selectivity rather than breadth. It is also the end of an empty lane. We have been telling the Winnow story as antigen-specific T cell targeting in a field crowded with B-cell depleters. There is now a well-funded company making a neighboring claim with the same modality. And the three words we settled on Tuesday, interruptible, titratable and redosable, describe their molecule as well as ours. So those words separate Winnow from cell therapy, not from this competitor. Against T-Therapeutics, the only defensible difference is the selectivity mechanism itself: what Winnow recognizes, on which cells, and why that depletes the pathogenic clone and leaves the rest. That needs to be one sentence, and it needs to be in the deck before their target is disclosed.

The second read is for Eddie and the Clasp file. This is the same pitch Clasp makes: a peptide-H-L-A engager that unlocks intracellular targets in solid tumors. The lead investor said so in nearly those words. The difference is the binder. T-Therapeutics uses an affinity-matured native receptor. Clasp uses an antibody that mimics one. Both are H-L-A restricted. The approved precedent for the native-receptor format is Immunocore's Kimmtrak, so this is not a speculative modality. Two things favor Clasp. It is already in the clinic with GUARDIAN-one-oh-one, and T-Therapeutics is raising to get there. And a target that is a hotspot mutation, like Clasp's p53 R-one-seventy-five-H, is truly tumor-specific in a way a self-antigen usually is not. Two things cut the other way. The company calls its lead oncology target pan-indication, and a self-antigen shared across tumor types will usually leave a wider eligible fraction than one mutation on one H-L-A allele, which is the ramp question we flagged for Clasp on October twentieth. And this round gives the market a fresh private price for the format. When Clasp next raises, the comparison an investor reaches for is two hundred and twenty-five million dollars into a preclinical company with four pharma shareholders. Know that number before the meeting.

Second item, for Eddie, and it is the third partner exit we have logged in five days. On Tuesday after the close, Nurix announced that Sanofi is discontinuing the first-in-human trial of its oral STAT-six degrader and terminating the program. Three weeks earlier, on October twelfth, Nurix booked a ten million dollar milestone from Sanofi for dosing the first patient in that same trial and said it remained eligible for about four hundred and fifty-five million more on this one program. That eligibility is now zero. No reason was given. Sanofi told analysts it still believes in STAT-six as a pathway but that this particular molecule will not move forward, and it has a second STAT-six program with the private company Recludix. RBC called the decision disappointing, given that the preclinical properties looked similar to Kymera's STAT-six degrader, which has a mid-stage readout due.

Put it on the ladder. On Sunday we logged Bristol Myers declining to advance Prothena's molecule after Phase One and leaving a fifty-five million dollar milestone unpaid. Yesterday we covered Novartis returning BioArctic's brain shuttle after fourteen months. Now Sanofi kills a molecule within weeks of first dosing. Three partners, three molecules dropped, and in none of the three cases has the licensor disclosed why. The new information in the Nurix case is structural. Nurix has earned about a hundred and thirty-nine million dollars from Sanofi since twenty twenty, starting from a fifty-five million dollar upfront. That money was earned in stages, from the upfront through option exercises to the dosing milestone. All of it was paid before the molecule had to work in a human. So the lesson from Sunday and Wednesday gets one step finer: it is not only the upfront that is real, it is every payment triggered by an event the licensor controls rather than an outcome the molecule has to earn. For our own licensing, put the milestones on things we can deliver, such as a candidate nomination, an I-N-D, or a first patient dosed, and negotiate the right to see the data that killed a molecule. For Winnow, the read is narrow: the oral convenience end of type-two inflammation just lost one entrant, but the target was not discredited, so it stays crowded.

Third item, short, for the COPD theme. Merck has started ENHANCE-three, a Phase Three trial of Ohtuvayre, the inhaled ensifentrine it acquired with Verona Pharma for about ten billion dollars. The trial enrolls about two thousand one hundred patients in twenty-eight countries. All of them have a moderate or severe exacerbation in the prior year on standard therapy, and they are randomized one to one to add the drug or a placebo. The primary endpoint is the annualized rate of adjudicated moderate or severe exacerbations over fifty-two weeks. There is no eosinophil cutoff.

The read is about what an approval does and does not buy. Ohtuvayre has been approved since June twenty twenty-four as a maintenance treatment, and it is already a ten billion dollar asset. Its owner is still paying for a two thousand patient, one-year trial to earn the exacerbation claim. That tells the COPD theme owner where the value sits in this disease. Exacerbations are the endpoint payers and prescribers price, and even an approved drug with a large buyer behind it has to buy that claim with a trial of this size. It also adds a third mechanism to the lane we described on Monday, the exacerbating patient on usual therapy, alongside the anti-I-L-thirty-three antibody and Boehringer's oral protease-pathway pill. This one is already on the market. Any Blackbird COPD program now needs its one-sentence answer for that patient against three competitors, and one of them is an approved drug.

Last item, for the oncology surface targets theme and for Eddie on structure. Alethio Therapeutics, an Oxford spinout, raised a ninety million dollar Series A co-led by Sofinnova and RA Capital. Its lead is A-T-X-zero-eleven, an antibody against G-six-b, a receptor on platelets and on the bone marrow cells that drive myeloproliferative neoplasms and marrow scarring. The company's bet is that hitting the disease-driving cell lineage works no matter which driver mutation the patient carries. Its first indication is essential thrombocythemia, which it puts at about a hundred and fifty thousand U.S. patients, more than polycythemia vera and myelofibrosis combined. The clinic is not until the first half of twenty twenty-eight.

Two reads. For Clasp, Alethio made the opposite targeting choice. Clasp aims at one mutation for specificity and accepts a narrower eligible population. Alethio aims at a lineage marker for breadth, and in doing so gives up some specificity, because platelets carry the receptor too. Both choices can be defended. Each company should be able to say why the trade it made fits its disease. For Eddie, look at who is on the cap table: the University of Oxford itself, Oxford Science Enterprises, and Cancer Research Horizons, the commercialization arm of Cancer Research U-K. That means the university, its affiliated fund and a disease charity all held their positions into a ninety million dollar round led by two specialist investors. That is the shape we want when a Johns Hopkins program spins out with Blackbird BioVentures behind it, and the timeline is the honest part: ninety million dollars buys a company more than two years to reach the clinic.

That is the watch for today. The short version: a selective T cell engager for autoimmunity just raised a hundred and thirty-three million dollars with four pharma shareholders, which validates Winnow's category and takes away its empty lane; Sanofi killed a molecule three weeks after paying for its first dose, and the only money that held up was money paid on events the licensor controlled; and an approved COPD drug is paying for a two thousand patient trial to earn the exacerbation claim. Talk tomorrow.
