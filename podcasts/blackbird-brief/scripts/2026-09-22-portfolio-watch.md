Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Tuesday, September twenty-second, and the most important development for the portfolio is a single patient. One man with a rare genetic form of A-L-S got a custom antisense drug built for his own mutation, and a year later his neurofilament light is inside the normal reference range, his function score has gone up rather than down, and he is still seeing patients as a physician. That is the first antisense drug to show benefit against a genetic A-L-S that runs through T-D-P forty-three pathology, and it lands on NuGate.

Six items. NuGate leads, then Parkinson's, the autoimmune theme, Organotics and the F-D-A, ninety seconds on Aletira's manufacturing question, and a closer on where the money for all of this comes from.

The gene is C-H-C-H-D ten. It encodes a mitochondrial membrane protein, and pathogenic variants cause a dominantly inherited, slowly progressive motor neuron disease — well under one percent of inherited A-L-S. The mechanism is the part to hold on to: the variants act by a toxic gain of function, and they produce prominent T-D-P forty-three pathology.

The patient is a fifty-one-year-old physician whose symptoms began in twenty twenty and whose father died of A-L-S. Testing found the R-fifteen-L variant and nothing else of consequence. His family contacted the n-Lorem Foundation — Stanley Crooke's nonprofit that designs individualized antisense drugs for patients too rare to interest a company — and the work was done with Mayo Clinic in Jacksonville. The foundation screened three hundred twenty candidate oligonucleotides, picked one on specificity and nonclinical safety, and put six doses into his spine over twelve months.

No serious adverse events, clean spinal fluid safety labs, mild headache and fatigue the day after dosing. Neurofilament light before treatment averaged twenty-eight point one picograms per milliliter against a normal ceiling of twenty-two point four; by three months on treatment it averaged sixteen point three — a fifty percent reduction, and inside the reference range. The A-L-S functional rating scale went from thirty-three to thirty-six; vital capacity from forty-eight percent of predicted to fifty-five; cognition stable. Untreated A-L-S loses about zero point eight nine points a month on that scale and two to three percent of vital capacity a month.

Now the comparison that makes this an item rather than a human-interest story. Put it beside tofersen, the approved antisense drug against S-O-D one. In the six-month VALOR study, in slowly progressing patients, neurofilament light fell forty percent and stayed markedly above normal; function still declined one point four points and vital capacity four point six percent. Same modality, same biomarker, same class of patient. Forty percent and still elevated bought slower decline; fifty percent and into the normal range coincided with improvement. The operative variable looks like normalization, not percentage knockdown — and that is a pharmacodynamic target you can write into a development plan.

Jon, Anthony — three things for the NuGate file.

First, and I want this owned this week. The most transferable part of the paper is not the clinical result, it is the safety argument they built before they dosed anybody. NuGate is a knockdown asset, and the question a reviewer asks about a knockdown asset is what happens to people who lose that protein. This group answered it without running a new experiment. They pulled the population constraint metrics — probability of loss-of-function intolerance zero, haploinsufficiency index seventy-seven percent — then went into U-K Biobank and counted: predicted loss-of-function variants in thirty-one controls and zero A-L-S cases. Then the mouse: knock the gene out entirely and survival is normal, while the disease-causing knock-in dies of mitochondrial cardiomyopathy. That is a reviewer-ready case that knocking the target down is tolerated, assembled from public human genetics and one existing mouse line. Whoever writes NuGate's pre-I-N-D should be able to produce the same four paragraphs for our target.

Second, the biomarker. The authors claim explicitly that neurofilament light works as a response marker in a single individual and not only across a series, and it moved by one month. That is the cheapest instrument anyone in A-L-S has, and it speaks to the question this brief has circled all month — whether your instrument can register the effect you expect at a sample size you can afford. But read the limitation honestly, because it is the same limitation: the patient fell and had a mild concussion after the fourth dose, and his neurofilament light ticked up. It will register a fall in a parking lot as readily as a treatment failure. Good primary pharmacodynamic endpoint, bad sole efficacy endpoint. And the three-point function gain sits just under the three point seven per three months proposed as the minimal clinically important difference, so the honest sentence is biomarker normalization with functional stability, not proven benefit.

Third, Hemaka, yours. The authors say that if this holds up it would be the first antisense drug with benefit against a genetic A-L-S linked to T-D-P forty-three pathology. We have two active sourcing leads on that axis — the Wong lab work at Johns Hopkins that was Saturday's Radar lead, and Jiou Wang's cell-type-resolved T-D-P forty-three biology. This is the first clinical evidence that intervening upstream of that pathology in a genetically defined patient moves a biomarker and holds function. It belongs in the next memo on either lead.

And one note on positioning. n-Lorem provides these drugs free for life and has expanded the program to other sites, so the mutation-specific ultra-rare lane in A-L-S has a non-commercial occupant a company should not compete with. That clarifies NuGate rather than threatening it: the pitch stays the pan-A-L-S and F-T-D target, and this drug is non-allele-selective, which is the same architecture at a smaller denominator.

Second item, the Parkinson's theme.

On Thursday a venture studio called Palomar Labs spun out its first company, Ariadne Bio, and the sourcing channel is more interesting than the drug. Palomar's thesis is to find candidates with a documented history of human use, in conditions of late life, and carry them to proof of concept. Their lead molecule, AB-three hundred, is built on a compound tested in humans in the nineteen seventies and documented then to lack hallucinogenic effects.

Eddie, Hemaka — that is a third sourcing channel, and it is not the one we opened on September fifth. That screen was on divested and shelved large-pharma assets, and on the nineteenth we learned its first filter: a shelved asset is only reachable if the original transaction was a license with reversion rather than an outright purchase. A molecule from the nineteen seventies has no counterparty at all — no reversion clause, no buyer to persuade, no purchase price. The asset is prior human safety data and a synthetic route, and the diligence question is not who owns it but whether the old human data lets you skip anything.

The drug is a non-hallucinogenic serotonin 2-A receptor agonist, apparently because of signaling bias — a paper in January found the parent compound preferentially engages the non-hallucinogenic intracellular pathway without losing the therapeutic effect — and off-target screening shows no measurable activity at dopamine receptors or the dopamine transporter.

Anthony, Jon — why this is a real read and not a psychedelics story. On September eighth we concluded that the constraint the Parkinson's field names for itself is diagnostic latency, not target choice, and that the available target lanes are contested or priced. Ariadne enters none of them. It picks a symptom domain: apathy, a persistent loss of goal-directed behavior, in roughly forty percent of Parkinson's patients over the illness, concentrated in mid and late disease, with no approved therapy in any indication — and caregiver-burden studies rank the neuropsychiatric symptoms ahead of the motor ones. The selection problem disappears, because the patient is already diagnosed and already in clinic. That is the cleanest answer I have seen to the September eighth rule that every Parkinson's value case must say which patient it treats and how that patient is found.

The preclinical package matters for a different reason. They use tetrabenazine, an approved dopamine-depleting drug, to induce a low-motivation phenotype, then measure effort with progressive-ratio lever pressing. AB-three hundred on top of tetrabenazine raised motivated behavior eighty-nine percent over tetrabenazine alone, at doses they predict are clinically relevant. And in the same model they reproduced the worsening of apathy that is clinically observed when S-S-R-Is are used to treat it. Virginia, Avi — they built a negative control out of a known clinical failure. That is a far stronger claim than our compound worked in our assay, and it is a template we should be asking for.

They have had their Type B pre-I-N-D meeting; the Phase one-b starts this quarter or next and ends with a twenty-eight-day placebo-controlled segment in patients with clinically relevant apathy, funded by Palomar plus the Michael J. Fox Foundation's therapeutics pipeline program. One file note: a receptor-selective, signaling-biased, non-hallucinogenic serotonin 2-A agonist is the plastogen class, which we track through the Gould lab at U-M-B. Ariadne is now the nearest clinical-stage comparator to anything out of that lead, which moves the question from whether the class is real to what we have that AB-three hundred does not.

Fourth item, regulatory, with a deadline.

Yesterday the F-D-A issued a direct final rule stripping the word "animal" out of its drug safety testing regulations. Where the rules said animal tests and animal studies they will now say nonclinical tests and nonclinical studies, with definitions aligned to the Food and Drug Omnibus Reform Act of twenty twenty-two. The operative change is not vocabulary — it removes the language implying animal testing is the only acceptable way to generate safety evidence before you go into humans, though the agency was explicit that it does not prohibit animal studies. Alongside the rule it published a database of twenty-five worked examples of new approach methodologies drawn from its own public review materials. The rule takes effect the fourth of February; comments close the seventh of December.
