## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Monday, November sixteenth. Four items today, and the thread running through three of them is the same one: the vehicle, the modality and the endpoint are all being repriced by other people's money this week, and each repricing lands on one of our programs.

Start with Aletira, because the money moved first thing this morning.

Seawolf Therapeutics came out of stealth in San Diego with a hundred-million-dollar Series A. Versant Ventures founded the company, and the round includes the Novartis Venture Fund, Eli Lilly and CRISPR Therapeutics. Announced in the same release: a multi-target research and exclusive licence agreement with Sanofi worth up to one point two billion dollars in milestones plus tiered royalties, pointed first at bleeding disorders. The platform is called Trident. It is a lipid nanoparticle that carries D-N-A rather than R-N-A, and the entire pitch is in one word — redosable. The claim is gene-therapy-like durability with the potency, safety profile and manufacturability of an R-N-A nanoparticle, and without the thing that defines adeno-associated virus: you get one shot, because the first dose raises antibodies that block the second. The chief executive is Elizabeth Alcamo, previously an entrepreneur in residence at Versant and before that in senior roles at CODA Biotherapeutics and 4D Molecular Therapeutics. The lead program is in metabolic disease and the company disclosed nothing further about it. So be clear on what exists today: animal data and a balance sheet. No human dosing.

Matt, Avi, here is the read, and it is a favorable one, which is not where I expected to land. Since September we have been pressing a specific question — which kind of safety does SELEXON actually buy? Our answer has been honest and narrow: it reduces expression where you don't want it, from a given dose of virus. It does not reduce the number of capsids you inject and it does not buy you a second injection. Seawolf is a well-funded bet that the field solves the second-injection problem by abandoning the capsid entirely. If that bet lands, it helps us. A nanoparticle carrying D-N-A goes where particles go — largely liver — and it has no capsid tropism to lean on for cell-type targeting at all. A cassette that restricts expression to the right cell type at the splicing level, independent of the delivery vehicle, is arguably worth more bolted onto non-viral D-N-A than onto an A-A-V, because there the cassette is the only selectivity in the system.

So the action is concrete, and it is a data-package action, not a strategy memo. Make sure SELEXON's package demonstrates the cassette working on a plasmid or nanoparticle-delivered D-N-A payload, not only inside an A-A-V genome. If that experiment is cheap, run it before the Series A, because it converts the platform story from an A-A-V add-on into a vehicle-agnostic selectivity layer, and that is a materially larger market. And note who wrote the cheques. Lilly is now inside a non-viral D-N-A company and also owns Akouos in hearing. Sanofi committed over a billion in milestones. Two strategics just told us with capital that the delivery vehicle is an open question.

Item two is for Winnow, and it is the full version of something we covered in topline form in September.

At A-C-R last week, Beeline Medicines presented the complete phase two of afimetoran in systemic lupus. Afimetoran is an oral, once-daily, equipotent inhibitor of the innate immune sensors T-L-R seven and T-L-R eight — the sensors that read nucleic acid and drive the interferon response in lupus. It was discovered inside Bristol Myers Squibb and licensed to Beeline in July of last year, when Bristol and Bain Capital carved five immunology assets into an independent company.

The data. Two hundred forty-eight patients, four arms, forty-eight weeks, on background medication with mandatory corticosteroid tapering. Lupus responder index response at week forty-eight was sixty-eight percent at two and a half milligrams, sixty-four at ten, sixty-six at thirty, against thirty-four percent on placebo. That is a placebo-adjusted advantage of roughly thirty points at every dose. The BICLA composite, which requires improvement in every organ system that was active at baseline, ran thirty-nine to fifty-one percent against twenty-eight. Low disease activity state, thirty-six to thirty-nine against twenty-one. Between seventy-two and seventy-five percent of treated patients reached or held a steroid dose at or below seven and a half milligrams a day, against fifty percent on placebo. Generally well tolerated over the full forty-eight weeks, no new safety signals. Phase three in lupus starts in twenty twenty-seven, and there is already a phase two-three running in the cutaneous form.

Eddie, Jon, two reads, and the first one is a trap I want us to avoid. Yesterday we covered the European recommendation for Gazyva in lupus, which carried a twenty-three-point gap on the same responder index. The tempting sentence is that an oral pill just beat an infused antibody by seven points. Do not say that sentence. Gazyva's placebo arm responded at fifty-four percent; Beeline's placebo arm responded at thirty-four. The absolute treated rates are about seventy-seven percent and sixty-six. In lupus, a cross-trial gap mostly measures the placebo arm, and the two trials recruited different patients.

The second read is the one that changes something. The effect is flat across a twelve-fold dose range — the lowest dose performed as well as the highest. There are two explanations and they point in opposite directions. Either the target is fully saturated at two and a half milligrams, which is good news for therapeutic index and for the eventual price of goods, or the responder index has hit its ceiling for this mechanism in this population and the instrument can no longer resolve the difference. Watch which dose goes into phase three; if they take the lowest one forward, that tells you the company believes the first explanation.

Put yesterday and today together and you get the strategic point for Winnow. In moderate-to-severe lupus that has not failed everything, a partner will shortly be able to choose between an old, cheap, glyco-engineered depleting antibody with a European recommendation and an oral pill heading into phase three, both clearing placebo on the responder index by twenty to thirty points. A T-cell engager does not win that patient on the responder index, and we should stop designing as if it could. It wins on depth and on duration off drug — drug-free remission by the stricter DORIS definition in genuinely refractory disease. Beeline did not report DORIS at all. That tells us both which endpoint Winnow's first clinical claim should be built on and which patient it should be built in. And for the divested-asset screen, Eddie: the Bristol Myers castoff that showed in September that these assets can be good now has a two-hundred-forty-eight-patient phase two behind it and a pivotal program in front of it. Bristol kept exposure through the Bain structure rather than selling outright. That licensor-with-residuals role is the one we have said repeatedly is ours.

Item three is also Winnow, and it is a new modality entering the lane from an unexpected direction.

Commit Biologics, in Copenhagen, nominated its first development candidate on Wednesday. The platform is called BiCE — bispecific complement engagers. Instead of recruiting a T cell to kill a target cell, which is what every engager we track does, these antibodies recruit the complement system. The candidate, COM-zero-zero-two, goes first into relapsed or refractory non-Hodgkin lymphoma with stated plans to expand into autoimmune disease. The company also hired a chief operating officer and a head of clinical development, and says first patient dosing is expected in early twenty twenty-eight. The supporting evidence they point to is non-human primate data from November of last year showing rapid, deep and sustained B-cell depletion with no treatment-related safety findings — and, the phrase they lead on, without systemic cytokine release.

Virginia, Avi — that last clause is the whole item. Every differentiation conversation we have had about engagers in autoimmunity has been about controlling the collateral of T-cell activation: cytokine release, step dosing, hospitalisation, the infection burden that comes with deep depletion. Here is a group claiming to get the depletion while routing around the effector cell that causes the collateral. In primates, in a release, with no human data and first dosing more than a year away, so it is not a near-term threat to anything. But it belongs on the competitive map for one reason: it is a third answer to the question Winnow's pitch answers. Depletion with a naked antibody is answer one. Depletion with a T-cell engager, selective or otherwise, is answer two. Depletion by recruiting complement is answer three, and its pitch is explicitly "the depletion without the cytokines." If our one-sentence selectivity claim is about sparing the immune system the collateral of broad killing, then we now need that sentence to distinguish us from a complement engager as well as from a C-D-twenty antibody. Jon, worth a look at their primate data package when it publishes — the B-cell depletion kinetics without cytokine release is either a real mechanistic advantage or an artifact of dose, and which one it is matters to our framing.

Last item, short, and it is a follow-up that prices a decision we covered at the time.

In September, Cellectis told the market that its board had approved a transformation out of allogeneic C-A-R-T and into in vivo gene editing. We ran it then as a fourth row in the Winnow target product profile — a company whose founder built his career on allogeneic cell therapy stating in its own release that bispecific antibodies had taken its market. On Wednesday that decision got its price tag. Cellectis disclosed that the realignment will cut up to seventy percent of its global headcount. The company had two hundred twenty-nine full-time employees at the end of last year, so that is on the order of a hundred and sixty people across Paris, New York and Raleigh, running through the rest of this year and into twenty twenty-seven, subject to the French works council process. Enrollment has stopped in both clinical C-A-R-T programs and site closeout is underway. What is left is two in vivo editing candidates in cardiovascular lipids — one base editor against APOC3 for severe hypertriglyceridemia, one epigenetic editor against PCSK9 for high cholesterol — both heading into investigator-initiated trials in China, with first clinical data in the second half of twenty twenty-seven and the second program's in the first half of twenty twenty-eight. A hundred forty-nine million in cash, runway stated into the second half of twenty twenty-eight.

Matt, Emily, Hemaka — two things worth keeping. The first is the proportion. A platform pivot that reads as a strategy slide in September costs seventy percent of the organization in November, and the new programs still do not produce human data for almost a year. When we model a pivot for a portfolio company, model the headcount and the dead time, not just the new thesis. The second is a sourcing note rather than a threat. Two clinical-stage allogeneic C-A-R-T programs with manufacturing behind them are being closed out and the company said in September it wanted partners for them. That is the shelved-asset screen we opened in September, except the shelf is a whole clinical cell-therapy franchise and the seller has publicly stopped believing in the category. Eddie, that is a phone call, not a build — and the honest answer may well be that the market is right and we pass. But we should know what the ask is.

That is the watch. A hundred million dollars plus a billion-dollar Sanofi deal say the delivery vehicle for genetic medicine is an open question, which makes a vehicle-independent selectivity cassette more valuable rather than less — so prove SELEXON works outside a capsid. Afimetoran's full lupus data make the responder index a commodity and push Winnow's claim onto depth and drug-free remission in refractory disease. A complement engager out of Copenhagen is a third answer to the question our engager answers. And Cellectis has shown us what a platform pivot costs when the bill arrives. Have a good week.
