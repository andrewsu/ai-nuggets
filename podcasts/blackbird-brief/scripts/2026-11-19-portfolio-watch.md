## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Thursday, November nineteenth. Three items. The first is the largest cheque anyone has written into our competitive lane, and it was written for three molecules that have never been in an animal study you could call pivotal.

Start with Winnow.

Yesterday Pfizer entered into an exclusive licensing agreement with Starna Therapeutics, a Chinese R-N-A company. Pfizer gets exclusive rights outside Greater China to three pre-I-N-D in vivo C-A-R T programs in autoimmune disease. It also gets rights to use Starna's in vivo C-A-R T technology across inflammation and immunology and across oncology, options to license compounds coming out of a separate research collaboration, and an option to extend the territory back into Greater China. Three hundred thirty million dollars upfront, up to two point three billion in milestones, plus royalties. Pfizer will manufacture clinical supply in the United States. Starna's platform is a proprietary targeted lipid nanoparticle delivering R-N-A. Pfizer's own framing, from Michael Vincent, their chief inflammation and immunology officer, is that in vivo C-A-R T is emerging and promising, and the patients who benefit most are the ones for whom today's options fall short. A Pfizer spokesperson confirmed this is the first time the company has worked on in vivo C-A-R T at all.

Three hundred thirty million dollars, upfront, in cash, for three preclinical programs.

Jon, Anthony — hold that number against the week it landed in. On Monday BioSpace published a tally of recent setbacks in autoimmune drug development, and the list is not kind. Novartis and Bristol Myers Squibb both paused C-A-R T trials in autoimmune conditions late this summer — Novartis after patient deaths, Bristol after safety signals. Artiva disclosed two patient deaths with its off-the-shelf natural killer cell therapy, raised questions about durability of response, and cut ninety-four percent of its staff. Argenx walked away from a Phase 3 in Sjögren's on futility. So in the same seven-day window, the clinical record of cell therapy in autoimmunity got worse and the price of preclinical access to it went up.

That is not irrational, and I want to be precise about why, because the reasoning is the thing we can use.

Recall the test we took from Mike Rea three weeks ago — name the premise that has to hold for the price to make sense, say when it gets tested, and define the result that would make you walk away. Pfizer's premise here is two-part. Part one: moving C-A-R T generation inside the body removes the manufacturing problem — the slot, the apheresis, the courier window, the scale-out economics we walked through in September. That part is almost certainly true, and Pfizer, which built lipid nanoparticle manufacturing at coronavirus-vaccine scale, is better positioned to make it true than almost anyone.

Part two is the one being bought without evidence. The premise that in vivo generation also fixes the safety problem. Look at what the setbacks actually were. Novartis and Bristol paused autologous products over deaths and safety signals. Artiva's deaths came with an allogeneic product. The toxicity in this field is not a manufacturing artifact — it is what a potent depleting effector does once it exists in a patient. Generating that effector from a nanoparticle changes who builds the cell. It does not change what the cell does afterward. And it takes away the one thing you had: you cannot un-dose a lipid nanoparticle, you cannot titrate a cell population that is expanding on its own, and you cannot stop.

Which is our sentence. Interruptible, titratable, redosable. Those three words have been Winnow's position since October, and the biggest deal in the category just made them sharper, not weaker. The correct read is not that engagers lost — it is that pharma has now paid three hundred thirty million dollars to take on a control problem that an engager does not have, because the convenience and cost prize is big enough to justify it. Our pitch is no longer "cell therapy is operationally impossible." Pharma has answered that one with its wallet. The pitch is "when this goes wrong in a lupus patient who was functioning on cheap rituximab, what is the off switch?"

Two more notes on this one.

Virginia, Avi — the selectivity claim is a targeted lipid nanoparticle, which makes this the fourth delivery vehicle in four days to make an enrichment claim and let it be heard as a restriction claim. Monday, lipid nanoparticles with essentially no tropism. Tuesday, an evolved capsid whose own authors called it lineage-restricted enhancement. Wednesday, a transferrin receptor conjugate whose selectivity is a gradient on a receptor every dividing cell carries. Today, a targeted lipid nanoparticle reprogramming T cells in vivo. Same structural answer every time. Targeting the vehicle moves the distribution; it does not set anything to zero. When Starna's selectivity data become public, read it as enrichment until proven otherwise.

And Eddie — study the deal shape, because it is not an asset deal. Pfizer bought three programs, plus platform rights in two whole therapeutic areas, plus options on compounds from a research collaboration that doesn't exist yet, plus a territory option. That is a platform deal wearing an asset deal's suit, and the upfront was twelve and a half percent of total deal value, which is a high cash ratio for preclinical work. Pharma paid that much cash because it was buying optionality across a platform, not a molecule. For the Winnow Series A, the implication is direct: the story is the selectivity mechanism as a platform, with the second and third targets named, not the lead program alone.

Item two is about Clasp, and it is a useful number right next to the one I just gave you.

This morning DISCO Pharmaceuticals of Cologne announced a strategic collaboration and option-to-license agreement with AbbVie to discover and develop AND-gated bispecific therapeutics for solid tumors — AND as in the logic gate, antibodies that only become fully active when both of their targets are present on the same cell. Forty million dollars upfront. Up to two and a half billion in option, development and milestone payments, plus tiered royalties.

Forty million dollars against two and a half billion is an upfront ratio of about one and a half percent. Pfizer's was twelve and a half. Same week, two deals, headline numbers within a hundred million dollars of each other, and nearly a tenfold difference in how much cash actually changed hands. The difference is what was bought. Pfizer bought three defined preclinical programs and platform rights. AbbVie bought an option on discovery that has not happened yet. Eddie, Emily — that is the calibration to carry into any conversation where someone quotes a deal "worth up to" a number. The biobucks headline tells you almost nothing; the upfront tells you what stage the buyer thinks the science is at.

Now the science, because this one lands directly on Clasp's thesis.

DISCO's platform maps the cancer cell surface — not the genome or the transcriptome, the actual proteins present on the outside of the cell and the neighboring proteins they cluster with, which the company calls protein communities. The output is not a molecule. The output is target pairs: two antigens whose intersection is tumor-specific even though neither one is. They have built the first surfaceome map in small cell lung cancer and have lead programs heading toward the clinic there and in microsatellite-stable colorectal cancer. The chief executive is Mark Manfredi, who ran Ikena Oncology and was a Takeda oncology biology vice president. The founder is Roman Thomas, a physician-scientist in Cologne. They closed a thirty-six-million-euro seed round last December, with AbbVie Ventures already on the cap table, and they signed Amgen in January on a deal worth up to six hundred eighteen million.

Virginia, Avi — ten days ago we took the J&J prostate result and framed it as two ways to buy specificity. Target-level, which is Clasp's bet: one target that only exists on a tumor because it is a mutant peptide presented on M-H-C. Or coincidence, which is the logic gate: two ordinary targets whose overlap is rare. AbbVie just put two and a half billion dollars of potential value behind the coincidence route, and here is the part that matters — the money did not go to antibody engineering. It went to the layer that finds the pairs. The field has decided the hard problem in AND-gating is not building the gate. It is knowing which two antigens to gate on.

That is good news for how we think about Clasp, not bad. The two approaches fail in different places. An AND-gate fails when some healthy tissue you did not map co-expresses both antigens — which is exactly why the surfaceome map is the asset and why AbbVie paid for the map rather than the molecule. Clasp's approach fails on antigen density and H-L-A restriction, which is a narrower, better-characterized failure mode and one you can test early. The competitive risk to Clasp is not that AND-gating wins; it is that the AND-gate route expands the addressable target space faster, because it can work with ordinary proteins, and target-level specificity cannot.

And Esther, Avi, one sourcing read. DISCO is an academic spinout whose core asset is a methods capability — a way of looking at cells. It has signed two pharma deals, worth a combined three point one billion in potential value, and it has no clinical asset and arguably no proprietary molecule in either deal. It sold targets. That is a licensing shape we can run from a methods lab at Hopkins or U-M-B, and it is worth putting in front of the capability-platform leads on our list: a tool that reliably generates targets is partnerable years before a molecule exists.

Last item, and it is for the neuropsych program.

Yesterday at the Alzheimer's clinical trials conference in Boston, Bristol Myers Squibb gave the first look at clinical data from the ADEPT program — xanomeline-trospium, which you know as Cobenfy, in psychosis associated with Alzheimer's disease. Two open-label datasets. In the twelve-week open-label lead-in to the first trial, two hundred seventy-three of three hundred twenty patients completed, and the clinician-rated hallucinations and delusions score fell by twelve points from a baseline of fourteen point four. In the fifty-two-week open-label extension, four hundred one enrolled, two hundred seventy-three completed, and the same score fell about five points from a baseline of nearly nine.

Hemaka, Maisha — ignore the efficacy numbers entirely. They are open-label, uncontrolled, descriptive, and an eighty-three percent reduction with no comparator in a fluctuating neuropsychiatric symptom is not a result, it is a baseline-plus-attention effect waiting to be measured properly. Truist said as much: a supportive bridge, incremental, and they acknowledged the difficulty of separating drug activity from symptom fluctuation. The actual primary endpoint of that trial is time to relapse during a twenty-six-week randomized withdrawal period, and that is still blinded. It reads out in twenty twenty-seven, after delays the company has blamed on enrollment.

The column to read is tolerability, because tolerability is our differentiation axis and we concluded two weeks ago that it is now the entry fee rather than the position. Treatment-emergent adverse events in fifty-seven percent of patients over twelve weeks — in an open-label lead-in, which by construction is the most favorable tolerability setting that trial design produces. Sixty-five percent over fifty-two weeks. Nearly a third of the extension cohort did not complete the year. And the detail that tells you the most: Bristol moved from twice-daily to three-times-daily dosing in these studies, specifically so patients could reach an effective dose without gastrointestinal side effects driving them off treatment. That is a company re-engineering the dosing schedule around tolerability three years after a fourteen-billion-dollar acquisition, on a mechanism whose peripheral anticholinergic burden is intrinsic and whose existing answer to it — a second drug in the capsule — is already in the product.

So the argument for a mechanism that does not need a peripheral blocker just got a worked example, with numbers, from the incumbent. That is the slide. Not "our tolerability is better," which every preclinical program says, but "here is what the approved first-in-class non-dopaminergic drug is still doing about tolerability in year three, and here is why our mechanism does not inherit that problem."

The commercial half is worth a line too. Truist models two point six billion dollars in worldwide Cobenfy sales, and a large part of that model is this expansion — an indication with no approved therapy where psychosis affects somewhere between thirty and fifty percent of Alzheimer's patients over the course of the disease. The schizophrenia label was never the asset. The reach of the receptor across C-N-S indications is the asset, and ADEPT is the worked example of how an incumbent monetizes that. Build the GPR52 story the same way, and put the twenty twenty-seven ADEPT readouts on the calendar, because they will price this category right about when our round is open.

That is the watch. Pfizer paid three hundred thirty million dollars in cash for three preclinical in vivo C-A-R T programs in the same week the clinical record of autoimmune cell therapy got worse, which means the convenience prize is now priced and the control problem is ours to sell against. AbbVie paid forty million dollars for an option on a surfaceome map, which tells you the field thinks the bottleneck in logic-gated specificity is finding the target pair, not building the gate. And the incumbent muscarinic drug is on its third dosing schedule and still shedding a third of its patients over a year, which is the best tolerability slide anyone could have written for us. Back tomorrow.
