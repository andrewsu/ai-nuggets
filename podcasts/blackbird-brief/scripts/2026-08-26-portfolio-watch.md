## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Wednesday, August twenty-sixth. Our anchor partner just published its own scoreboard, and read next to two funding reports that landed the same day, it says something uncomfortable and useful: Johns Hopkins is generating inventions at industrial scale and capital at artisanal scale, and the gap between those two numbers is the exact space Blackbird occupies.

Start with the scoreboard. Johns Hopkins Technology Ventures released its fiscal-year twenty twenty-six annual report — posted on the seventeenth, picked up by the university's own news desk yesterday — covering the year that ended June thirtieth. Revenue was just under one hundred million dollars. Sixty-five million of that is licensing. Four hundred sixty-two invention reports came in. One hundred eleven new United States patents, against a managed estate of nearly thirty-five hundred active patents. One hundred twenty licensing agreements. Three hundred forty-nine products on market. Ninety-eight active research-based corporate partnerships. Seventeen new startups formed, one hundred forty-two active startups in the portfolio, and those companies raised one hundred fifty-one million dollars in venture capital, of which one hundred sixteen million — seventy-seven percent — stayed in Baltimore. Christy Wyskiel's framing is that the return is not only financial: patients with glioma living longer, prostate cancer caught earlier.

Now do the arithmetic she didn't do, because three of those numbers are the whole story for us.

The first is concentration. The majority of that sixty-five million in licensing revenue comes from two products. Pylarify, the prostate imaging agent built on Martin Pomper's chemistry, approved in twenty twenty-one. And vorasidenib for low-grade glioma, approved in twenty twenty-four, the first effective new drug in that tumor type in thirty years. Two products. Both approved in the last five years, both licensed out of science that was done many years before that. Which means the institution's licensing income today is a lagging indicator of decisions made a decade and a half ago, and the honest read is that the tail underneath those two is thin. That is not a criticism of Hopkins — it is the shape of every tech-transfer office's revenue curve. But it tells you what the institution's incentive actually is right now, and it is not extracting a better royalty from us on a preclinical asset. It is finding the next Pylarify. Eddie, Matt: that is the frame for every conversation at Ashland Avenue. We are not asking them for a favor on terms. We are the mechanism by which the number in the fiscal twenty forty report gets written.

The second number is the funnel. Four hundred sixty-two invention reports came in. Seventeen startups came out. That is a conversion rate under four percent, and it is not because the other ninety-six percent are bad — most of them are licensing candidates or too early or have no champion willing to leave the bench. The constriction sits at exactly one point: turning a disclosure into an investable package with an operator attached. That is the entire Blackbird thesis stated as a ratio. It is also, candidly, an argument for measuring ourselves against it. Hemaka, Jonathan, Anthony, Virginia, Matt Lawler — if the venture-partner model works, our conversion rate on the disclosures we actually look at should embarrass that number, and we should know what it is.

The third number is the one I want you to sit with. One hundred forty-two active Hopkins startups raised one hundred fifty-one million dollars in a year. That is roughly one million dollars per company per year across the whole cohort.

Hold that against what else came out yesterday. MassBio published its twenty twenty-six industry snapshot. Massachusetts biopharma raised three point four five billion dollars in venture in the first half of this year alone, up twenty-five percent, a quarter of all United States biopharma venture, second only to California. Inside that headline is the number that matters: the average Series A in Massachusetts ballooned to just under eighty million dollars, while the average seed round fell to four point six five million, down from seven point six five million in the first half of last year. Seed shrank by nearly forty percent in a year in which total capital grew twenty-five percent.

So the entire Johns Hopkins startup cohort — one hundred forty-two companies, a full year — raised less than two Massachusetts Series A rounds.

That is the barbell, and it is worse than a slow market, because a slow market is symmetric and this is not. Kendalle Burlin O'Connell's line was that a healthy ecosystem needs the renewed momentum to also reach the front end of the pipeline, and her stated worry is the earliest startups with the riskiest science. Ben Bradford's version of what founders now have to show is three words — novelty, commercializability, and confidence in leadership — and I'd note that the third one is the one money is actually pricing.

The top end confirms the shape. Twenty-five biotechs have gone public year to date, five of them in August, the busiest year since twenty twenty-one, against just eight in all of last year, with roughly thirty projected by year end. Leerink's Jack Bannister calls it approaching escape velocity and says the difference from twenty twenty-one is selectivity — investors writing hundred-million-dollar-plus checks with conviction rather than spraying. He names two yellow flags that would end it: preclinical companies rushing to get public, and teams overvaluing themselves. Barclays' Emily Field told CNBC on Monday that the mergers-and-acquisitions freeze is over and the capital markets are open. So: exits open, growth rounds enormous, seed starving.

Three consequences, and they are not abstract.

First, for Aletira. Geoff, Hugh — the Series A you are walking toward is being priced in a market where the mean round is near eighty million dollars and the investors writing it are explicitly buying near-term inflection and conservative valuation. Both of Bannister's yellow flags are things a well-funded incubated company can trip over without noticing: going out too early because the platform story is compelling, and pricing off the platform rather than off the asset. The pre-investigational-new-drug package we have been building for six days of episodes now — the endpoint agreed in writing, the long-term safety dataset, the surveillance plan — is not just regulatory hygiene. In this market it is the inflection point that makes the round fundable. Same instruction, different reason.

Second, for the model itself. A one-hundred-million-dollar nonprofit grant deploying into de-risking academic IP is a countercyclical instrument, and the cycle just moved in our favor in the least comfortable way possible. The seed round the market has stopped writing is precisely the check Blackbird Laboratories writes as a grant. At four point six five million dollars, a market seed no longer buys a package that clears an eighty-million-dollar Series A — which means for a great many good academic programs there is now no bridge at all unless someone builds it deliberately. Maisha, Matt: that is the sentence for the Bisciotti Foundation and for the state. Not "we fund early science." We manufacture the specific asset the capital markets have stopped producing and still demand.

Third, for recruiting. Massachusetts shed more than thirty-six hundred life-sciences jobs last year, its first annual decline in more than two decades, with research and development down almost four percent — while capital in the same geography went up twenty-five percent. Capital and employment have decoupled up there. Emily, Bridget, Yixuan: a soft Boston labor market in research roles, at a moment when we have wet-lab benches at City Garage, is a hiring window, and it will close when the capital finally reaches payroll.

Last item, and it comes back to Hopkins. Alongside the annual report, Johns Hopkins Technology Ventures named nine translational-funding awards across three vehicles — the Cohen fund for engineering, the Thalheimer fund for proof-of-concept and validation work across the university, and the Zizic fund, which is running its first biomedical cycle. Amounts are not disclosed. What matters is that this is the institution telling you, in public and before publication, which nine of its own projects it believes are closest to translation. That is a pre-competitive longlist, and it is free.

Three of the nine sit directly on our theses. Jonathan Schneck in Pathology is generating chimeric antigen receptor T cells in vivo using targeted polymeric messenger-R-N-A nanoparticles, for autoimmune disease — that is cell-type-targeted nucleic acid delivery, which is the same problem SELEXON attacks one layer down, and it is adjacent to the Mao lab nanoparticle work already in our file. Won Jin Ho at the Kimmel Center is working on immunotherapy against immune-resistant tumors, which touches both the Clasp and aSKY landscapes. And Yun Guan in Anesthesiology is developing allosteric modulators of M-R-G-P-R-X-one for non-addictive analgesia.

That third one deserves a beat, because of what we said on Friday and again on Monday. Roughly three hundred ninety-five million dollars went into pain assets in five days — Ambros and Werewolf, then Slate and Fulcrum — both through a reverse merger into a Nasdaq shell, and neither one on a molecule the buyer discovered. The money in pain is real and it is currently importing its chemistry from Italy and China. Hopkins just put translational funding behind a novel, non-opioid receptor mechanism on our own campus. Hemaka, Esther: get the full nine-project list and the program officers behind those three funds, and find out where Guan's program sits on intellectual property — whether there is a filing, and whether it is claimed. The pain buyers are paying for a stratification package on a de-risked target class. A first-in-class receptor mechanism at proof-of-concept stage is the earlier, cheaper version of the thing they are bidding for.

Quickly, the quiet threads. No new gene-therapy development beyond yesterday's Regenxbio hold; analyst commentary is that it feeds the F-D-A-unpredictability leg of the gene-therapy bear case, which is a sentiment problem layered on the genotoxicity problem, and both of them price into our raise. Zero G-P-R fifty-two items and still no named buyer for the Nxera asset. Nothing from CAMP4 since the quarter. Silence from Clasp, Adventris, aSKY, Skylark and Sensorion. Nothing touching a Blackbird program in last Friday's regional roundup, and no mentions of Blackbird Laboratories or Blackbird BioVentures anywhere in the window. Ahead: Ultragenyx's Sanfilippo decision on September nineteenth, CAMP4's analyst day on September twenty-eighth, the Nxera out-license, the Clasp monotherapy readout this year, and Capricor on November twenty-second.

To close. Our anchor institution produced four hundred sixty-two inventions and one million dollars per portfolio company last year, in a market where a single Series A now averages eighty. The bottleneck is not discovery and it is not exit capital — both are abundant. It is the twenty-four months in between, and that gap widened by forty percent in a single year. Every argument Blackbird makes to a founder, a foundation, or a state legislator should be built on that one number. I'll see you tomorrow.
