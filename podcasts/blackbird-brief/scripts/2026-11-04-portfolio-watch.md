## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Wednesday, November fourth. The most important development for the portfolio landed after the close yesterday: Ultragenyx, a company that won two gene therapy approvals from the F-D-A inside a single month this summer, is cutting a fifth of its workforce and halving research spending. The money that carries it into profitability is not coming from either drug. It is coming from the two vouchers the approvals came with.

Here is what the third-quarter release says. Ultragenyx sold the two rare pediatric disease priority review vouchers it received with Genglycos, its gene therapy for glycogen storage disease type one-A, and Fayuvi, its gene therapy for Sanfilippo syndrome type A. Two hundred and ten million dollars for the first, two hundred and three million for the second, four hundred and thirteen million combined. The company expects net proceeds of about three hundred and fifty million. Put that next to its cash balance at the end of September, which was four hundred and one million. The vouchers are worth almost as much as everything the company had in the bank.

At the same time, headcount goes down about twenty percent to roughly a thousand people, twenty twenty-seven research spending comes in under three hundred and fifty million dollars, which is at least fifty percent below twenty twenty-five, and two programs close. One is the Angelman antisense program, GTX-102, which we used in September as the worked example of an open-label package that could not carry a neurodevelopmental claim. All remaining studies are being wound down. The other is UX701, an A-A-V nine gene therapy for Wilson disease. Its safety was clean across every cohort. It failed on a specific bar: the company required that a majority of patients reach a biochemical response and come off standard of care before it would go to the pivotal stage. Some patients showed activity. Not a majority.

Three reads. The first is for Emily and Eddie, and it settles an argument. In September William Blair wrote that neither of these approvals was projected to become a blockbuster and that the sellable vouchers were what would shore up the balance sheet. Yesterday that became the plan of record. A company with two first-in-disease gene therapies on the market is financing its turn to profit on regulatory paper rather than on product revenue. For Aletira, the open pre-I-N-D question is still whether a congenital hearing loss program could qualify for a voucher and what one would be worth when we received it. We now have two clean prints just above two hundred million dollars, and a reminder that the gap between four hundred and thirteen gross and three hundred and fifty net is real. On the first voucher, twenty percent of the gross went to the N-I-H under a twenty eighteen patent license. A licensor's share of a voucher is a term somebody negotiated. Every Blackbird grant and license that could end in a pediatric rare disease approval should say in writing who owns the voucher.

The second read is for Avi and the SELEXON team. The Wilson failure is not a safety story and not a delivery story. It is an expression story. The bar was getting enough function, durably enough, that patients could stop their existing therapy, and an A-A-V nine vector that was safe and active at four dose levels did not reliably clear it. That is the same gap SELEXON is pitched against, coming from the opposite side. Our problem has been too much expression in the wrong cells. Theirs was not enough in the right ones. Both come down to how precisely you control expression, and a lost Wilson program is a cleaner comparator for the deck than a hypothetical.

The third read is for Matt Lawler and the board, and it is about what sits underneath a launch. This is the most accomplished rare-disease gene therapy developer in the United States, with two approvals in two months, and its first move after them is to shrink. Gene therapy approvals fund one thing well, which is their own voucher. The commercial ramp of a one-time therapy is slow enough that the company bridging to it needs a second financing source. If Aletira ever gets to a launch, plan for that bridge from the start rather than assuming the product will pay for it.

Second item, for Hemaka and NuGate. Specific Biologics, a Toronto gene editing company, closed an oversubscribed thirty-five million dollar Series A led by Hatteras Venture Partners. The money takes its lead candidate through a first biomarker cohort in an adaptive Phase One-Two trial in C-nine-orf-seventy-two A-L-S. The technology is a compact two-site editor called Dualase, small enough to fit in a single A-A-V vector delivered to the central nervous system, and it is designed to cut the pathogenic repeat expansion down at the D-N-A level while leaving the normal sequence intact. In patient-derived cells, the company says it collapsed the expansion almost completely, with no detectable off-target editing in animal models.

What matters for NuGate is the shape of the competitor, not just its existence. This is one shot, permanent, and limited to one genetic subset. NuGate is repeat-dosed, titratable, and aimed at all forms of A-L-S and F-T-D. That is the same split we drew for Winnow yesterday, and the logic carries across. A one-time edit wins on convenience in the patients it can treat. It also carries risks a repeat-dosed drug avoids: you cannot stop it, you cannot adjust the dose, and an off-target finding in year three cannot be withdrawn. Two things to watch. Investors are funding the C-nine subset at Series A size with only preclinical data, so the C-nine slice of NuGate's market is the one where a single treatment will eventually compete. And their first clinical readout is a biomarker cohort. Whatever they choose to measure becomes the benchmark the field quotes, so NuGate should know what it is before it publishes.

Third item, for Eddie, and it is the third Biossil data point in three days. On Monday, Biossil, the A-I-native company backed by OpenAI and Founders Fund, bought the antibiotic-degrading enzyme portfolio of Theriva Biologics. Theriva is based in Rockville, Maryland. The upfront was three hundred and fifty thousand dollars. The rest is contingent: up to three million dollars in regulatory milestones, up to eighty-eight million in sales milestones, and low single-digit royalties. The lead asset is ribaxamase, an oral enzyme given alongside intravenous antibiotics to break down the excess drug in the gut before it destroys the microbiome. In a Phase Two-b trial in patients receiving antibiotics for lower respiratory infection, it reduced C. difficile infection and preserved the microbiome. Two preclinical follow-ons are included, and Biossil will take over a paused transplant study that had finished two of its three cohorts.

Two days ago we logged Biossil buying a Phase Two C-D-forty antibody for seven hundred and fifty thousand dollars and called it a rebuke, because the shelved-asset screen we first flagged on September fifth is still not running. Today the rebuke gets local. A Maryland company with a Phase Two-b asset in our chronic infections theme sold it to a Toronto buyer for three hundred and fifty thousand dollars of certain money, and it did not cross our desk first. The candid caveat is that ribaxamase sits closer to microbiome protection than to chronic infection as we have scoped that theme. But the screen does not need to pick winners. It needs to make sure a Rockville asset reaches us before it reaches Toronto. Esther, this is a good first test case for the named owner we still have not assigned.

Fourth item, short, on structure. Novartis terminated its collaboration with BioArctic fourteen months after signing it. The deal was thirty million dollars upfront and up to seven hundred and seventy-two million in milestones to pair BioArctic's BrainTransporter, a transferrin-receptor shuttle that carries biologics across the blood-brain barrier, with an undisclosed Novartis antibody. Both sides say the decision has nothing to do with the shuttle. Novartis cites a review of early research priorities, and in September it exercised its option on Sironax's brain-delivery platform for a hundred and twenty-five million dollars. BioArctic keeps the full thirty million and books the rest as revenue this year.

For Eddie, this is the clean seller-side version of the option-on-an-I-N-D-package lesson we took from Bristol Myers and Prothena on November first. When the partner's own antibody gets deprioritized, the platform goes back on the shelf with nothing proven, and the only money that was ever real is the upfront. BioArctic structured that upfront to stand on its own, and it did. For NuGate, it is a reminder that big-pharma interest in getting into the brain runs on platform choices, and those can be dropped in a portfolio review that never looks at the data.

Last item, and it is a single data point for the Parkinson's theme. Teva and its partner MODAG stopped the Phase Two TOPAS trial of emrusolmin in multiple system atrophy after a pre-specified interim futility analysis. Emrusolmin is an oral small molecule designed to inhibit alpha-synuclein aggregation. The trial was sized at about three hundred and fifty patients over forty-eight weeks, with a primary endpoint on part one of the multiple system atrophy rating scale, and no safety signal was seen. Read it narrowly. Multiple system atrophy is the synucleinopathy with the fastest progression and the clearest pathology, which is why aggregation inhibitors go there first. This one could not separate from placebo on a functional scale in under a year. Any Parkinson's program that leans on the aggregation hypothesis now needs to argue why its molecule differs from this one, because a reviewer will ask.

That is the watch for today. The short version: a gene therapy company with two approvals is financing its turn to profit with the vouchers, not the products; a single-shot C-nine editor just got Series A money, and that subset is where NuGate will face a one-time competitor; and a Maryland Phase Two-b asset sold for three hundred and fifty thousand dollars to a buyer that runs the screen we have not built. Talk tomorrow.
