## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Sunday, November twenty-second, and the day's most important development for the portfolio is that a randomized, placebo-controlled lupus cell-therapy trial missed its primary endpoint on the same responder index we have spent two weeks arguing is a commodity, and the way it missed tells you exactly which half of the Winnow pitch is load-bearing.

Start there, because two lupus results landed in the same seventy-two hours and they are arguing with each other.

The first is the MiSLE trial, funded by the Lupus Foundation of America and reported Thursday. Eighty-one patients with active systemic lupus across nine U.S. centers, randomized to a low dose, a high dose, or placebo, each receiving a single infusion of umbilical-cord-derived mesenchymal stromal cells. The primary endpoint was response at week twenty-four on a modified S-R-I four, the responder index that pairs a disease-activity improvement with limits on how much steroid and immunosuppressant you are allowed to be taking. Thirty-eight point nine percent of treated patients responded versus twenty-three point one percent on placebo. That favors the cells. It is not statistically significant.

Now unpack it, because the sub-analyses are where the lesson is. Split by dose, the high-dose arm hit forty-eight point one percent, the low dose twenty-nine point six, placebo twenty-three point one. The high-dose-versus-placebo comparison was close to significance and did not get there. On the unmodified S-R-I four — the version without the steroid and immunosuppressant constraints — the high dose separated from its own within-cohort placebo at weeks twelve, sixteen and twenty, with p-values around point oh three to point oh four. And in the per-protocol analysis, which drops patients with major protocol deviations before week twenty-four including people who took high-dose steroids for reasons unrelated to lupus, week-twenty-four response was sixty-eight point four percent on high dose versus thirty percent on placebo, and that was significant.

Safety was unremarkable for the modality. Serious adverse events in twenty-seven point eight percent of cell recipients versus fourteen point eight percent on placebo over fifty-two weeks, none attributed to the study drug, one death in the placebo arm, no deaths among the fifty-four people who got cells. The investigators say they will run a confirmatory trial of the high dose and will look at repeat dosing.

Jon, Virginia — here is the read, and it sharpens the note we filed on the sixteenth off afimetoran. We said then that S-R-I four has become a commodity endpoint and that an engager cannot win a non-refractory lupus patient on it. This trial says something more specific and more useful. The difference between a miss and a sixty-eight-percent response rate in the same trial was the *steroid rule*, not the biology. Modified S-R-I four penalizes you for background steroid; strip that penalty out or exclude the people who took steroids for something else, and the same cells look like a drug. That cuts both ways for us. It means a competitor can generate an impressive-looking number by choosing the permissive version of the endpoint, and it means our own first clinical claim has to be stated in a form that survives the strict version. If Winnow's argument is depth and time off drug, then the *steroid-free* component of the responder index is not an inconvenience in the endpoint. It is the claim. Write the protocol so the steroid taper is prespecified and the primary analysis is the modified index, and then the result means what we say it means.

The second lupus item is the opposite experiment, and it is why I am pairing them.

Published Wednesday in Cellular and Molecular Immunology: an allogeneic, off-the-shelf natural-killer-cell therapy combined with rituximab, given without any lymphodepleting preconditioning, in refractory mixed class four plus class five lupus nephritis — which is the most treatment-resistant histology there is. The cells are engineered three ways. They express N-K-G-two-D, which recognizes stress ligands on autoimmune B cells. They carry high levels of C-D-sixteen, which is the Fc receptor that drives antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity, so the rituximab becomes the targeting arm. And they carry membrane-bound I-L fifteen with its receptor alpha, which is what keeps them alive in the body. In mice the engineered cells persisted up to ninety days with a thirty-fold higher peak than unmodified N-K cells.

The human experience is two patients. Both refractory, both with massive proteinuria and multiorgan involvement, both having failed intensive immunosuppression. No cytokine release syndrome, no neurotoxicity, rapid B-cell depletion, proteinuria down seventy-three percent and fifty-two percent, and sustained S-R-I four responses on low-dose steroids.

Two patients is two patients. But file the structural point, because it is a competitive one. This is a third group arriving at the same conclusion from a different direction: the thing customers want removed from autoimmune cell therapy is the conditioning regimen. We filed the argument on the nineteenth that in vivo C-A-R-T buys you the manufacturing fix and that the safety premise was bought without evidence. This does something narrower and more credible — it keeps the effector cell allogeneic and off the shelf, skips lymphodepletion entirely, and uses an approved antibody as the targeting module. If that holds in more than two patients, the competitive field in autoimmune depletion is no longer "autologous versus in vivo." It is "who needs to chemo-condition the patient and who does not," and anything that still requires conditioning is carrying a cost the others are not.

Anthony, Jon — the diligence question this creates for us is narrow and answerable. Our selectivity argument says the engager spares the compartments a blunt depleter destroys. The N-K-plus-rituximab approach makes a different claim: the collateral is acceptable because you never ablate the host compartment in the first place. Those are not the same claim and they are not obviously ranked. Get the comparison onto one slide before someone asks it in a partnering meeting.

Third item, and it is for the diagnostics side of the book.

On Wednesday Guardant Health launched Guardant Reveal Ultra in the U.S. clinic. This is tumor-informed minimal residual disease testing for solid tumors — you sequence the patient's tumor, build a personalized panel of that patient's own alterations, then look for them in blood. The claim is detection sensitivity at one part per million or below across major tumor types, validated on thousands of clinical samples, drawing on thousands of genomic targets spanning all major variant classes including structural variants. The detail I would actually underline is the input requirement: it has been validated on tissue with as little as five percent tumor cellularity, against an industry norm around twenty percent. And Guardant's own chairman frames the pitch defensively — one part per million has become the benchmark, he says, and what matters is delivering it reliably across real clinical samples.

Virginia, Eddie — two reads for Artemyx and DELFI, and neither is a threat in the direct sense.

The first is that this is not a competitor to a screening test and you should not let anyone in a meeting treat it as one. Tumor-informed M-R-D requires a tumor. It is a monitoring product for people who already have a cancer diagnosis and a resected specimen. DELFI's FirstLook and anything Artemyx builds operate in populations with no tumor to sequence, which is precisely why the tissue-free approaches work on fragmentomics and methylation instead of patient-specific variants. Different problem, different physics.

The second read is the one that costs us something. The number one part per million is now in the air, and clinicians and investors do not reliably distinguish where it came from. A tissue-free screening assay cannot reach that floor and does not need to — its job is performance against a defined population at a defined specificity, which is the whole point of the November eighth framing on the autoimmune cohort, where a twenty-percent positive predictive value is a triage instrument in the right population and a catastrophe in the wrong one. But if "one part per million" becomes the lay shorthand for a good blood test, then every tissue-free pitch spends its first five minutes explaining why it is not that. Build the two-sentence answer now and put it in the DELFI and Artemyx decks: here is the population, here is the question, here is why patient-specific variants are unavailable in it, and here is the metric that actually governs clinical utility.

The lower-stakes detail worth keeping is the five-percent tumor cellularity validation. That is a sample-eligibility claim, not an analytical one, and it is the sort of thing that expands a test's addressable population without changing its sensitivity story at all. Worth remembering as a template: when the assay is at the physical limit, the next increment of market comes from relaxing what you will accept as an input.

A short look at the week ahead. Kymera reports Phase 2b results for its oral S-T-A-T-six degrader in atopic dermatitis tomorrow. That is not a portfolio line, but it is the clearest public test yet of whether an oral degrader can take a biologic's indication, and an answer either way moves how a partner prices oral-versus-biologic positioning in immunology generally. Second, the F-D-A still has no confirmed commissioner and no nominee, which per Wednesday's note means division-level engagement is what we control and commissioner-level initiatives keep slipping. Nothing new happened there over the weekend, and that is itself the update.

That is the watch. A randomized lupus cell-therapy trial where the difference between failure and a two-fold response rate was the steroid rule rather than the drug, which makes the strict responder index our claim rather than our obstacle; an off-the-shelf engineered N-K cell plus rituximab achieving deep B-cell depletion in refractory lupus nephritis with no conditioning at all, which reframes the autoimmune depletion field around who has to condition the patient; and a tumor-informed M-R-D launch at one part per million that is not a competitor to anything we own but has just reset what a clinician thinks a sensitive blood test sounds like. Today's Sourcing Radar is in your feed alongside this, and its lead is a Hopkins injectable scaffold with a Hopkins Technology Ventures patent application that makes C-A-R-T cells inside the patient without taking any cells out. **ITEM 1 (Winnow) — LEAD. THE DIFFERENCE BETWEEN A MISS AND A 68% RESPONSE RATE WAS THE STEROID RULE, NOT THE DRUG.**
MiSLE Phase 2 (NCT02633163), Lupus Foundation of America–funded; reported 2026-11-19/20. Full text read via Clinical Trials Arena (lupus.org release is behind a JS client challenge).
https://www.clinicaltrialsarena.com/news/lupus-foundations-umbilical-cord-cell-therapy-trial-misses-primary-endpoint/
- **Design:** n=81, nine U.S. centres, randomized to low-dose MSC / high-dose MSC / placebo; **single infusion** of umbilical-cord-derived mesenchymal stromal cells.
- **Primary endpoint MISSED:** modified SRI-4 at week 24 — **38.9% MSC vs 23.1% placebo**, favouring MSC, not significant.
- **By dose:** **high dose 48.1%**, low dose 29.6%, pooled placebo 23.1%; high-dose-vs-placebo p just shy of significance.
- **Unmodified SRI-4** (no steroid/immunosuppressant constraint): high dose separated from within-cohort placebo at **weeks 12, 16, 20, p≈0.03–0.04**.
- **Per-protocol week 24: 68.4% vs 30.0%, significant.** SLEDAI fell in all three arms (−4 both MSC cohorts vs −2 placebo at wk 12).
- **Safety:** SAEs 27.8% MSC (44.4% low dose, 11.1% high dose) vs 14.8% placebo at 52 wks; **none attributed to study drug**; one death, placebo arm, unrelated; **no deaths among 54 MSC recipients**. Confirmatory trial of the high dose planned, including repeat dosing.
- **⇒ READ (Jonathan Robbins + Virginia Burger) — SHARPENS THE 11-16 AFIMETORAN NOTE.** The gap between "missed" and "68%" inside one trial is the **steroid/immunosuppressant constraint in the modified index**, not biology. Consequence both ways: (a) a competitor can manufacture a headline number by choosing the permissive index — check which SRI-4 any cross-trial comparison used; (b) **the steroid-free component is Winnow's claim, not its obstacle.** Prespecify the taper and make the modified index the primary analysis so "time off drug" is evidenced rather than asserted.
- **CANDID:** single infusion, so this is not a durability test; the per-protocol number excludes patients who took high-dose steroids for non-lupus reasons, which is exactly the population the modified index is designed to penalise — do not quote 68.4% without that caveat.
