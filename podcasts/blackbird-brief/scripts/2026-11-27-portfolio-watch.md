## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Friday, November twenty-seventh. The most important development for the portfolio came in overnight. A blood test that reads both cell-free DNA fragment size and methylation is now registered in Singapore as a regulated diagnostic kit. It's the first blood-based multi-cancer screening test on that register, and it's the clearest picture yet of a regulatory route DELFI hasn't taken. The tape was thin over the holiday, so it's three items and a short episode.

Start with the lead, because it lands on DELFI and, a step further out, on Artemyx.

Gene Solutions is a Vietnamese company with a Singapore arm. Its test, SPOT-MAS ten, has been registered by Singapore's Health Sciences Authority as a Class C in vitro diagnostic. Class C is the moderate-to-high-risk tier, and the World Health Organization recognizes that authority as a stringent regulator for this class of device. The company says that, going by the register as of November twenty-fifth, this is the first blood-based multi-cancer screening kit registered there. What's being registered is the product itself: the reagents plus the analysis software. The software reads two things from cell-free DNA, methylation and fragmentomic signatures, and a machine-learning model calls a cancer signal and predicts where in the body it came from. The intended use covers ten cancers: breast, lung, liver, colorectal and gastric, plus five with no standard screening, which are ovarian, pancreatic, esophageal, endometrial and head and neck. It's for asymptomatic adults forty and older at elevated risk, and it's labeled as an add-on to guideline screening, not a replacement. The same test got F-D-A Breakthrough Device designation in May.

The evidence behind it is worth hearing, because it's better than most people assume about tests from this part of the world, and also smaller. The pivotal study was a prospective one called K-DETEK, published in B-M-C Medicine last year. It enrolled about nine thousand asymptomatic adults across seventy-five hospitals in Vietnam and followed them for twelve months. Forty-three people tested positive, and seventeen of them turned out to have cancer, which is a positive predictive value of about forty percent. The negative predictive value was ninety-nine point nine percent and specificity was ninety-nine point seven. Then the two numbers that matter. Sensitivity was about seventy-one percent, but that's computed on roughly two dozen cancers in the whole cohort, so the confidence interval runs from about fifty to eighty-five. And the tissue-of-origin call, the thing that tells the doctor where to look next, was right in nine of the seventeen true positives, about half. The company also says more than a hundred and five thousand people have now been tested in routine care across ten Asian countries.

Three reads.

The first is for Eddie and Emily, and it's about route. Since September our standing question to DELFI has been whether FirstLook Lung goes for a P-M-A, and if not, what the plan is for twenty twenty-eight, when Medicare's multi-cancer screening benefit opens only to F-D-A-approved tests. We've been treating the choice as two options: run a lab-developed test under CLIA, or put up the cost of a U-S premarket approval. Gene Solutions is running a third. Register the test as a kit with a stringent regulator outside the U-S, sell it through partner laboratories across a region, collect real-world volume, and hold an F-D-A Breakthrough designation in parallel. A kit spreads through other people's laboratories, while a lab-developed test only grows as fast as your own lab does. For a company whose only lab is in Baltimore, that difference decides how far it can reach. So add a line to the DELFI conversation: does FirstLook, or a next-generation version, have a kit future anywhere? Is there a stringent-regulator market where registration is cheaper than a P-M-A and the data would count toward one?

The second read is competitive, and it's candid in both directions. This is a fragmentomics-plus-methylation test, the same hybrid the Fred Hutch m-Frag paper argued for two weeks ago, the one with a DELFI co-founder on it. The field keeps landing on the view that fragment patterns are strongest when you combine them with methylation, not when you use them alone. But the K-DETEK numbers also show where every one of these tests is still weak: about two dozen cancers to estimate sensitivity, and a tissue-of-origin call that's right about half the time. When DELFI's position gets compared with a competitor that has a registration, the honest answer is that a registered test isn't the same thing as a proven one. Registration is a regulatory fact. Sensitivity estimated on twenty-four cancers is still an open question.

The third read is for Jonathan and the Artemyx team. Artemyx's whole thesis is that cell-free DNA can say which tissue is being injured. Here, in cancer, where the signal is strongest and the training sets are largest, a commercial test with a hundred thousand samples behind it places the origin correctly about half the time. That isn't a reason for doubt. It's the bar. When Artemyx pitches a tissue-of-origin call in chronic disease, a sponsor will ask how often it's right on the first read, and the answer needs to be a number set against that fifty percent.

The second item is a regulatory delay, and it touches Winnow and the autoimmune theme.

On Wednesday evening Vertex said the F-D-A has pushed back its decision on povetacicept in I-G-A nephropathy by up to three months, from November thirtieth to no later than February twenty-eighth. Povetacicept is a dual inhibitor of BAFF and APRIL, the two cytokines that keep B cells and plasma cells alive. Vertex's account is that the agency sent an information request near the end of the review, Vertex answered promptly, and the F-D-A classified the answer as a major amendment, which starts a fresh clock. Vertex says it's confident in the data. No other detail was given.

Two reads. For Jonathan and Virginia, on the landscape: the dual BAFF and APRIL mechanism is no longer novel in I-G-A nephropathy. Vera's atacicept got accelerated approval in July as the first drug of the class there, with a forty-six percent cut in proteinuria, and Novartis and Otsuka are in the same fight. Povetacicept will be second in class, not first. Blocking B-cell survival signals is turning into a crowded, competitive category in autoimmune kidney disease. That's the backdrop for Winnow's pitch. A selective approach that removes the disease-driving cells has to show it goes deeper or lasts longer than chronic dosing with a dual cytokine blocker, now that patients can get one off the shelf.

For Hemaka and Yixuan, on process: this is Vertex, a top-tier regulatory team on a priority file, and a late question still cost it a quarter. We've flagged that we should plan for an acting commissioner through most of next year. The part we control is division-level engagement, and the cheapest protection is to answer the predictable questions before they're asked. Every I-N-D and pre-I-N-D package in the portfolio should be read once by someone whose only job is to guess the late-cycle question.

The third item is short, and it's an operations lesson for every program heading toward the clinic.

Pharming has paused production of Ruconest, its acute treatment for hereditary angioedema, and told the F-D-A a supply interruption is likely. The cause isn't the drug substance. A media fill failed at its third-party fill-finish manufacturer. A media fill is the routine test where you run the sterile filling line with growth medium instead of drug, to check that nothing gets contaminated. Production won't restart until the root cause is found and the process is requalified, and the company says current supply lasts through mid-December. The stock dropped on the news.

The read is for Esther, Avi, Yixuan and Emily, and it builds on Monday's point about the C-G-T investor bar moving to manufacturing. One commercial product, one outside filling site, one failed sterility simulation, and a company is telling patients it may run out within three weeks. For Aletira's A-A-V program, and for any biologic in the portfolio, the question to put in the C-M-C plan now is simple: who is the second fill-finish site, and what would qualifying it cost before we need it rather than after? It's a small line in a Series A budget, and an investor who has watched this week's news will ask for it.

That's the watch for today. To sum up: a fragmentomics-plus-methylation multi-cancer test now sits on a stringent regulator's register as a kit, which gives DELFI a third route to weigh and gives Artemyx a tissue-of-origin bar of about one in two; Vertex's three-month slip is a reminder that late-cycle questions cost even the best regulatory teams, in a B-cell cytokine category that's already crowded; and a single failed fill-finish run is enough to interrupt a marketed drug. Have a good long weekend, everyone. We'll be back tomorrow.
