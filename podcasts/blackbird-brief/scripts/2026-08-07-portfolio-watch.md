Good morning, Blackbird Team. This is your Portfolio Watch for Friday, August seventh.

The direct-catalyst tape on our own companies is quiet for a twelfth straight day — no readout, financing, deal, or filing landed squarely on a Blackbird program this week. The two threads that did move earlier in the week are settled, and I won't re-litigate them: CAMP4's fifty-million-dollar second close is done, and the AstraZeneca–Bristol Myers merger rumor was knocked down Wednesday with no follow-on since. So rather than manufacture a catalyst, I want to spend today on a genuinely fresh landscape story that lands on the flagship — Aletira — because the gene-therapy safety picture that's been validating our precision thesis all year just got materially darker this week, and it deserves a candid read, including where it does and doesn't actually help us.

Here's what surfaced. Two more pediatric gene-editing deaths in China came into public view over the last few days. The first, disclosed Wednesday by a Shanghai company called Huida-Gene, was a boy in a Duchenne muscular dystrophy trial who died last August after a high dose of a CRISPR-based treatment — the company attributes it to acute respiratory distress that developed during a severe immune reaction. Three other boys dosed in the same study are reportedly stable. The second case is uglier on the ethics: a six-year-old girl who died in March of last year, seven days after an investigator-led team infused trillions of viral particles into her spinal fluid to base-edit a gene in her brain. The cause of death was a severe immune reaction with a small-vessel clotting complication, and the hospital's own ethics board judged it definitely related to the treatment. The reason it's news now is that the death was kept quiet and left out of a subsequent journal paper — there are now calls for retraction and questions about whether the parents ever understood the risk.

Now the so-what for Aletira, and I want to be disciplined about this, because it cuts in a way that matters for how we pitch. The easy read is "more AAV safety carnage, more validation for a platform that controls where a transgene turns on." And directionally that's right — the whole field is being forced toward precision, and that's the tailwind under SELEXON's Series-A story. But look at what actually killed these children. Both deaths were immune-mediated, driven by dumping an enormous dose of viral particles broadly into the body or the central nervous system. That is the capsid-dose and biodistribution axis — how much virus you inject and where it goes. And SELEXON does not govern that axis. SELEXON governs expression selectivity — which cell type reads out the transgene once it's inside — not the dose of capsid or where the capsid lands. So we cannot walk into a diligence meeting and say SELEXON would have prevented this, because it wouldn't have. If our team leans on these deaths as direct validation, a sharp investor will take that exact seam apart.

What is legitimately ours to claim is more subtle, and I think more durable. To the extent expression selectivity lets you avoid transgene readout in the wrong tissues — some of which feeds the immune response — and to the extent it lets you reach a therapeutic effect at a lower vector dose, because you're not wasting expression in cells that shouldn't have it, then yes, it's part of the precision toolkit the field now demands. That's an argument about therapeutic index, not a headline about preventing capsid toxicity. Lead with the axis we actually own.

And there's a second angle here that's genuinely new and, for Aletira, more pointed. The outrage in the girl's case is centered on running a risky gene therapy in a non-fatal condition — she had a neurodevelopmental disorder with a normal life expectancy. Aletira's lead indication, hereditary hearing loss, is also non-fatal. The clear direction of travel is that the risk-benefit bar for gene therapy in non-lethal, pediatric indications is going up — regulators, ethics boards, and parents are all going to demand a much cleaner therapeutic-index story before they accept vector risk in a child who isn't dying. That's precisely the argument for a lower-dose, better-targeted construct — but it also means the safety-and-dose bar Aletira's own hearing-loss program has to clear is rising. So the action for Hemaka and Eddie ahead of Geoff Lynn's raise is a two-parter: sharpen the precision pitch around the axis SELEXON actually controls and the therapeutic-index story, and pressure-test the hearing-loss indication strategy against a world where non-fatal gene therapy gets held to a higher safety standard than it did even a year ago.

One related datapoint for how we underwrite platform genetic-medicine plays in general. Aurora Therapeutics — the personalized gene-editing startup Jennifer Doudna co-founded to scale bespoke treatments like the widely-covered Baby-K-J case — killed its lead program and cut staff on Tuesday, about seven months after launch, citing competition, intellectual-property hurdles, and manufacturing problems. Not a portfolio hit, but a useful reminder as we look at genetic-medicine platforms: the science clearing a heroic one-patient bar is not the same thing as a business that can manufacture and commercialize at scale, and the failure modes there are increasingly economic and manufacturing-driven, not just biological.
