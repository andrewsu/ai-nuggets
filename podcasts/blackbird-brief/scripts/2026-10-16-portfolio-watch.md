## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Friday, October sixteenth. The most important development for the portfolio is that the F-D-A has opened a formal rewrite of how it reviews rare disease drugs, and the open comment window is exactly when Aletira and NuGate should be asking for what they need.

On Wednesday the agency launched what it calls the Rare Disease Framework Initiative, with a ten-page white paper titled "A New Framework for Rare Disease Review." The core promise is one sentence long. Advice and decisions on rare disease programs will be tailored to the evidence that can reasonably be generated, given the disease, how it starts and progresses, the patient population and the product, and that tailoring will be communicated clearly throughout development. Fit-for-purpose evidence, in the white paper's words, becomes the norm for every rare disease application rather than an exception a sponsor has to argue for.

Three details matter more than the headline. First, the scope runs from pre-I-N-D engagement through post-approval obligations, and it names chemistry, manufacturing and controls review and pre-approval inspections specifically as places for risk-based approaches. Second, the agency says it may revise regulations, not just guidance, to write fit-for-purpose evidence standards into the legal foundation. Third, it is cross-center, covering both the drugs center and the biologics center, with the stated goal of consistency across review divisions. There's a public meeting on October twenty-eighth with the Reagan-Udall Foundation, a Federal Register request for information to follow, and roundtables by sector. No date has been set for an actual framework.

Here's why the consistency point matters more than the flexibility point. The flexibility already exists on paper. The problem sponsors have had is that it gets applied unevenly. BioSpace reminds us that the agency reversed itself on uniQure's Huntington's gene therapy last November, after earlier advice had accepted a natural-history external control, and that an externally controlled study was a major issue in Capricor's rejection. The lawyers at Hyman, Phelps put it well. Today, flexibility comes from guidance, which isn't binding and can change, and it leaves reviewers feeling they are being asked to color outside the lines. Leerink's Joseph Schwartz said the F-D-A isn't lowering the bar for rare disease, but it may be making the path to approval less of a guessing game. The Alliance for Regenerative Medicine said the real test for cell and gene therapy will be whatever guidance and rules come out on single-arm trials and natural-history data.

Here's what it means for us. For Aletira, the guessing game is the risk. A cochlear gene therapy in a small monogenic population is never going to run a big placebo-controlled trial, and the natural design uses the untreated ear as a within-patient control. As of yesterday, nothing in writing says a review division has to accept that. This initiative is the place to make it stick. Last month we noted that Ultragenyx asked the agency to name an internal baseline control in the substantial-evidence guidance. Anthony and Jon, I'd file comments on the record that make the same ask, with the untreated ear as the worked example. For NuGate, the line to watch is the external-control question, because a genetically defined A-L-S or F-T-D population is the textbook case for natural-history comparison. And Yixuan, the sentence on chemistry, manufacturing and controls is not filler. For an adeno-associated virus program, manufacturing review and inspection timing is where a small company quietly loses a year. One candid caveat: this is a plan to build a plan, run by an agency that has changed leadership and direction more than once in eighteen months. Comment as if it will become durable, but don't put it in a financing model until something is actually written into regulation.

Second item, and it's a good companion to the first. Opus Genetics presented new data from two retinal gene therapies at Retina in Rio. We first used Opus last month as an endpoint template, and this update makes the template sharper. In the Leber congenital amaurosis type five program, three adults have kept their gains in visual acuity through twenty-four months, and three adolescents showed restored cone-mediated vision, with sensitivity reaching the normal range by six months. Light sensitivity improved compared with each patient's untreated eye. Opus went into Phase three at the low dose, one times ten to the tenth vector genomes per eye, without escalating the dose any further. The Phase three primary endpoint is retinal sensitivity at sixteen central test locations, and the natural-history and treatment cohorts are fully enrolled, with dosing to start this quarter. In the bestrophin program, three of the four evaluable low-dose patients improved, and the high-dose cohort of eleven is fully enrolled.

The part I'd want Avi to read closely is the natural-history finding. Opus showed that retinal sensitivity declines mostly in the transitional zone around damaged tissue, while measurements averaged over the whole retina look relatively stable. The company is now redesigning its assessments to measure the regions most likely to respond. That's the most useful sentence in the release for Aletira. Average across the whole organ and a real effect disappears into a stable-looking mean. In the cochlea, the equivalent is choosing which frequencies and which thresholds to measure before dosing, based on where untreated ears actually decline, rather than reporting an average hearing threshold. Two more things are worth noting. The untreated eye is doing the job of the control arm here, which is the same structure we want for the ear. And a sponsor taking its starting dose straight into a pivotal trial is a reminder that in a privileged compartment, the efficacious dose can be very low, which supports the payload-budget argument for SELEXON. These are tiny numbers, six patients in one program and four evaluable in the other, so read it as a design lesson, not as efficacy validation.

Third item is a financing note for Emily and Eddie, and it adds to what we said yesterday. City Therapeutics, a clinical-stage R-N-A interference company, priced an upsized I-P-O last night: about eleven point one million shares at eighteen dollars, the midpoint of its seventeen-to-nineteen-dollar range, for about two hundred million dollars in gross proceeds, up from the roughly one hundred seventy-five million dollars it originally targeted. It starts trading today. City's lead programs are an R-N-A-i drug against factor eleven for thrombosis, one against R-B-P-four for Stargardt disease, and one for anemia of chronic disease. It sells itself on three things: engineered triggers, tissue-selective delivery, and genetics-driven choice of targets.

Yesterday we said the window is open for clinical data in a hot target class and not for platforms. City complicates that a little, and it's worth being precise about how. City is an oligonucleotide platform, but it went public with clinical assets on well-validated targets, in a modality the market already knows how to value. Iambic came the day before at a price below its last private round. City came at the midpoint and upsized. What separates them isn't platform versus asset. It's whether the platform claim is backed by a clinical program on a target investors already believe. Add OrbiMed closing about three billion dollars in new life sciences funds, including more than a billion for Asia, and the late-stage money is clearly there. For NuGate, the practical takeaway is that an oligonucleotide company can still get public money, as long as its delivery story is attached to a validated target in a human. That's another reason the clinical proof point has to come before the platform pitch.

Last item, short, and it's a Maryland one for the oncology surface targets theme. MacroGenics, in Rockville, dosed the first patient in a Phase one study of M-G-C zero-thirty, an antibody-drug conjugate against a target it hasn't disclosed. The company says no approved therapy hits that target. The trial enrolls urothelial, colorectal, gastric, esophageal and pancreatic cancers, with up to fifty-four patients in dose escalation. It's MacroGenics' third A-D-C in the clinic, and the linker-payload is licensed in. It's Synaffix's exatecan-based topoisomerase-one payload, at four drugs per antibody. The read for us is that the conjugation chemistry is now something you rent, so a Maryland antibody company's edge is the target and the antibody, not the payload. That's the right lens for any surface-target program we fund. The question to ask at diligence is whether the target is genuinely new and whether the antibody is selective, because everything after that can be licensed off the shelf. It's also a Maryland company to keep on Eddie's map once the target is disclosed.

That's the watch. The F-D-A's rare disease public meeting is October twenty-eighth, and Kyverna's full stiff person syndrome dataset lands in Toronto next Wednesday, which is the next real data point for Winnow. Have a good weekend.
