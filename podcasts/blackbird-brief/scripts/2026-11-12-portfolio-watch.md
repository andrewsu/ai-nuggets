## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Thursday, November twelfth. The most important development for the portfolio landed in Nature yesterday, and it is a direct answer to a position we took on this show in September: a London group has published evidence that correcting a single cryptic splicing event in U-N-C thirteen-A restores synaptic function in human motor neurons — the exact compartment where a Johns Hopkins mouse study said the same correction does nothing at all. Two labs, two answers, and the difference between them is the most useful thing in the paper.

The group is Pietro Fratta's at University College London, with King's College London, the Crick, and the Leibniz institute in Berlin. The biology is familiar to anyone who has sat through a NuGate deck. In roughly ninety-seven percent of A-L-S patients the protein T-D-P forty-three leaves the nucleus, and when it does it stops repressing cryptic exons — junk stretches of RNA spliced into hundreds of transcripts, ruining them. U-N-C thirteen-A is one of those, and not a random one: it carries the second-strongest genetic risk signal in A-L-S, and patients carrying two copies of the risk variant lose five to twelve months of median survival.

What the paper shows is this. They built human stem-cell-derived neurons where T-D-P forty-three can be degraded on command in mature cells rather than knocked out from the start. Lose it and the neurons lose almost all their U-N-C thirteen-A protein — under five percent remains after ten days — and synaptic transmission collapses along with the network's ability to fire in a coordinated way. Delete just the one cryptic exon, out of hundreds of mis-splicing events, and about eighty percent of synaptic function comes back. Then they built antisense oligonucleotides to do the same thing pharmacologically, and those restored splicing to over ninety percent and protein to between thirty and fifty percent of normal.

Thirty percent is the number to hold onto. Prior work says deficits appear only once U-N-C thirteen-A falls below roughly thirty percent of normal, and people carrying one broken copy are clinically fine. So the therapeutic bar is not full restoration — it is clearing thirty percent, and this paper says their oligos do.

Now the part worth the briefing. On September twentieth this show led a Sourcing Radar on two Philip Wong preprints out of Hopkins. Those deleted the same cryptic exon in mice and found that in the forebrain it rescued behavior but saved no neurons, and in spinal motor neurons it did nothing measurable at all. Our read then was blunt: the asset Lilly paid forty-five million dollars up front for, QurAlis's Q-R-L two-oh-four, looks inert in the spinal compartment and symptomatic-only in the cortex. That read is now contested, and the contest is informative rather than embarrassing.

Three differences explain the two results, and all three favor taking the new paper seriously. First, species. The U-N-C thirteen-A cryptic exon is a human-specific event — intron twenty in people, intron one in mice, and they are not the same element. Fratta's group answered that by building a humanized mouse carrying the human intron and showing the oligo corrects splicing in its spinal cord. Wong's mice were deleting the mouse version in a gene the mouse cord barely expresses. Second, the endpoint. Wong counted surviving neurons; Fratta measured whether neurons talk to each other. Both can be true at once, and the paper says so: it describes a pre-degenerative state where neurons are structurally intact and viable but have gone functionally silent. Third, the system — human cortical and spinal neurons versus a mouse knockout.

So the synthesis for Hemaka and Jon is not that one lab is wrong. It is that U-N-C thirteen-A splice correction is probably a functional-restoration mechanism rather than a neuroprotective one, and that is a statement about which endpoint a trial is built on, not about whether the target works. If what you are selling is recovery of signaling in neurons still alive, your primary endpoint is function and your enrollment criterion is early disease, because the mechanism has nothing to offer a neuron already gone. The standing internal test — does NuGate's cryptic target sit above or below the sufficiency bar — now needs a second column. Not only does correcting it preserve neurons, but does it restore function in the neurons that remain, and can we measure that.

There is a commercial half, and it corrects a line in our own reference file. Trace Neuroscience, the South San Francisco company founded on this exact work, is on U-N-C thirteen-A, not on S-T-M-N two — our file had that wrong and it is fixed today. Fratta is a founder and shareholder; he and two co-authors hold a granted U-S patent and a pending European application on using oligos to correct this splicing event. And Trace is already dosing. In June it started a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled Phase One-Two of about thirty participants in North America and Europe, plus an investigator-initiated study of about twenty-five in China. Eligibility is worth reading closely — eighteen to seventy-five, symptom onset within two years, slow vital capacity at least sixty percent, S-O-D-one and F-U-S patients excluded. That is an early, high-function population, which is precisely what you enroll if you believe the mechanism rescues function rather than prevents death.

The candid note for Eddie: the mechanistic paper arrived five months after the trial started, and the underlying preprint has been public since June of twenty twenty-four. The Nature stamp is new; the biology is not. What is new for us is that two serious groups have published apparently opposite answers on the same target, a company is in the clinic with a defined patient population, and Lilly owns the other oligo.

Second item, the other half of the same week, and it is money rather than biology. VectorY Therapeutics, out of Amsterdam and Boston, raised seventy-four and a half million euros — about eighty-three and a half million dollars. New investors include Angelini Ventures, Invest-N-L and the European Investment Bank; returning investors include Forbion, E-Q-T Life Sciences, Insight Partners, the Merck research arm, and Eli Lilly. The lead program is a vectorized antibody against pathological T-D-P forty-three — an A-A-V that makes the neuron manufacture the antibody itself, dosed once, rather than an oligo redosed intrathecally forever. The money funds the final readout of a Phase One-Two they expanded in September from twelve participants to thirty-six.

The read for Avi and Hemaka is about the modality field around NuGate. In one week the same underlying pathology attracted a Nature paper backing splice-switching oligos, an active global oligo trial, and eighty-three million dollars for a one-shot vectorized antibody. Three modalities, one biology, and Lilly is in two of them. That is a validated target space and a crowded one, so the differentiation question is not whether the biology is real but which modality owns which part of the patient journey. Note also what VectorY put in its secondary endpoints: neurofilament light trajectories, novel T-D-P forty-three pathway biomarkers, the functional rating scale, slow vital capacity, hand-held dynamometry, and survival. That is the package we have been building toward, and it is now a public comparator.

Third item, and it reopens something we thought we had closed. On Wednesday Addex Therapeutics announced that its spinout Neurosterix closed a one-hundred-five-million-dollar Series B, mostly to fund a Phase Two of N-T-X two-fifty-three, a selective M-four muscarinic positive allosteric modulator for schizophrenia. Addex did not participate and now holds eight and a half percent, valued at eighteen point six million dollars. Neurosterix was spun out in twenty twenty-four on a sixty-five-million-dollar round led by Perceptive Advisors and Acorn Bioventures; its chief executive is a Perceptive managing director. The new syndicate was not named. Endpoints reports the company has brought in people who worked on Cobenfy.

This matters because of what we said here on Sunday. The lead then was that three years after AbbVie paid eight point seven billion dollars for Cerevel and Bristol Myers fourteen billion for Karuna, neither muscarinic bet has produced a winner — emraclidine, an M-four P-A-M, failed two Phase Twos, and Cobenfy disappointed on real-world tolerability rather than efficacy. A reasonable inference was that the next cycle would price neuropsychiatric assets more conservatively. Three days later, a hundred and five million dollars went into a different M-four P-A-M, from a syndicate reading the same tape.

Two reads. For Matt and Maisha, the Sunday conclusion survives but needs narrowing: investors repudiated the molecules, not the receptor class, and capital is available at Phase Two for a differentiated allosteric modulator. Mildly good news for the funding environment around GPR fifty-two. The harder read is for Eddie. Neurosterix's own pitch is reducing psychosis symptoms while avoiding the movement disorders and metabolic complications of dopamine antagonists — which is, word for word, our differentiation axis. When a better-tolerated M-four program has a hundred and five million dollars and people who have launched in this indication, "better tolerated than the dopamine antagonists" stops being a position and becomes the entry fee. What distinguishes us has to be the receptor and the negative-symptom claim.

Last item, for Aletira, and it is good news about the size of the market rather than about anyone's data. A team co-led by Professor Choi Byung-yoon at Seoul National University Bundang Hospital — the same clinician whose cautions we covered yesterday — asked whether children who already have cochlear implants are still candidates for gene therapy. The worry has been that inserting an electrode damages the surviving cells a gene therapy would need, which is why essentially every O-T-O-F trial enrolls only children who have not been implanted.

They looked at eleven children with O-T-O-F-related hearing loss who had received implants, testing outer hair cell function two to eleven years after surgery. Among five children implanted in their first twelve months and tested less than six years later, they found preserved function in seven of ten ears. In the twelve ears of the six children who did not meet both criteria, none. Choi's framing is that implants and gene therapy may eventually be used in sequence rather than as alternatives, and that if gene therapy is a later possibility, surgeons should preserve as much cochlear structure as they can.

Be careful with this one. Eleven children, outer hair cells only — which are not the cells an O-T-O-F therapy aims at — and the authors say plainly they did not establish that gene therapy would work after implantation. But the read for Geoff, Hugh and Avi is real. Yesterday's item was that the implant is the comparator and the adoption bar is higher than the regulatory bar. Today's is the complement: the implanted population may not be permanently lost, which would meaningfully enlarge the addressable population for any inner-ear program including ours. Two things follow. In the market model, the already-implanted cohort should carry a probability rather than a zero. And if sequencing becomes clinical norm, hearing-preserving implantation technique becomes a gating variable we do not control but should track — worth asking whether our own eligibility criteria, when we write them, exclude implanted patients by default or by evidence.

That is the watch. A Nature paper says correcting one cryptic exon restores synaptic function in human motor neurons where a Hopkins mouse study found nothing, and the reconciliation — human-specific splicing, and function measured instead of survival — reframes this target class as functional restoration rather than neuroprotection, which is an endpoint decision before it is a biology decision. Eighty-three and a half million dollars went into a one-shot vectorized antibody against the same protein, with Lilly in both camps. A hundred and five million went into another M-four muscarinic program three days after we concluded the category had been repriced, which says the market blamed the molecule and not the mechanism. And eleven implanted children in Seoul suggest the cochlear implant may be a stage rather than a door closing. Back tomorrow.
