# Blackbird Brief — Portfolio Watch, 2026-09-03

## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Thursday, September third. The most important development for the portfolio is a failure that lands directly on NuGate: Ultragenyx's antisense drug for Angelman syndrome missed everything in Phase three, and it missed after one of the most believed open-label packages anyone has assembled in a neurodevelopmental disease.

The drug is apazunersen, formerly G-T-X one-oh-two. In Angelman syndrome the maternal copy of the U-B-E three A gene is missing or broken and the paternal copy is present but silenced by an antisense transcript; this intrathecal oligonucleotide binds that transcript, lifts the silencing, and the intact paternal gene starts making protein. Breakthrough designation from the F-D-A, PRIME from the E-M-A, and a long-term extension that the company and the patient community both read as real developmental gains.

The Phase three, called Aspire, enrolled roughly a hundred and twenty-nine children aged four to seventeen with a confirmed full maternal deletion. Primary endpoint was change from baseline in the Bayley four cognitive raw score; key secondary was net response on a Multidomain Responder Index built from cognition, receptive communication, behavior, gross motor function and sleep. It missed both. And the sentence that matters most is about the components — no differences between treated and control on the composite, and none on the mean change in any of the five individual domains either. Safety was consistent with Phase one-two, so nothing went wrong with the chemistry. Ultragenyx says it will decide what to do with the program and, in the same breath, implement significant expense reductions.

Anthony, Hemaka — three things. First, this is not a near miss and not an underpowered trial. Five independent domains, zero separation on all five, is the signature of an effect that was never there. Second, what failed is not the oligonucleotide and probably not target engagement. What failed is the inference from an open-label developmental package to a controlled one — children with a neurodevelopmental disorder improve on developmental scales whether or not you dose them, and a control arm subtracts out maturation, expectation and rater drift. Everyone knows that in the abstract. Ultragenyx just paid for the demonstration.

Third, and this one has money attached: Ionis has the same target and the same mechanism in an ongoing pivotal. Obudanersen, formerly I-O-N five-eight-two, is in the Phase three REVEAL study — about two hundred patients, two-to-one against placebo, fifty-two weeks, still recruiting as of last week, and it also carries Breakthrough designation earned on its own open-label Phase one-two. That de-risking precedent evaporated yesterday. Whether the failure is the mechanism or the endpoints now gets answered by a competitor, on Ionis's money, and we should watch for it.

For NuGate the discipline is this. A-L-S and frontotemporal dementia give us something Angelman does not — a fluid biomarker. Tofersen missed its primary functional endpoint and reached the market on neurofilament. Build neurofilament as the evidentiary spine of the program, not as a supporting secondary. And note the epilogue: Ultragenyx is a commercial-stage company with approved products and it is cutting expenses hard the morning after. For a single-asset spinout, one readout like this is the company.

Second item, and it is the third datapoint in four days on the same lane. Typewriter Therapeutics came out of stealth yesterday with a fifty-six million dollar Series A led by A-N Venture Partners and R-A Capital. The platform is target-primed reverse transcription, built on the R two retrotransposon out of Haruhiko Fujiwara's work at the University of Tokyo. The therapy is two pieces of R-N-A — one messenger R-N-A encoding the R two protein, a second carrying the therapeutic gene. They assemble inside the cell, insert the gene at a defined site, and the machinery then degrades along with the messenger R-N-A. Lipid nanoparticle delivery, no viral vector, redosable. Mikael Dolsten, Pfizer's former chief scientific officer, is on the board and Stanley Riddell is on the scientific advisory board. First indications are in vivo CAR T and genetic liver disease; first monkey studies late this year.

Line them up. Monday, ArsenalBio cut ninety-nine people to make in vivo CAR T its entire strategy. Tuesday, Novartis and Bristol Myers had their autoimmune trials of the ex vivo version halted. Yesterday, fifty-six million dollars into a non-viral, redosable insertion platform that has not yet been in a primate.

Virginia, Avi — that is a direct comparable for the Mao lab anionic lipid-nanoparticle work at the Hopkins nanobiotechnology institute, and it prices the round. Note carefully what they sold. Not a target. Not even a cargo. They sold insertion-site control and redosability — properties, not biology, which is the same shape as the frame we drew off Generate's half-life data last week, and it keeps being the shape that gets funded. Two things to hold against it: the R two family of retrotransposons natively lands in the ribosomal R-N-A gene repeats, and whether that is where you want a payload to sit in a human T cell is the first diligence question, not a detail. And permanently inserting a receptor gene into a T cell inside a living patient carries an integration and clonal-expansion question a transient messenger-R-N-A CAR does not. Jon — none of this changes the Winnow read. An engager remains the only version of this biology that is a dosed drug.

Third item bundles two stories from the same day that are really one story. Two previously undisclosed deaths in Chinese investigator-initiated gene therapy trials became public. The first is a six-year-old girl treated in March of last year for Snijders-Blok-Campeau syndrome, caused by a mutation in the C-H-D three gene. The base editor was too large for one vector, so its instructions were split across two A-A-V nine vectors and injected into her spinal fluid — trillions of viral particles. She died seven days later of thrombotic microangiopathy, which the hospital's own ethics board judged definitely related. Here is the part that should stop you: all four monkeys in the preceding toxicology study had developed moderate-to-severe liver damage, one of them kidney injury too, and the ethics committee approved the trial before it had reviewed the final toxicology report. The death was not disclosed when the related preclinical work published in Nature in February. The second case is a boy in a HuidaGene trial of C-R-I-S-P-R for Duchenne who died of acute respiratory distress syndrome; the company did not confirm it for about a year. China's response is Order eight-one-eight, in force since May, which now requires large-animal models, G-M-P material, five-business-day reporting of serious adverse events, and automatic suspension when one occurs.

The same week, four senior F-D-A officials — from the drug center, the biologics center, the device center and the oncology center — published an editorial saying inspection access is not, in their words, an administrative footnote, and that a sponsor building a regulatory strategy around data from sites the agency cannot reach should think again.

Geoff, this is the Aletira item, and I want the uncomfortable version on the table before an investor puts it there. The consensus reading of that first death is that the vector dose killed her, not the edit — complement activation and thrombotic microangiopathy at very high capsid load, the same toxicity behind the run of A-A-V clinical holds we have been logging all month. Cell-type-selective expression does not fix that. SELEXON restricts where the transgene is expressed; it does not reduce the number of capsids you inject. So if the Series A narrative leans on safer gene therapy, the question back is which safety, and the honest answer today is transgene safety, not capsid safety. There is a version of the claim that does reach dose — if restricting expression lets you get a therapeutic effect at lower total vector, that is a dose argument and a strong one. But it has to be shown as a dose-response in the target tissue, not asserted from the mechanism. Get that experiment into the data room. And do not let this file itself as a China story: the toxicology package was not missing, it was ignored.

Eddie — for the business development comparator we have been building for two weeks, forty-five million dollars to DualityBio and seventy-five million to Simcere, the F-D-A just told sponsors that the inspectability of the sites behind ex-U-S data is a material factor in their regulatory strategy. That does not reverse the comparator. It attaches a discount that did not exist a week ago, and it is the first thing in a while that argues for the value of a package run under F-D-A inspection.

Fourth, and the design is worth more than the drug. Teva reported positive topline Phase two-A data for its anti-interleukin fifteen antibody in celiac disease. Fifty adults on a gluten-free diet with minimal intestinal damage at baseline, a single dose, then two weeks later a six-week daily gluten challenge. At week eight the drug hit its primary endpoint with a significant and clinically meaningful reduction in gluten-induced intestinal damage versus placebo, plus lower gastrointestinal symptom scores. Well tolerated, Fast Track already in hand, peak sales estimates of one and a half to two billion dollars.

Fifty patients, one dose, a histology readout in eight weeks, in a chronic autoimmune disease. They bought that by provoking the disease on a schedule instead of waiting for it to flare. Jon, Anthony — that is a question to ask of every autoimmune and inflammation program on our pre-spinout list: is there a provocation that turns a chronic endpoint into an eight-week one? For the T-cell-mediated diseases Winnow is aimed at, a challenge model is not obvious and may not exist. But it is worth an hour before the next target product profile revision, because a company that can run a fifty-patient proof of mechanism is structurally cheaper to finance, and that shows up in the term sheet. Note the contrast inside one week, too: a cytokine antibody reads out in fifty patients with no safety signal and a two-billion-dollar forecast, while a living cell product depleting B cells has now killed three people. That is not proof that cytokine blockade is better science. It is evidence that the risk-adjusted center of gravity in autoimmunity is moving back toward dosed, controllable, interruptible drugs, which is the Winnow thesis.

Fifth item is for the sourcing side. Novartis reported Phase three results yesterday for remibrutinib, marketed as Rhapsido, in relapsing multiple sclerosis. It is a B-T-K inhibitor, already approved last September in chronic spontaneous urticaria. In the REMODEL one and two studies it beat teriflunomide on annualized relapse rate, cut inflammatory brain lesions harder, and showed benefit on confirmed disability progression at three and six months — nominal significance, with the actual numbers not yet released. No liver signal, which matters in a class where Roche's fenebrutinib sat on clinical hold in late twenty twenty-three over liver injury. Novartis files next year; Roche files this year.

Esther, Hemaka — this is the competitive context for the Kornberg L-X-R remyelination work, our August sixteenth Radar lead. Two B-T-K inhibitors now show a disability-progression signal, and every one of them acts on inflammation inside the central nervous system. Not one repairs myelin. That cuts both ways. The good half: progression is demonstrably a movable endpoint, and two large pharma now have a commercial reason to want something that stacks on what they are about to launch. The hard half: the control arm for any remyelination asset from here is a patient already on a B-T-K inhibitor, so the trial is an add-on trial and the effect has to be visible on top of a drug that is already slowing the disease.

So the question to take back to Kornberg and Calabresi is no longer whether L-X-R activation restores oligodendrocyte precursor maturation under interferon gamma — their paper answered that in mouse and human cells. It is what the add-on readout is. Calabresi runs M-S trials for a living; he will know whether that is a myelin imaging endpoint, a visual pathway measure, or something else, and his answer determines whether this is a financeable program or a very good paper. Thirty-minute conversation.

Last item is short and it is infrastructure. The federal health research agency Arpa-H put up to a hundred and twenty-five million dollars across five teams into systems that can make a personalized R-N-A medicine in under a week; the largest award, up to fifty-four and a half million, goes to a Waterfall Scientific consortium that includes Ginkgo Bioworks. Ginkgo is also the company deploying eleven robotic units into the seventeen point three million dollar N-S-F cloud lab at the Institute for Bioscience and Biotechnology Research in Rockville that we logged on Sunday — the facility U-M-B co-owns. Same vendor, two federal programs, one of them inside a partner institution. Maisha, that is a concrete line for the Baltimore-nexus deck, and Yixuan, it is a better reason to make the UM Ventures instrument-access call than the one we had four days ago. The strategic read: federal non-dilutive money is currently flowing to manufacturing, not discovery, so a program whose hard problem is a making problem has a funding channel a target-discovery program does not.

On to what did not happen. Zero G-P-R fifty-two items again — nothing from Nxera, no partner announcement, and the out-licensing window the company guided to for this year has under four months left in it. Nothing from Skylark, Sensorion or Lilly's Akouos in hearing-loss gene therapy since the first SONIX dosing on August eleventh. Nothing directly on Clasp, Winnow, Artemyx, Biolinco or eleven-oh-four health, and BioBuzz has published nothing newer than August twenty-eighth, so no net-new Baltimore item today. Barinthus is where Geoff Lynn was chief scientific officer after Avidea was acquired, and a corporate combination there moves people. Worth a glance for talent, not for science.

Three things to close on. First, two of today's items are the same lesson from opposite ends. Ultragenyx had an open-label package everyone believed and no control arm; Teva had fifty patients, a provocation, and a controlled eight-week histology readout. The second company will know whether its drug works for a tenth of the cost.
