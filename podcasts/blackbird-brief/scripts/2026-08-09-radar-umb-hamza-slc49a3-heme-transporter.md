Start with the readable one, from Iqbal Hamza's lab at the University of Maryland School of Medicine. The biology is heme transport — how a cell moves heme, the iron-containing molecule at the center of hemoglobin and a lot of mitochondrial chemistry, in and out of its compartments without poisoning itself, because free heme is toxic. Hamza's group ran a genetic screen in a roundworm and found that when you knock out the main intestinal heme exporter, the animal dies, because heme gets trapped inside internal vesicles. Then they found the bypass, and it's the interesting part: a previously uncharacterized family of transporters that hands heme off from the importer to an exporter inside the cell, and they nailed down the human version — a transporter called S-L-C-forty-nine-A-three — as a conserved heme exporter in its own right. Knock it down in zebrafish and in human red-cell precursors and you get heme overload, premature hemoglobin, and the cells die. And structurally they argue it has a heme-binding pocket distinct from the heme transporters we already know about, which matters, because a distinct pocket is what gives you a shot at a selective drug.

Why does that matter commercially. Heme maldistribution sits underneath a set of real diseases — the anemias, disorders of red-cell production, and mitochondrial disease — and the authors say so explicitly, framing this circuit as something you could target to correct heme imbalance. A brand-new druggable transporter, a new node in a pathway that has mostly been undruggable, is exactly the kind of novel-target-class story a specialist hematology or rare-disease investor will at least take a meeting on.

Now the candid catch, and it's a real one. This is early — worm, then zebrafish, then cultured human cells. There is no molecule, no lead, no in-vivo disease model showing you can fix an anemia by drugging this transporter. It is target identification, full stop. And here's the part you need to know before anyone gets excited: the competing-interest statement says the corresponding author is the president and founder of a company called Rakta Therapeutics, which is already working on heme-transporter-related products. Now, Rakta is described as a diagnostics company, so a therapeutic against this specific transporter may well be open ground — but you cannot evaluate this as a clean Blackbird build without first understanding what Rakta controls, what the university has filed, and where the inventor's allegiance sits. When the discovering scientist already has his own vehicle in the exact space, that is a freedom-to-operate and alignment question you answer before anything else, not after. So my read is watch-and-understand-the-IP, not found-and-lead. For Eddie: the first call is to U-M Ventures — is there a filing on this transporter as a therapeutic target, is it inside or outside the Rakta relationship, and is the inventor even available to found something new. For Avi: is a transporter like this genuinely tractable as a small-molecule or biologic target, or is it another elegant node that nobody can actually drug.

The second item is the one I'm genuinely interested in and deliberately not leading on, because I couldn't finish the diligence. Jennifer Elisseeff's lab at Johns Hopkins — and she is a proven serial founder, with companies already spun out of her tissue and senescence work — posted an atlas this week of cellular senescence in uterine fibroids. Fibroids are a huge, badly served market: they are extremely common, and the only real options today are hormonal treatment or surgery. The paper maps the senescent bad-actor cells that drive the matrix stiffening and the abnormal blood vessels that make fibroids what they are, and points at them as targets for a non-surgical, senescence-directed therapy. That is a large-unmet-need, anti-fibrotic and senotherapeutic angle, from a founder who has done this before, and the funding is a live Hopkins–Maryland collaboration, which is our sweet spot. Here's the honest problem: the preprint went up two days ago and bioRxiv hasn't rendered the full body yet — I could read the abstract but not the actual results — so per our own rule I will not lead on a paper I haven't read cover to cover. And it is a descriptive atlas, so even fully read it is a would-take-the-meeting on strength of the market and the founder, not an asset. I'm putting it at the top of next week's re-pull.
