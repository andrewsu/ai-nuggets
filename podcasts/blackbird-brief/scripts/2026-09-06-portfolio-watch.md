## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Sunday, September sixth. The most important development for the portfolio is a Phase three trial that did everything right and still failed: Novartis lowered L-P little a by eighty percent in eight thousand three hundred patients and changed nothing about their cardiovascular outcomes.

The drug is pelacarsen, an antisense oligonucleotide partnered with Ionis. Lp-a-HORIZON enrolled eight thousand three hundred and twenty-three patients with elevated L-P little a and established cardiovascular disease, all on guideline-directed therapy. The primary endpoint was the standard hard composite — cardiovascular death, non-fatal heart attack, non-fatal stroke, and urgent coronary revascularization requiring hospitalization. It missed. Novartis's chief medical officer put it plainly: lower levels were observed, and the findings did not demonstrate that this translated into reduced cardiovascular risk. The reduction itself was in the range seen in earlier trials, roughly eighty percent. Full data goes to a medical congress.

William Blair had about six billion dollars of peak United States sales riding on this, and their analyst calls it meaningful risk for the competitor programs. Those competitors — Amgen's olpasiran, which has hit better than ninety-five percent suppression in mid-stage work, Lilly's lepodisiran, Silence's zerlasiran — are all arguing that deeper is different. Jefferies wants to see whether outcome tracks with baseline level and with the magnitude of reduction. That subgroup analysis is the whole future of the class, and it is not public yet.

Here is what I want us to take from it, in four parts.

First, this completes a week. Since Thursday this brief has covered three antisense events. Ionis won a standard approval in Alexander disease on one prespecified functional endpoint in fifty-four patients. Ultragenyx separated on nothing across five domains in a hundred and twenty-nine Angelman children. And now the largest antisense outcomes trial ever run suppressed its target by eighty percent in eight thousand patients and moved no outcome. Three different failure modes, and not one of them is the chemistry. The first was about endpoint selection. The second was about inferring from open-label developmental scales. This one is target validation, and it is the most expensive kind, because the molecule did exactly what it was built to do.

Second, and this is for Jon and Avi on NuGate. On Thursday the instruction from the Angelman miss was to build neurofilament as the evidentiary spine, the way tofersen did. That instruction is still right, and today's result tells you why it is not sufficient on its own. Neurofilament is a target-engagement biomarker, and target engagement is precisely the thing pelacarsen proved beyond argument while failing anyway. Tofersen reached the market on a biomarker because superoxide dismutase one A-L-S has a monogenic causal chain — the mutation causes the disease, so lowering the protein has a mechanistic claim on the outcome. Any NuGate target where the link from the R-N-A to the clinical phenotype is associative rather than causal is running the pelacarsen bet, not the tofersen bet. Ask that question of every target in the program before the biomarker plan is written: is this causal, or is it correlated?

Third, the instrument point, which is now three for three in this brief. Two weeks ago it was relaxin-receptor trials measuring hemodynamics instead of fibrosis. Last week it was a cardiac gene therapy measuring ejection fraction and six-minute walk instead of scar. This week is the opposite case and worth naming as such: the right instrument, honestly applied at enormous scale, returning a clean negative. That is a decisive trial, and for the field it is a good outcome — the hypothesis actually got tested. What we should take from all three together is that the argument about the endpoint belongs at design, and the question of whether the target is causal belongs before development-candidate declaration, not after eight thousand patients.

Fourth, and Emily and Eddie, this is the actionable half. Royalty Pharma paid Ionis five hundred million dollars and split it deliberately: a hundred and fifty million against pelacarsen royalties, three hundred and fifty million against Spinraza royalties. Pelacarsen just went to zero — no milestones. Royalty Pharma still expects to recoup the entire five hundred million and earn a modest positive return, because the Spinraza interest reverts to Ionis only once payments reach five hundred and fifty million, which is a one-point-one times return on everything funded. They reaffirmed their twenty-thirty portfolio target the same day the asset died. That is the structure: they bought the binary as an option sitting on top of an annuity, sized so a zero costs them the upside and not the principal. BioVentures can copy that directly on any single-asset, single-readout position — pair the binary with a de-risked or cash-flowing interest in the same counterparty, and size the binary so that going to zero costs you the return rather than the capital. Clasp's GUARDIAN-101 monotherapy readout is the live example. Ask what our downside structure looks like there.

Second item, and this one is a tailwind for a pre-spinout theme. A paper in Science on Thursday from M-D Anderson shows that psilocybin, given before neurotoxic chemotherapy, prevents the development of pain hypersensitivity in mice — with protection maintained up to eight months.

The mechanism is the reason I am putting it in this brief. Chemotherapy freezes the transport machinery that moves mitochondria down peripheral nerve fibers to the distal endings, and without that energy supply the endings die before they can recover once the toxin clears. Psilocybin activates serotonin two-A receptors on the peripheral neurons themselves and preserves that mitochondrial transport. They showed it directly with live-cell imaging of mitochondrial movement in human peripheral nerve in a dish. Patrick Dougherty and Moran Amit are the senior authors. Chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy affects up to sixty-eight percent of patients during treatment and nearly thirty percent beyond six months, and the standard of care is ice packs and duloxetine. Their Phase two prevention trial in breast, colorectal and head-and-neck patients opens in about a month.

Three reads for the non-opioid pain theme. The first is that the mechanism is peripheral. If the therapeutic action is at a serotonin two-A receptor on a sensory neuron and not in cortex, then the hallucinogenic effect is a liability to engineer out rather than the mechanism to preserve. That is very close to Todd Gould's argument at U-M-B — his event-driven pharmacology framework says the plastogen class gets mis-developed when you optimize for stable receptor occupancy. This paper hands that class something it has not had: a peripherally restricted indication where a non-brain-penetrant serotonin two-A agonist would be a strictly better drug, not a compromised one. Hemaka, that reframes the second-and-third-asset conversation for the neuropsych pipeline. Peripheral restriction is a much more tractable chemistry problem than central signaling bias, and there is an established U-M-B bench with the pharmacology.

The second read is the trial design, and it is the same lesson as the Teva celiac study we flagged on Thursday. Prevention of chemotherapy neuropathy gives you a population identified in advance, a scheduled insult, a defined dosing window before the insult, and an endpoint that reads out on the chemotherapy calendar. Compare that with chronic neuropathic pain, where you enroll established disease and fight a thirty percent placebo response over twelve weeks. Every non-opioid pain program we look at should be asked whether a prevention framing with a scheduled insult is available to it — surgery, chemotherapy, radiation.

The third is the caveat. This is mice plus human nerve in a dish, and the design requires dosing a psychedelic in cancer patients before chemotherapy, which the authors themselves flag as demanding careful control. And note where the capital in this field has gone: AbbVie paid one-point-two billion for Gilgamesh's program, Otsuka seven hundred million for Transcend, Lilly two-point-eight billion upfront for AtaiBeckley — all psychiatry. Nobody is bidding for peripheral serotonin two-A. That is either an opening or a signal, and the Phase two is how we find out which.

Third item closes an open question we wrote down yesterday. BioPharma Dive's Friday chart set put a number on Lilly. Since the start of twenty-twenty-six, Lilly has announced twelve acquisitions — four times its nearest competitors, Gilead, G-S-K and Novartis — for at least thirty-one and a half billion dollars, double the next most active acquirer. Roughly half of those twelve were preclinical or Phase one stage, including this week's Merida deal at up to two-point-nine billion with an undisclosed upfront.

Yesterday we opened an item to add Lilly to the bidder set for the Lieber schizophrenia program and rank it. Here is the ranking. Lilly is not one bidder among Otsuka, Neurocrine, AbbVie and the rest — on twenty-twenty-six behavior it is the most active acquirer in the industry by a factor of four, and it is the one buying at our stage. Half of twelve deals done at preclinical or Phase one is the single most relevant fact on our exit map, because it means the G-P-R fifty-two program does not have to reach a Phase two readout to be transactable. Eddie, rank Lilly first, and then ask the harder follow-up: half of twelve means Lilly has a repeatable diligence process for buying assets with no clinical data. We should know what that process wants to see, because it is a specification for our development-candidate package.

One discipline note from the same chart set. Alumis fell to about ten dollars on its lupus miss and Ultragenyx halved on Angelman. Both mid-caps with one defining readout. That is the same lesson as the Royalty Pharma structure in the lead item, read from the equity side instead of the royalty side.

Fourth and briefly, a competitive read on our own funnel rather than on a molecule. MassBio and S-C-bio announced their fall accelerator cohort on Friday: ten startups, five techbio and five therapeutics. The techbio half is aimed at glioblastoma, pancreatic cancer, Parkinson's and drug resistance using generative protein design, causal inference and virtual cell modeling; the therapeutics half at A-L-S, Alzheimer's, rare pediatric neurology, liver cancer and obesity, across small molecules, antibodies, gene editing, gene therapy, oncolytic viruses and cell therapy. Seven of ten are United States based, four in Massachusetts. The program is free — eight weeks of curriculum, weekly mentors, demo days in Boston and Charleston, and bench access through a partner institute. The eligibility cap is under one and a half million dollars of prior equity.

Esther and Maisha, the read is about our top of funnel. Two state trade associations just ran a national call and are giving ten companies eight weeks and lab access for nothing, and that eligibility cap puts the whole cohort upstream of where a Blackbird Labs grant usually lands. It costs the organizers curriculum and mentor time rather than capital, and it is a competing first touch for exactly the founders who might otherwise come to Baltimore. Two questions worth an hour: do we have a no-capital touchpoint for founders earlier than a Labs grant, and would a BioHub demo day with bench access be a cheaper sourcing instrument than we currently assume? Note also what they selected for — half the cohort is computational, which is the same signal the Kathuria case gave us last Sunday.

Week ahead. The myeloma meeting in Glasgow, September twenty-third through twenty-sixth, carries the full CERVINO dataset — that is Jon's open item. The Lp-a-HORIZON full dataset goes to an unnamed upcoming congress, and the number to find is the outcome broken out by baseline level and by depth of reduction, because that single analysis decides whether Amgen's and Lilly's deeper-suppression programs live. And the psilocybin prevention trial opens in about a month.

Closing thought. Two of today's four items are about structure rather than science. Royalty Pharma lost a binary and kept its principal because it bought the binary as an option on an annuity. Lilly bought twelve companies, half of them with no clinical data, because it has a process for doing that.
