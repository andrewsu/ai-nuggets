## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Sunday, October eighteenth. The most important development for the portfolio is that AstraZeneca has put a published price on A-I target discovery — forty million dollars down against a one and a half billion dollar headline — and it bought that work in cardiomyopathy and fatty liver disease, which is our cardiac fibrosis theme, not long after two of its own partnered antisense assets failed in those same indications.

Start there. On Thursday, AstraZeneca and insitro announced a drug discovery collaboration worth up to about one and a half billion dollars. The structure is the part worth reading twice. Insitro gets forty million dollars up front, up to one point four six billion in development and commercial milestones, and a single-digit royalty on sales. Insitro runs its Virtual Human platform to identify causal disease biology and takes AstraZeneca-nominated targets from discovery to development candidate. After that, AstraZeneca owns exclusive worldwide rights, and AstraZeneca funds and runs everything clinical. The disease areas are metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis, the fatty liver disease now usually called MASH, and cardiomyopathies. Sharon Barr, who heads biopharmaceuticals research and development there, framed it exactly as you would expect: disease biology is complex, and one of the most critical decisions in research is selecting the right target.

Three reads, and the first one is for Hemaka and Jon. This is the second time in a matter of weeks that a large pharma has bought target discovery, rather than an asset, in the cardiovascular space. The first was Boehringer and Variant Bio, more than two hundred and fifty million dollars in total value, upfront not disclosed — and I flagged at the time that the undisclosed upfront was the missing number. We now have the number from the next deal in the same lane. Forty million dollars against one and a half billion is a two point seven percent down payment. Put that next to the carve-out we covered on Thursday, where a founder-built vehicle took five cell therapy programs off a public company — two of them close to human testing — for eight million euros against two hundred and thirty million in milestones. Three and a half percent down. Two completely different transactions, one a pharma buying computational discovery and one a startup buying divested assets, and both cleared at low single digits of headline value. That is a usable anchor. When a Blackbird program gets a term sheet with a nine-figure headline, the only number that is real is the first one, and the market rate for work that has not produced a molecule is two to four percent of the total.

Second read, and this one is for Virginia. We have been running a rule since late August: when a program leads with A-I, ask what the A-I actually buys. The answer has usually been a property — a longer half-life, better developability, a quarterly injection instead of a monthly one — and I argued that competing on properties is cheaper and more reliable than competing on target discovery. This deal is the counterexample, and it is instructive precisely because of what it cost. AstraZeneca is buying target selection outright, and it paid forty million dollars for it. Compare that with the Alector deal two weeks ago, where Genentech paid one hundred million dollars up front for a single preclinical engineered enzyme whose patient is found by genotype and whose defect is measurable. A de-risked preclinical asset with a findable patient was worth two and a half times the entire upfront for a computational target engine across two disease areas. The hierarchy is not subtle, and it has not changed. Target discovery, including the A-I kind, is still the cheapest thing in the stack to buy.

Third read is structural, and Eddie should carry it. Insitro has now signed Gilead, Bristol Myers Squibb, Eli Lilly, and AstraZeneca. It also cut twenty-two percent of its staff in May of last year to extend runway. That is a platform company that could not carry its own pipeline and has converted into a discovery shop that sells the same capability four times at two or three percent down. For any Blackbird program pitching itself as a platform, that is the failure mode to name out loud in the deck: a platform that cannot fund its own first asset does not get platform economics, it gets service economics. The candid caveat on the whole item is that AstraZeneca's motive here is partly defensive. Its Ionis-partnered drug Wainua failed a Phase three trial aiming to expand into transthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy, and an earlier Ionis-partnered MASH candidate was discontinued in November of last year. Money moved upstream to target discovery right after two assets failed downstream. That is a reason to be a little skeptical about how committed the spend really is.

Second item, and it sits directly on top of yesterday's lead. OrbiMed announced on Thursday that it has raised more than three billion dollars across two new funds. Its tenth private investments vehicle, for U-S and European companies, holds one point nine billion dollars. Its sixth Asia Partners fund holds more than one billion. The comparison that matters is with the prior cycles. The U-S and Europe fund raised one point eight six billion in twenty twenty-two and one and a half billion in twenty twenty-one, so this one is essentially flat. The Asia fund raised seven hundred and fifty-one million dollars in twenty twenty-three and eight hundred million in twenty twenty-one, so this one is up by a third or more. Managing partner Carl Gordon named two drivers: the rise of China, and A-I. And OrbiMed is not alone — TCGX closed an oversubscribed six hundred million dollar Asia fund the week before, and is opening offices in Hong Kong and Shanghai.

The number inside that story is the one to write down. According to a new Locust Walk report, China-headquartered biotechs have accounted for fifty-eight percent of all licensing deal value so far in twenty twenty-six, against twenty-six percent for U-S companies. In twenty twenty-three, the split was fourteen percent Chinese and forty-eight percent American. That has inverted in three years.

Here is the read for Eddie and Matt, and it has to be held together with yesterday's item. Yesterday China drafted a rule that would prohibit selling ownership of a Chinese cell and gene therapy or small nucleic acid platform abroad, while leaving asset licensing on automatic. Today the largest dedicated life sciences investor in the world is putting its growth capital into Asia. Those two facts are consistent, and together they describe a market where the molecules keep flowing west and the platforms stay put. Our yesterday conclusion stands — a clean-title U-S academic platform gets scarcer — but the harder half is the financing environment it has to be sold into. The marginal dollar at OrbiMed is going east, and the Western fund is flat in nominal terms, which after three years is down in real terms. The candid caveats are real: a fund close is capital commitment, not deployment, and OrbiMed at this stage writes growth and crossover checks, not seed checks, so this does not change who leads a Blackbird Series A. What it changes is who is in the room at the Series B, and what they are comparing our asset to.

Third item is for the Winnow file and the autoimmune theme. Novartis has terminated its Phase two trial of Fabhalta in lupus nephritis. There was no press release. It appears in the federal trials registry, which was updated on Thursday, and the stated reason is this, quoted: the decision to conclude the study after Part one last-patient-last-visit is based on strategic considerations by the sponsor. The trial enrolled eighty-one patients with active class three or four lupus nephritis, with or without class five. The primary endpoint was the proportion achieving complete renal response at week twenty-four without renal flares. It started in August of twenty twenty-two, and the primary completion date had already passed — March nineteenth of this year.

Three things make this worth four minutes. First, the reason is neither safety nor futility. It is portfolio strategy, and it arrives after the sponsor has seen Part one. Second, Fabhalta is not a failed drug. It is iptacopan, an oral factor B inhibitor already approved in paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria, in C3 glomerulopathy, and, under accelerated approval, in I-g-A nephropathy to reduce proteinuria. Novartis is not abandoning a mechanism; it is declining to extend a marketed kidney drug into lupus. Third, look at what it would have been competing against. In September, Adicet's gamma delta C-A-R T cell therapy reported twelve-month lupus remissions with F-D-A alignment on outpatient dosing. Autolus has R-M-A-T designation to take an already-approved C-A-R T into systemic lupus and lupus nephritis. Against therapies promising durable, drug-free remission after one administration, a daily oral agent that reduces proteinuria has to win on convenience.

The read for Hemaka and the Winnow team: the competitive question in lupus nephritis has shifted from whether a mechanism works to how long the patient stays off drug, and an incremental mechanism now gets pruned by its own owner before it even reaches Phase three. That goes straight into the open item we already have on Winnow — the one that says a chronically dosed engager is competing against products selling drug-free remission, and that the off-treatment durability arm is the product claim rather than a nice-to-have. A large pharma just made that trade explicitly, in the other direction, with a marketed drug. The candid limits: strategic considerations is a deliberately empty phrase that can mean a crowded internal portfolio just as easily as an unblinded look nobody liked, and no efficacy data have been released, so treat the inference as an inference. There is also a process point for Yixuan. We found this because someone reads registry updates. Novartis never announced it, and the trade press picked it up off the same database a day later. For the handful of competitor programs that matter most to Winnow, Aletira, and NuGate, a weekly registry diff is cheap intelligence.

Last item, short, and it is a Maryland one. A consortium called SEEDS has been awarded up to fifty-one point seven million dollars over four years by ARPA-H, announced October twelfth. It is co-led out of Georgetown and the Buck Institute, and the University of Maryland's Pratyush Tiwary is a co-principal investigator on the Georgetown-led arm. Tiwary holds appointments at both College Park and Baltimore, and directs therapeutic drug discovery at the University of Maryland Institute for Health Computing. The scientific target is intrinsically disordered proteins — proteins that have no stable shape and no binding pocket, which is why they have been called undruggable for thirty years. The approach is a chemistry-first generative model that predicts how a disordered protein's ensemble of shapes changes with p-H, molecular crowding, metabolites, oxidative stress, and post-translational modifications, validated experimentally rather than left in silico. The first two proteins are tau, in Alzheimer's, and the E-W-S F-L-I one fusion, in Ewing sarcoma. Ten academic institutions and seven industry partners are in the consortium.

Two reasons this is ours rather than general science news. The first is the channel. Tiwary is dual-appointed across College Park and U-M-B, which is precisely the cross-campus route we have been tracking through the St. John Center as a sourcing channel rather than a lead. This is that channel winning a large federal award, and it repeats a pattern we first logged on the chronic infections theme: the serious money for this region keeps arriving federal or state and non-dilutive rather than venture. Our own National Center to Accelerate Cures opened at the BioHub three weeks ago on three million dollars of congressionally directed funding. That is the same shape. The second reason is the shape of the asset. The stated bottleneck here is not the algorithm, it is the absence of training data for disordered proteins — there is no equivalent of the structural database that made protein folding prediction work. So what gets created over four years is a dataset and a validated model, and that, not any molecule, is the licensable thing. Esther and Yixuan, two questions worth an email before the data exists: who the seven industry partners are, and what U-M-B's position is in the consortium's intellectual property terms. A university's share inside a ten-institution federal consortium is negotiated, not given, and the time to understand it is now rather than in year three. Candid: this is a research award and not a company, and the two pilot proteins are among the most heavily worked targets in medicine, so the commercial object here is the platform, not the asset.

That's the watch. Two deadlines before the week ahead. ARPA-H solution summaries for the SPECTRA program's fourth technical area close at five o'clock Eastern today, which is the Lieber and Kennedy Krieger item. And applications for the F-D-A's Expedited I-N-D pilot close at the end of the thirtieth, which is the one that needs a decision from us this week on whether we apply, with which program, and with which institution as the partner. Then: Kyverna presents its full stiff person syndrome dataset at the joint ACTRIMS–ECTRIMS meeting in Toronto Wednesday through Friday, the next real data point for Winnow. The human genetics meeting runs in Montreal across the same days, and Pearl Diagnostics presents at IDWeek on Thursday. L-B Pharmaceuticals holds its research and development day on the twenty-seventh, the F-D-A's rare disease framework public meeting is on the twenty-eighth, and comments on China's export draft are due November fifteenth. Have a good Sunday.
