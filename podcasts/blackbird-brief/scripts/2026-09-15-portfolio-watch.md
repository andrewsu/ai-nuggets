Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Tuesday, September fifteenth. The most important development today is not a readout. It is a door that opened this morning with a deadline six weeks out, and the eligibility criteria describe Blackbird almost exactly.

The F-D-A opened applications for Operation TrialBlazer. Applications close October thirtieth.

Here is the shape of it. Operation TrialBlazer is the health department roadmap launched on June twenty-second to keep early clinical research in the United States. The piece that matters to us is the expedited I-N-D pilot. It went out for comment in late June under a docket that closed on August twenty-fourth, and as of today it is no longer a request for information. It is a program taking applications.

The mechanism is this. A drug sponsor pairs with what the agency calls a qualified research institution — an academic medical center, a contract research organization, a health system with real scientific depth in the relevant area. The pair applies together. In exchange, the agency reviews and accepts individual components of the I-N-D on a rolling basis during the pre-I-N-D phase, rather than waiting for the whole package to be assembled before anybody looks at anything. Karim Mikhail, who runs the biologics center, said the goal is to identify and resolve issues earlier, reduce the risk of clinical holds, and make the path to first-in-human trials more predictable.

The initial cohort is eight to ten sponsor-institution pairs. Selection and launch in the fourth quarter.

Now, why this is our item and not just a policy item. Every Blackbird program lives in the window this pilot is designed for — nonprofit grant, de-risked academic asset, spinout, and then the long march to a first I-N-D. Aletira is pre-I-N-D. NuGate is pre-I-N-D. The G-P-R fifty-two NewCo is heading toward a development candidate later this year and an I-N-D after that. And the qualified research institution on the other side of the application is Johns Hopkins, or U-M-B, or Hopkins Medicine — institutions we already have grant relationships with, which is precisely the thing most sponsors would have to go build.

Then look at what we have been reporting for two straight weeks. Biohaven's partial clinical hold, which landed between signing and closing on a three-hundred-fifty-million-dollar license, over nonclinical data the buyer had already seen. The REGENXBIO hold. The run of A-A-V holds this summer. The single most expensive thing that can happen to a Blackbird asset in the year before its first patient is a hold on the first-in-human protocol, and the agency has just offered eight to ten companies a structured way to find those problems early.

Two candid things before anyone gets excited. Eight to ten slots against the entire American industry is a lottery, so treat an application as cheap optionality, not as a plan. And the version that went out for comment in June had the qualified institution doing advisory pre-review only, with the agency keeping full decision authority and the thirty-day I-N-D clock unchanged. If that is still the shape, the benefit is process discipline and early signal, not calendar time. Confirm which it is before a program commits to it.

There is a companion piece, because the agency holds its PDUFA VIII public meeting tomorrow, setting priorities for fiscal years twenty twenty-eight through twenty thirty-two. Two things in that docket are ours. The agency has proposed cutting new drug application fees by fifty percent — about two point three million dollars — for products whose early-stage trials run in the United States. Jeremy Levin at Ovid gave the honest critique: the incentive arrives near the end of development, but it has to change a decision made years earlier. He is right, and that is exactly why TrialBlazer is the more interesting half — it arrives at pre-I-N-D, which is where the decision actually gets made. The second is the rare disease ask, with several groups pushing for a standing, cross-functional rare disease review capability inside the agency rather than ad hoc expertise. That is the function Aletira and NuGate will both be standing in front of.

So the action is Matt, Maisha and Avi. Decide this week whether we apply, and with which program and which institution. The deadline is October thirtieth and it will not move.

Second item, and it lowers the rung on a ladder we have been building in this brief for three weeks.

G-S-K announced today that it is acquiring a trispecific T-cell engager from Chimagen Biosciences for up to seven hundred fifty million dollars. The asset binds T cells and two separate tumor-associated antigens simultaneously, aimed at multiple myeloma. Hesham Abdullah, who runs oncology R-and-D there, said it complements their existing myeloma portfolio.

Here is the number that matters. The asset is preclinical. It is expected to enter Phase one in twenty twenty-seven.

Run the ladder we have been keeping. DualityBio, forty-five million dollars up front for discovery through Phase one A. Simcere, seventy-five million for an early-clinical asset. HUTCHMED, one hundred ten million for a Phase one asset. Solstice and Harbour, fifty million in cash for a Phase two-ready antibody. Every one of those rungs had a molecule characterized against a clinical or near-clinical bar. This one has not been in a person and will not be for more than a year, and G-S-K bought full global rights to it outright.

Chimagen is Chinese — Shanghai and Chengdu, founded twenty sixteen, a multi-specific antibody platform aimed at both cancer and autoimmune disease. This is G-S-K's second deal with them. In twenty twenty-four G-S-K paid three hundred million dollars up front plus five hundred fifty million in milestones for a C-D-nineteen by C-D-twenty engager that is now in Phase one in B-cell malignancies and B-cell-dependent autoimmune disease.

Two reads, and the second one is for Jon.

The first is for Eddie and Matt. The ex-U-S comparator column now extends all the way down to preclinical, which is precisely the stage at which a Blackbird asset gets sold. Our answer to that column has been an unencumbered composition-of-matter position, and that answer holds. But note what did not get printed in this release: the up-front number. Two years ago the same buyer and the same seller disclosed three hundred million up front. Today neither side put a figure on the page. That is an inference and not a fact, but the usual reason an up-front goes undisclosed is that it is small relative to the headline — and if a preclinical trispecific from an experienced platform company commands an up-front nobody wants to print, that is the number our preclinical package is being compared against.

The second read is Winnow's, and it cuts in our favor. G-S-K's autoimmune engager bet, bought from this same seller, is B-cell depletion. Winnow is not. Winnow redirects cytotoxic T cells at a defined autoimmune antigen, which is the T-cell side of the disease. B-cell depletion in autoimmunity is now extremely crowded — C-A-R T, engagers, everybody. Antigen-specific T-cell targeting is not. That distinction belongs in the first paragraph of the Winnow deck, not the fifth, because the buyer we would most want has already spent money on the other half of the immune system and can tell the difference.

Third item, and it is an eight-day-old call of ours that needs sharpening.

On September fourth the F-D-A gave accelerated approval to AstraZeneca's ETCAMAH — camizestrant — in hormone-receptor-positive advanced breast cancer, on detection of an E-S-R one mutation during therapy, before radiographic progression. Guardant's liquid biopsy was approved alongside it as the first F-D-A-approved longitudinal companion diagnostic in breast cancer. We covered it on the seventh as the monitoring precedent Artemyx needs, and our candid half was that the diagnostic got its indication as a passenger on somebody else's Phase three.

Yesterday AstraZeneca reported that SERENA-four missed. Same drug, plus palbociclib, in first-line advanced breast cancer against an aromatase inhibitor plus palbociclib — no biomarker selection, no molecular switch, just the drug in the big population. Numerical improvement in progression-free survival, not statistically significant. Susan Galbraith said the outcome sharpens their focus on maximizing the number of patients who can benefit based on SERENA-six. Leerink cut peak sales from two point nine billion dollars to eight hundred thirty million.

So revise the read, and revise it upward. The molecularly guided indication is not a niche carve-out of a large franchise. It is the entire franchise. The version of this drug that needed no diagnostic did not work; the version that does need one is the only version that exists. The assay did not ride along on a Phase three — the assay is the reason there is a product at all.

The harder half, for Eddie. That makes the diagnostic's value entirely contingent on one sponsor's one trial, and SERENA-six was an accelerated approval with a confirmatory trial still owed. If that confirmation fails, the companion diagnostic indication goes with it. So the instruction from the seventh stands and gets more urgent: Artemyx should be inside more than one sponsor's protocol-design conversation.

Fourth item, short, and it is the epidemiology line in every one of our decks.

GeneDx, up in Gaithersburg, announced a partnership with Beren Therapeutics on Niemann-Pick type C — a rare pediatric neurodegenerative disease of cholesterol trafficking. The program offers genome sequencing plus targeted variant testing at no cost, no insurance required, forty-eight-hour turnaround on urgent cases. Beren finances the whole thing. Beren is developing a treatment for the same disease.

The number is what I want you to hear. Roughly four hundred seventy-five American children are believed to have infantile-onset Niemann-Pick type C. About one hundred seventy-five of them have a diagnosis. Something like two-thirds of U-S patients with this disease are unidentified, and the therapeutic sponsor is paying for the search.

The read is for Geoff and for Avi, and it is uncomfortable in a useful way. When we write that G-J-B two accounts for roughly two hundred thousand people across the U-S and Europe, that is a prevalence number. The number that matters to a trial and to a launch is how many of them carry a confirmed molecular diagnosis. Newborn hearing screening detects hearing loss. It does not genotype anybody. Skylark's trial requires confirmed biallelic G-J-B two variants for enrollment, which means patient identification is already a rate limiter on a ten-child study. So two questions worth having answered: what fraction of severe-to-profound congenital hearing loss in this country actually carries a molecular diagnosis, and who pays for that panel today. If the honest answer is a minority and nobody, then patient-finding is a cost line we own, it belongs in the Series A budget, and it is also a legitimate reason to build a relationship with a sequencing company years before an approval.

Last item, quick, on where the agency's tolerance currently sits.

Cullinan and Taiho said today they will file zipalertinib in first-line lung cancer. The Phase three enrolled two hundred eighty patients with E-G-F-R exon twenty insertions. Progression-free survival was fourteen and a half months against eight and a half on chemotherapy alone, about a fifty percent reduction in risk, with an interim overall survival benefit of twenty-eight percent. Three patients in the treatment arm died of septic shock and the companies determined the deaths were related to the study regimen. Grade three or worse adverse events, eighty-seven percent versus fifty-four. William Blair called the efficacy robust.

Set that beside Saturday's approval, where both p-values were nominal and the agency approved anyway. The pattern is that current review discretion is running on the magnitude of the benefit, not the cleanliness of the safety table. Three treatment-related deaths buy you a filing if you have a six-month survival story.

Now invert it, because that is the half that applies to us. Winnow's diseases do not kill people on a six-month clock. In autoimmunity there is no survival delta available to purchase that tolerance with, which means the safety table is not a section of the package — it is the product. The same agency that is about to accept three septic-shock deaths in refractory lung cancer will accept approximately zero in ankylosing spondylitis. That is the whole argument for antigen selectivity, and this week the oncology side of the trade got priced in public.

Quiet where it matters, and I checked. Nothing on Clasp — the C-L-S-P ten twenty-five monotherapy readout is still guided to this year. No movement on G-P-R fifty-two or Nxera, with under four months left in the partnering year they committed to publicly. Nothing new on NuGate, on Biolinco, or on 1104health. And no fresh otology or A-A-V selectivity readouts since Friday.

That is the brief. The one thing to carry out of today is the October thirtieth deadline. Have a good Tuesday.
