# The Blackbird Brief — Portfolio Watch — 2026-10-10

## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Saturday, October tenth. The most important development for the portfolio: a Parkinson's cell therapy built on an academic license that cost one thousand dollars has filed to go public. The filing shows how a platform gets from a professor's lab to Nasdaq in eight years. It also shows exactly how little of that value the institution kept, and that is a design question for Blackbird.

The company is Aspen Neuroscience in San Diego, and it filed its I-P-O registration on Thursday evening under the proposed ticker A-S-P-E. Its lead product, sasineprocel, is an autologous cell therapy. Aspen takes a three-millimeter skin biopsy from the patient, reprograms those cells into induced pluripotent stem cells, turns them into dopamine neuron precursors, and a surgeon implants them back into the same patient's brain. Because the cells are the patient's own, nobody in the trial has needed immunosuppression. That is the line Aspen draws against the leader, BlueRock, the Bayer subsidiary, whose embryonic-stem-cell product can require a year or more of tacrolimus and steroids.

Here is the data, and it is thin. The Phase One-Two-A trial is open-label with no control arm. Fifteen patients are dosed across four cohorts, and the efficacy numbers rest on four to eight patients at each time point. On the motor exam in the off state, patients improved by fourteen to sixteen points on average at six, twelve and eighteen months, and the four low-dose patients held a sixteen-point gain at two years. Good on-time, meaning hours without troublesome dyskinesia, rose by about two to almost three hours. Aspen compares that against natural-history data in which patients worsen about two points a year. There was no graft-induced dyskinesia. One patient had two serious adverse events, both tied to the surgery. The F-D-A has told Aspen the Phase Three will need a control group, possibly a sham surgery, with skin biopsies starting by the end of this year and dosing in the second half of twenty twenty-seven. Aspen has raised about three hundred forty million dollars and spent about two hundred sixty-five million of it.

Now the part that matters most to this team. The platform came from Jeanne Loring's lab at Scripps Research. The license was signed in July twenty eighteen, and a nonprofit, the Summit for Stem Cell Foundation, was a party to it. That foundation assigned the license to Aspen in October twenty eighteen. Scripps took a one-thousand-dollar upfront fee and a one percent royalty on net sales. It gets fifteen percent of sublicense income if a deal is signed in the first three years, and five percent after that. Those are the terms on the platform now heading to Nasdaq. The license is exclusive and worldwide, with diligence benchmarks that let Scripps convert it to non-exclusive if Aspen stalls.

Three reads. First, for Eddie and Esther, this is the cleanest public template we have for our own structure. A nonprofit sits between the institution and the newco, helps hold the license, and then assigns it at formation. Aspen's filing does not show the foundation keeping any economics under the license after it assigned it, and nothing we read says whether it got equity some other way. Before our next assignment, decide in writing what Blackbird Laboratories keeps: equity, a royalty share, a reversion right, or a field of use. The Scripps terms also tell you what a sophisticated institution accepted in twenty eighteen for an unpartnered platform. Expect our partner institutions to ask for more now, and have this filing on the table when they do.

Second, for Anthony and Jon on the Parkinson's theme. This week we had a symptomatic adjunct win a Phase Three in Cerevance's G-P-R-six drug. Now a disease-modifying replacement therapy is heading to public investors on fifteen open-label patients. The bar it will face is a sham-controlled trial with eighteen-to-twenty-four-month endpoints. That is the regulatory cost of claiming disease modification in Parkinson's, and any cell-replacement or neurorestoration idea that reaches us should be priced against it. The autologous-versus-allogeneic split is also now a public-market debate. Autologous buys freedom from immunosuppression and pays for it in one manufacturing run per patient. Watch where this I-P-O prices.

Third, a quick one for Matt. This is a Scripps platform. It is worth a call to ask what Aspen would do differently on its license if it started again.

Same night, a second filing worth reading side by side. Bambusa Therapeutics filed for its own I-P-O. Bambusa was incorporated in March twenty twenty-four, so it is two and a half years old. It in-licensed its two lead bispecific antibodies from BioNTech's Chinese subsidiary, the company formerly known as Biotheus. It paid four million dollars upfront for those assigned programs and one million for a later license. It has raised a hundred thirty-six million dollars since then. The lead, B-B-T-zero-zero-one, blocks I-L-four receptor alpha and I-L-thirty-one together for atopic dermatitis and chronic urticaria. The second, B-B-T-zero-zero-two, blocks I-L-four receptor alpha and I-L-five, and it has a four-week proof-of-concept readout in C-O-P-D due in the first half of twenty twenty-seven.

Virginia, that C-O-P-D readout goes on the theme calendar. An I-L-five arm is an eosinophil mechanism, so this is another program whose C-O-P-D case will depend on the eosinophil-high patients. That is the same stratum question we logged against tozorakimab last month. A four-week study will show biomarkers, not exacerbations, so do not over-read it when it lands. Eddie, put the two filings next to each other. Aspen spent eight years and three hundred forty million dollars taking an academic platform to the clinic. Bambusa took a China-sourced, clinic-ready pair of antibodies to the same doorway in thirty months for less than half the capital. For a studio that funds academic science, that is the competing model for the same I-P-O window, and it is cheaper.

Next, for Winnow, Jon and Anthony. On Thursday, Biohaven licensed its I-g-G degraders to Ono Pharmaceutical for Japan, South Korea, Taiwan and Southeast Asia. Ono pays eighty million dollars on closing and twenty million more on a milestone expected within a year, plus royalties of about twenty percent. Biohaven keeps the United States, Europe and China. The lead, B-H-V-thirteen-hundred, is a small molecule that pulls I-g-G out of circulation through the liver and spares I-g-G three, so some infection protection stays intact. It is in Phase Three for Graves' disease. In a Phase One-B study it cut the autoantibody against the thyroid-stimulating hormone receptor deeply and normalized thyroid hormones. The chemistry came from David Spiegel's lab at Yale.

This is the counterweight to yesterday's argenx story. In Sjögren's, an antibody-lowering drug failed. Analysts blamed placebo response, but one reading is that in Sjögren's the autoantibody is more a marker than the cause. Graves' is the opposite case: the antibody is the disease, because it switches on the thyroid directly. So I-g-G lowering is reaching Phase Three where the pathogenic antibody is the causal agent. That is the screen Winnow should apply to its own list. Where an autoantibody causes the disease, antibody depletion competes with us. Where the disease runs through T cells, it does not. The deal shape matters too, for Emily and Eddie. A regional license on Asia, eighty million dollars upfront with a twenty percent royalty, is non-dilutive money that keeps every major market. It is the develop-to-proof-then-license role we have been arguing is ours, and it worked off a Yale lab's chemistry.

One more for the G-P-R fifty-two NewCo, Hemaka and Avi. On Friday the F-D-A approved Teva's Weltruza, the first once-a-month olanzapine injected under the skin, for adults with schizophrenia. It comes in three doses matched to ten, fifteen and twenty milligrams of oral olanzapine. It needs no loading dose and no oral pills alongside it. Teva says it needs no observation period after each injection either, which has been the known burden of the older intramuscular olanzapine depot. The label still carries the warning that post-injection delirium has happened with that other product, and says it is not known whether it can happen with this one. Approval rests on SOLARIS, a six-hundred-seventy-five-patient placebo-controlled Phase Three that met its symptom endpoint. Launch is in the coming weeks.

The read for the NewCo is about the comparator. The most prescribed atypical antipsychotic is now a monthly shot with no monitoring, so adherence is no longer an argument a novel mechanism can win on. That leaves the arguments a G-P-R fifty-two agonist was built for: better tolerability, with no weight gain or sedation, and benefit on negative and cognitive symptoms that olanzapine does not touch. Put that comparison on the deck, and make sure the first patient study measures weight and metabolic markers from day one.

On the watch list. BioPharma Dive's weekly charts this week are on biotech's recent pullback, and TRex Bio priced at the bottom of its range on Thursday. Read Aspen and Bambusa against that backdrop. For DELFI and Artemyx, Natera's multi-cancer blood test got F-D-A Breakthrough Device designation on Friday, off data in twelve cancer types, with no performance numbers released. That puts Natera alongside Grail in the race for an F-D-A-backed multi-cancer screen. City Therapeutics and Iambic have set terms for their own offerings, so the I-P-O queue is now four deep. Kyverna presents its full stiff person syndrome CAR-T dataset at the joint ACTRIMS and ECTRIMS meeting in Toronto, October twenty-first through twenty-third, which is the next data point on autoimmune cell therapy for Winnow.

That's the Portfolio Watch for Saturday. Enjoy the weekend, Blackbird team.
