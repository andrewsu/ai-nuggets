## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Wednesday, November twenty-fifth, and the day's most important development for the portfolio is an approval that answers the question we keep hitting on the chronic-infections theme: where does the exclusivity come from when the molecule is decades old and the whole idea is delivery?

Start there.

On Tuesday the F-D-A approved PolyPid's Bacfenssi to reduce surgical-site infections in colorectal surgery. It is a doxycycline paste placed into the wound at closure, built on the company's Kynatrix technology, designed to release the antibiotic locally for about thirty days. The pivotal Phase three, SHIELD two, enrolled about eight hundred patients in its primary analysis, all with incisions longer than twenty centimeters. The composite primary endpoint — infection, death from any cause, or a reintervention — fell from eighteen point one percent on standard of care to ten point nine percent, a forty percent relative reduction. Infections alone fell from nine and a half percent to three point eight. Azurity will commercialize in the U-S, with launch guided to early twenty twenty-seven, into a footprint the company puts at more than seventeen hundred hospitals.

Avi, Hemaka — this is the second example in two days of the same structure, and the pairing is what makes it worth your time. Yesterday we talked about Arikayce inside the Sanofi deal: a sixty-year-old antibiotic class made valuable by being put into a liposome and inhaled, so it reaches the place the bacteria actually live. Today it is a tetracycline that has been generic for decades, made into an approved product by placing it where the infection starts and holding it there for a month. Neither is a discovery play. Both are delivery and exposure plays. That is the thesis under our chronic-infections sourcing, and it now has two priced examples in one week.

The regulatory economics are the part I want in the file. Bacfenssi was approved through the five-oh-five-b-two route, which lets a sponsor lean on what is already known about an old drug instead of rebuilding it from scratch. It carried Breakthrough Therapy, Fast Track and Priority Review. And it held the Qualified Infectious Disease Product designation under the GAIN Act, which adds five years of exclusivity on top of the three years the new clinical work earns — eight years in total, on a molecule nobody could patent. That is the answer to the question Eddie asks every time one of these comes up: what stops a generic shop from copying it? The exclusivity comes from the infection designation and the formulation, not the chemistry. Any re-delivery program we consider on this theme should be designed to qualify for that designation from the start, because it is worth more than the composition-of-matter position we will not have.

Two cautions from the same release, both of which we have seen before in other programs. First, the label follows the subgroup. The indication is specifically incisions longer than twenty centimeters, and the label says efficacy has not been established at twenty centimeters or less. That is the label-width lesson again, and this time it is in an approved product, not a hypothetical. Design the trial around the population where the mechanism dominates, and expect the label to end exactly there. Second, the money gate is still open. The hospital add-on payment application is under C-M-S review with a decision expected in August twenty twenty-seven — eight months after launch. A hospital-administered product that competes with "do nothing extra" lives or dies on that payment. If we model a surgical or hospital-delivered anti-infective, the revenue curve does not start at approval; it starts at the payment decision, and there is a gap in between the company has to fund.

Second item, for Artemyx.

Tagomics, a University of Birmingham spinout in the U-K, closed a thirteen-million-dollar seed extension on Tuesday, bringing total funding to about twenty-five million. The product is LiquiPath, launched in August: a cell-free D-N-A blood test that traces fragments back to the tissue they came from, using methylation read against cell-type-specific reference atlases. The use case is not cancer screening. It is drug safety — detecting organ injury during drug development, in animals and in people. The company says its early-access program is oversubscribed, with projects running at six of the world's largest pharmaceutical companies. The round was co-led by the venture arm of Werfen, the diagnostics company, with Illumina Ventures among the participants, and two investor board seats added.

Virginia, Esther, Eddie — two reads. The first is uncomfortable, because this is our own business-development argument executed by someone else. Since September we have said Artemyx has to be inside more than one drug sponsor's protocol-design conversation, not only in front of clinicians. Tagomics skipped the clinical market entirely at this stage and sold organ-injury monitoring straight to sponsors. Think about why that works: a pharma-funded study needs no reimbursement code, no device clearance, and no guideline committee. It is the fastest revenue an organ-of-origin cell-free D-N-A platform can book, and it generates exactly the performance data you later need for a clinical claim. Six large sponsors running projects at seed stage is a real demand signal. The question for the Artemyx plan is whether a sponsor-facing safety or drug-response module should come before the clinical claim rather than after it.

The second read is competitive and specific. Tagomics reads tissue of origin from methylation patterns. Artemyx and Delfi read tissue and disease signal from fragmentation patterns. A pharma safety team will eventually ask which approach tells them which organ is injured, how early, and with what false-positive rate. Note also that Tagomics stresses its platform works across species — and in toxicology the first readout is always in animals. A cross-species claim is a genuine commercial advantage in that buyer's workflow, because it lets the same assay follow a molecule from preclinical into the clinic. If our fragmentomics approach cannot make that claim, we should know it before a sponsor makes the comparison for us. Candid limits: no performance data came out with this financing, it is a seed-stage company, and the lung-cancer asset they are also funding is pre-clinical. This is a market signal, not a validated competitor.

Third item, and it is a regulatory change that touches several files at once.

Since Monday, the F-D-A requires that patient labeling — the information sheet that goes home with the prescription, not the prescribing information written for physicians — state when a drug was approved under the Accelerated Approval Program, with a link to a page explaining what that means. Until now that fact lived only in the professional label. Industry pushed back on this for years, arguing it would confuse patients.

Jon, Anthony — the read is not about the sentence; it is about what the sentence does to the commercial plan. Several of the comparators we track are accelerated-approval assets, and the whole portfolio-construction argument for a surrogate-endpoint path assumes that an approval behaves like an approval in the market. Now every patient starting one of these drugs reads, on the leaflet, that the evidence is provisional. That raises the cost of a confirmatory trial that runs late, because the gap between "approved" and "confirmed" is no longer invisible to the person taking the drug. It also puts a question into every physician conversation that a sales team now has to answer. For any program where we are considering a surrogate endpoint, add two lines to the plan: who finishes the confirmatory study and when, and what the field force says when a patient asks about that sentence. This is cheap to answer now and expensive to answer in year two.

Two quick ones to close.

Eddie, Emily — Kymera priced its follow-on Monday night: one point seven five billion dollars, upsized, at about one hundred fifty dollars a share, with the offering closing today. That is an extraordinary raise off a Phase two-b readout, and it sets the tone for the window Winnow will walk into. Clean oral data in immunology is being funded at a scale we have not seen this year. The corollary is the one we have now made four times in a week: the bar for what counts as clean keeps moving.

And a landscape note on the in vivo cell-therapy lane we have been tracking all week. Legend Biotech's dual-targeting CD nineteen / CD twenty in vivo CAR-T, cleared by the F-D-A in October, is moving into its Phase one-two, and the trade press this week added Pfizer's new licensing position in the same modality. Taken with Monday's first human data from CARsgen, that is a lane going from preclinical argument to clinical competition inside a single quarter. Nothing in it changes a Winnow decision today, but it is the backdrop for the Series A conversation.

That is the watch. PolyPid gives us the exclusivity math for re-delivering an old anti-infective — eight years from a designation rather than from chemistry — plus a reminder that the money starts at the reimbursement decision, not the approval. Tagomics shows the sponsor-first route for an organ-of-origin blood test, with six big pharmas already paying. And the accelerated-approval sentence is now on the patient leaflet, which makes confirmatory-trial discipline a commercial issue rather than a regulatory one. Talk tomorrow.
