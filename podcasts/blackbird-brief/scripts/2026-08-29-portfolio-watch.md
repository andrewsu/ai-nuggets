Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Saturday, August twenty-ninth. Nine days ago I told you that Merck and Moderna's Phase 3 win validated the premise sitting under Clasp and under Adventris — teach T cells to see a tumor-specific mutation and outcomes improve. Yesterday BioNTech and Genentech killed the other trial testing that premise. The two results together are far more useful than either alone, because the variable that separates them is not the platform. It is whether you ask the vaccine to do the killing by itself.

Here is what happened. BioNTech and Genentech terminated the Phase 2 trial of autogene cevumeran — their individualized messenger-R-N-A immunotherapy, built per patient against up to twenty of that patient's own tumor mutations — in resected high-risk stage two and stage three colorectal cancer. Every patient had already had surgery, already had chemotherapy, and still tested positive for circulating tumor D-N-A, meaning measurable disease was left behind. The vaccine went in alone, against watchful waiting. The independent data safety monitoring board found a numerical imbalance in overall survival between the arms and concluded further follow-up would not change the efficacy outcome. No new safety signal. The stock fell about seven percent.

Two things about that. The companies did not disclose which direction the survival imbalance ran, and I am not going to pretend otherwise — but a monitoring board does not stop a trial over an imbalance that favors the drug. And the futility boundary was crossed in October of last year. The board looked at it then, called the data immature, and let the trial run ten more months.

Now put it next to the Merck and Moderna result. Same class of medicine. That one won, in resected melanoma, on top of Keytruda. This one lost, in colorectal, with nothing underneath it. The failure isolates priming as the weak step. Raising neoantigen-specific T cells is the part this field can apparently do; getting them to find and kill residual disease in a tumor that already survived surgery and chemotherapy is the part that needs a checkpoint inhibitor released underneath it.

That splits our two positions in opposite directions. For Adventris, downstairs in the BioHub, this is uncomfortable, because the failed design is structurally their design — a mutant-K-RAS peptide vaccine, given alone, into minimal residual disease, against surveillance. Notice too that this trial did the thing we have been telling ourselves is protective: it selected patients on circulating tumor D-N-A, two gates instead of one, and selection did not save it. Eddie, when you next sit with Adventris, the questions are what combination data exist and what the comparator arm is going to be.

For Clasp it runs the other way, and this is a better sentence than the one I gave you on the twentieth. A T-cell engager does not ask the patient to mount a response. It supplies the bridge — one arm on the mutant peptide displayed on H-L-A, one arm on C-D-three — and drags a T cell to the tumor cell. If the step that just failed in public is priming, then Clasp's modality deletes the step that failed. Hemaka, Virginia, Anthony: that belongs in Clasp's partnering conversations this quarter, and it belongs there worded as narrowly as I just worded it. The vaccine result does not prove engagers work. It tells you where the class breaks, and Clasp is not built on that joint.

Second item, and it is the same lesson wearing different clothes. AstraZeneca and Ionis reported the big outcome trial for eplontersen in transthyretin cardiomyopathy on Friday — over fourteen hundred patients, a hundred and forty weeks — and it missed. The explanation is the part to keep. Eighty-one percent of patients here were already on a stabilizer, against fifty-three percent in Alnylam's earlier positive trial. The drug worked — silencing transthyretin does what it says on the label.
