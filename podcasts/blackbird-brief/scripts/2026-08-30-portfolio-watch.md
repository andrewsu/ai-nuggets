## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Sunday, August thirtieth. The F-D-A put a gene therapy on clinical hold this week for something nobody was looking for — structural findings on spine imaging in patients dosed three to six years ago, all of them symptom-free, all of them doing fine clinically. That is the item that matters most to Aletira, and it matters for a reason that has nothing to do with the platform and everything to do with how long the risk window stays open.

Here is what happened. REGENXBIO, up the road in Rockville, announced a regulatory update on R-G-X one twenty-one, its A-A-V gene therapy for Hunter syndrome, a lysosomal storage disease where children lack an enzyme and the disease takes the brain. The therapy is delivered directly into the central nervous system, into the cerebrospinal fluid, by intracisternal or intraventricular injection. Under enhanced imaging surveillance the company had put in place after an earlier issue, radiologists found small nodules or cystic masses on spine M-R-I in five participants dosed three to six years earlier. The findings are asymptomatic. Radiologists called them likely benign. No brain abnormalities. All five participants continue to do well and are stable or improved on neurocognitive testing. The F-D-A placed the program on clinical hold anyway. The company will not resubmit its marketing application in the near term, and the stock fell more than twenty percent.

Note what preceded it. In January the agency held both this program and its sister program for a different storage disease, after a central nervous system tumor turned up in a patient on that sister trial. In February the marketing application was rejected. In April a partial hold came off. In June the agency agreed to review a resubmission on existing data. In August, a new hold on a different signal — four regulatory events in eight months on a program whose clinical data are, by the company's account, fine.

Three reads, and Geoff Lynn, Hugh Wells, Avi — the third is the one I want acted on.

First, and be precise about this, because it would be easy to over-read: nothing here implicates cell-type-selective expression. Nodules in the spinal canal after intrathecal A-A-V are most plausibly about vector dose, capsid, route and where transduced cells end up — not about whether the transgene was correctly restricted once it got there. SELEXON is not the answer to this problem, and I do not want anybody putting a slide up that says it is. Claiming your platform solves a toxicity it does not address is the fastest way to lose a technical diligence session.

Second, the real lesson is the shape of the risk, and it lands directly on our lead indication. This signal appeared three to six years after dosing, in patients who felt nothing, and only because somebody was doing the imaging. Aletira's lead indication is congenital hearing loss. Skylark's competing trial enrolls children between nine months and seven years old for a single unilateral intracochlear injection. Those patients have sixty, seventy, eighty years of follow-up in front of them. A one-shot genetic medicine into a pediatric compartment does not have a safety readout at twelve months — it has a safety readout that never closes. Skylark has guided preliminary data by year-end, and we have already said in this brief that the primary endpoint there is safety only, in ten children. What this week adds is that a clean twelve-month safety report in a first-in-human pediatric gene therapy tells you materially less than the market will price it as telling you. Do not over-read their good news this winter, and do not over-brace for their bad news.

Third, the actionable one. REGENXBIO found this because it had implemented enhanced M-R-I surveillance after the January event — the monitoring plan is what surfaced it, and the monitoring plan is also what made the company look responsive rather than surprised. For a preclinical program, that is a cheap decision made early and an expensive one made late. So the ask is concrete: does the Aletira development plan already specify long-term structural imaging and a decades-long follow-up registry for the cochlea and the auditory pathway, and is that written into the pre-I-N-D package rather than added after somebody's agency asks? And the second half of it, Eddie — that plan is a diligence asset in a Series A, not just a regulatory cost. Every investor who has watched the last eight months of A-A-V holds is now pricing long-tail safety, and a program that has already thought about a thirty-year monitoring obligation reads differently from one that has not.

Second item, and it belongs to Winnow. On the twenty-fourth the F-D-A approved Imaavy, nipocalimab, from Johnson and Johnson, as the first treatment ever approved for warm autoimmune hemolytic anemia — a disease where the patient's own antibodies destroy their red blood cells. The mechanism is a blocker of the neonatal F-c receptor, which is the recycling system that keeps immunoglobulin G in circulation. Block it and pathogenic antibodies get cleared faster. In the Phase two-three trial, one hundred fifteen patients, roughly twenty-four percent on the approved dose achieved a durable hemoglobin response by twenty-four weeks against about eight percent on placebo, with median first response at four weeks against twelve.

Now, this is an antibody-mediated disease and Winnow works on T-cell-mediated autoimmunity, so do not stretch the analogy. The read is about the axis, not the target. Yesterday I flagged the dermatomyositis approval as handing Winnow both a market-size answer and a whitespace, because that drug delivers its value through broad kinase inhibition carrying a five-item boxed warning. Set this week's second approval beside it, because it makes the same point from the other side: Johnson and Johnson's own framing for nipocalimab is that it selectively reduces pathogenic immunoglobulin G while preserving broader immune function. Two first-ever approvals in rare autoimmune disease in one week — one selling potency with a boxed warning, one selling selectivity as the product. Selectivity is now an approvable, marketable and commercially rewarded axis in rare autoimmunity, not an academic preference. Jon Robbins, that is the second half of the target product profile row I asked for yesterday, and it is a stronger position than the one I described then.

There is also a candid disease-size note underneath both. Warm autoimmune hemolytic anemia runs one to three new cases per hundred thousand a year, dermatomyositis about one per hundred thousand. Companies are winning first-ever approvals in populations of that size and analysts are still modeling billion-dollar franchises. That is the empirical answer to the objection that antigen-selective autoimmunity indications are too small to build on, and it is worth having the citation ready rather than the argument.

Third, briefly, and it is a calendar item rather than a result. At the European Society of Cardiology meeting in Munich this morning, AstraZeneca presented the Phase two-b trial of its oral relaxin receptor agonist in chronic heart failure — roughly three hundred sixty patients, twenty-four weeks, three doses against placebo, split into a reduced-ejection-fraction cohort measured on end-systolic volume index and a mildly-reduced cohort measured on systemic vascular resistance index. That is the bar. The full numbers are not public yet; the trade coverage that landed this morning reads it as improving cardiac function in some patients but not all, and I am not going to characterize it further until the data are readable.

What I will say is what makes it relevant. Cardiac fibrosis is one of our named pre-spinout themes, and relaxin is the canonical antifibrotic hormone — which is exactly why this receptor keeps drawing capital and keeps disappointing. The injectable version failed in acute heart failure years ago. AstraZeneca's own peptide agonist was dropped for efficacy. This oral molecule is the class's third act, and notice what every one of those trials measured: hemodynamics, over weeks to months. Nobody has run relaxin biology against a fibrosis endpoint on a fibrosis timescale. So the durable read for Anthony and Jon, whatever the numbers turn out to say, is that this receptor has not actually been tested as an antifibrotic, and any Blackbird program pitching antifibrotic mechanism needs an endpoint and a duration that can register fibrosis — not a surrogate that reports vascular tone at twenty-four weeks and gets scored as a mechanism failure.

Last item, and it is infrastructure, but the useful kind. The National Science Foundation awarded the University of Maryland seventeen point three million dollars over four years to build an artificial-intelligence-enabled, remotely programmable automated biomanufacturing facility — one of twenty nodes in a roughly four-hundred-million-dollar national network. It will sit at the Institute for Bioscience and Biotechnology Research in Rockville, which is a joint venture of University of Maryland College Park, U-M-B, and the National Institute of Standards and Technology. Ginkgo Bioworks is deploying eleven robotic units and the software. Researchers anywhere can program experiments remotely, thirty biopharmaceutical companies get initial access, and there is a workforce hub attached drawing on nine University System of Maryland institutions.

Two things for us. It touches a partner institution directly — U-M-B is a co-owner of the host institute, so this sits inside the relationship we already have. And it answers a real objection: when we tell a founder the company has to be built in Baltimore, what they push back on is process development and manufacturing capacity. Maisha, Emily — this belongs in the Baltimore-nexus deck with the access mechanism spelled out. Yixuan, the question for U-M Ventures this week is what the actual path is for a Blackbird BioHub company to get instrument time, and what it costs.

Nulls, quickly. No G-P-R fifty-two item. Nothing on hearing-loss gene therapy from Skylark, Sensorion or Lilly since the eleventh. Clasp, Adventris, NuGate, Artemyx and Biolinco all silent. No mention anywhere of Blackbird Laboratories or Blackbird BioVentures. And the weekend is genuinely quiet — the trade press has produced almost nothing net-new since Friday afternoon, which is normal for a Sunday and not a gap in the sweep.

So, the through-line. The gene therapy hold is a reminder that in a one-shot medicine given to a child, the safety question does not have a closing date, and the company that plans for that reads better than the one that reacts to it. The two autoimmune approvals say selectivity now sells. And the relaxin readout is a reminder that the wrong endpoint can retire a correct mechanism. I'm the Blackbird Brief. Back tomorrow.
