## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Sourcing Radar for Sunday, November fifteenth. Two picks this week, both read in full, both with a Hopkins senior author, and both of them are about the same uncomfortable idea: that the thing standing between a good mechanism and a usable therapy is not the target, it is the patient you are putting it into. The lead is a Hopkins engineering paper with a patent application already filed that says the T cells you collect from an older donor are mechanically broken in a specific, measurable, and — this is the part that matters — reversible way. The second is an antisense oligonucleotide that completely rescues an autism model where two different approved-style drugs failed, from Rick Huganir's department.

Start with the lead. It published Thursday in Nature Communications out of Jude Phillip's lab in Hopkins biomedical engineering, with Konstantinos Konstantopoulos and Sean Sun also on it, and with Chris Ward and Jeanine Ursitti from the U-M-B School of Medicine orthopedics group and Kimberly Stroka at College Park as collaborators. So this is a Hopkins-led, cross-institution Maryland paper, which is the shape we keep saying we want.

Here is the setup. T cells do three things when they patrol tissue: they migrate, they sense local chemical and physical cues, and they deform to squeeze through tight spaces. Everybody knows older people's immune surveillance degrades. Nobody has been able to say which of those three fails. The group profiled primary human T cells from fifty-seven healthy donors aged eighteen to ninety-three, with time-lapse imaging and single-cell analysis rather than population averages.

The first result is the one I would put on a slide. Measured the conventional way — average speed, average persistence, averaged across the population — aged T cells look fine. No overt difference. The defect only appears when you stop averaging. At single-cell resolution, aged cells migrate about as well as young ones but have lost the ability to deform, and have lost the ability to change their behavior in response to local cell density, to chemical gradients, and to physical cues. They move. They just do not respond to anything.

Then the part that makes this a company conversation rather than a biology conversation. They primed the aged cells acutely with culture medium of elevated viscosity — literally thicker fluid, a mechanical signal, no genetic modification and no drug. That recovered a young-like surveillance phenotype, concentrated in the CD4 memory population, and they traced the mechanism: higher CXCR4 expression, which is the receptor for S-D-F-one, plus increased membrane tension, more cortical F-actin, and more Arp3. So the cell rebuilds the machinery it needs to deform and sense, in response to a purely physical input.

Three reads, and the first is the commercial one.

Avi, Virginia — every autologous cell therapy in the portfolio conversation starts from apheresis of a sick, usually older patient, and the industry's answer to variable starting material has been to engineer the receptor harder. This says there is a second axis nobody is selling: condition the starting material. A viscosity priming step is a bioprocess change, not a new construct. It does not touch the C-A-R or the engager. It sits in the manufacturing suite and it is, on its face, cheap. Phillip, Dance, Amitrano and Konstantopoulos are co-inventors on a patent application related to this work, which tells you Hopkins Technology Ventures has already seen it, and it tells you the claim is probably on the method rather than on a molecule.

The second read is for diligence, and it applies to companies we do not own. If aged T cells are mechanically and sensorially impaired, then any cell-therapy potency assay that reports an average over the population will miss it — the paper demonstrates exactly that failure in its own bulk analysis. So when a cell therapy company shows us potency data, the question is whether the assay resolves single cells and whether the donors were age-stratified. A product that works beautifully on cells from a thirty-year-old volunteer and gets tested in seventy-year-olds has a problem that will not show up until the clinic. That is a free question to ask in a first meeting and it is now backed by a published, peer-reviewed result.

The candid limits, because they are real. The effect is transient, and the authors say so plainly — they do not know how long the primed state lasts, and sustaining it is listed as necessary follow-up work. They did not measure cytotoxicity at all, so there is no evidence yet that a primed cell kills better, only that it surveils better. Everything here is ex vivo human cells. And "elevated viscosity" is a lab condition; nobody has shown you can hold it through a manufacturing process. So this is not a company today. It is a platform claim with filed I-P, a named mechanism, and an obvious first customer, which is precisely the stage where we are supposed to be having the conversation. Esther — worth a call to the Phillip lab to ask what the patent claims cover and whether anyone has approached them.

Second pick, and it is a landscape read rather than a license, but the lesson travels.

Published Wednesday in Neuron, senior-authored by Shu-Ling Chiu at Academia Sinica with Rick Huganir from the Snyder Department of Neuroscience at Hopkins as co-senior author. I read the full text through the authors' own preprint of the same work.

The biology. There is a recurrent de novo mutation in GRIA1, the gene for the GluA1 subunit of the AMPA receptor, called A636T, found in children with autism and intellectual disability. It sits in a conserved gating motif in the third transmembrane helix. The team built a knock-in mouse. The mutation alone is sufficient to produce the core phenotypes — impaired sociability, impaired social novelty recognition, impaired spatial learning. And it causes something quite specific: the receptors stay calcium-permeable. Normally, during the second and third week of postnatal life, AMPA receptors switch from calcium-permeable to calcium-impermeable. In these mice they never make the switch, so the hippocampus sits under persistent calcium-driven excitotoxic stress. Dendrites atrophy, neurons die, microglia and astrocytes activate, and the CA1 layer thins — progressively, with age.

Now the therapeutic part, which is why this is in the brief. They first tried the obvious thing: block the receptor. Perampanel, which is an approved AMPA antagonist, and J-N-J-five-five-five-one-one-one-eight, a selective blocker of the hippocampus-enriched TARP gamma-eight-associated receptors. Both reduced cell death significantly. Neither restored the CA1 layer thickness. And perampanel, at a dose that worked, caused severe motor side effects. A blunt instrument on a receptor the whole brain depends on does part of the job and costs you the rest.

So they built an allele-specific antisense oligonucleotide — a gapmer that degrades the mutant transcript and leaves the normal allele alone. One intracerebroventricular dose at postnatal day two or three. It knocked the mutant transcript down to about a quarter of control with no effect on wild-type levels, and it rescued everything: cell death back to wild-type levels, neuroinflammation gone, dendritic complexity restored, sociability normalized, spatial memory improved.

There is a detail in there I want the team to hold onto, because it is a drug-development lesson disguised as a methods note. Their best oligo killed more than half the treated pups. The fix was not a new sequence — it was adding cytosine methylation at CpG motifs in the same oligo. Survival went back to control levels and, as a bonus, knockdown got deeper. The toxicity was a chemistry problem, not a target problem.

Hemaka, Anthony — the read for NuGate. We have been building the case that antisense in the central nervous system is a real modality with a real regulatory path, and this is another brick: allele-specific silencing, one dose, full rescue, in a dominant gain-of-function disease. But notice the two things that make this paper easy and our problems hard. The dose went in at postnatal day two, before pathology started — in human terms, before anyone knows the child is affected. And the rescue is of prevention, not reversal; nothing here says a damaged hippocampus recovers. Any program built on this logic needs newborn screening or carrier-driven prenatal diagnosis to deliver patients inside the window, and that is a diagnostics and policy problem sitting in front of a solved pharmacology problem.

On chain of title, be clear-eyed: this is Academia Sinica's program, funded by Taiwan's National Science and Technology Council, with Huganir's N-I-H grant supporting the Hopkins contribution. Huganir declares he is a scientific co-founder and advisory board member of Neumora and an advisory board member at MAZE. So there is no untouched Hopkins asset here to license. What there is, is visibility into what the Snyder department's leadership thinks is worth doing, and the standing note in our file still applies — we have no formal channel into that department, and this is the second Huganir-lab paper this quarter that lands next to a portfolio thesis.

That is the radar. A Hopkins engineering result with a filed patent saying the cells we collect from older patients are mechanically impaired and can be mechanically repaired, which is a manufacturing-step business nobody is selling; and an allele-specific oligo that fully rescues a neurodevelopmental disease in mice, where the binding constraint turns out to be finding the patient early enough rather than making the drug. Today's Portfolio Watch is in your feed alongside this, and its lead is the European regulator backing an anti-CD20 antibody in lupus while Roche quietly runs a T-cell engager behind it. Have a good Sunday.
