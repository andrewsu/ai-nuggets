## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Friday, November twentieth. Today's most important development for the portfolio: the congressional biotech commission has published its last big warning on China, and for the first time it names the NewCo model itself, the same vehicle we use to build companies, as the channel through which American venture capital is flowing to Chinese drug developers. Behind that, Regeneron has taken the myasthenia gravis convenience bar from once a week to four times a year, two days after the weekly drug arrived. Two items today, and both land on how we pitch.

Start with deal flow, because it touches every BioVentures conversation and, through yesterday's Pfizer deal, Winnow.

On Wednesday the National Security Commission on Emerging Biotechnology, the eleven-member panel Congress set up in twenty twenty-two that sunsets at the end of this year, released a five-page paper titled "Made Innovated in China." I read the paper itself. Three findings matter to us.

First, the commission analyzed fifty-seven deals between the fifteen largest drugmakers operating in the U.S. and forty-two Chinese biotech companies, from January twenty twenty-three through October of this year. Upfront payments are rising, and the deals are increasingly based on preclinical data. Big pharma is buying earlier in China, at exactly the stage where a Blackbird program sits.

Second, it describes an escalation ladder: a single-asset license, then a multi-asset license, then a co-development or co-commercialization deal built around the discovery platform itself. The commission's concern is that each rung teaches the Chinese partner how to develop, register and sell in the U.S. market.

Third, and this is the one that should make us sit up, it names the NewCo structure directly. A Chinese company licenses an asset to a new company backed by U.S. investors, takes an upfront and milestones, keeps equity, and often keeps mainland China rights. When that NewCo goes public or gets acquired, money flows back through equity and royalties. The commission's words: this "redirects U.S. capital away from U.S. innovation." It also flags that in October Beijing proposed export restrictions on certain advanced biopharma technologies, including proprietary drug discovery platforms, with exemptions for specified licensing agreements. Those rules are not final.

On policy, the commission endorsed a Treasury proposal to bring biotech under outbound investment restrictions, and its chair, Caitlin Frazer, said the deals "are starting to favor the Chinese startups." Separately, STAT reports that Merck, Lilly, AbbVie, Pfizer and Bristol Myers Squibb have told the House China committee they will not open new trial sites in Xinjiang or at hospitals linked to the Chinese military. BioPharma Dive counts more than a hundred and twenty licensing deals between Western and Chinese drug developers since the start of last year.

So what for Blackbird? Three reads.

The first is for Eddie and Emily. A China-sourced NewCo is the direct competitor for the same Series A dollars as a Johns Hopkins or U-M-B NewCo. It usually arrives with human data and a lower price, and that is why investors like it. What changed this week is that a congressional body has put that structure in writing as a national-security concern, and Treasury has a live proposal that could make it harder to fund. Be candid about the limits: this is a report, not a law, and the commission goes away in six weeks. But a syndicate partner who is weighing a China-licensed asset against one of ours now has a regulatory question about the first that the second doesn't carry. Where U.S. origin is true, say it out loud: U.S.-invented, U.S.-licensed, built in Baltimore. And add one line to the BioVentures diligence template for any company in-licensing from China: who holds equity, which rights were kept, and what happens to the deal if those export rules are finalized.

The second is for Jon and Anthony on Winnow. On Wednesday Pfizer agreed to pay Starna three hundred and thirty million dollars upfront, and yesterday we called it a platform deal in an asset deal's suit, because Pfizer also took rights to use Starna's technology across immunology and oncology. That is the top rung of the commission's ladder, and it is exactly the kind of platform Beijing's proposed rule is aimed at. We do not know how the licensing exemption will be drawn, so this is a watch item, not a threat we can quantify. But the best-funded in vivo competitor to Winnow's thesis now carries a supply and policy risk that a Baltimore-built T-cell engager doesn't.

The third is the sentence for Matt. Since August, when Genentech paid forty-five million upfront for a Chinese A-D-C platform, the comparison column for any Blackbird program in pharma business development has been "what could we license from China instead." That column is still there. What is new is that it now comes with a footnote written by Congress.

Now Winnow, and the myasthenia gravis bar.

On Wednesday the F-D-A approved Livsayis, cemdisiran, from Regeneron, discovered by Alnylam and licensed in twenty nineteen. It is a small interfering R-N-A that switches off the liver's production of complement C5. Adults with acetylcholine-receptor antibody-positive generalized myasthenia gravis inject it under the skin four times a year. It is the first R-N-A-interference drug approved in an autoimmune disease. The list price is four hundred and ten thousand dollars a year.

The phase three, called NIMBLE, read out at twenty-four weeks. On the daily-living scale, four and a half points of improvement against two point two on placebo, a placebo-adjusted two point three. About seventy-seven percent were responders against forty-four percent on placebo. On the clinician-scored scale, a placebo-adjusted two point eight. Benefit showed up as early as two weeks. Analysts are split: R-B-C expects a "relatively challenging launch environment" against entrenched products, while T-D Cowen models a billion and a half dollars at peak.

On Tuesday we covered AstraZeneca's gefurulimab, approved Monday, a weekly at-home autoinjector in the same indication, and called it the convenience bar where Kyverna's cell therapy is in a registrational trial. Two days after that approval, the bar is four injections a year. One caution: don't put the two point three next to gefurulimab's one point six on a slide. Those are different trials with different populations.

Here is the read for Jon and Virginia. Winnow cannot win on dosing frequency in myasthenia gravis, and that fight is now over. But read the label. It carries a boxed warning for serious meningococcal infection and a restricted-access program, and the patient guide tells people to carry a safety card for twelve months after the last dose, because the risk can persist for months once dosing stops. The convenience comes from durability, and durability means it cannot be switched off. That is the interruptibility argument we made yesterday about in vivo C-A-R T, coming from a completely different modality. A quarterly knockdown cannot be stopped quickly, and a C-A-R T made inside the body cannot be stopped at all. Winnow's pitch is a drug you can titrate and stop that still aims at deep, drug-free remission.

The economic read, for Emily: four hundred and ten thousand dollars a year, indefinitely, is what "control" is now priced at in this disease. A therapy that buys remission over a finite course gets compared against that number multiplied by years. Build the pricing story on that comparison, and pick first indications where nobody is yet offering four shots a year.

That's the watch for this week. Have a good weekend, Blackbird team.
