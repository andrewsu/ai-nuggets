## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Portfolio Watch for Saturday, August twenty-second, and the most useful thing that happened yesterday is a lesson in how you get a pain drug funded — because a molecule that already failed a U.S. Phase 3 just raised two hundred seventy-five million dollars in eight months and took a Nasdaq listing, having changed nothing about the drug and everything about who gets into the trial.

Werewolf Therapeutics, a Cambridge oncology company that has been running a strategic review since February, agreed to merge with a private San Diego company called Ambros Therapeutics. All-stock. Ambros takes the Nasdaq spot and the ticker, with a concurrent private placement of one hundred fifty million dollars, oversubscribed, co-led by R-A Capital and Janus Henderson. Ambros is valued at five hundred million, Werewolf at forty-seven and a half, and legacy Werewolf holders keep six point eight percent. That is on top of a one-hundred-twenty-five-million-dollar Series A in December — hence the two hundred seventy-five.

The asset is neridronate — an amino-bisphosphonate, a bone drug, licensed from Abiogen in Italy, where it has been approved for complex regional pain syndrome type one since twenty fourteen and given to something like six hundred thousand patients across its indications. Type one is an orphan pain disorder, roughly sixty-five thousand new U.S. cases a year, and there is no F-D-A-approved treatment.

Now the part that matters. Grünenthal already tried this, with the same molecule. They took it into a U.S. registrational program, won the same Breakthrough designation, and ran two Phase 3 trials. In twenty nineteen an independent statistician looked at a pre-planned pooled interim and called futility — the separation from placebo could not clear even a three-tenths-of-a-point threshold on an eleven-point pain scale. Both trials stopped. No U.S. approval.

I put the protocols side by side this morning. The cumulative dose is the same: four hundred milligrams intravenous, four infusions over ten days. The primary endpoint is the same: change from baseline in average pain intensity at week twelve. Everything that changed is the entry door.

Grünenthal enrolled the syndrome broadly, up to two years since onset, no subtype restriction, no imaging requirement, and required patients to have failed at least two prior treatments — a chronic, refractory, heterogeneous population across eight countries. Ambros enrolls type one only, single limb, within six months of onset, warm subtype only — edema plus at least two of redness, a one-degree temperature rise, or moderate-to-severe swelling — and requires increased uptake on a centrally read triple-phase bone scan. It excludes type two, excludes cases with no identified inciting event, excludes anyone scoring forty or above on the Pain Catastrophizing Scale, and runs an electronic-diary compliance gate during screening. Two hundred seventy patients, twenty-four U.S. sites, enrolling now, topline twenty twenty-eight.

Five enrichment gates, on an unchanged drug at an unchanged dose against an unchanged endpoint. And a syndicate that knew about the first failure paid five hundred million for the second attempt.

Three reads, and the first is for the gut program. Since the Fibrx deal we have been saying that inflammation and fibrosis in Crohn's are spoken for and pain is the last unstaked axis, and that Barbara Slusher's gut-restricted G-C-P-two program has to lead on it. This tells us what staking it costs, and the currency is not mechanism. Nobody paid for novelty here — it is a decades-old bisphosphonate. They paid for a stratification package. So the question for Hemaka and the Slusher team is not whether G-C-P-two inhibition relieves visceral pain in a mouse. It is: which inflammatory bowel disease patients, identified by what objective measure, before dosing? If there is an enrichable subpopulation — a visceral-hypersensitivity phenotype, an imaging or biomarker readout, a disease-duration window — that is the registration path, and it belongs in the program plan now, not after a Phase 2 misses on an all-comers population.

Be candid about the limits. Neridronate works on bone resorption and has nothing to do with the enteric nervous system, and an orphan indication with no approved therapy is a far cleaner setup than pain in Crohn's, where every patient is already on an approved anti-inflammatory and you have to show something on top of it. What transfers is the trial architecture and the financing, not the biology.

The second read is for Eddie. This is the second reverse merger into a Nasdaq shell in eight days, after Skye and Redx became Fibrx on the fourteenth, in a year that has now seen roughly eighteen of them — the most since twenty eighteen. And this one prices the template: Werewolf at forty-seven and a half million, legacy holders at six point eight percent. The shell is cheap. For a Phase-two-ready Baltimore asset that does not want to run a conventional offering, that arithmetic is now visible on two deals in one week.

The third read is the uncomfortable half, and it is about Werewolf itself. Werewolf was a platform company — conditionally activated cytokines, an interleukin-two and an interleukin-twelve engineered to switch on inside the tumor. Precision delivery in oncology, a first cousin to how several things in this portfolio describe themselves. It could not get a clinical asset over the line and became a listing vehicle worth six point eight percent of the thing that took its ticker. The read for Clasp is not alarming, it is clarifying: the platform story is not what gets funded right now. The GUARDIAN-101 monotherapy readout is.

Second item, and it is a calendar item: today is Capricor's action date for deramiocel in Duchenne. Recall the sequence — an advisory committee voted nine to three against on July twenty-ninth, but voted on cardiac function, a secondary endpoint, not on upper-limb function, which was the primary and which the trial hit. The company then said it would amend its filing with twenty-four-month extension data and narrow the indication to the endpoint it actually hit, which may push the date.

For Aletira, this is the test of the framing we have been using since the eighteenth: an agency rigorous on evidence and flexible on path, one that rewards sponsors for narrowing claims to what they can prove. If F-D-A takes the amendment and lets Capricor narrow, that is the strongest available evidence for the sentence under our expression-control pitch — a platform restricting expression to the cells that need it, sold into a regulator that rewards restriction. Watch today and Monday; Ultragenyx printed four days early this week, so a weekend date does not mean nothing happens.

The second thing is about burn. Yesterday an investor called Kaos Capital went public demanding two board seats, a strategic alternatives committee, and a capital-preservation plan — the day before the action date. Capricor's cash went from three hundred eighteen million at year-end to two hundred thirty-eight million at the end of June. Eighty million in six months while waiting on a filed application.

Third, on the gene-therapy climate. Two House members, John Moolenaar and Ben Cline, formally asked F-D-A to stop accepting Chinese clinical data unless the trial sites have had a recent F-D-A audit, and to review products already approved on the strength of it. The trigger is three deaths — two we covered on the seventh, and a third that is new here: a systemic sclerosis patient who died after an in-vivo C-A-R-T therapy from RiboX, disclosed August ninth, after F-D-A had already cleared a U-S application in that program.

The line that matters for us is the mechanism now stated in public for the Duchenne death: acute respiratory distress in the setting of severe complement and cytokine activation, following high-dose systemic administration of an adeno-associated virus vector. That is the cleanest external statement I have seen of exactly the sentence Hugh and Avi were asked to tighten on Thursday. The immune problem in systemic A-A-V is the capsid and the dose. It is not the cassette. Do not let anyone in a pitch imply SELEXON would have prevented that death — a sharp investor pulls the thread, and the platform loses credibility on the claim that is actually true. What it does support is the beachhead: a local micro-dose into a small, semi-enclosed compartment does not pay that tax at all. And for Eddie, the policy wind is blowing toward data provenance, so an in-licensing package leaning on Chinese investigator-initiated data is now a diligence line item.

Fourth, on 1104health, and I owe you a correction. Yesterday I said Friday was the last business day to get onto the Inspector General docket. That was wrong — the comment period closes Monday at five p.m. Eastern, so there is one working morning left, not zero. I re-pulled the docket this morning: still eight comments, most recent still August fourteenth, Massive Bio still on it, 1104health still not.

The rest is short and mostly null. No G-P-R fifty-two news, and Nxera still has no named buyer. Otsuka scrapped an early psychedelic trial from its Mindset acquisition — worth a mention only because Otsuka sits on the short list of specialty neuroscience buyers for that asset, and a company trimming early neuro bets is not the obvious one to write a large check for a Phase-two-ready program. One discontinued trial is thin evidence, so hold it loosely. Nothing from CAMP4 since the quarter on the thirteenth; the September twenty-eighth analyst day and fourth-quarter first dosing both stand. No fresh mutant-K-R-A-S catalyst for aSKY, no Clasp or Adventris update, nothing from Skylark or Sensorion. Ultragenyx's Sanfilippo decision is still September nineteenth. Zero mentions of Blackbird anywhere.

To close. A drug that failed once for futility is now worth five hundred million because somebody built a door narrow enough that only likely responders walk through it, and that — not a novel target — is what a fundable pain program looks like. Capricor finds out today or Monday whether the agency really does reward narrowing. And there is one working morning left on a docket a competitor got onto three weeks ago. I'll see you tomorrow, with a Sourcing Radar.
