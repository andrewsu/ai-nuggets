## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Sourcing Radar for Sunday, August thirtieth. Three leads this week, and the first one comes with an uncomfortable footnote: it is the best translational platform paper out of Johns Hopkins in the window, and by the time it posted, the intellectual property was already licensed and the company was already hiring in Maryland. So the lead is not a licensing lead. It is a question about how fast we see things.

Start with the science. Annie Kathuria's lab in Biomedical Engineering at Johns Hopkins, with a Neurosurgery affiliation and with Genevieve Stein-O'Brien on the spatial work, posted a preprint on Wednesday describing something they call the Neural Impact Score.

Here is the setup. Ninety percent of drugs that clear animal testing fail in humans, and central nervous system programs are worse — the paper cites a ninety-nine point six percent failure rate for Alzheimer's candidates. Every regulator in the developed world is now pushing human tissue models as replacements for animals, and brain organoids are the obvious candidate. The problem is that organoids generate enormous multimodal datasets and nobody has agreed what a good answer looks like. You run sequencing, proteomics and electrophysiology on the same treated tissue and get three separate stories, with no way to say whether the compound moved the tissue in a good direction or a bad one.

The Neural Impact Score is the answer. It takes every readout and returns a signed number in each of four fixed categories — neurodevelopment, neuroinflammation, neurodegeneration, and cellular resilience. Positive means the tissue moved the way you want. The categories are locked, so the same rules run against any dataset without retraining.

The substrate is where the technical depth sits: a vascularized human stem-cell-derived organoid containing cortical, midbrain and brainstem lineages together with an endothelial compartment, aged two hundred days, its cortical compartment matching a mid-gestational human window. Five mechanistically distinct compounds went through it — a G-L-P one agonist, bupropion, fluoxetine, fingolimod, and an Akt activator. The G-L-P one agonist scored highest in the panel on resilience while scoring negative on inflammation and degeneration, which is exactly the split verdict a single composite ranking would hide and a program team needs.

Then the result that makes it a platform rather than an assay. They scored external rodent datasets with the same locked framework. For bupropion and fluoxetine, agreement between the human organoid and rat brain at the level of individual genes was near chance. But the category-level scores agreed in sign. That is the whole commercial argument in one experiment: gene-by-gene concordance was always the wrong benchmark, and interpretation-level agreement survives where gene-level agreement does not. For the G-L-P one comparison the two systems actually inverted, which they attribute to the organoid's endothelial compartment — those agonists act on vascular endothelium, and a neuron-focused rodent dataset cannot see that by construction.

Now the footnote. Kathuria is a co-founder and equity holder of Organotics, which has already licensed this technology from Johns Hopkins, and she and two of her students are inventors on a Hopkins provisional application covering it. Organotics launched in July, is Maryland-based, is hiring artificial-intelligence scientists and research technicians here, and is pointed at schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, major depression, post-traumatic stress, and Alzheimer's in women. Funding is not disclosed.

So read it three ways. This is not a Labs project — it is a BioVentures conversation and possibly a BioHub tenancy conversation, and Eddie, that makes it your first read rather than Esther's. There is also a services angle worth more than it sounds: if the Lieber program needs a human-tissue liability screen ahead of a development-candidate declaration, there is now a Maryland company whose entire product is exactly that, in overlapping indications. And Virginia, this is your remit precisely — an artificial-intelligence-plus-human-tissue platform out of Hopkins Biomedical Engineering, spun out in July, with the technical paper landing in August. I am not asserting we missed it. I am asking whether we saw it, and if not, what sensor should have fired on a July company formation and did not.

Be candid about the science too. Five compounds, all approved drugs or tool molecules, in healthy control tissue, with hand-defined categories. Nobody has shown this score predicts a clinical outcome, and until somebody does the cross-species result is unfalsifiable in the direction that matters: when human and rodent disagree, the framework cannot tell you which is right. So the diligence ask is specific — score a blinded set of compounds with known, divergent human and animal outcomes, and show it would have called them.

Second lead, out of the Lieber Institute. Keri Martinowich's group, with Stephanie Hicks in Johns Hopkins biostatistics and Thomas Hyde and Joel Kleinman on the tissue, published a spatial transcriptomic atlas of the human amygdala — nine neurotypical donors, over one point one million cells, three spatial platforms stitched together, thirteen domains resolved across the basolateral complex, the central and medial and cortical nuclei, and the intercalated islands. Three transcriptionally distinct intercalated neuron populations, two of them forming physically separate islands, which is genuinely new anatomy in a structure people have been drawing for a century.

Then they localized psychiatric genetic risk onto it, using two methods that disagree. The conservative one, which partitions heritability spatially, smeared risk across all gray matter with essentially no subnuclear specificity — and the authors are admirably honest that schizophrenia topped the traits only because its genome-wide association study is the most powered, not because the amygdala is especially schizophrenic. The expression-based method finds real structure: the basomedial and lateral nuclei rank most enriched across donors and traits, with the schizophrenia signal in the lateral driven largely by neurogranin.

So the specificity claim is method-dependent, and anyone pitching this will meet exactly that objection. Which is why I am calling it substrate rather than a lead. You cannot license an atlas into a company, the donors are all neurotypical, there are no cases and no controls, so this localizes risk that was already known rather than nominating anything new. Its value to us is as a filter downstream of the target-generation work the Lieber group is already doing, and the question for Hemaka is whether the next atlas in the series is being chosen against a program or against a grant. Interests are clean.

Third, and this one is a real build candidate. Som Chatterjee at U-M-B, with Vance Fowler at Duke and Ben Howden's group in Melbourne, on antibiotic tolerance in staph aureus. Tolerance is not resistance: a tolerant strain survives prolonged exposure to a drug it is formally susceptible to, so the standard test in every hospital microbiology lab reports it as treatable when it is not.

They pin the mechanism. An enzyme called G-D-P-P degrades a bacterial second messenger, cyclic di-A-M-P. Knock it out, the messenger accumulates, and the bug tolerates beta-lactams — the whole class, including the advanced cephalosporins used for complicated infections, and independent of the classical resistance genes. Specifically beta-lactams: not vancomycin, not daptomycin. Tolerance tracks the messenger concentration, so it is a dial rather than a switch, and in an insect infection model the mutant killed more animals than wild type under beta-lactam treatment.

Then the clinical half. Mining a completed bacteremia trial, they found five independent truncating mutations in that gene — every one during bloodstream infection, every one after beta-lactam exposure, a nine-fold enrichment over background, and zero events across six hundred sixty-three episodes of nasal colonization. In one patient, two independent mutations arose in the same episode. The mutation is selected by the treatment, in the bloodstream, and nowhere else.

Two commercial shapes, and I would pursue the first. As a diagnostic, this is a binary genotypic marker that says your beta-lactam is going to fail, and it appears during therapy, which is precisely where a test has to live to be worth anything. As a resensitizer it is thinner — bacitracin reverses the tolerance, but bacitracin is generic and systemically nephrotoxic, so that is a probe, not a product, and you cannot inhibit your way out of a problem caused by the loss of an enzyme. You would have to build against something downstream. The candid limit is that the clinical enrichment rests on five events, so the honest next step is a large retrospective sequencing cohort of bacteremia isolates before anyone writes a check. Funded by the National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases, the U-M-B group declares nothing, and the route is U-M Ventures. Chronic infections is a named program theme for us, and this is the first thing on it in weeks with both a mechanism and patient data.

A Johns Hopkins Bloomberg group posted a companion argument the same week from the opposite direction — fibrin barriers physically block vancomycin from reaching staph inside abscesses. One paper says the drug never arrives, the other says the bug does not care. If we build on this theme, that pair is the framing slide.

The rest of the sweep, quickly. Fourteen hundred forty-five bioRxiv preprints in the window, eighteen with a partner-institution corresponding author. A Johns Hopkins pathology tool for recovering unannotated splice junctions is interesting for neoantigen discovery, but the authors say the recovered models are partial reconstructions needing cloning to confirm — a method, not an asset. A chronic fibrosis paper from the Translational Tissue Engineering Center touches our cardiac fibrosis theme but is model characterization. Also passed: a ribosome regulator, an allostery study, and a Down syndrome phenotyping paper. Nothing usable on medRxiv.

So that is the radar. One platform we cannot license because someone already did — which makes it an investment call and a hard question about sourcing latency. One Lieber resource that is substrate rather than a company. And one U-M-B mechanism with patient data behind it, the best chronic-infections lead we have had this month. I'm the Blackbird Brief. I'll be back with the Portfolio Watch.
