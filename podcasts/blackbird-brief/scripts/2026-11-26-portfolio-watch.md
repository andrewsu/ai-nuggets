## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team, and happy Thanksgiving. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Thursday, November twenty-sixth. The most important development for the portfolio is a lupus readout from Bristol Myers Squibb: two near-identical Phase three trials of the same oral pill, one positive and one negative, with the company saying the difference was the placebo arm. It's a short one today. The holiday emptied the newswire, and three items is what cleared the bar.

Start with the lupus result, because it lands squarely on Winnow.

On Wednesday, Bristol Myers reported topline results from POETYK S-L-E one and POETYK S-L-E two, the pivotal program for Sotyktu, deucravacitinib, in moderate-to-severe systemic lupus. Sotyktu is an oral, selective inhibitor of TYK2, the kinase that sits under the interleukin-twelve, interleukin-twenty-three and type one interferon pathways. It is already approved in psoriasis and psoriatic arthritis. The two trials were designed alike: about five hundred ten and five hundred thirteen patients, three milligrams twice daily against placebo, both arms on top of background standard of care, with a primary endpoint of SRI-4 response at week fifty-two.

The first trial met its primary endpoint. It also hit significance on BICLA, on SRI-4 with a steroid taper, and on patient-reported fatigue. The other key secondaries were numerically better but not significant. The second trial missed. In the company's own words, Sotyktu showed "a level of response consistent with POETYK S-L-E one," but the primary endpoint "was not met due to a higher placebo response." Safety was clean in both trials, and discontinuations for adverse events were low. No response rates were released. Bristol says it will look at Phase two and Phase three together, take the package to health authorities, and present at a future meeting.

Three reads.

The first is for Jon and Virginia, and it confirms something we wrote into the Winnow file ten days ago. When we compared Beeline's afimetoran with Gazyva, the conclusion was that in lupus a cross-trial gap mostly measures the placebo arm. Now there's a within-program version of the same lesson. Same molecule, same dose, same design, same endpoint, roughly a thousand patients across two trials, and the result split on placebo. Phase two looked unambiguous: fifty-eight percent SRI-4 response on three milligrams twice daily against thirty-four percent on placebo at week thirty-two. That is the dose they took forward. Phase three didn't reproduce it twice. If a sponsor the size of Bristol can't guarantee separation on SRI-4 in add-on, non-refractory lupus with five hundred patients a trial, a company at Winnow's stage should not plan its first efficacy claim there.

The second read is about the competitive map. On the sixteenth we wrote that a partner looking at non-refractory lupus would soon see two things: a cheap depleting antibody with a European recommendation, and an oral pill heading into Phase three, both twenty to thirty points over placebo on SRI-4. Sotyktu was the more advanced oral in that picture. It isn't dead, since it has one positive pivotal, a positive Phase two and a regulator conversation ahead. But a one-for-two record is a harder filing, and the Morand quote in the release, "if approved, Sotyktu could be the first targeted oral treatment," now carries more weight on the word "if." Afimetoran still enters Phase three next year, so the oral threat to the mild end of the market is delayed, not removed. That does not change Winnow's positioning. It gives it more time.

The third read is the design lesson, and it belongs in the clinical-development plan now rather than at the I-N-D. Placebo response in lupus is driven by what's allowed in the background: steroids, antimalarials, immunosuppressants, plus how disease activity is scored at entry. The endpoint where Sotyktu still separated in the positive trial was SRI-4 with a steroid taper, which is the same family of strict index the MiSLE cell-therapy trial missed on last week. Our position has been that the strict index is our claim rather than our obstacle, because a depleting or resetting mechanism should show its advantage as time off steroids and depth of response, in refractory patients, where placebo has the least room to move. Today's result is the cleanest evidence yet for that choice. Build the first efficacy claim on depth and time off drug in refractory disease. Don't add another SRI-4 add-on trial to a field that has just shown it can't reliably read one.

One date for the calendar: Bristol says the Sotyktu Phase three in Sjögren's reads out in twenty twenty-seven. That is the next test of whether this mechanism separates in a systemic autoimmune disease outside the skin.

The second item is shorter, and it raises the convenience bar again.

Also on Wednesday, GSK reported that its Phase three OCEAN trial met its primary endpoint. OCEAN compared Exdensur, depemokimab, given twice a year, with GSK's own monthly Nucala, mepolizumab, in relapsing or refractory eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis, a rare vasculitis. The trial ran in a hundred sixty-two patients across nineteen countries and was designed for non-inferiority. The remission bar was demanding: no vasculitis activity on the Birmingham score and an oral steroid dose of four milligrams a day or less, at both week thirty-six and week fifty-two. Twice-yearly depemokimab was non-inferior to monthly mepolizumab, with similar adverse events. GSK plans filings in the first half of twenty twenty-seven. Depemokimab is an anti-interleukin-five antibody engineered for a long half-life. It is already approved in severe asthma, and GSK says it is being explored in COPD.

Two reads. For Winnow, this extends the convenience thread from Tuesday's episode. Kymera's oral STAT6 degrader showed that a pill can come close to Dupixent in eczema. GSK has now shown that an injectable given twice a year can match a monthly biologic on a steroid-sparing remission endpoint in an autoimmune vasculitis. Partners comparing chronic immunology products are going to be anchored on two injections a year or a daily pill. The pitch for an engager has to be about something neither of those offers, which is what we've been saying: depth, a reset, and time off drug entirely. "Fewer doses than the alternative" is no longer available as a pitch.

For the COPD theme, note that depemokimab is being studied in COPD. That puts a twice-yearly anti-I-L-five antibody into the type-two-high lane, where Sanofi already holds Dupixent. Any COPD program we look at should assume the eosinophil-high patient goes to a long-acting biologic. That pushes our interest toward patients those drugs don't serve, which is where we were heading anyway.

The third item is a deal, and it touches Clasp and the oncology surface-targets theme.

Bayer has licensed HS-20122 from Hansoh Pharma, worldwide outside Greater China. HS-20122 is a bispecific antibody-drug conjugate: one molecule that binds both E-G-F-R and c-MET and carries a cytotoxic payload. It's in Phase one in advanced solid tumors, led by non-small-cell lung cancer, and Hansoh presented early efficacy and safety data at E-S-M-O this year. The terms are a hundred fifty million dollars upfront and up to three point one billion in milestones, plus tiered royalties. The deal is subject to antitrust clearance.

Eddie, Hemaka, Avi — put this next to the AbbVie–DISCO deal from the nineteenth. DISCO got forty million dollars upfront against two and a half billion, about one point six percent, for a discovery platform that finds AND-gated target pairs. Hansoh gets a hundred fifty million against about three point two five billion, a little under five percent, for one dual-targeting molecule with human data. That's how the field prices the step from map to molecule to patient: upfront roughly triples once there is clinical activity.

The scientific read is the one Avi will care about. E-G-F-R and c-MET are both broadly expressed on normal epithelium. Johnson and Johnson's Rybrevant hits the same pair as a naked bispecific antibody. So the selectivity of a dual-targeting conjugate like this comes from density and avidity: tumors that overexpress both receptors bind it better. It is a preference, not a gate. Healthy tissue that carries both receptors at moderate levels is still in range, and with a payload attached that's where the toxicity will show up. Clasp's argument runs on a different axis entirely, a mutant peptide on a specific H-L-A type that normal cells simply don't present. The question for Clasp's partnering deck is whether a partner treats "two common antigens, preferentially co-expressed" as competition for "one antigen that only tumor cells have." It probably will at the business-development screen, so the deck should say plainly why a density gradient and a hard on-off switch fail differently. We made the same argument against TfR1 conjugates on the eighteenth: when there's a payload, the expression floor is the toxicity.

The practical read for Eddie: Bayer just paid real cash, a hundred fifty million upfront, to a Chinese originator for a Phase one asset, the same week a congressional panel urged curbs on exactly this kind of licensing. Big pharma's appetite for China-sourced clinical assets is not slowing down for the policy debate. For anything Clasp partners, the competitor in the room is often a Chinese Phase one dataset sold at that price.

That's the watch for today. To sum up: Sotyktu's split result makes the case for a strict-index, refractory-patient first claim for Winnow; twice-yearly dosing is now the convenience bar in chronic immunology; and the dual-targeting conjugate market is paying for human data, not maps. Enjoy the holiday, everyone. We'll be back tomorrow.
