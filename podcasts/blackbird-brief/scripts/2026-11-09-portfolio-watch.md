## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Monday, November ninth. The most important development for the portfolio today is a retrospective rather than a readout: three years on from the two biggest schizophrenia acquisitions in recent memory, the analysts covering them have concluded that nobody won, and the reason they give is tolerability in the real world rather than efficacy in the trial. That is a direct statement about how a buyer will price the G-P-R fifty-two company. After that, the FDA's rare-disease leadership has given its first substantive interview on the new framework, and it moves the patient voice from optional to expected while leaving the external-control question conspicuously open. Then a cardiovascular trial that just amended its own protocol because its imaging endpoint turned out to be more variable than the sponsor expected, which is a lesson in biomarker endpoints we should take for free. And a prostate cancer combination that gets its specificity from requiring two signals instead of one.

Start with the schizophrenia retrospective, because this one is about valuation and the whole team should hear it.

In December of 2023, within two weeks of each other, AbbVie bought Cerevel for eight point seven billion dollars and Bristol Myers Squibb bought Karuna for fourteen billion. Both deals were built on schizophrenia drugs acting on muscarinic receptors. Cerevel's lead, emraclidine, failed two mid-stage trials less than a year later. Karuna's drug reached the market as Cobenfy, the first genuinely new schizophrenia medicine in more than three decades. On paper that should have settled the argument. It has not.

Cobenfy brought in a hundred and fifty-five million dollars in 2025, less than half what some analysts had predicted, and it kept missing expectations through this year. Graig Suvannavejh at Mizuho put it plainly — you certainly do not want to pay fourteen billion dollars to get that kind of sales trajectory. And when asked why, he did not point at efficacy. He pointed at the side effects, saying the gastrointestinal adverse events are likely to be much worse in the real world than they looked in the Phase Three studies. Another analyst framed the same problem mechanistically: Cobenfy pairs a muscarinic agonist with a blocker meant to keep that agonist out of the gut, and the balance has to be exquisite, so patients vary a lot in how they tolerate it. AbbVie's side of the ledger, meanwhile, got a lift from a Parkinson's drug nobody was thinking about at the time of the deal, now approved as Juvmo — though William Blair projects it at one point four billion dollars in 2040 and says even several times that would not fully justify what AbbVie paid.

Three reads for the Lieber company, and I would put them in this order.

The first is for Hemaka and Maisha, and it is the one to put in the deck. The commercial failure mode in schizophrenia right now is not that the drug does not work. It is that patients will not stay on it. A first-in-class mechanism with a landmark approval and no competition has underperformed by more than half on tolerability. Our differentiation argument for G-P-R fifty-two has been tolerability — on weight, metabolic effects and sedation — plus the negative-symptom claim. This is the strongest external evidence yet that tolerability is not a secondary selling point in this category. It is the commercial variable.

The second is harder, and it is for Matt and Eddie. If you are the business-development person at a large pharmaceutical company looking at a neuropsychiatric G-P-C-R asset today, you are carrying the scar tissue from both of these deals — one that paid fourteen billion for an approval that did not convert, and one that paid eight point seven billion for a Phase Two asset that failed. The lesson that organization will have drawn is to buy later and pay for real-world tolerability data rather than trial tolerability data. That is a specific, foreseeable objection, and the answer is to decide now which tolerability evidence we will have generated by the time we are at that table. A three-week inpatient study will not answer it.

The third is a caution about reading the Cerevel side as vindication of early deals. The buyer who paid for certainty was disappointed by the market; the buyer who paid for optionality was rescued by an asset that was not the thesis. Neither is a template, and both point the same way — neuropsychiatric assets will be priced more conservatively in the next cycle than in the last one, and our Series A timing should assume that rather than hope otherwise.

Second item, and it bears on Aletira and on NuGate. The FDA's two center directors — Karim Mikhail at the biologics center and Michael Davis at the drugs center — gave their first substantive interview on the rare-disease framework unveiled last month.

The headline change is about when patients enter the conversation. Today, Mikhail says, patient input is effectively optional, and when it arrives at an advisory committee it is far too late, because the clinical development is already defined. Under the new framework the patient perspective becomes an essential component of the regulatory submission, shaping the choice of endpoint and the benefit-risk judgment, starting at the point where a company first asks permission to dose humans. Davis adds that sponsors should arrive at those early meetings ready to discuss endpoint selection, trial methodology including the number of trials, the control group and the analysis plan.

The read for Avi, Anthony and Jon: the pre-IND meeting is being repositioned as the place where the endpoint gets negotiated, and patient-derived evidence is part of the price of admission. For Aletira that is favorable — hearing restoration has an unusually clear patient-meaningfulness story and a machine-measured endpoint behind it. For NuGate it is sharper: yesterday's Radar covered a Hopkins paper where a deliberately undemanding evoked-potential paradigm still yielded analyzable data in fewer than half the participants in one syndrome. If the endpoint must now be argued on patient-meaningfulness at pre-IND, the yield problem and the meaningfulness problem have to be answered in the same meeting, not sequentially.

The second half of that interview is the part to watch, and it is an absence. External controls — using historical or real-world data instead of a concurrent placebo group — have driven recent rejections, including a gene therapy for Hunter syndrome, and reversals of prior guidance, including on a Huntington's gene therapy. The new framework champions innovative trial designs and novel statistical approaches, and then stops short of actually addressing external controls. The most useful testimony in the piece comes from Nasha Fitter of the FOXG1 Research Foundation, who built a control group from the medical records of more than a hundred children because no natural history existed. Her own academic advisors pushed back, not because the data were bad, but because there was no guidance for it and they wanted predictability. The trial was accepted into the agency's ultra-rare evidence program; whether the records-based control survives to a filing is, in her words, yet to be seen.

That is the sentence to sit with. The advisors balked at the absence of guidance, not at the evidence. Regulatory ambiguity does not just risk a rejection at the end — it costs you your own experts in the middle, when they decline to sign off on a design the agency has not blessed in writing. Hemaka, Jon: where any of our programs is contemplating a non-concurrent control, the thing to secure early is not a statistical argument, it is something in writing from the agency, because that is what the people around the table will ask for.

Third, and briefly, a biomarker-endpoint lesson available to us at no cost. Kardigan announced it has completed enrollment in its mid-stage trial of ataciguat in aortic stenosis — a hundred and fifty-six patients, with the primary endpoint being the amount of calcium in the aortic valve measured on imaging at week forty-eight.

The interesting part is the pre-planned look at baseline characteristics. Valve calcium at entry ranged from about three hundred to nearly two thousand units — a six-fold spread — and that spread overlapped heavily with whether or not a patient had ventricular dysfunction. Because calcium accrues at each patient's own rate across that range, the company amended the protocol to allow more time to detect a treatment difference, and added an internal interim look at week twenty-four to inform the Phase Three design.

The lesson for Avi and Hugh is about a class of endpoint rather than this drug. An imaging biomarker feels like the safe choice because it is objective and machine-measured — exactly the argument we make for auditory brainstem response in the cochlea. But objective does not mean low-variance, and the variance that matters is not measurement error, it is between-patient differences in the baseline value and in the rate of change. Kardigan found that out after enrolling the whole study. The cheap version of that discovery is a pre-planned baseline analysis with an explicit rule for what you do if the spread is wider than the power calculation assumed. The expensive version is a protocol amendment and an extra year.

Last item, from the cancer immunotherapy meeting, and it is for the oncology surface-targets theme and for Clasp. Johnson and Johnson reported first-in-human results for a combination of two bispecific antibodies in metastatic prostate cancer: pasritamig, which targets a prostate protein called K-L-K-two, and a second antibody targeting P-S-M-A. Eleven of fifteen patients at the recommended regimen had their prostate-specific antigen fall by at least half, and seven of fifteen by at least ninety percent, with all seven of those still on treatment past six months without progression. Dosing is every six weeks and no severe treatment-related adverse events were reported in this early analysis.

The number is not the point; fifteen patients and a blood marker is an early signal, not an efficacy claim. The architecture is the point. This is the first dual-tumor-antigen bispecific regimen tested clinically in prostate cancer, and the design logic is that each antibody supplies one of two complementary signals a T cell needs — so the full response is assembled only where both antigens are present. That is specificity bought by requiring a coincidence rather than by finding a better single target.

For Clasp, whose proposition is a target that is genuinely tumor-specific because it is a mutant protein fragment presented on the cell surface, this is a useful contrast rather than a threat. Clasp solves specificity at the target. This solves it at the logic gate, with two ordinary targets. Virginia, Avi — the question worth putting to the team is which is more robust commercially, because they fail differently. A truly specific target limits you to the patients who carry it, which is the eligible-fraction problem we have been circling since October. A two-signal gate works on common antigens and reaches far more patients, but its specificity is only as good as the number of healthy tissues that happen to express both proteins. A partner will ask which of those risks we think we are buying.

That is the watch. The two big schizophrenia acquisitions have no winner, and the stated reason is tolerability in the real world, not efficacy in the trial; the FDA wants patients in the room at pre-IND and still will not say what it thinks about external controls; an imaging endpoint turned out to be high-variance only after a trial finished enrolling; and specificity in oncology can be built from a coincidence of two ordinary targets instead of one extraordinary one. Have a good Monday.
