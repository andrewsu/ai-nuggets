Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Friday, October twenty-third. The most important development for the portfolio is that one of our own companies sold a preclinical asset to a European pharma for forty-five million dollars up front, and kept everything that matters.

Georgiamune, in Gaithersburg, signed an exclusive worldwide license and collaboration with Stemline, the oncology subsidiary of the Menarini Group, for GIM-621. Georgiamune takes forty-five million dollars in upfront and near-term payments, is eligible for preclinical, development, regulatory and commercial milestones up to a total of one point one billion dollars, plus royalties. Stemline gets worldwide rights to develop, manufacture and commercialize. Menarini is a five-and-a-half-billion-dollar-turnover company selling in a hundred and forty countries.

Two things make this worth leading with. First, GIM-621 is preclinical. A first-in-class oral small molecule for solid tumors, with no clinical data, drew forty-five million dollars of near-term cash and a nine-figure-plus milestone package. Second, look at what Samir Khleif did not sell. Georgiamune keeps all three clinical-stage programs — the oral regulatory-T-cell activator in autoimmune disease, the dual-functioning antibody in oncology, and the oral regulatory-T-cell inhibitor in oncology. He out-licensed from the preclinical bench, not from the clinic.

So what for Blackbird, and this is for Eddie and Hemaka. We hold Georgiamune as the live proof that the Intergalactic Center thesis attracts third-party capital — one immune platform pointed in both directions, suppress regulatory T cells for cancer, activate them for autoimmunity. A partner has now put a price on the *discovery engine* rather than on a clinical asset. That is the more valuable validation, because it says the platform generates sellable programs faster than one company can develop them, and that is exactly the argument our incubated spinouts have to make to a Series A syndicate. It is also a non-dilutive financing template worth writing down: partner the program that is furthest from your own capability, keep the clinical pipeline, keep the royalty. Esther and Avi, that is a one-page case study for the project leads.

Second item. The first Phase Three win for a cancer vaccine came out two days early because somebody leaked it, and the number that matters is not the headline. Merck and Moderna's melanoma results were screenshotted on social media yesterday, and ESMO responded by publishing the full details ahead of tomorrow's Madrid presentation. Eleven hundred and thirty-seven patients with completely resected stage two-B through stage four melanoma, randomized two to one, the individualized vaccine plus Keytruda against Keytruda alone. You sequence each patient's tumor, pick up to thirty-four mutations unique to it, and build a messenger RNA product that teaches their T cells to hunt exactly those. Recurrence-free survival hazard ratio, zero point five eight — a forty-two percent reduction. Distant metastasis, zero point six two. Two-year recurrence-free survival, eighty-two percent against seventy.

Moderna fell about a quarter on that, because the street wanted forty to fifty and the mid-stage trial had posted forty-four. Now the part worth your time. The companies ran a prespecified analysis splitting the curve at six months. For the first six months the arms are identical — hazard ratio zero point nine nine for recurrence, one point zero one for distant metastasis. Nothing. After six months, zero point four two, and distant metastasis halved. All of the benefit arrives once the immunization course is finished. The headline forty-two percent is the average of a dead zone and a very good drug.

So what, and this is for Eddie with Adventris downstairs. The biology question is settled — this beat the hardest control arm available, Keytruda, which by itself cut recurrence forty-three percent against placebo. What is not settled is whether bespoke is the right product form, and the dead zone is why. That gap is not biology. It is sequencing, manufacturing and a dosing ramp. An off-the-shelf shared-neoantigen vaccine does not pay it — Adventris's mutant-KRAS program and the Nouscom Lynch-syndrome program we covered Monday both dose on day one, because the product already exists. Until yesterday that was a cost-and-convenience argument. It is now an efficacy argument with a number attached. Adventris should have a slide that answers the obvious follow-up: when does our curve separate, and can we show it separates earlier than six months. The candid limit, because this cuts both ways — no off-the-shelf vaccine has cleared a randomized bar, BioNTech's individualized program just failed mid-stage in colorectal cancer, and off-the-shelf trades the dead zone for a weaker shared antigen set. For Clasp, read this as premise validation, not modality validation.

Third, and this one is for Jon and Anthony. Roche used its third-quarter call to cut about seven early assets, and one of them matters to us. It has discontinued the off-the-shelf CAR-T against C-D-nineteen and C-D-twenty that it acquired in the one-and-a-half-billion-dollar Poseida takeover last year. The Phase One programs in lupus and multiple sclerosis have been withdrawn, and the Phase One in B-cell malignancies is terminated. Thomas Schinecker was careful on the call — they believe in the mechanism, they stopped working on that molecule, and other Poseida molecules aimed at the same pair are "even better." Roche lists none of them on its pipeline page.

Take the careful phrasing at face value and it still says something. The largest-funded attempt to make an allogeneic, off-the-shelf cell therapy work in autoimmune disease just lost its lead molecule, and it lost it before producing data. Against that, Kyverna presented its one-year stiff-person-syndrome poster in Toronto on Wednesday — and be precise here, those are the numbers we covered on September twenty-fifth, now formally presented. What is genuinely new is the exploratory set: the six-minute walk distance did not plateau, it deepened, from a median gain of eighty-nine meters at sixteen weeks to a hundred and nineteen at one year, against a minimally important change of twenty. And B-cell reconstitution happened without losing efficacy.

So the autoimmune cell-therapy field split this week. The autologous product with a conventional construct is heading into a rolling B-L-A with deepening one-year responses, and the off-the-shelf product with the big balance sheet behind it is dead. For Winnow the read is sharper than "cell therapy is hard." The thing Kyverna is selling is a single dose that produces drug-free remission, and the thing Roche could not deliver was manufacturing convenience. Our engager competes with the first and does not need the second. Keep the comparison on remission depth and durability, not on logistics. One footnote for Avi: in the same cull Roche dropped an antisense oligonucleotide for Alzheimer's. That is not a program risk for NuGate, but it is one fewer large-pharma validator for central-nervous-system antisense, and the Series A deck should not be counting on big-pharma enthusiasm as a tailwind.

Fourth, for Hemaka. The F-D-A has accepted Biohaven's resubmitted application for troriluzole in spinocerebellar ataxia — the same drug it rejected last November. The agency treats it as a complete response, has set a goal date in the first quarter, and will convene the central nervous system advisory committee. The stock rose sharply. Here is the detail that makes this our item rather than a trade headline: the original rejection was not about the molecule. It was about bias in the external control — Biohaven ran a comparison against natural-history data showing fifty to seventy percent slower decline, roughly a one-and-a-half to two-year delay over three years, and the agency did not accept the comparison. The resubmission contains new analyses. Not a new trial.

That is the Aletira pre-I-N-D conversation with a worked example attached. We have an external-control ask standing in that package. This is the agency telling us, in public, exactly which part of an external control it will attack — not the effect size, the comparability of the cohort. So the ask should change shape: do not ask whether an external control is acceptable, bring the natural-history source and the matching method and ask them to agree to it in writing now. What rescued Biohaven was a second look. A second look is not a plan.

Last, for Eddie and Emily, three numbers on where capital is going. Gilead agreed to buy an oral interleukin-twenty-three-receptor program from PepStar for one point two seven five billion dollars in upfront cash — all of it at closing, no milestones disclosed — for a Phase One macrocyclic peptide with no published clinical data, from a company three years old. It takes about eighty cents off Gilead's fourth-quarter earnings per share. Second, the sector has now done thirty-one initial public offerings this year against eight in all of last year. Third, Lycia priced last night at three hundred and seventeen and a half million dollars, having filed to raise two hundred and twenty-eight.

The pattern underneath those is one thing: buyers are paying full price earlier, and the public market is open for platform stories. Gilead did not negotiate a milestone structure on a Phase One asset — it wrote a check. That is a seller's market for a differentiated mechanism with a clean worldwide rights package, and it is the market our spinouts are walking into. Worth noting one name in the IPO queue for Jonathan — Aspen Neuroscience, the autologous stem-cell Parkinson's program, which is the comparator column in our Parkinson's theme and is about to have a public price.

That is the watch. Nothing in the window touched Aletira, 1104health, Biolinco, Artemyx, DELFI, Islex or the G-P-R fifty-two program directly. Maisha, worth deciding this week whether we show up to both. And applications for the expedited investigational-new-drug pilot close a week from today. Have a good weekend.
