# Sourcing Radar — 2026-07-26 (inaugural weekly roundup)

## Script

This is Sourcing Radar — and it's the first of our new weekly editions, so today it's a roundup: the three best commercializable leads to come out of our partner institutions this week, ranked by how ready they are for a conversation. Then a quick note on what's still cooking.

Start with the one I'd move on first. Out of the University of Maryland School of Medicine — the Suk lab, in neurosurgery and Maryland's new neuroscience institute, U-M-MIND — there's a cancer-immunotherapy conjugate that looks like a genuinely differentiated platform. The target is C-D-forty-seven, the "don't eat me" signal that tumors use to hide from macrophages. Anti-C-D-forty-seven antibodies already exist — Gilead, ALX Oncology, and Pfizer have all chased them — but they share a known problem: they get the macrophage to eat the tumor, and then nothing happens. The clearance is immunologically silent, so the cancer comes back. What the Suk lab did is covalently tie the anti-C-D-forty-seven antibody to a small piece of DNA that switches on T-L-R-nine, an innate-immune alarm. The clever part is location. T-L-R-nine lives inside the endosome — the same compartment where a macrophage digests what it just ate. So the exact macrophage that swallows the tumor cell also gets the danger signal, in the right place at the right time, something you simply cannot achieve by dosing two separate free drugs. And it works: in mouse models of lymphoma and of cold, immunosuppressive triple-negative breast cancer, you get durable tumor clearance that depends on macrophages, plus real immune memory — the animals rejected a re-challenge more than a hundred days later.

Here's why it matters for us. Every immune-stimulating antibody conjugate in the clinic today — Bolt Biotherapeutics and the rest — hangs its payload off a tumor-specific antibody, so it only works where that one antigen is. This one rides a checkpoint that's on essentially every cancer, and it works independently of the antibody's F-C tail, which means you can build an F-C-silent version to cut toxicity. Antigen-agnostic and F-C-independent is a differentiated platform position. And the deal timing is live: there's a filed patent application with Suk as an inventor, and it's funded by the Maryland Innovation Initiative through TEDCO — state translational money — which means the U-M Ventures licensing window is open right now. Suk trained in the Johns Hopkins nanomedicine cluster and is now building at Maryland, so he sits squarely on the U-M Ventures and Blackbird co-investment surface. So the action is: Eddie, get the filing status and find out whether Suk is thinking about a vehicle. Avi and Yixuan, the diligence is freedom-to-operate against the anti-C-D-forty-seven antibody estate and the conjugate-linker patents — that's where both the value and the risk live.

Second lead, from Johns Hopkins, is a cleaner near-term venture story even though it got less airtime this week — a resistance-breaker out of Jim Berger and Vasan Yegnasubramanian's groups at the Kimmel Cancer Center. The target is an enzyme called D-C-T-P-P-one, which polices the cell's nucleotide pool. Here's the angle. The approved blood-cancer drugs decitabine and azacitidine work by getting incorporated into DNA — and D-C-T-P-P-one is exactly the enzyme that clears those kinds of modified nucleotides out before they can act. So it's a built-in resistance factor. Inhibit it, and you make an already-approved, generic drug work better. They ran a screen, found inhibitors down to sub-micromolar potency, solved crystal structures of three chemical classes sitting in the pocket, and showed synergy with decitabine in prostate-cancer cells. The commercial shape is attractive: a new chemical entity — the inhibitor — layered on top of a cheap generic backbone, in a market that already exists. A provisional patent is filed, so the Hopkins licensing window is open, and the founding bench is heavyweight — the Kimmel director, the head of the basic biomedical sciences institute, the precision-medicine lead. One candid caveat: the full paper is paywalled, so this is a flag-and-chase, not a finished read. The open questions are how novel the chemical matter is and freedom-to-operate, because they describe both new and previously-known inhibitor classes.

Third, and the most time-sensitive on IP, is a target called h-L-M-R-one, from Xiangbo Ruan's lab at Hopkins endocrinology. It's a long non-coding RNA that acts as a brake on the liver's ability to burn amino acids and run the urea cycle — and it's both liver-specific and human-specific. Knock it down in a humanized-liver mouse and those pathways switch back on. What makes it a company is the whitespace: the whole MASH field — fatty liver disease — is crowded with drugs that work on fat and fibrosis. Nobody is drugging the liver's nitrogen metabolism. And because the target is a liver RNA, the obvious modality is the de-risked playbook Ionis and Alnylam have already proven — an antisense drug or a sugar-tagged interfering RNA that homes only to liver cells, exactly where this target lives. It even opens an orphan lane in urea-cycle disorders. It's early — target validation, no molecule yet — which is precisely the right stage for a venture studio to found around ownable biology. The urgent part: the paper disclosed no patents, so the Hopkins filing window may still be open. Eddie, that's a time-sensitive conversation with Ruan and the tech-transfer office.

One last note on what's still cooking, because three partner-institution preprints this week are genuinely interesting but their full text hasn't rendered yet, so I'm holding them for next week rather than summarizing an abstract. There's a Hopkins platform for maturing stem-cell-derived heart muscle by incubating it inside a living heart. There's a Maryland proposal for single-molecule protein sequencing that swaps the static nanopore for a dynamical translocase. And there's a Hopkins mechanism they've named skoupocytosis — where microglia reach in and prune an oversized garbage-disposal organelle out of a neuron, which points at a specific druggable enzyme in neurodegeneration. I'll read all three in full when the bodies post.

That's the week's radar. Three actionable leads — Suk's C-D-forty-seven conjugate at Maryland first, then the D-C-T-P-P-one resistance-breaker and the h-L-M-R-one liver target at Hopkins. Have a good week.
