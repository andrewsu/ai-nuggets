## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Sunday, September thirteenth. The most instructive item of the week is a licensing story: a company signed away a platform for three hundred fifty million dollars at closing, and nine days later, before that closing happened, the F-D-A put a partial hold on the lead asset over a finding that both sides had already seen.

Here is the sequence, because the sequence is the whole point.

On August twenty-sixth, Biohaven granted S-K Biopharmaceuticals an exclusive worldwide licence to its K-v seven ion-channel platform, led by opakalim — formerly B-H-V seven thousand — a selective activator of the K-v seven point two and seven point three potassium channels, in Phase two-three development for focal epilepsy. The terms: three hundred fifty million dollars in cash at closing, another fifty million next year, a hundred fifty million in development and regulatory milestones, tiered royalties from the mid-teens to the low twenties on U-S net sales, and up to seven hundred ninety-five million across the platform in total. S-K also takes on Biohaven's obligations to Knopp Biosciences, the company that originally developed the molecule.

On September fourth, the F-D-A issued a partial clinical hold. The cause was not a patient event. It was a metabolite identified in rodent toxicology, and the agency's position was that it did not have enough information to assess that metabolite's risk to humans. New enrolment pauses until clarifying nonclinical studies are done. Patients already enrolled keep dosing — more than twelve hundred people have received the drug across the programme and more than six hundred are still on it. One of the two pivotal trials finished enrolling in June and is untouched, with topline still guided to the second half of this year. The other pivotal trial cannot enrol anyone new.

And the sentence in Biohaven's filing that everyone should read twice: all metabolite and clinical data were disclosed to S-K before signing. The Hart-Scott-Rodino filings are still being completed, which means the transaction has not closed.

Four things fall out of that, and they are all about us as a licensor rather than about epilepsy.

First, the money is payable at closing, and the hold arrived in the gap between signing and closing. Whatever this particular contract says about material adverse change, that gap is where three hundred fifty million dollars was sitting when the letter came. Eddie and Emily — the question is not whether we have a material-adverse-change clause. It is whether anyone has decided, in advance, what regulatory events inside the signing-to-closing window we are willing to carry and which ones we are not. That is a cheap decision to make in a quiet week and an expensive one to make in the week it happens.

Second, and this is the more uncomfortable one: disclosure is not alignment. Both parties had the same rodent data in front of them. The buyer read it and paid. The regulator read it and stopped enrolment. A clean data room transfers information; it does not transfer regulatory judgement, and the diligence that matters is not "did they see it" but "did anyone ask the agency." For a Blackbird asset going into a corp-dev process, the pre-I-N-D or Type C meeting minutes are worth more in the data room than another hundred pages of primary data, because they are the only document that tells a buyer what the regulator thinks rather than what we think.

Third, look at where the failure was. Not efficacy. Not a clinical safety signal after twelve hundred dosed patients. A metabolite in rodent toxicology. The nonclinical package is the cheapest part of a development programme and the one nobody wants to spend the extra six months on, and here it stopped enrolment in a Phase three. Avi and Jon — that applies directly to three of ours. NuGate's antisense programme, the GPR fifty-two development candidate that is still guided for later this year, and Aletira's first vector. Metabolite and impurity characterisation is not where you find schedule savings.

Fourth, the royalty stack. S-K assumed Biohaven's obligations to Knopp, the original developer. When a Blackbird programme is licensed out, we are Knopp in that sentence. The academic-origin obligations travel with the asset through every subsequent transaction and they get renegotiated by nobody, which is an argument for getting the first licence right rather than fast.

Second item, and it is the rare piece of drug-pricing news that points our way.

The consultancy Numerof and Associates published a survey on Thursday called Pharma's Commercial Reset, built on interviews with a hundred fifty-seven pharma leaders across sixty-seven companies. The headline is that launching a drug is no longer, in one respondent's phrase, a straight math exercise. Under most-favoured-nation pricing, companies are sequencing launches country by country based on what the price they accept in one market does to the price they can get in another, and some are reconsidering low-price markets entirely. One interviewee described the policy environment as an earthquake that is not going to settle down soon.

Three structural consequences came out of that survey. Pricing considerations now enter clinical development from Phase one and Phase two onward. Market access and distribution teams are being pulled into programmes years earlier than they used to be. And — this is the one — the portfolio preference is shifting toward targeted therapies for smaller, well-defined populations and away from multi-indication blockbusters.

On September first we logged the M-F-N agreements covering roughly eighty-nine percent of the branded market and the durable read was that the buyer has pre-committed the launch price of a drug that does not exist yet. The action item was to revisit the U-S price assumption in every programme value case. This survey is the same force arriving as a portfolio preference instead of a price cap, and the direction is favourable. The asset shape a Blackbird programme naturally produces — one mechanism, one tightly defined population, a narrow first indication — is the shape the buyers now say they want. That is worth saying out loud in a partnering conversation.

The uncomfortable corollary is symmetrical. If pricing enters their development at Phase one, it enters ours at the grant decision. Emily — the value case should carry a launch-sequencing assumption, not just a U-S price. Which countries, in what order, and what does each one cost the next.

Third item, short, and it widens a column we have been tracking for two weeks.

BioSpace published a piece on Wednesday arguing that the ex-U-S asset search has moved past China. Five destinations, with transactions attached. South Korea, where Hanmi did two point three billion with Roche and one point two billion with Lilly, and where GSK and Lilly both put money into A-B-L Bio. Australia, where Telix and Regeneron did a four point three billion radiopharmaceutical deal in April, and where the argument is specifically that F-D-A and European regulators accept the trial data. Hong Kong, now the second-largest biotech listing venue in the world with more than seventeen and a half billion raised since the twenty eighteen reforms. India, where Sun Pharma bought Organon for eleven and three-quarter billion this year and patent filings are up more than fourfold since twenty sixteen. And Canada, favoured for Phase two work and gene therapy specifically, where AbCellera and Jazz did two and a half billion.

Our standing line has been that the differentiator has to be something the buyer cannot source in Shanghai or Seoul. That line now has five more cities in it. And one of them weakens an argument we made ten days ago: when the F-D-A published its editorial on foreign site inspectability, we read that as attaching a discount to ex-U-S clinical packages and therefore as a point in favour of American human data. Australia is a direct counterexample — an established venue whose data the F-D-A already accepts, with no inspectability discount to speak of. So the inspectability argument is narrower than we thought. What survives, and I think we should now say only this, is an unencumbered composition-of-matter position. That is the one thing in the column that cannot be bought in another time zone.

Fourth item. On Tuesday, Luma Group in New York closed its first fund, LumaBio Fund One, at four hundred ten million dollars. Joshua Fink is managing member, Themasap Khan is co-founder and partner. They invest early, in companies combining biology, chemistry, physics and artificial intelligence — the portfolio since twenty twenty-three includes Altos Labs, Arsenal Biosciences, Integrated Biosciences and Character Biosciences — and they took their first exit this year when Eli Lilly bought Vaccine Company for up to one and a half billion.

The number that matters is not four hundred ten million. It is fifteen years. That is the fund's structure — a fifteen-year vehicle, explicitly built to hold a company from discovery through commercialisation, backed by financial institutions, sovereign wealth funds, insurance companies and family offices.

On Friday we logged Frazier adding more than a billion to a public fund and concluded that crossover capital is available on the far side of a de-risking event. Luma is the other end of the same barbell, and it is the end we thought was ours. Patient capital is the structural advantage a nonprofit with an endowment-scale founding grant has over a ten-year fund. A dedicated venture firm has now raised that duration as a product, from the L-P base where duration actually lives, which is sovereign wealth.

Two consequences. The first is that "we can wait longer than a fund can" is a weaker differentiator this week than it was last week, and it should come out of the pitch as a standalone claim. The second is constructive, and it answers a question we left open on Friday when we looked at the Kura and Caspian carve-out and asked which venture-philanthropy or long-duration fund sits beside each of our themes. A fifteen-year fund is the natural co-investor for a programme whose value event is six years out, which describes most of what Blackbird Laboratories funds. Luma is a specific name to put in that column, and the T-one-D Fund is the other. Eddie — that column should have entries, not question marks.

Last item, and it is short but it closes a gap.

Reporting on Friday confirmed that the F-D-A has named Jared Seehafer, previously a senior adviser in the Office of the Commissioner, as the agency's first-ever Deputy Commissioner for Technology and Artificial Intelligence. The remit as stated is accelerating reliable A-I innovation, modernising federal technology, and building a regulatory framework for emerging technologies. This is the same week Karim Mikhail at C-B-E-R and Michael Davis at C-D-E-R converted from acting to permanent directors, which we covered on Wednesday, and where Davis said his first priority is morale and retention.

Virginia — we have two assets whose regulatory surface is software rather than chemistry. Artemyx's whole product is machine-learning classification of cell-free D-N-A fragmentation, and 1104health is a workflow product inside clinical practice. Until this week there was no single person at the agency who owned the question of how those get reviewed. There now is, and the post is a deputy commissioner rather than a centre-level role, which matters specifically because an A-I-driven diagnostic falls between C-D-E-R, C-B-E-R and C-D-R-H, and a deputy commissioner sits above that seam. Worth finding out early who that office listens to.

Quiet where it matters this week. Nothing on Clasp, where the monotherapy readout from the first-in-human trial is still guided to this year. Nothing new on GPR fifty-two or on Nxera's out-licensing process. Nothing on NuGate, Biolinco or Winnow beyond what we covered Friday. The Sourcing Radar published separately this morning has three partner-institution leads, including one that changes our position on the COPD theme. That is the watch. Have a good Sunday.
