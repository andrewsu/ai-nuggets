Georgiamune, in Gaithersburg, about thirty people, has raised roughly ninety-three million dollars in a Series B, structured across a B-one and a B-two tranche, at a pre-money valuation of about two hundred ninety-six million dollars. Here is the science. Founder and chief executive Samir Khleif is a medical oncologist and immunology researcher, and the platform came out of his own lab: reprogramming immune signaling to restore what the company calls immune balance. Three clinical-stage assets. G-I-M one twenty-two is a first-in-class dual-functioning monoclonal antibody for solid tumors that have stopped responding to checkpoint inhibitors. G-I-M five thirty-one is a first-in-class oral therapy that selectively targets regulatory T cells while sparing other immune cells — also for checkpoint-refractory cancer. And G-I-M four-oh-seven is an oral, first-in-class selective regulatory T cell activator, currently in a Phase one healthy-volunteer study, being developed for inflammatory bowel disease, lupus and type one diabetes.

Read those last two together, because that is the whole story. One platform. One cell type. Suppress regulatory T cells and you release anti-tumor immunity. Activate them and you restore tolerance in autoimmune disease. Same target class, opposite direction, two disease areas, both oral, both in the clinic, in one company, in Maryland. That is precisely the thesis behind the Intergalactic Center for Autoimmunity and Immuno-Oncology that Blackbird funded, and somebody has now put ninety-three million dollars behind the bet that a single platform can be pointed both ways.

And it did not happen alone this week. On Monday, RegCell — Emeryville, California — closed a forty-four-million-dollar Series A led by Playground Global, with Global Brain, Mitsui Chemicals, L-G Technology Ventures, Alumni Ventures and Medical Incubator Japan, plus a second non-dilutive grant from Japan's Agency for Medical Research and Development worth three point four billion yen, about twenty-two million dollars. Sixty-six million dollars combined. The co-founder is Shimon Sakaguchi, the Nobel laureate who discovered regulatory T cells in the first place. Chief executive Michael McCullar. The platform is non-gene-engineered epigenetic reprogramming: instead of suppressing the immune system broadly, it converts the pathogenic T cells that are actually driving the disease into stable, disease-relevant regulatory T cells. The money funds an investigational new drug filing and a Phase two proof of concept in a second autoimmune indication.

So the read, and this one is for Jon and Virginia. One hundred fifty-eight million dollars flowed into regulatory T cell modulation in three days, and neither company is a T-cell engager. On Wednesday we concluded that Winnow cannot win on convenience and has to compete on depth and durability — one course, durable drug-free remission. Look at what just got funded on either side of that position. The convenience pole is now an oral capsule making the selectivity claim directly: G-I-M four-oh-seven is a pill that says it targets one cell population and spares the rest. The durability pole is now a reprogramming platform whose entire pitch is restoring tolerance at the source, permanently, which is the strongest available version of the sentence we want Winnow to own. Both poles of Winnow's competitive space got capitalized in one week, and the middle of that space — a periodically dosed engager — is the part that now has to justify itself against both ends.

Two candid limits before anyone panics. G-I-M four-oh-seven is in healthy volunteers. RegCell has not filed an investigational new drug application yet. Second item, and it is a structural correction to a standing action we opened two weeks ago. Bristol Myers Squibb has discontinued O-R-M six one five one, a degrader-antibody conjugate it had been testing in a Phase one study in acute myeloid leukemia and myelodysplastic syndromes. The company killed it after reviewing the clinical data, and the disclosure came in a regulatory filing from the originator, the Korean biotech Orum Therapeutics, on Thursday.

Here is the part that matters. Bristol Myers bought that asset from Orum in twenty twenty-three for one hundred million dollars up front against up to eighty million more in milestones. But it was an asset purchase agreement, not a license. Orum's chief executive, S-J Lee, put it plainly: there are no reversion rights to Orum. So Orum keeps the hundred million, owes nothing back, collects no milestones, and does not get the molecule back. It sits inside Bristol Myers, dead, permanently.

On September fifth we opened a standing screen on divested and shelved large-pharma assets across our theme areas, after Pfizer out-licensed a fully built Seagen antibody-drug conjugate to Medicus for twelve million dollars up front. That screen is a good idea and it should stay open. But this week tells us how to scope it. Pfizer could move that asset because of how Pfizer held it. Orum's molecule cannot move, because of how Orum sold it. So the first question on any shelved asset is not what it does or what stage it reached — it is whether the original transaction was a license with reversion and diligence obligations, or an outright purchase. That is a two-minute check on a deal structure, and it decides whether the rest of the diligence is worth doing at all.

The mirror of that is the half that should actually change our behavior. When Blackbird licenses academic intellectual property out of Johns Hopkins or U-M-B, and when a portfolio company sells a program to fund the rest of its pipeline, the reversion clause is the thing that determines whether a shelved program is ever recoverable. Orum made a trade — a hundred million dollars for permanent loss of control — and honestly it may have been the right trade, because they are sitting on about a hundred eighty million dollars in cash, they are not laying anyone off, and their second degrader-antibody conjugate cleared the F-D-A last month for blood cancers. Serapha is what the market rate for that bench looks like right now, and the smaller note underneath it is that the founding chief executive of this region's anchor gene-therapy company is now running a base-editing company. Last item, thirty seconds, and it is a design principle rather than a deal. Remedy Plan Therapeutics in Gaithersburg reported on Monday that patients in the first cohort of its Phase one trial completed a full twenty-eight-day treatment cycle on R-P-T one G, a first-in-class inhibitor of the metabolic enzyme N-A-M-P-T. Cohort two is enrolling, the F-D-A accepted an investigational new drug application to take the drug into solid tumors, and the company closed a roughly thirty-million-dollar Series A extension led by Schooner Capital. Chief executive Greg Crimmins called the cohort-one result a first: therapeutic inhibition of that enzyme sustained in cancer patients for longer than a few days without limiting toxicity, which the field had considered impossible.

The generalizable part is the mechanism they call hyperbolic inhibition — controlling the enzyme's activity rather than shutting it off completely, so healthy cells keep enough metabolic function while cancer cells lose it. That is a therapeutic-window strategy, not a selectivity strategy, and it is worth holding onto, because we keep running into targets that are perfectly good biology and unusable because full blockade kills the patient.
