## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Monday, November twenty-third, and the day's most important development for the portfolio is that in vivo C-A-R T finally has human data — six patients of it — and the number that matters is not the response rate. It is that nobody got lymphodepleted and nobody got sick.

Start there, because we have spent the last week arguing about this category without any clinical evidence in it.

On Sunday, CARsgen Therapeutics, a Hong Kong-listed cell-therapy company, released the first clinical data on C-T-eleven-eighty-two, an in vivo C-A-R T candidate targeting C-D nineteen and C-D twenty in relapsed or refractory B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma. One intravenous infusion of a viral vector with a T-cell-specific promoter; the C-A-R T cells are then made inside the patient. No cells are collected, nothing is manufactured for that patient, and — this is the load-bearing part — no lymphodepleting chemotherapy is given first.

Six patients, from an investigator-initiated trial, with data cut at the end of October. Five with diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, one with blastoid mantle cell lymphoma. All stage four, a median of three prior lines, median tumor burden just under two thousand square millimeters, and two of the six had already failed a T-cell engager. Three complete responses, two partial responses, all ongoing at cutoff, the longest running close to five months. A complete response was seen at the lowest dose tested.

Now the safety column, which is the actual news. No treatment-related serious adverse events. No dose-limiting toxicities. No infusion reactions. No neurotoxicity, no hemophagocytic-type syndrome, and no biphasic cytokine elevation. Cytokine release syndrome occurred, but only grade one. Hematologic toxicity was mild — one grade-three leukopenia that may have been disease-related, and nothing else above grade two. The company says this profile supports outpatient administration, notes that every responder developed complete B-cell aplasia, and then says the quiet part out loud: that this suggests application to other B-cell-driven diseases, including autoimmune disease. They plan to file for a registrational trial in the first half of twenty twenty-seven.

Jon, Anthony — here is the read, and it is a genuine update to the position we filed on the nineteenth.

Three days ago we said that in vivo generation buys the manufacturing fix and that the safety premise was bought without evidence. That was the right call on the nineteenth. It is now a weaker call, and we should say so internally before a partner says it to us. Six patients is six patients, it is an investigator-initiated trial in China reported by press release, and three point two months of median follow-up is nothing. But the specific thing that was missing — any human evidence that skipping conditioning and generating the effector in situ does not reproduce autologous C-A-R T toxicity — is no longer entirely missing. And it arrives one day after the off-the-shelf N-K-plus-rituximab paper we covered yesterday, which also skipped lymphodepletion. That is two independent groups, two completely different modalities, both removing the conditioning regimen and both reporting clean early safety.

So update the axis. The competitive question in B-cell depletion is no longer autologous versus in vivo. It is, as I said yesterday, conditions the patient versus does not — and as of this weekend there are two clinical data points on the "does not" side and zero on ours.

But hold the line that still holds, and it is the sharper one now, not the weaker one. Every responder in this trial has complete B-cell aplasia from a single infusion of a vector that keeps producing C-A-R T cells. In lymphoma, that is the point. In a lupus patient who was functioning on rituximab, that is the exposure. You cannot un-dose it, you cannot titrate it, and the better this category's safety looks on day thirty, the more the whole argument moves to month twelve and month twenty-four — hypogammaglobulinemia, infection risk, vaccine response, and what a patient does when they want to come off. Interruptible, titratable, redosable is still the sentence. It just has to be argued on durability of exposure now rather than on acute toxicity, because acute toxicity is the argument we are starting to lose.

Virginia, Avi — one more cross-read, and it points back at our own sourcing file. Yesterday's Radar lead was the Hopkins injectable niche platform out of the Mao lab, which makes C-A-R T cells inside the patient by recruiting host T cells into a vector-loaded scaffold, with a Hopkins Technology Ventures patent application already filed. Twenty-four hours later a company has shown that systemic in vivo generation works in humans without conditioning. Read that as category validation and as competitive pressure at the same time. The scaffold's differentiating claim was spatial confinement — keeping transduction local. If a simple intravenous vector is producing complete responses with only grade-one C-R-S, then confinement has to earn its complexity against a product you can give from a bag. Put that question to the Mao group directly when we take the meeting. It is answerable, and better to answer it ourselves than to have an investor raise it.

Second item, and it closes a row on the Clasp board we opened on the first of September.

On Friday, at the E-O-R-T-C-N-C-I-A-A-C-R symposium in Barcelona, P-M-V Pharmaceuticals presented the primary analysis from the pivotal Phase 2 portion of PYNNACLE for rezatapopt — the first-in-class oral small molecule that reactivates p53 by binding the pocket created by the Y-two-twenty-C mutation. In sixty-five platinum-resistant or refractory ovarian cancer patients, the confirmed response rate was forty-eight percent with a median duration of response of ten point four months and an eighty-three percent disease control rate. Across all one hundred sixty-eight evaluable patients in five tumor cohorts, thirty-six percent and eight point one months. Toxicity mostly grade one to two, six percent discontinuing for treatment-related adverse events. The N-D-A for accelerated approval in platinum-resistant ovarian is guided for the first quarter of twenty twenty-seven.

Two things changed from the interim data we covered on the first of September. First, the number held. Forty-six percent at the interim on seventy-six evaluable ovarian patients; forty-eight percent now in the defined primary analysis population, with the duration slightly longer. A single-arm registrational response rate that holds as the denominator fills is the version of that dataset most likely to survive review. Second, they added a biomarker result: among fifty patients with circulating tumor D-N-A data, ninety percent had at least a fifty-percent drop in the Y-two-twenty-C variant allele fraction within three to six weeks of starting treatment.

Eddie, Hemaka — the read is the eligible-fraction row, and it is now urgent rather than academic. Rezatapopt needs one gate: the mutation. C-L-S-P ten twenty-five needs two multiplied gates: the p53 R-one-seventy-five-H mutation and H-L-A-A star oh two oh one. In about four to six weeks the comparator becomes a filed accelerated-approval package — a once-daily pill, taken at home, with chemo-free activity in eight tumor types at a response rate just under fifty percent in the lead indication. That is the thing a licensing partner will put next to our engager. Depth and durability in refractory disease is the only answer, and it has to be quantified against ten point four months, not asserted.

The second-order point is one Avi should take. Their ctDNA result is a pharmacodynamic proof of mechanism that is cheap, fast, and measured in weeks rather than months. For a TCR-mimic engager, the equivalent instrument is harder — you are depleting antigen-presenting tumor cells, not reversing a folding defect — but the principle transfers. If we can show target-driven molecular response inside six weeks in GUARDIAN-one-oh-one, that is worth more in a partnering conversation than another two patients of imaging.

Third item is shorter and it is about how we are going to get asked questions, not about a result.

BioSpace published a dispatch this morning from Jefferies and Cell and Gene Therapy Live in London, reporting what investors are now putting cell and gene therapy companies through on manufacturing. The claim from the venture side, from Sven Kili at Saisei Ventures, is that the question has shifted from "can it be made under Good Manufacturing Practice" to "can it be scaled, how much, and at what cost." Manufacturing, chemistry and controls deficiencies are now a leading cause of complete response letters in this field — which we watched play out in real time with Capricor twice. Investors are reportedly asking for a de-risked manufacturing strategy before first-in-human, not after; for cost-of-goods projections as a diligence metric alongside safety and efficacy; and for evidence that the company has the people, in-house or contracted, to actually run a contract manufacturer. The grim statistic in the piece: roughly fifty thousand patients worldwide have received a commercial C-A-R T therapy since the first approval in twenty seventeen, despite billions invested.

Hemaka, Emily, Avi — three actions, and they are cheap.

One, for Aletira: cost-of-goods assumptions get baked in at preclinical, not at scale-up. The warning in the piece cuts both ways — under-invest in automation and you lock in a labor-intensive process that never becomes commercially viable; over-invest and the payback never arrives for the indication size. For a hearing-loss program with a defined, genetically-defined population, that is a decision with a right answer and we should write it down before the Series A, not during it.

Two, the staffing question is one we can answer unusually well and probably are not answering at all. Kili's point is that even a seed investor now asks whether there is someone who owns the C-M-C journey the way a program manager owns translation. We have that function — Esther, Avi and Yixuan sit across programs with exactly that shape. Name it explicitly in the materials.

Three, and this is the one that compounds: if cost-of-goods and scalability are now diligence gates at seed, then they are also sourcing criteria. A platform whose manufacturing route is obviously ugly is worth less to us at license time than it was two years ago, and a platform that is boring to make is worth more. Add the question to the standing diligence template so it gets asked at first read rather than at the end of a six-month evaluation.

Quickly, the day ahead. Kymera reports its Phase 2b atopic dermatitis results for the oral S-T-A-T-six degrader today — flagged yesterday, still the cleanest public test of whether an oral degrader can take a biologic's indication. Invivyd holds a call today on a Phase 3 interim analysis. And Endpoints is reporting this morning that Lilly is buying the U-K Huntington's biotech LoQus twenty-three for up to seven hundred twenty-five million dollars, which would be another early-stage C-N-S acquisition for the divested-asset screen — but the only source so far is behind a paywall and neither Lilly nor the company has posted anything, so treat it as unconfirmed until tomorrow.

That is the watch. The first human data in in vivo C-A-R T show complete responses with no conditioning and only grade-one C-R-S, which costs us the acute-safety half of our argument and moves the whole Winnow case onto durability of exposure; the p53 comparator's registrational package held at forty-eight percent and is weeks from filing, which makes Clasp's two-gate eligible fraction a number we need rather than a question we defer; and the investor bar in cell and gene therapy has moved to cost-of-goods and scalability before first-in-human, which is a diligence template change, not a talking point. Back tomorrow.
