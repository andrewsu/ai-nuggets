## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Sourcing Radar for Sunday, October fourth. Three leads this week, all from Johns Hopkins, and the first one is not a therapeutic — it is a manufacturing defect that almost everyone in gene therapy is carrying, including, with high probability, us.

Fred Bunz's lab, in Radiation Oncology and Molecular Radiation Sciences at Johns Hopkins, posted on Monday with Vinod Jaskula-Ranga, who is at Hunterian Medicine. The question they asked is old and unglamorous. The inverted terminal repeats that flank every recombinant A-A-V cassette are famously unstable when you grow the plasmid in bacteria. The field's response for thirty years has been workarounds — special bacterial strains, cold growth, and, most consequentially, chopping the repeats down so there is less palindrome to misbehave.

Their answer is that the instability was never a property of the repeats. It is a property of where you put them.

Here is the experiment. They took the original cloned A-A-V-two isolate from the nineteen eighties, sequenced its repeats with long-read nanopore at single-molecule depth, and found that the repeat sitting near the plasmid's origin of replication degraded while the far one stayed clean. They rebuilt the same genome into a backbone that moved both repeats more than one point four kilobases from the origin. The instability went away — in ordinary D-H-five-alpha bacteria, at ordinary temperature, with no tricks. Then they did it again in a high-copy vector, where the origin sat four hundred seventy-seven base pairs from the right repeat, and got the same result: after prolonged culture, twenty-seven percent of molecules had flipped the inner loop of the origin-proximal repeat while the distal one stayed over ninety-nine percent uniform. Inserting a seventeen-hundred-base-pair spacer between the origin and the repeat fixed it, and the resulting vector packaged a four thousand seven hundred fifty base pair all-in-one C-R-I-S-P-R cassette at better than five times the specified yield, seventy to eighty percent full capsids depending on whether you believe mass photometry or electron microscopy.

Now the part that makes this a sourcing conversation rather than a methods note. They went to Addgene and screened the whole repository — a hundred seventy-seven thousand plasmids, of which seven thousand forty-one had detectable A-A-V repeats. Sixty-eight percent of them carry a specific eleven-base-pair deletion. And in ninety-five point three percent of the analyzable cases, that deletion sits in the repeat nearest the origin. Thousands of labs have been propagating the same positional defect, inherited from a cloning decision made decades ago, and calling it normal. The dominant architecture in the repository is not two intact repeats. It is one truncated repeat and one truncated-and-deleted repeat.

They also show the defect is not cosmetic. The deleted repeat is more resistant to restriction digest at limiting enzyme, meaning it sits more stably in its folded conformation, and in an integration assay they built, virus from mutant-repeat constructs produced more drug-resistant colonies and, in a non-cancer cell line, a lower proportion of those were on-target homologous events. That is a worse insertional-mutagenesis ratio. Preliminary, one assay, two cell lines — but the direction is the wrong one.

Three things to do with this, and only one of them is a licensing question.

First, and most urgent, Avi and Virginia. Our cell-type-selective cassette is delivered in A-A-V. Find out, this week, which entry vector it is built in and how far its repeats sit from the origin, and whether anyone has ever sequenced them with long reads rather than looked at a restriction digest. The paper's whole point is that a digest can look fine on a plasmid population that is a third mutant. If our construct is in a standard pUC-derived backbone — and the base rates say it probably is — we are carrying the common deletion and we do not know it.

Second, the thing worth having here is not a patent, and we should be clear-eyed about that. This work was funded through the Bespoke Gene Therapy Consortium, the N-I-H-and-F-D-A public-private partnership, and the authors deposited the stabilized vector in Addgene with a catalogue number. They gave it away. That is a precompetitive standard, not an asset. What it is for us is free de-risking of a platform program and, more interestingly, a diligence instrument — the nanopore pileup workflow they describe, run on raw reads, would let us ask any incoming gene-therapy opportunity a question its founders probably cannot answer. Hemaka, Jon, Anthony: add repeat integrity by long-read sequencing to the technical diligence list for every A-A-V deal we look at. It costs almost nothing and it discriminates.

Third, a note for Eddie about where the value went. The first author is at Hunterian Medicine, a company built on Hopkins A-A-V work — and its address on this paper is Cambridge, Massachusetts. Another one left. We keep seeing this shape: Hopkins vector science, company formed, company relocates. Second lead, and the honest framing is that this one is not a sourcing lead at all — it is portfolio intelligence that happens to arrive as a preprint, and the reason is in the disclosure block.

Victor Velculescu's group at the Sidney Kimmel Comprehensive Cancer Center posted a study of structural variation in repeat elements. Start with the normal-tissue result, because it is the one that reframes the diagnostics problem. They did long- and short-read whole-genome sequencing on forty-seven normal tissues across a hundred sixty-eight samples from multiple individuals, and found mosaic structural variants in every individual and in every germ layer — a median of two hundred eighty-five kilobases per genome. Nearly half the breakpoints validated independently. Most of it is repeat-mediated, and eight point three percent of it overlaps functional elements, which is an enrichment relative to germline variation. In other words, healthy tissue is structurally noisier than the field has been modelling, and the noise is concentrated in exactly the repetitive sequence that short-read pipelines usually throw away.

Then the finding that matters commercially. They extended the analysis across fifteen cancer types and found that tumour-related repeat variation is similar in scale to normal mosaic variation. Read that as the specificity problem stated quantitatively. If the tumour signal and the healthy background are the same size, a population-level reference will not separate them.

Their answer is the part worth owning. Instead of a population reference, they compare the repeat landscape in a patient's plasma against the repeat landscape in that same patient's own white blood cells. In seventy-two women with ovarian cancer, how closely the plasma resembles the buffy coat tracks tumour burden — and after neoadjuvant chemotherapy, the patients whose plasma had drifted back toward their own white-cell baseline lived longer. The baseline is the patient. That is a monitoring assay that needs no disease-specific classifier and no cohort to train on, which is a meaningfully different regulatory and commercial object from a screening test.

Now the disclosure. The competing-interest statement says Velculescu is a founder, board member and shareholder of both Delfi Diagnostics and Artemyx, that he is an officer of Artemyx, that Johns Hopkins owns equity in Delfi, and that the underlying cell-free D-N-A patents are already licensed to both. So there is nothing here to go license. We are already on both sides of it.

Which makes the action an internal one, and it belongs to the Artemyx file. Artemyx sells monitoring of chronic disease, not cancer screening — liver fibrosis, lupus, rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease. The within-patient baseline trick is indication-agnostic, and for a chronic-disease monitoring product it is arguably a better fit than it is for oncology, because the patient is already enrolled, already bled repeatedly, and the clinical question is change over time rather than presence or absence. Matt Lawler and Jon — the question for Artemyx is whether the repeat-landscape feature set is inside their existing licence or sits in the next tranche, and whether anyone there has run it on the longitudinal chronic-disease cohorts they already hold. The second half of that is a weekend of compute, not a programme.

Third lead, and it is the opposite kind of item — early, platform-shaped, and the most interesting thing in it is a negative result.

Emmanouil Tampakakis in the Division of Cardiology, with Winston Timp in Biomedical Engineering, posted a study of how chemical modification of messenger R-N-A controls the heart's metabolic transition during development. The biology: as the heart matures it switches from glycolysis to oxidative phosphorylation, and if you knock out the methyltransferase that writes the most common internal R-N-A mark, mouse embryos develop cardiomyopathy and die. They trace that to a post-transcriptional axis running through the transcription factor Y-Y-one onto the oxidative phosphorylation genes.

The method is why it is on the radar. Two instruments, both of which make this a platform rather than a result. The first is direct nanopore sequencing of R-N-A, which reads the modification on individual molecules and therefore gives you the fraction of transcripts modified at a given site rather than a bulk average. The second is a C-R-I-S-P-R dead-Cas-thirteen system in human stem cells that installs the mark at a chosen site — a write head, not just a read head.

What they found with those two tools together is a bimodal code. Transcripts with a high fraction of modified molecules get degraded. Transcripts with a low fraction get stabilized. Same mark, opposite outcomes, and the variable is stoichiometry. That reframes the mark as a rheostat rather than a switch, and it explains why a decade of bulk-level studies disagreed with each other.

Then comes the candid part, and it is the reason I would not build a company on this today. When they used the write head to install the mark at defined sites, the effect on the target was, in their words, strongly locus- and context-dependent. Which is to say: the programmable epitranscriptomic editor works, and what it does depends on where you point it in a way they cannot yet predict. For a therapeutic platform whose pitch would be "we tune gene dosage without touching D-N-A," that is the whole ballgame. A modality you cannot predict is a research tool.

So the honest read is: not a build, a watch, and a relationship. Matt Lawler — this is a Hopkins group pairing a clinical cardiology lab with the nanopore group, funded by the National Heart, Lung and Blood Institute and by Maryland Stem Cell Research Fund awards — the same state programme whose latest award cycle we walked through yesterday. The competing-interest statement is clean apart from Timp's unrelated nanopore patents, which suggests the position is open. The thing to ask is not about Y-Y-one. It is whether the stoichiometry rule generalizes — whether low-fraction versus high-fraction predicts outcome on transcripts outside the heart. If it does, the write head becomes steerable, and then it is a modality. If it does not, this stays a beautiful mechanism paper. That is a one-question diligence call and we should make it.

A third item I wanted to bring you did not clear the bar, and I will say why rather than skip it. The Lieber Institute posted a multi-omic spatial map of the human habenula this week — Kristen Maynard and Leonardo Collado-Torres, nine neuronal cell types across the medial and lateral subregions, with chromatin accessibility linked to depression and addiction loci. That is squarely in our neuropsychiatric lane and it is exactly the kind of target-generation substrate the Lieber relationship exists to produce. bioRxiv has not finished rendering the full text, so I have read the abstract and nothing else, and this show does not summarize papers it has not read. It stays on the list for next week.

That is the radar. Three Hopkins items, and the through-line is instrumentation: the durable thing is the measurement — long reads on D-N-A, long reads on R-N-A, and the repeat fraction of a genome nobody used to count — and in each case the measurement is what tells you the thing you were shipping was not what you thought. Portfolio Watch is next.
