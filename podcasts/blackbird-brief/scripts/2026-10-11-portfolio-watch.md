## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Sunday, October eleventh. The most important development this week is a document, not a deal. The F-D-A posted its full review package for the first broad RAS inhibitor, and buried in it is an allele-by-allele efficacy map that repositions both of our K-RAS-adjacent bets — and not in the direction you would guess.

Revolution Medicines got Rasonque — daraxonrasib — approved in late August for previously treated metastatic pancreatic cancer, on a Phase 3 that roughly doubled survival: thirteen point two months against six point six, hazard ratio point four zero. Clean win, broad label. On October seventh the agency posted the multidisciplinary review; on the eighth the stock fell six percent. Analysts mostly called the selloff overdone, and on the headline numbers they are right. But I have read the review, and the reason it moved the stock is the reason it matters to us.

The trial randomized five hundred patients regardless of RAS status. Forty-three percent carried K-RAS G-twelve-D, thirty-six percent G-twelve-V, twelve percent G-twelve-R. Now the exploratory response rates by allele. In G-twelve-V, the drug produced a fifty percent response rate against fifteen percent for chemotherapy, and an overall survival hazard ratio of point three two. That is spectacular. In G-twelve-D — the single most common allele, the biggest slice of the population — the response rate was twenty-four percent against ten, with a survival hazard ratio of point four nine. Real, meaningful, clearly worse than G-twelve-V. And in G-twelve-R, the response rate was seven percent for the drug against fourteen percent for chemotherapy. Two responders out of twenty-seven. The progression-free hazard ratio in that group is point eight zero with a confidence interval running from point four one to one point five six — it crosses one. Survival point six one, interval point two nine to one point three zero — also crosses one.

Two caveats before anyone over-reads this. These are exploratory subgroups, the numbers in each are small, and the agency itself says the G-twelve-R group still shows a trend toward benefit on survival with point estimates favoring the drug. Nobody has shown harm. And the approved label is completely allele-agnostic — it requires no RAS testing at all, just metastatic pancreatic adenocarcinoma after one prior line.

But here is the part I want the deal team to sit with. The agency did not just publish the table, it published a mechanistic explanation for it, and the explanation is about physics. Valine is bulky but neutral — no electrostatic conflict with the drug, and the best switch-one flexibility for the inhibitor to engage. Aspartate is smaller and accessible, but its negative charge creates competing interactions. And arginine is bulky and positively charged: the review's language is that the side chain sterically occludes the binding pocket in switch two and the charge electrostatically repels the inhibitor. On top of that, those tumors are less dependent on canonical RAS signaling in the first place.

Read that as an investor rather than a chemist. Every one of those failure modes is a property of fitting a small molecule into a pocket. Not one is a property of whether the mutant peptide gets chopped up, loaded onto an H-L-A molecule and displayed on the tumor surface. Those are different physics entirely.

So, Clasp. C-L-S-P-fifty-two-eighty-two is a T-C-R-mimic T-cell engager against K-RAS G-twelve-V presented on H-L-A-A-star-oh-three-oh-one, and it has been positioned — explicitly, since A-A-C-R — to layer on top of or after the small-molecule RAS class, and to work harder in tumors that have become resistant to it. The F-D-A has now told us, with its own data, that G-twelve-V is the allele where the small molecule works best. Fifty percent response rate, hazard ratio point three two. That is the hardest segment in which to show incremental benefit and the one with the least residual unmet need. Meanwhile the white space the agency just mapped in public — G-twelve-R, where a marketed drug underperforms chemotherapy on response, plus the non-G-twelve and Q-sixty-one tail where progression-free hazard ratios are above one — belongs to nobody.

I am not saying the G-twelve-V program is wrong — resistance is a real and growing population and the layering thesis stands. I am saying the competitive framing changed this week, and Hemaka and Eddie should want an answer to one question: does G-twelve-R generate a presentable peptide on a common H-L-A allele, and if so, has anyone claimed it? A large charged substitution is, if anything, the kind of change that makes a neoepitope more distinguishable, not less. That is a hypothesis, not a result — but one Clasp can test on its own platform in about a week, and the agency just handed them the commercial reason to run it. It also goes straight into the standing eligible-fraction row, because G-twelve-R is only twelve percent of patients and the H-L-A gate multiplies on top.

And Adventris, downstairs in the BioHub, gets a quieter gift. Their mutant-K-RAS vaccine covers the six common mutations as a set, G-twelve-R included. Nine days before this review posted, Merck paid four hundred million dollars upfront for a preclinical G-twelve-D-selective inhibitor. So: the market prices allele-specific chemistry at four hundred million with no human data, and then the agency publishes the dataset explaining why allele-specific chemistry is necessary — and shows one allele where pocket chemistry may never work well. A modality indifferent to the pocket, covering the alleles the pocket cannot reach, is a better story today than it was last Tuesday. Eddie, that belongs in the next conversation with that team.

Second item, shorter, and it touches Biolinco.

Chai Discovery announced a collaboration with G-S-K on Friday — their sixth large-pharma partner in nine months, terms undisclosed. The qualifying exercise is what matters. Chai's models generated binder designs for G-S-K's targets zero-shot, with no target-specific training; G-S-K ran them in its own wet lab and, per their Christopher Austin, found binders for every target tested with strong affinities across a diverse panel. No hit rates, no affinity values, no target count — treat the specifics as marketing until someone publishes.

The direction of travel is the point, and it is the same one we logged on Biolinco at the end of September, when four large pharmas made "machine learning competes on properties, not on targets" into industry policy. If generating a binder against an arbitrary target is becoming a commoditized zero-shot step, the scarce thing is not the design — it is expressing, characterizing and discriminating among thousands of candidates fast enough to matter, which is precisely what barcoded antibody production is for. Every one of these deals makes the front end cheaper and the back end the bottleneck. Avi, that is the framing for Biolinco's next partnering conversation: not "we make antibodies," but "we are the throughput layer the design models just created demand for."

Two things for Aletira, both out of the ophthalmology meeting in New Orleans, and together they bracket the problem.

The first is Saturday evening's release from Merck on remigromig, their tri-specific Wnt pathway agonist for diabetic macular edema — first-in-class mechanism, and the first thing in more than twenty years that is not an anti-V-E-G-F to clear a pivotal bar in that disease. Nine hundred eighty-four patients, randomized three ways against monthly ranibizumab, dosed every four weeks. At week fifty-two the two remigromig doses gained nine point one and eight point seven letters. Ranibizumab gained eleven point eight. Non-inferiority was met on both doses, and Merck is careful to say the gap is inside the margin and not clinically meaningful — but read the sentence that follows, which is that no secondary endpoint demonstrated superiority over ranibizumab. And the safety line is the real news: proliferative retinopathy adverse events ran six point seven and six point one percent against zero point nine for ranibizumab, with discontinuations four point nine and four point five against zero point nine. Merck's explanation is mechanistic — the drug repairs the blood-retinal barrier rather than suppressing new vessel growth, so it does not hold back neovascularization the way anti-V-E-G-F does. That may well be right. It is also a six-fold difference, and analyses are still underway.

Why this matters to us: it is the efficacy bar. A novel mechanism, a pivotal trial, nearly a thousand patients, and the result is non-inferior-but-numerically-behind the incumbent, with a safety signal attached. Any retinal program — gene therapy very much included — is being measured against monthly anti-V-E-G-F, and beating it on letters remains unsolved. When we build the Aletira retinal case, the honest comparator is eleven point eight letters, not the natural history.

The second item is the opposite philosophy. Also in New Orleans, on Friday, Nanoscope presented on son-vec — sonpiretigene isteparvovec — and said they are taking it straight into a randomized Phase 2 in geographic atrophy, with no separate Phase 1 in that population. In Stargardt patients with acuity of twenty-two-hundred or worse, the subgroup whose atrophy was confined to the macula gained an average of twelve E-T-D-R-S letters, with no serious adverse events reported. For calibration, their published six-patient Stargardt trial reported a mean gain of five and a half letters at forty-eight weeks, so twelve letters in a selected subgroup is a favorable slice, and the primate geographic-atrophy data supporting the Phase 2 is unpublished.

The reason I am putting this in front of Avi and Hugh is philosophical, not competitive. Son-vec delivers an opsin to the bipolar cells that survive after photoreceptors die and makes those cells directly light-sensitive. It is mutation-agnostic by construction, it goes in by a single office-based injection, and it does not care what gene broke — which means it sidesteps entirely the problem SELEXON exists to solve. No native promoter to respect, no expression level to tune, no dosage sensitivity, because it is not restoring a gene, it is installing a new function in a cell that never had one.

That is the competing philosophy in degenerative retina, and it is now moving to a randomized Phase 2 in the largest indication in the field. It does not threaten the cochlear program, where restoring a specific protein in a specific cell at a specific level is the whole job. But it sharpens a question we should be able to answer crisply: where is faithful expression control actually load-bearing, and where is a blunt gene-agnostic graft simply good enough? We should know which indications are which before a partner asks.

That is the watch. The RAS review is the item — read the table, not the headline. Talk soon.
