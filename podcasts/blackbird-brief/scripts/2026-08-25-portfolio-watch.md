## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Tuesday, August twenty-fifth. Yesterday I told you the agency is being flexible with gene therapy sponsors and gave you three cases to prove it. Within about eighteen hours, one of those three was back on clinical hold — for a reason that reaches every adeno-associated virus program we own or will ever own. It is not an efficacy problem. It is a genome problem with a four-year fuse.

Start with what happened. Yesterday morning Regenxbio disclosed that F-D-A has placed a clinical hold on its Hunter syndrome gene therapy. The trigger is imaging. In five of forty-eight participants in the pivotal program, spine M-R-I found either a small nodule or a small cystic mass. Those patients were dosed intracisternally or intraventricularly — into the cerebrospinal fluid — roughly three to six years ago. Nobody has symptoms. Investigators called the findings non-serious, radiologists believe they are likely benign, nothing turned up on brain imaging, and the company is careful to say there is no clinical or pathological evidence confirming what these are or what caused them — and it makes the fair point that spine M-R-I is not routine in Hunter syndrome, so nobody knows the background rate in these children. The consequence is unambiguous. The resubmission that F-D-A agreed in June needed no additional studies and no untreated control arm is off the table in the near term, and the stock fell twenty-two percent before the open. Curran Simpson's line was that the findings are unique and limited to the Hunter syndrome program.

Now the part that actually matters, which is why anyone was looking at these spines at all. Back on January twenty-eighth, the sister program — the Hurler syndrome therapy, same central-nervous-system delivery route, same platform — was put on hold because an asymptomatic five-year-old, four years after intraventricular dosing, was found to have a tumor in the ventricle. Genetic analysis of that tumor found an adeno-associated virus vector integration event associated with overexpression of a growth-promoting gene, P-L-A-G one. F-D-A held both programs at the time, on the explicit reasoning that they share risk. That single event is what prompted the expanded M-R-I surveillance. The surveillance is what found the five.

Three things for us out of this, and the first is a correction to something I told you five days ago.

Hugh, Avi — on the twentieth I gave you the ladder. Constitutive viral promoter, then synthetic tissue-specific promoter, then native regulatory element, which is Ultragenyx at the promoter and Skylark at the three-prime U-T-R, and then SELEXON at the splicing layer. That ladder is real and it is still the strongest external citation the thesis has. But every rung on it answers the same question — where does the transgene get read. Insertional mutagenesis is a different question: where does the vector land in the genome. SELEXON governs neither the capsid nor the integration site. Two weeks ago the durable line was that expression control is not an immunology answer. Add the harder one: it is not a genotoxicity answer either. Say both sentences out loud in a Series A meeting before an investor says them to you, because the claim that survives all of this intact is the narrow one — SELEXON solves off-target expression, the problem that decides whether the therapy works in the cell it was aimed at. That claim is untouched this morning. The general safety-platform framing is now falsifiable in public twice over.

Second, and this is the actionable one — look at the clock. Four years to the tumor. Three to six years to the spinal masses. Skylark's trial in our lead indication completes primary follow-up in December of twenty twenty-seven, across ten children. No hearing gene therapy program in the world, theirs or ours, will have imaging at this latency by the time it asks the agency for anything. And the only reason anyone found these lesions is that a regulator made a company go look after a tumor turned up in a sibling program. Geoff, Hugh: put a long-term imaging and neoplasm surveillance plan into the pre-investigational-new-drug package now, before it is asked for, and price a long follow-up commitment into the raise rather than discovering it inside a review cycle. Being the sponsor that proposed the surveillance is worth real credibility with an office that has now seen two of these inside eight months. And note that an intracochlear injection is also delivery into a small closed fluid compartment through a defined route — vector concentrated in a confined space is the shared feature here, not the indication.

Third, this amends yesterday's instruction, and sharpens it rather than softening it. I said get it in writing, because a productive conversation with an acting official does not survive that official. Still true. But look at what just happened to the best possible version of written. Regenxbio had a Type A meeting in June and came out of it with F-D-A reaffirming, on the record, no additional studies and no untreated control arm. That is as clean a written agreement as a sponsor ever gets, and it is worth nothing this morning — not because anyone changed their mind, but because the safety database moved underneath it. So the instruction is now two-part. Get the endpoint and the statistical analysis plan in writing, and separately assume that no written agreement is durable against new safety data. That reorders the pre-I-N-D agenda: the highest-value thing to negotiate is not the endpoint, it is the long-term safety dataset you will be judged against, because that is the document with the power to void the other one.

Eddie, one diligence note from the same event. In January the agency held both programs, and the stated reasoning was shared risk across the platform — one integration event in one indication stopped an unrelated program running on the same capsid. For any adeno-associated virus company we look at, that becomes a specific question: what else runs on this capsid, whose trials are they, and can somebody else's imaging finding stop ours. That is a question about dependencies rather than science, and it is cheap to ask early.

That is the lead. Next, the item that closes yesterday's open question. Capricor. Late yesterday the company announced that F-D-A extended the action date on the Duchenne cell therapy from August twenty-second to November twenty-second. The mechanism matters more than the date. Following the July advisory committee, Capricor submitted an amendment carrying twenty-four-month open-label extension data from the pivotal trial plus additional robustness analyses, and asked the agency to review the whole package in support of a refined proposed indication focused on upper limb function — the primary endpoint the trial actually hit. C-B-E-R accepted the amendment, citing the significant unmet need in Duchenne, classified it a major amendment, and took the three months that classification allows. The stock rose as much as sixteen percent.

Two readings. The quick one is about our own discipline: there were two unconfirmed accounts in the record yesterday, a sourced report on the twentieth that this was heading for a rejection and the company's own comment implying an extension, and declining to pick between them was the right call. A sourced pre-decision scoop is not a decision.

The substantive reading is that this is the cleanest example we now have of an agency rigorous on evidence and flexible on path. An advisory committee voted nine to three against on a cardiac claim whose statistics the agency itself disputed. The sponsor's response was not to keep arguing about the heart. It was to stop making the claim it could not prove and refile around the one it could. The agency took that. For a platform whose whole premise is restricting expression to the cells that need it, this is the same instinct one layer down — a regulator rewarding sponsors who narrow. Use it in the room.

The candid limits, in one breath. A major amendment is not an approval; it is ninety more days and, if it works, a narrower label than the company originally wanted. And there is a governance half nobody should skip. On the twenty-first, Kaos Capital — a significant and growing shareholder — demanded an immediate meeting, two independent directors, a board-led mergers and strategic alternatives committee, and a capital-preservation plan, noting cash had fallen eighty million dollars to two hundred thirty-eight million by the end of June. Their sentence is the one to keep: conviction in a lead program is not a license for a board to concentrate all of a public company's capital, risk and future in a single regulatory outcome. That is not a public-company-only problem. Aletira is a single-asset company at seed stage heading toward a regulatory gate, and what the board is doing about concentration risk is a question a good Series A lead will ask.

Last, a short amendment to yesterday's lead, because I left out something material. I told you the review chain above our future application is held by one person wearing two acting hats. That remains true of the Office of Therapeutic Products — no permanent director since June of last year. What I failed to mention is that on Wednesday the nineteenth the president nominated a permanent F-D-A commissioner: Heidi Overton, M-D, Ph.D., currently deputy director of the White House Domestic Policy Council, pending Senate confirmation. She did her general surgery residency at Johns Hopkins and her Ph.D. in clinical investigation at the Bloomberg School. The prospective commissioner of F-D-A is Hopkins-trained.

Don't overweight it. R-B-C's read is that she has essentially no drug-regulatory experience and would lean heavily on the White House and the Health and Human Services secretary on review flexibility, so nobody should model a friendlier agency — and it does not touch the vacancy that actually governs us, so yesterday's instruction stands. But the top of the agency is about to stop being temporary, which makes the survive-the-turnover argument a near-term one rather than a permanent condition. And Matt, Maisha, there is a quiet network question worth answering before anybody needs the answer: who at Hopkins overlapped with her in training, and is there a legitimate channel for the institution to be useful on cell and gene therapy policy. Not lobbying. Being a known quantity.

The gene therapy field just learned that its most dangerous failure mode has a latency measured in years, is invisible without imaging nobody was doing, and can stop a second program that never had the event.
