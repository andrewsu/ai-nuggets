## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Saturday, October thirty-first. The most important development for us this morning is a company formation that reads like an instruction manual for a problem we will have: when Jazz Pharmaceuticals bought Actio Biosciences in August for its lead epilepsy asset, it agreed at signing to spin the rest of the pipeline back out. Yesterday that leftover pipeline launched as Setara Biosciences, with ninety million dollars behind it and Jazz holding a minority stake in the thing it just declined to keep.

Here is the shape of it. In August, Jazz paid eight hundred and twenty million dollars in cash upfront, against a headline of up to one point three billion, to acquire Actio. What Jazz wanted was one molecule — a blocker of a malfunctioning potassium channel, for epilepsy caused by mutations in a gene called K-C-N-T-one. Everything else Actio had built stayed behind. David Goldstein, the human geneticist who founded and ran Actio, is president and chief executive of the new company. His chief scientific officer and chief financial officer are both Actio alumni. Many of Actio's original investors re-upped — Deerfield, Canaan — and Jazz itself took the minority position it negotiated back in August. The lead asset going forward is a T-R-P-V-four inhibitor, formerly A-B-S zero eight seven one, now S-T-A-R zero eight seven one, aimed at Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease type two-C and, separately, at overactive bladder. It has cleared a Phase One in healthy volunteers and goes into a Phase One-b in patients with the severe form of Charcot-Marie-Tooth by the end of this year. Behind it sit discovery programs in other rare diseases, epilepsy and migraine.

There are three reads here and the first one is structural. This is the third independent confirmation in seventy-two hours of the rule we wrote into the reference on Wednesday and restated on Thursday: entities follow assets, not platforms. Formation Bio's six-hundred-million-dollar fund puts one corporate entity around each program over one shared development organization. Flagship consolidated three platform companies into Etcelera and simultaneously carved the one real clinical asset out into its own vehicle. Setara is the same rule arriving from the third direction, and it is the direction that actually applies to us — not a studio reorganizing itself, but an acquirer taking the asset it wants and handing the rest back to the team that made it, with a stake retained. For Matt and Eddie, the actionable version is a sentence in a term sheet, not a slide: the carve-out was negotiated at the time of the acquisition, not improvised afterward. If we expect a Blackbird spinout's lead program to be the thing a buyer wants — and for most of our programs that is the base case — then the disposition of everything else belongs in the deal documents at signing, along with whether the originating entity keeps a piece. Jazz did it. Astellas did a cousin of it on Thursday with the Australian co-promote retention. Write it down as standard.

The second read is the one I would spend an hour on, and it goes to Avi and Anthony and the endpoint question that has been open since September tenth. Listen to what Goldstein said about why the two halves of the same company separated. Epilepsy, he said, has clearly defined goals — you lower the seizure rate, and you can count seizures. Charcot-Marie-Tooth is multifaceted and slow-progressing: a variety of muscle groups get weaker and compromise function in complicated ways, and then you have the question of what is the best way to assess the impact of the disease and whether therapy is improving it. That is the same molecular platform, the same team, the same genetics-first thesis on both sides of the line. The half with a countable endpoint was worth eight hundred and twenty million dollars in cash. The half without one was worth a ninety-million-dollar Series A. I am not saying the biology is equivalent — it is not. I am saying that the single largest visible driver of the valuation gap inside one company, split on one day by one buyer, was whether the primary endpoint is a number a machine produces or a judgment a rater makes. That is a price tag on the open item. For Aletira it strengthens the argument we are already making: auditory brainstem response thresholds are machine-measured, per-ear, with an untreated ear available as its own control, and that should be stated in the pre-I-N-D narrative rather than left for a reviewer to infer. For NuGate it is the exposure, not the comfort — the functional endpoint in ALS and frontotemporal dementia is the rater-scored side of that line, and we should be writing against it now.

The third read is smaller and commercial. One molecule, two indications: a rare genetic neuropathy, and overactive bladder, which affects tens of millions of Americans. The rare indication carries the mechanism and the regulatory path, the common indication carries the market, and the same chemistry serves both. Several of our early-stage themes could be framed that way and currently are not.

Second item, and it is a bad morning for a single-asset company. Acumen Pharmaceuticals reported topline Phase Two results for sabirnetug, also known as A-C-U one ninety-three, in early Alzheimer's disease. The trial was called ALTITUDE-A-D. At eighteen months there was no meaningful difference from placebo on cognition and function, and it missed every key secondary endpoint. The drug was generally well tolerated, with about ten percent of treated participants showing amyloid-related imaging abnormalities with edema. Acumen has terminated development, including the open-label extension, is implementing cash conservation measures and is exploring strategic options. The stock fell roughly eighty percent in premarket trading, to forty-four cents. Sabirnetug was the company's only program.

Two things for us. The first is a hypothesis, not a molecule. Sabirnetug was designed to bind selectively to toxic soluble amyloid-beta oligomers, on the theory that the oligomers — not the plaques — are the early driver of neurodegeneration. That is a clean, specific, well-credentialed hypothesis, and eighteen months of dosing in the right patients said no. For Hemaka and the NuGate design team, this is the negative case that pairs with the Alnylam Huntington's result we discussed on the twenty-seventh. There we said target engagement is not prior probability. Here is the other end of it: a mechanistically coherent story, confirmed binding, a fast-track designation, and no clinical effect. That is precisely the situation the F-D-A's substantial-evidence redraft has in mind when it says the appropriate significance level depends on the pretrial probability that the drug works. Second, note what survives at Acumen. It is not the antibody. It is a blood-brain-barrier delivery partnership signed last year with J-C-R Pharmaceuticals. The company is reviewing that as a strategic option. A delivery technology with no molecule behind it is a thin asset, and the lesson for portfolio construction is the obvious one — this is why a one-program company with an eighteen-month binary is a structure, not a plan.

Third item, and it lands directly on one of our early-stage themes. Aer Therapeutics reported topline Phase Two-a results for fexlamose in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and it failed on both axes. Fexlamose is an inhaled thiol that cleaves the disulfide cross-links between mucin polymers — chemically liquefying the mucus plugs that block airways in up to half of moderate-to-severe C-O-P-D and asthma patients. Ninety milligrams by nebulizer daily for twenty-eight days, a hundred and nineteen moderate-to-severe patients across six countries, more than three-quarters with severe or very severe airflow limitation. No improvement on trough F-E-V-one, no improvement on forced vital capacity or other spirometry. And no overall reduction from baseline in the mucus plug segment score, the imaging biomarker the trial carried to show target engagement and pharmacodynamics. More respiratory adverse events on drug than on placebo, including exacerbations, and considerably more discontinuations.

The read for the C-O-P-D theme, and this goes to whoever picks up that file. When we logged the alarmin layer as crowded, the open question we left was whether Blackbird's C-O-P-D work points downstream of T-S-L-P, and whether that positioning survives. Fexlamose is as far downstream as the field goes — it does not modulate the biology that makes the mucus, it dissolves the mucus. And the uncomfortable part is not that the clinical endpoint missed. It is that the imaging biomarker missed too, which means this readout does not tell us whether mucus plugs are the wrong target or whether this compound simply did not do enough to them. A trial where the biomarker moves and the function does not is informative and expensive. A trial where neither moves is only expensive. If we are going to put a program downstream in the airway, the P-D instrument has to be sensitive enough that a negative function endpoint still leaves us knowing something, and we should decide that before the money goes in, not after.

Fourth, two large buyers described their appetite on the same Friday morning, and the descriptions agree in a way that matters for exit planning. AstraZeneca announced a three-year program it is calling Reshaping for Sustainable Growth: two billion dollars of cost to deliver one and a half billion in savings, which Pascal Soriot was emphatic is reallocation and not shrinkage — no layoffs expected, with reskilling, hiring and technology in the mix. The quarter underneath it was strong, sixteen point two billion dollars in revenue and eight percent ahead of consensus, and Soriot named the areas he is shopping in: oncology, cardiovascular, rare disease, immunology and respiratory. Sanofi, the same morning, was blunter. Belén Garijo said the company will use its cash flow for business development and M-and-A with a focus on later-stage and commercial opportunities, and gave the reason — moving to later-stage commercial assets lets them de-risk scientifically and clinically while leveraging the commercial organization. That is against a quarter in which Dupixent grew nearly thirty-five percent and now accounts for thirty-nine percent of the company, facing a twenty thirty-one patent expiry.

So what for us. Both of these companies have named rare disease, immunology and respiratory as shopping lists, which covers a lot of our board. But read the maturity signal honestly: the stated preference at both is later-stage and commercial, which is several rungs above where a Blackbird asset is when it is ready to move. That does not make them irrelevant — it makes the structure of the first transaction the thing to get right. The realistic counterparty for a de-risked, pre-clinical-stage Blackbird program is not a large-cap paying for commercial certainty. It is an option on an I-N-D package of the Astellas and Audax shape we logged Thursday, or a specialist buyer, or a fund like Formation's. The big-pharma conversation is the second transaction, and we should stop designing the first one as though it were the second.

To close, one carry item and one piece of housekeeping. Gilead's Kite unit disclosed a partial clinical hold on two C-A-R-T cell therapy trials, reported Friday alongside a pushed-back F-D-A decision date for Agios. I could not read the underlying coverage this morning — the outlet is blocking us and Gilead's own quarterly release discloses nothing specific — so I am not going to characterize which trials or why. It is flagged to carry; if it touches the autoimmune side rather than oncology it is a Winnow item and I will have it tomorrow.

Quiet elsewhere. Nothing new on Aletira's own program, on the G-P-R fifty-two NewCo, on Clasp, Biolinco, D-E-L-F-I, Georgiamune or 1104health, and nothing dated from Johns Hopkins, U-M-B or the Lieber Institute inside the window. Tomorrow is Sunday, so you get both a Sourcing Radar and a Portfolio Watch. Have a good Saturday.
