Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief, Portfolio Watch for Wednesday, July twenty-ninth. The most important development for the portfolio today is a regulatory one, and it lands squarely on eleven-oh-four health: the FDA finalized three guidances that pull down the eligibility walls around cancer clinical trials. It reads as a structural tailwind for the one non-therapeutic asset we own — the community-oncology enrollment marketplace — and it comes wrapped in a bigger federal push worth understanding.

Here is what happened. On Monday the FDA's Oncology Center of Excellence finalized three guidance documents, published in the Federal Register on Tuesday, that tell trial sponsors to stop excluding patients for reasons that aren't scientifically justified. The first covers performance status — for years trials have enrolled only the fittest patients, the ones who score well on the standard functional scales, and this guidance says broaden that to include people with more real-world functional impairment. The second covers laboratory values — stop using blanket blood-count and lab cutoffs that screen out patients whose numbers are off because of their disease or their age or their comorbidities rather than any actual safety risk. The third covers washout periods and concomitant medications — justify the waiting periods between therapies scientifically instead of applying a one-size blanket exclusion. The throughline is the same in all three: make eligibility criteria fit the population you actually intend to treat, not an idealized trial-only cohort.

The number the FDA leads with is the one that matters for us. Fewer than five percent of cancer patients in treatment are enrolled in a clinical trial, while more than seventy percent say they'd be willing. That gap — between willingness and enrollment — is precisely the market eleven-oh-four health exists to close. And these guidances don't sit in isolation. They're an execution step inside Operation TrialBlazer, the department-wide HHS initiative announced back on June twenty-second to rebuild U.S. clinical-research infrastructure — spanning the FDA, the NIH including the National Cancer Institute, ARPA-H, and, notably, the HHS Office of Inspector General. So this is not a one-off guidance drop; it's the front edge of a coordinated federal effort to widen the funnel into trials.

So what does it mean for eleven-oh-four health, candidly. The clean read is tailwind. Recall the thesis: the company pivoted from a patient-facing app to a clinician-embedded marketplace that surfaces trial options inside the community oncologist's workflow, and it uses pharma-sponsor subsidies to pay for the trial-coordination work that has historically gone unreimbursed. Loosening eligibility criteria directly expands the pool of patients a given community oncologist can actually match to an open trial. When the eligibility net was narrow, a lot of workflow-embedded matching effort dead-ended at "your patient doesn't qualify." Widen performance-status, lab-value, and washout criteria across the board, and the match rate at the point of care goes up — which is exactly the metric that makes a workflow-embedded product sticky and makes the pharma subsidy economically rational for the sponsor. More eligible patients per screen is more value per screen. That's the good news, and it's real.

Now the nuance, because the team should hear both sides. Broader eligibility increases the matching surface, but it does not by itself solve the reimbursement gap — the reason community sites don't run trials isn't only that their patients get screened out, it's that the coordination work doesn't pay. That unmet need is the core of eleven-oh-four health's model, and this guidance doesn't touch it; if anything it raises the stakes on the subsidy mechanism being clean. And that brings up the one genuine watch-item. Operation TrialBlazer explicitly includes the HHS Office of Inspector General and a payment-and-access-reform workstream. We've flagged before that the pharma-funded physician-coordination-subsidy model lives close to anti-kickback and safe-harbor questions. A federal initiative that is simultaneously trying to accelerate enrollment and has OIG at the table is the exact policy space where the rules around who can pay a physician for trial-related work could get either clarified — helpfully — or tightened. Rose Wang's team should be reading the TrialBlazer payment-reform track as closely as they're reading the eligibility guidances, because the same initiative that expands their addressable market also governs the legality of their revenue mechanism. Net-net: a clear tailwind on the demand side, a watch-item on the payment-model side, and a reason to make sure the compliance story is airtight before the national rollout.

One more competitive note in the same lane. The trial-matching incumbents are moving on the same thesis — Massive Bio's tie-up with the FOMAT community-site network last week, Tempus's TIME network expanding into phase-one activation. A federal push to widen eligibility is a rising tide that lifts all the matching platforms, so the tailwind is not proprietary to us. Eleven-oh-four health's differentiation has to stay on the clinician-workflow-plus-subsidy model rather than on raw AI matching, because the matching layer is getting commoditized fast.
