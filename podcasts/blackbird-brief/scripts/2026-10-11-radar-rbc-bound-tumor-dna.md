## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Sourcing Radar for Sunday, October eleventh. Two leads this week. The first says that most of the tumor DNA circulating in a lung cancer patient is not in the plasma at all — it is stuck to the outside of their red blood cells, and the plasma assay everyone has built a company on is reading the smaller fraction. The second hands an already-drugged receptor a brand-new fibrotic indication, from a Hopkins lab that owns the receptor family.

Start with the red cells, because if it holds up it touches two of our positions directly.

The paper comes from Nilam Mangalmurti and Steven Albelda at Penn's Perelman School, in the Lung Biology Institute and the pulmonary critical care division. Their prior work established something most oncology people have never thought about: red blood cells are not inert bags of hemoglobin. They carry Toll-like receptor nine on their surface, they bind circulating DNA, and in sepsis that binding drives red cell senescence. This paper asks the obvious next question. If red cells scavenge host and pathogen DNA, do they scavenge tumor DNA?

They do, and the details are what make it interesting. Red cells acquire tumor DNA within five minutes of exposure. The signal is detectable from as few as one thousand red cells. It survives seven weeks frozen at minus twenty. And critically, it is on the surface, not inside — treating intact, non-permeabilized cells with DNase strips the signal off. So this is not uptake, it is tethering.

Then they show it is regulated rather than passive stickiness. Blocking Toll-like receptor nine with an antibody significantly reduces the mutant copies a red cell picks up. Stripping sialic acid off the surface with neuraminidase increases capture, and increases how much of that receptor is detectable — the glycocalyx was shielding it. Do both, and the enhancement goes away. That is a mechanism with a knob on it.

In mice carrying human lung adenocarcinoma xenografts, the mutant K-RAS signal on red cells tracked tumor burden by bioluminescence. In a subcutaneous model, mutant DNA was detectable on red cells in every single animal, and completely absent from matched plasma from those same animals.

Then twelve patients with non-small-cell lung cancer, nine late stage and three early. Every one of them had a mutation already identified by tissue sequencing — a mix of E-G-F-R and K-RAS drivers. The red cell fraction recovered the correct tissue-identified mutation in all twelve. When they compared equal volumes, five microliters of red cells against five microliters of plasma, the tumor DNA was significantly higher in the red cells. At the patient level, more than ninety-nine percent of the detectable mutant DNA was red-cell-bound rather than plasma-derived. And among the six patients who also had a commercial plasma-based liquid biopsy run on them, that commercial assay found the driver mutation in three. Three out of six, against twelve out of twelve.

Now the candid read, because I do not want anyone walking into a meeting overselling this.

Twelve patients is twelve patients. More importantly, this is the easy direction of the problem. They knew the mutation from tissue first, then went looking for it with q-P-C-R and droplet digital P-C-R, which are confirmatory assays. Finding a mutation you already know about is a fundamentally different task from screening an undiagnosed person for something you have not specified. Nothing here is done at genome scale, and the modern fragmentomics thesis depends on genome-scale patterns, not single-locus calls. The mouse plasma-negative result is in N-S-G mice, which are immunodeficient and where plasma tumor DNA is low to begin with, so that comparison flatters the red cells.

But the structural point survives. Every liquid biopsy workflow in existence spins the blood, keeps the plasma, and throws the red cells in the biohazard bin. If a meaningful share of the informative molecules is riding on the discarded fraction, that is not an incremental sensitivity improvement. That is a claim that the field has been sampling the wrong compartment.

For us that splits two ways. For Artemyx, this is squarely supportive. Artemyx is not a screening company — it is longitudinal monitoring in chronic disease, where you know what you are tracking and you are measuring it over and over in the same patient. Known target, serial measurement, better sensitivity, and a sample that survives seven weeks in a freezer is almost exactly the use case this data supports. Someone should put the question to that team this week: what happens to our limit of detection if the input is the red cell pellet instead of, or in addition to, the plasma.

For DELFI it is a harder and more interesting question, and I would rather we ask it than have a competitor ask it first. DELFI's thesis is genome-wide fragmentation patterns in plasma. If a large fraction of tumor-derived molecules is sequestered on red cells, and that sequestration is receptor-mediated and modulated by surface glycans, then the plasma fragment distribution is not a neutral readout of what the tumor shed. It is what is left over after a selective filter removed some of it. That does not invalidate anything — fragmentomics plainly works — but it is a real question about the input, and Victor's group is the right one to answer it.

On the asset itself: it is Penn, not a partner institution, which under our mandate is fine as long as the company gets built in Baltimore. The IP shape is unusual and worth understanding before anyone gets excited. The red cell as a sample compartment is close to unpatentable on its own. What is potentially claimable is the tunable part — using glycan remodeling and receptor engagement to deliberately increase capture — which is a process claim with some teeth, and whatever enrichment chemistry you need to get clean tumor DNA off a red cell pellet at scale. Hemaka or Virginia should read the methods and tell us whether this is a company or a feature that a Guardant or a Natera absorbs in eighteen months.

The second lead is shorter and it is a partner institution.

Xinzhong Dong's lab at Hopkins — Snyder Neuroscience and Dermatology, and he is Howard Hughes — posted on systemic sclerosis with Ami Shah, who runs the Hopkins Scleroderma Center. Dong's lab defined the Mas-related G-protein-coupled receptor family in the first place, so the pedigree here is about as good as it gets.

The claim is that mast cells drive scleroderma skin fibrosis through one specific receptor. In the bleomycin skin fibrosis model, knocking out the mouse version of that receptor gives you less collagen deposition, lower T-N-F-alpha, interleukin one beta and matrix metalloproteinase eight, and mast cells that do not accumulate in the skin at all. They also checked the obvious artifact — whether bleomycin just degranulates mast cells directly through the human receptor — and it does not.

The piece I find commercially most interesting is the itch. This is the first report of pruritus in this fibrosis model. Wild-type mice start scratching more by day seven; the knockouts do not. That matters because itch affects something like half of scleroderma patients, it shows up early, and it is almost entirely unstudied — there has never been an itch-focused study in any preclinical model of this disease.

The human side is eleven patients with paired lesional and non-lesional biopsies. Lesional skin has more mast cells, consistently. Lesional mast cell count correlates with the modified Rodnan skin score at an r of point six three and a p of point zero five two, and with patient-reported skin tightness at a rho of point seven six.

So be honest about that: the headline severity correlation does not clear significance. Eleven patients, and a p of point zero five two is a p of point zero five two. This is early, and it is target validation, not an asset.

Which is exactly why I think the right move is not a newco. The human version of this receptor is already a clinically pursued target — there are antagonists in development for chronic urticaria and mast-cell-driven disease. Nobody needs us to discover it. What Hopkins has that those companies do not is the Scleroderma Center: the cohort, the scoring expertise, and now a mechanistic rationale for a new indication. That makes this a method-of-treatment and biomarker play, not composition of matter, and the move is a conversation with whoever already owns a clinical-stage antagonist.

One tactical note on why the itch angle is the wedge. Fibrosis endpoints in scleroderma are slow, expensive and have buried a lot of programs. Pruritus reads out early, it is patient-reported, and it is a real unmet need with nothing approved. A trial that takes itch as the proximal endpoint in an orphan indication is a much cheaper first shot on goal than a Rodnan score. Eddie, the diligence question for J-H-T-V is simply whether anyone has filed on method of use for this indication, and if not, how fast they can.

One deferral to flag. The University of Maryland School of Medicine's neurosurgery department posted a blood-brain-barrier-crossing multivalent T-G-F-beta trap built on a human ferritin nanocage for glioma — systemically dosed, engineered to remodel the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and potentiate a checkpoint inhibitor. On the abstract that is a composition-of-matter platform at a partner institution aimed at a problem nothing systemic has solved. I could not get the full text; bioRxiv rate-limited that specific document on every channel I tried, repeatedly, over an hour. Under our own rule I will not summarize a paper I have only read the abstract of, so it does not get a slot today. It stays on the list and I will come back to it this week.

That is the radar. The red cell paper is the one to act on — two phone calls, one to Artemyx and one to Victor's group, and both of them should happen before someone else makes them. Talk soon.
