## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Portfolio Watch for Tuesday, August eighteenth. The most important development on this board is not from yesterday — it's from a week ago, and it went by us. A company came out of stealth and put the first child into a clinical trial of a gene therapy for the most common genetic cause of deafness, using an architecture that is, in its two essential parts, the architecture Aletira sells. I'd rather hand you that late than not at all, so it leads.

The company is Skylark Bio, out of Cambridge, Massachusetts. On August eleventh they announced the first patient dosed in a study called SONIX, a Phase one-two for hearing loss caused by mutations in the gene G-J-B-2. That gene encodes connexin twenty-six, a gap-junction protein, and mutations in it are the most common genetic cause of non-syndromic deafness — on the order of two hundred thousand people across the United States and Europe, with no approved treatment that addresses the cause. STAT's coverage called it the holy grail of hearing-loss targets, and that isn't hype. It's the volume target in this field.

Here's the part to sit with. The therapy is a single injection into one cochlea, and it pairs an engineered A-A-V capsid designed for broad transduction with what the company describes as cell-specific expression control, in order to deliver a working copy of the gene to the cochlear supporting cells that natively express connexin twenty-six and rebuild the gap-junction network. Read that back as a sentence about platform design: get in broadly with the vector, then restrict where the payload is actually expressed. That is the SELEXON premise, and somebody else just put it into a child. The trial runs in children from nine months to seven years, with preliminary data guided for the end of this year and more in twenty twenty-seven.

The read cuts three ways, and two of them are good for us.

The first is validation, and it's uncomfortably precise validation. Our pitch has been that the vector gets you delivery and the expression-control layer is where the value and the safety live. A clinical-stage company has now bet a first-in-human program on exactly that division of labor, which means the thesis gets clinical proof from someone else's trial on someone else's money. That's genuinely useful for Geoff Lynn's Series A, and it belongs in the deck as a slide about the field converging on our architecture, not as something an investor surfaces at you across the table.

The second is competitive, and it's why this matters more than a landscape note. The hearing-loss gene therapy field has consolidated onto this target. The other target, otoferlin, is effectively spoken for — Regeneron's Otarmeni took accelerated approval on April twenty-third as the first gene therapy through the national priority voucher program, and that indication is roughly fifty American babies a year. Sensorion discontinued its own otoferlin program in June and named its G-J-B-2 candidate the lead instead. So the small beachhead is taken, and the large target now has a clinical-stage first mover plus a funded European fast-follower. If hereditary hearing loss is still the lead indication signal for Aletira, that lane got materially more crowded while we were looking elsewhere.

The third is technical, and it's what I'd put in front of Avi and Hugh Wells today. Connexin twenty-six has to go into supporting cells, not hair cells, and it has to land at roughly the right level, because gap junctions are dose-sensitive and overexpressing a connexin in the wrong cochlear cell is its own kind of toxicity. That is the sharpest use case for splicing-based expression restriction anywhere in the ear. Skylark says cell-specific expression control; they haven't said how. So the diligence question is narrow and urgent — what is their control element? If it's a cell-type-specific promoter, we have a real differentiation story about doing this at the R-N-A level with better fidelity and a smaller cassette. If they've filed on splicing-based control, we need to know before the raise, not after. Pull the trial protocol and the patent estate, and get an answer this week.

That's the lead. The fresh tape is thin, but it has one item that sharpens something I've been telling you slightly wrong.

On Friday the fourteenth, Capricor's stock jumped sixty-eight percent after its chief executive said the company will amend its filing for deramiocel — a cell therapy for Duchenne muscular dystrophy — adding twenty-four-month extension data and new analyses, and narrowing the indication down to the primary endpoint it actually hit, upper-limb function. The F-D-A has indicated it's willing to review that amendment and extend the decision date once it arrives. Recall that an advisory committee voted nine to three against effectiveness at the end of July, on a cardiac claim whose statistics the agency itself had disputed.

Here's why that lands on us. I have been framing the Ultragenyx decision as a read on whether the gene-therapy safety bar ratcheted up after the Elevidys deaths. That's still true, but it's half the picture, and the Capricor sequence shows the other half. This is an agency that is rigorous on your evidence and flexible on your path — it will hold you to what your statistics actually support, and it will not treat an advisory committee vote as the end of the conversation if you're willing to narrow the claim to what you proved. That's consistent with what's happened since the spring leadership turnover, where the rare-disease posture has visibly moved from adversarial toward partnership.

And that reframing is a better argument for our platform than the one I've been making. An agency that rewards narrowing is an agency that rewards precision. A company whose whole proposition is restricting expression to the cells that need it is selling into a regulator currently rewarding sponsors for restricting claims to the patients they can prove. Same instinct, one layer down — a stronger and more durable story for Hemaka and Eddie to put under the raise than "the bar went up, so selectivity matters more."
