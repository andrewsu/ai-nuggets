# The Blackbird Brief — Portfolio Watch — 2026-10-06

## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Tuesday, October sixth. The most important development for the portfolio: the first FDA-authorized treatment for the negative symptoms of schizophrenia arrived yesterday, and it is not a drug. That changes a sentence in the GPR fifty-two pitch, and it gives us an endpoint and a bar to beat.

Start there. Click Therapeutics announced that FDA granted five-ten-k clearance to Motivista, a prescription smartphone app, as an add-on to antipsychotic therapy for adults whose positive symptoms are stable. It is the first FDA-authorized product indicated specifically for negative symptoms: low motivation, loss of pleasure, social withdrawal. The evidence is CONVOKE, a Phase Three published last month in JAMA Network Open. Four hundred fifty-seven patients across sixty-six US sites were randomized to the app or a sham digital control for sixteen weeks. The primary endpoint was the motivation-and-pleasure subscale of the Clinical Assessment Interview for Negative Symptoms, known as CAINS-MAP. The app improved it by six point eight points against four point two for control. That is a difference of two point six points and a Cohen's d of about a third, which the authors themselves call small to moderate. No treatment-related serious adverse events, and roughly seventy percent of patients were still using it at week fifteen.

Three reads for the GPR fifty-two NewCo. First, the deck. "There is no approved treatment for negative symptoms" is now technically false. The accurate line is that there is no pharmacological one, and the only authorized option is a behavioral adjunct with a modest effect. That is still a strong unmet-need argument, but Hemaka and Maisha should fix the sentence before a partner fixes it for us.

Second, the endpoint and the bar. Negative symptoms have been where drugs go to fail. Minerva's roluperidone, filed specifically for negative symptoms, drew a complete response letter in early twenty-twenty-four. Now there is a named instrument and a cleared product built on it, and a GPR fifty-two agonist that claims negative-symptom benefit will be measured against an effect size of about a third. The candid caveat is that this was the device center ruling on software, not the drug division ruling on a molecule, so it is a precedent for the instrument, not a guarantee. But a named endpoint is the useful thing to take into a pre-I-N-D conversation, rather than an argument we have to make from scratch.

Third, the counterparty. CT-155 was co-developed with Boehringer Ingelheim, which in April handed all commercial and marketing rights to Click along with a fifty-million-dollar Series D. Put that next to what we already have on file: Boehringer's glycine-transporter drug failed Phase Three in cognitive impairment, and Boehringer walked away from its option on Nxera's GPR fifty-two agonist in December. The only Boehringer schizophrenia asset to reach the market is software, and Boehringer chose not to sell it. Eddie, move Boehringer down the GPR fifty-two bidder list. Its money still goes into schizophrenia, but it has stopped wanting to sell schizophrenia products itself. There is also a design question for later. Future negative-symptom drug trials will enroll patients who may already be using this app, so the protocol needs a position on background digital therapy. That works either as a confounder or as a combination story.

And a local footnote. One of the CONVOKE authors is Faith Dickerson at Sheppard Pratt in Towson. A Maryland site with Phase Three negative-symptom experience is worth a call when the GPR fifty-two clinical plan gets drafted.

Second item, on the Parkinson's theme. Alector licensed AL050 to Genentech yesterday: a hundred million dollars up front, up to one point one seven billion more in milestones, plus royalties. AL050 is preclinical. It is an engineered glucocerebrosidase enzyme, built for higher activity and longer half-life, fused to Alector's own blood-brain-barrier carrier. The target is GCase deficiency, most pronounced in carriers of GBA1 mutations. Alector came into the deal with a market cap around a quarter of a billion dollars, its GSK collaboration terminated and its lead Alzheimer's trial stopped for futility. The stock jumped more than sixty percent pre-market.

Here is the read. In September we wrote down the rule this field keeps paying for: every Parkinson's value case has to say which patient it treats and how that patient is found before it gets to mechanism. AL050 answers both by construction. The patient is a GBA1 carrier, found with a genotype. The defect is an enzyme activity, and the lipids that build up when it is missing can be measured. That is how a preclinical Parkinson's asset gets a hundred million dollars up front. Roche owns a brain shuttle of its own, the one behind trontinemab, so it is a fair inference that it was paying at least as much for the engineered enzyme and the defined population as for the delivery.

The second read is structural. Alector keeps the brain-carrier platform and licenses only the asset. That is the answer to the question we raised when tavapadon was approved, that a platform which is one molecule is not a platform. Anthony and Jon: for the Parkinson's program, the screen is a findable patient and a measurable defect. For company design, here and for Aletira's SELEXON cassette, the template is to license the asset and keep the chassis.

Third, on type one diabetes, which touches Islex, the Hamad lab at Hopkins, and Winnow. Zag Bio in Cambridge put its lead program, ZAG-101, into the clinic yesterday. It is a bifunctional antibody that carries an insulin-derived antigen to antigen-presenting cells in the thymus. The idea is to generate long-lived, insulin-specific regulatory T cells that migrate to the pancreas and protect the remaining beta cells. The first-in-human study is a Phase One-b two-a called LANTERN. It enrolls adults with stage three type one diabetes, runs single and multiple ascending doses both intravenously and subcutaneously, and uses C-peptide as the early efficacy signal. Adolescents and earlier-stage disease come later.

Two points. For Islex, this is a venture-backed antigen-specific antibody now in the clinic in the same stage three population Islex is aimed at, using C-peptide to read out. That competes for both the patients and the investor's attention, but it also validates the entry design: start in established disease, read beta-cell function, move earlier once safety is in hand. Eddie, it belongs in the Islex first-read, because it changes Islex's fundraising pitch from "first antigen-specific antibody" to "different antigen, different mechanism."

For Winnow and the Hamad work, look at what antigen-specific actually means here. Zag's own preclinical claim is tissue-specific bystander suppression. The antigen chooses the organ, and then the regulatory cells suppress whatever is attacking in that organ. Hamad's x-mab cuts by the receptor on the pathogenic cell. Winnow restricts which antigen the engager can see. Those are three different strengths of the same word. With Kyverna about to win the generic safety argument, as we said yesterday, Jon should be ready to say exactly which kind of antigen-specific Winnow is, because the field is about to use the word loosely.

That is the watch. One date: GRAIL presents NHS-Galleri and PATHFINDER Two data at the early detection conference in Edinburgh starting today, and at ESMO in Madrid from October twenty-third. With Galleri's PMA panel behind it, that is the read-through for DELFI. The day's theme is that in all three items the buyer cared less about novelty than about a patient it can define and a measurement it can trust. Back tomorrow.
