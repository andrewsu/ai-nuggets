Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Portfolio Watch for Monday, September twenty-eighth, and the most important development for the portfolio happened Thursday evening in a disease that affects about three hundred people in the United States. The F-D-A approved a third drug for it. Five weeks ago it approved the second. The newest one cleared a full approval on a single Phase two trial of sixty-three patients with a twenty-four-week imaging endpoint, and that is the closest thing we will get to a worked example of the regulatory argument Aletira has to make. Five items and a closer.

Start there. Thursday the F-D-A approved Atebrioz, generic name zilurgisertib, from Incyte and Mirum Pharmaceuticals, for fibrodysplasia ossificans progressiva. The disease is caused by activating variants in one gene, A-C-V-R one, which encodes the receptor kinase A-L-K two. When A-L-K two is switched on inappropriately, bone forms inside muscle, tendon and ligament; lesions accumulate and movement is progressively lost. About three hundred patients in the United States, nine hundred worldwide.

Atebrioz is a once-daily oral A-L-K two inhibitor at one hundred milligrams. The approval rests on Cohort one of a study called PROGRESS — a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled Phase two, sixty-three patients aged twelve and older, randomized one to one, twenty-four weeks blinded, then an open-label extension. The efficacy result is a volume measurement: at week twenty-four, mean total new bone-lesion volume fell three point two cubic centimeters on drug and rose twenty-four point six on placebo, and the effect held through week forty-eight. Mirum disclosed no p-value. Adverse reactions were headache, joint pain, upper respiratory infection, nosebleed and nausea, with no discontinuations and no dose reductions.

Now put it beside what came before. The first drug in this disease was Sohonos, palovarotene, an oral retinoic acid receptor gamma agonist from Ipsen, approved August twenty twenty-three after a withdrawn filing and a complete response letter, and refused authorization in Europe. The second was Regeneron's Pasatru, garetosmab, an antibody against activin A, approved August nineteenth of this year on a Phase three called OPTIMA: also sixty-three patients, but three arms, intravenous every four weeks, and fifty-six weeks long. New lesions by full-body C-T at week fifty-six were nineteen across twenty-one placebo patients versus one and two in the drug arms, and it also cut clinician-assessed flare-ups. So: three approved drugs, three nodes of one pathway, three hundred American patients.

Geoff Lynn and Hugh Wells, the first read is that the ultra-rare objection is now answered with a number rather than an argument, and the mechanism of the answer is the part to copy. Zilurgisertib got a full approval off one Phase two because the pre-trial probability was high in exactly the way the revised substantial-evidence draft guidance describes. Monogenic disease, fully specified mechanism, and by the time the application was under review there was already an approved drug hitting the same ligand-receptor axis one node upstream, on the same imaging endpoint. That is Door one almost verbatim — confirmatory evidence from another approved product, judged on similarity of pathophysiology, mechanism and endpoint. Aletira has all three ingredients available: monogenic loss of function, an approved genetic medicine in the same organ measured on auditory brainstem response, and an endpoint that is machine-read, per ear, with an untreated ear as its own control. The honest qualifier, and say it before a reviewer does: Otarmeni holds an accelerated approval, so it still owes its own confirmation. That makes it a weaker anchor than a full approval, and the pre-I-N-D package should name the gap rather than let it be found.

Anthony Accorsi and Jon Robbins, the second read is what the small package cost, because the trade runs in both directions. The Phase three sponsor bought the broader efficacy claim — lesions and flare-ups both in the label — and got the narrower population, adults only. The Phase two sponsor bought the broader population, twelve and older with cohorts down to age two still enrolling, and got the narrower claim: reduce the volume of new heterotopic ossification, full stop. No flare-up claim, no function claim. Label scope tracks the evidence you brought, and you get to choose the axis. For a congenital sensory disorder, population breadth beats claim breadth, so the pediatric cohort belongs inside the pivotal program, not after it.

Eddie Cherok and Emily Wilkinson, the third read is one line in the release and it is a license term. Hemaka Rajapakse and Yixuan Qiu, hold the two items together, because the pairing is the lesson. Sixty-three patients cleared a full approval. One hundred eighty-seven failed to clear an accelerated one. The difference is not size and it is not the response rate. One sat on a fully specified monogenic mechanism with a randomized placebo control, a quantitative endpoint and an approved precedent on the same axis. The other was a single-arm activity signal in a competitive indication that already has an approved option — Amgen's D-L-L three by C-D three T-cell engager — with a rival B seven H three conjugate coming behind it. Third item. At the International Myeloma Society meeting on Saturday, I-A-S-O Bio presented late-breaking first-in-human data for I-A-S-O two-oh-six, a B-C-M-A-directed in vivo C-A-R T-cell therapy. One intravenous infusion, no apheresis, no lymphodepleting chemotherapy. Ten patients, three dose levels, median three prior lines, ninety percent with high-risk cytogenetics and seventy percent ultra-high-risk. Objective response rate ninety percent, minimal residual disease negativity ninety percent, both one hundred percent in the high-dose cohort. No dose-limiting toxicities, no neurotoxicity syndrome, no treatment-related deaths; seven of ten had grade one cytokine release syndrome with a median duration of two and a half days. C-A-R vector copies peaked between days twelve and twenty-one and stayed detectable in most patients out to six months, and two patients had serious viral infections from low immunoglobulin levels and recovered on immunoglobulin replacement.

This lands on the Winnow control-architecture row and cuts both ways. The half that hurts: our argument has been that a cell product cannot be titrated or stopped, and that manufacturing and lymphodepletion are the operational price. This removes manufacturing and lymphodepletion entirely while keeping the cell, with a cytokine release profile milder than most engager step-up schedules. The half that helps, and it is the stronger half: a functional C-A-R still detectable at six months, with low immunoglobulins requiring replacement therapy, is exactly the non-switchable long tail our argument names — and it arrives in a population whose alternative is death from myeloma. In an autoimmune patient that is a different benefit-risk calculation entirely, which is the inversion rule from the fifteenth: in autoimmunity the safety table is the product. Carry the limits too — ten patients, single-arm, investigator-initiated, short follow-up, no comparator.
