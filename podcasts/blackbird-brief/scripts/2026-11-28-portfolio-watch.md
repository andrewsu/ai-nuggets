## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Saturday, November twenty-eighth. The most important development for the portfolio this week came from campus, not from the trade press. Johns Hopkins named the first sixty winners of its new internal Life Sciences Research Initiative, and the list lands on five of our program themes by name. The holiday tape was thin otherwise, so it's three items today, and the first one gets most of the time.

Start with the list, because for Esther, Hemaka and the Venture Partners it works as a sourcing longlist that someone else has already peer-reviewed.

On Tuesday, Hopkins announced the inaugural High-Impact Individual Awards under the initiative it launched in June to backfill federal research cuts. More than four hundred proposals arrived in under a month. Sixty were funded, at up to five hundred thousand dollars each, covering a hundred and fifty-nine faculty across seven divisions. Thirty-eight of the sixty went to the School of Medicine. Ashani Weeraratna, the Bloomberg Distinguished Professor of cancer biology, runs the program, and the money comes from more than a hundred and twenty alumni and donors. The full program is eighty million dollars a year for two years, split evenly. Half goes to these individual awards. The other half goes to Transformational Science Team Awards, which start at ten million dollars per project and run for up to four years. Those team awards are due to be announced next month.

Here's what on the list touches our programs.

The first and strongest hit is non-opioid pain. Yun Guan in Anesthesiology was funded to develop an orally bioavailable positive allosteric modulator of the human M-R-G-P-R-X-one receptor for pain. We've had Guan on the longlist since August, when the Johns Hopkins Technology Ventures translational fund backed the same M-R-G-P-R-X-one program. That makes two separately reviewed internal Hopkins awards for one program in one fall, and the word "orally bioavailable" in the new title says the work has moved from tool compound toward something that could become a drug. Matt Lawler and Avi, this is the one to call about first. The questions haven't changed. What has Johns Hopkins Technology Ventures filed? Where does the selectivity against M-R-G-P-R-X-two stand, given that Evommune's X-two antagonist failed twice in inflammation this summer? And what would the first Phase two primary endpoint be, since in pain the endpoint is where programs fail, not the mechanism. A second receptor from the same family shows up on the list too. Naina Gour, an assistant professor in Otolaryngology who opened her lab a year ago, was funded to study epigenetic reactivation of M-R-G-P-R-X-four as a mutation-independent, druggable driver of melanoma. It's early, but a surface receptor that's switched back on in tumor cells fits our oncology surface-targets theme.

Second, autoimmunity, for Jon and Virginia. Felipe Andrade in Rheumatology was funded to study interferon-independent pathways in lupus. That matters this week in particular. On Thursday we covered Bristol's Sotyktu, a TYK2 inhibitor that sits partly on the interferon axis, splitting its two lupus Phase threes. Lupus patients whose disease isn't driven by interferon are the group that interferon-targeted drugs keep failing to help, and they're where a selective T-cell engager like Winnow's would need to show depth. Joel Pomerantz was also funded, on immune surveillance of hyperactive B cells. Both are labs Winnow should know by name.

Third, Aletira. Ulrich Mueller in Neuroscience was funded to study mitochondrial function in hearing loss, which the Hopkins announcement frames around the most common form of age-related hearing loss. Mueller was co-senior author on the tip-link paper we led the November first Radar with, and his mice sit behind the Doetzlhofer cochlear-regeneration work. He's the hearing biologist Hopkins keeps funding. For Hugh and the Aletira team, the point is market size. Age-related hearing loss is a far larger population than any single-gene deafness, and a mitochondrial mechanism there is the kind of second-indication story a Series A investor will ask about.

Fourth, diagnostics. Sarah Khan in Medicine was funded to test whether cell-free DNA has diagnostic and prognostic value in pulmonary hypertension linked to interstitial lung disease. That's a cell-free DNA readout in a chronic, non-cancer disease, which is Artemyx's entire thesis, in a lung indication that also touches our COPD theme. Virginia, Artemyx should treat her as a possible collaborator or a possible competitor, and it should find out which before she publishes.

Fifth, the neuropsychiatry franchise. Richard Huganir was funded on AMPA receptor variants in neurodevelopmental disorders and schizophrenia. Edward Twomey was funded for what he calls a structure-based therapeutic foundry for delta-type glutamate receptors, a receptor family with almost no drugs aimed at it. Neither competes with G-P-R fifty-two. Both are the sort of second-target substrate the Lieber NewCo will eventually want close at hand.

The structural read is for Matt, Maisha and Eddie, and it's about where Blackbird sits next to this program. The application form asked every applicant for translation pathways and plans for follow-on funding. Blackbird's nondilutive grants and BioVentures checks are exactly that follow-on. The program page also says proposals may be shared confidentially with trusted university partners to find additional funding. We should ask Weeraratna's office to put Blackbird on that list. Being there means seeing the more than three hundred and forty proposals that weren't funded, and not just the sixty that were. That pool is where our grant dollar goes furthest, because nobody else is funding it. Last, the money runs for two years. Whatever these sixty projects produce will need its next check in twenty twenty-eight. Watch for the team awards in December. At ten million dollars or more and four years each, they'll show which Hopkins groups won't need our money for a while, and which ones will have investor-ready packages when they do.

The second item is for Hemaka, Anthony and the Aletira team, and it's a read on the gene therapy regulatory climate. In an interview Fierce published Wednesday, Tim Hunt, the chief executive of the Alliance for Regenerative Medicine, the trade group for more than four hundred cell and gene therapy companies, walked through what he called a wild twelve months with the F-D-A. He named three cases as the low points: the agency's harder line on uniQure's Huntington's gene therapy, the Atara and Pierre Fabre rejection, and the February rejection of Regenxbio's Hunter syndrome program, where the agency asked for more patients and an untreated control arm instead of natural-history comparison. He now says the agency has corrected course. uniQure has priority review with a decision due in May, F-D-A agreed to review the Regenxbio filing on its existing studies, and Karim Mikhail was made permanent head of the biologics center in September.

Two details are worth more than the headline. First, when ARM surveyed about thirty member chief executives, the answers split into two camps. One group said its review team was intact and things were going well, even while the news looked chaotic. The other group was running into trouble. That's the same conclusion we reached on November eighteenth: with an acting commissioner likely through most of next year, what happens at the review-division level is what we can actually control. Second, Hunt said openly that this generation of gene therapies is not perfect, and he listed safety, efficacy and, in his words, legitimate questions about durability of response. When the industry's own trade group concedes durability in public, a reviewer will raise it. For Aletira, the durability-of-expression question from the tip-link paper belongs in the pre-IND package as a section we wrote ourselves, before anyone asks.

The third item is short, and it's for Yixuan, Esther and everyone who diligences assets that already have a clinical history. The F-D-A has posted formal notices of noncompliance to two drug makers for failing to put trial results on ClinicalTrials-dot-gov. Reports say these are the first such notices since August twenty twenty-five. One went to Duchesnay, over a Diclegis study in pregnant adolescents that ended in twenty seventeen. The other two went to Addex Pharma, over a Phase two-b-three trial and an open-label extension of dipraglurant in levodopa-induced dyskinesia in Parkinson's disease. Both companies had a warning first, Addex in July of last year, and both had started entering results without finishing. The agency's letter says an unfinished submission doesn't count. Each company has thirty days, and the law allows civil penalties of up to ten thousand dollars a day. In March the F-D-A reminded more than twenty-two hundred sponsors about some three thousand trials, and its own analysis found that nearly thirty percent of trials subject to the rule had posted no results.

The read connects to the divested-asset screen and to the Parkinson's theme. A program that comes to us with a terminated trial behind it, which describes most of the cheap assets on that screen, can carry an overdue results obligation, and enforcement on those obligations has started. Add a results-posting check to the diligence checklist, next to the C-M-C questions.

That's the watch for today. To sum up: Johns Hopkins' first sixty LSRI awards are a peer-reviewed longlist that names our pain, autoimmune, hearing, cell-free DNA and neuropsychiatry themes, and the move is to get on the confidential-sharing list before the team awards land in December. The gene therapy trade group says F-D-A has corrected course but admits durability is an open question, so Aletira should answer it before a reviewer asks. And unposted trial results are now an enforcement item, which belongs on every diligence checklist. Enjoy the rest of the weekend, everyone. We'll be back tomorrow with the Sunday Radar.
