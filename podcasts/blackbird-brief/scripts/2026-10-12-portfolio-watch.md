## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Monday, October twelfth. The most important development for the portfolio this weekend is a diagnostics company showing what it costs to improve a test after the F-D-A has approved it, and that bears directly on the regulatory question we put to DELFI last month.

Freenome got SimpleScreen C-R-C approved in late July. It's a blood test for colorectal cancer screening, built on cell-free D-N-A methylation, and Abbott is selling it alongside Cologuard. The approval paid Freenome a one-hundred-million-dollar milestone from Abbott. The registrational study, PREEMPT, enrolled more than forty-eight thousand average-risk adults. Headline performance: eighty-one percent sensitivity for cancer, about ninety percent specificity, and thirteen point seven percent sensitivity for advanced precancerous lesions, which are the lesions you actually want to catch if the goal is prevention.

On Sunday, eleven weeks after approval and a few weeks into the commercial launch, Freenome announced that an updated version of the test went through its own pivotal clinical validation study. It will be presented as a late-breaker at the gastroenterology meeting in Nashville on Wednesday. Sensitivity for advanced precancerous lesions rose from thirteen point seven to eighteen point two percent, and for the lesions with high-grade dysplasia it rose from about thirty-one to almost forty-two percent. Cancer sensitivity held at about eighty percent. The C-E-O calls this an "up-versioning strategy": the assay and algorithm get refined, and each version is revalidated.

There are two caveats before anyone gets excited. First, the specificity isn't defined the same way. The approval cited ninety point four percent specificity for advanced neoplasia, and the new release cites ninety percent for a clean colonoscopy, so part of the lesion gain may be a threshold move rather than a better assay. We'll know Wednesday. Second, eighteen percent on precancerous lesions still means a blood test misses more than four of every five of them. This is still a detection test, not a prevention test.

The reason it leads today is what it tells us about DELFI. On September twenty-fourth we flagged that FirstLook Lung is a laboratory-developed test under CLIA, not F-D-A approved, and we asked DELFI whether it has a premarket approval path and a timeline, because the Medicare benefit for multi-cancer screening that starts in twenty twenty-eight covers only F-D-A-approved tests. Freenome now shows the other side of that trade. Under a lab-developed test, a fragmentomics model can be retrained whenever the data improve. Under an approval, each meaningful improvement has to pay for a new validation study before it reaches patients. Freenome has decided to make that cost part of its strategy, and it has a commercial partner paying milestones to fund it.

So Eddie and Emily, when you have the D-E-L-F-I conversation, there's a third question to add to the first two. If FirstLook Lung goes the approval route, how often would its model need to change, and who pays for each revalidation? A machine-learning test that improves continuously and a single locked approved product are different businesses. Abbott's hundred-million-dollar milestone shows what a large diagnostics partner will pay for the approved one.

Second, a Winnow watch-item that resolves within hours of this episode. First Tracks Biotherapeutics reports topline data from the first cohort of its Phase 1b study of A-N-B-zero-thirty-three in celiac disease this morning, with a webcast at eight-thirty Eastern. A-N-B-zero-thirty-three is a C-D-one-twenty-two antagonist. That's the beta chain shared by the I-L-fifteen and I-L-two receptors, so it's the same axis as the two antibodies we have already logged. Teva's anti-I-L-fifteen antibody won in a fifty-patient gluten challenge on September second, and Forte's anti-C-D-one-twenty-two, now argenx's, won its hundred-twenty-six-patient Phase 2 last Thursday. The First Tracks cohort is about thirty patients: three subcutaneous doses two weeks apart, then a gluten challenge, then a biopsy to see whether the mucosal damage was prevented.

I won't predict the result. The bar is set, though, and you should hold it to the bar when the release lands. The argenx program showed protection of the villus-to-crypt ratio at a p-value of about point zero two with a hundred twenty-six patients. Thirty patients can't reproduce that statistically, so look for a direction on histology, a clean safety table and a subcutaneous dosing schedule. If it's positive, three companies have now validated the same provocation design on the same axis inside six weeks. Celiac then becomes a crowded indication. The thing worth taking from it is the design itself: a challenge model that turns a chronic disease into an eight-week readout. Jon and Anthony, that's still the priority diligence question for every Winnow indication. If it's negative, it's the first crack in the axis, and the question becomes whether subcutaneous antagonism or the antibody format is the reason.

Third, a lesson about dosing that applies to two of our programs, out of the heart failure meeting in Phoenix on Sunday.

Cardurion presented Phase 2 data for tovinontrine, an oral P-D-E-nine inhibitor meant to enhance natriuretic peptide signaling in the cardiomyocyte. In the reduced-ejection-fraction trial, with five hundred twenty-nine patients on full guideline therapy, the two lower doses cut N-T-pro-B-N-P through twelve weeks. The best result came at the lowest dose, two and a half milligrams twice a day. The top dose, fifty milligrams, did nothing. The company's explanation is that maximal enzyme inhibition drives cyclic G-M-P so high that it triggers counter-regulatory neurohormonal feedback. That's an inverse dose-response, and they say the plasma data support it.

The second trial is where it costs them. Their preserved-ejection-fraction study, two hundred seventy-seven patients, tested only the fifty-milligram dose, and it came back flat. A whole Phase 2 in the bigger indication was spent at the one dose that, in hindsight, didn't work. Cardurion now says lower doses in that population are "warranted."

Virginia, for the cardiac fibrosis theme, this is a precedent to cite whenever a program proposes enhancing a second-messenger pathway. Map the full dose range before you pick the dose for the second indication, and don't assume more target engagement means more effect. The same applies to the Neuropsych NewCo. A G-P-R-fifty-two agonist works by raising cyclic A-M-P in the indirect-pathway neurons, and Gs-coupled signaling is known for desensitization and feedback. I'm not claiming G-P-R-fifty-two will behave this way. I'm saying the first patient study should cover the low end of the dose range deliberately, not just escalate to the maximum tolerated dose. Hemaka, that belongs in the pre-I-N-D book next to the Cerevance precedent from last week.

Two shorter items.

For the Neuropsych NewCo again: LB Pharmaceuticals presented four posters at the European neuropsychopharmacology congress in Munich over the weekend. LB-one-oh-two is a modified amisulpride, a D-two, D-three and five-H-T-seven antagonist, now in a pivotal schizophrenia trial. Most of it was design posters and encores. Two points matter. First, a rat microdialysis study argues that the drug blocks presynaptic D-two autoreceptors and so increases dopamine release, which they're pitching as a mechanism for anhedonia. Second, a post hoc analysis from their Phase 2 claims the cognitive benefit was mostly a direct drug effect rather than a side effect of better overall symptom control. Both are weak evidence: one is preclinical, the other post hoc. But they show where the incumbent dopamine class is moving, which is cognition and motivation, the same differentiation story we've been building for G-P-R-fifty-two. Their R-and-D day is October twenty-seventh. Watch for how they plan to measure cognition prospectively.

And for the chronic infections theme: on Friday evening the F-D-A approved Hovilpri, pritelivir, developed by AiCuris. It's an oral helicase-primase inhibitor for herpes simplex lesions in immunocompromised adults whose infection failed acyclovir, valacyclovir or famciclovir. In the Phase 3, lesions healed by day twenty-eight in sixty-three percent of patients against thirty-four percent on investigator's choice, under priority review. The lesson is the same indication logic we keep coming back to. A new antiviral class got approved by starting in the refractory, immunocompromised niche, where the comparator is weak, the unmet need is obvious and a randomized trial can be small. Any chronic-infection program we source should be able to name its version of that niche on day one.

That's the watch. Read the Freenome specificity footnote on Wednesday, and check the First Tracks release against the argenx bar this morning. Talk soon.
