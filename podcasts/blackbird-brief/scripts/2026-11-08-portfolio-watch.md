## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Sunday, November eighth. The most important development for us this week is not a readout — it is a competitor. A fifty-million-dollar fund jointly owned by the University of Pennsylvania, BioNTech, and a venture firm that specializes in university equity has made its first investment, and the company it built looks remarkably like one of ours. After that, a crystal-arthritis company launching with forty million euros on a mechanism that goes after the crystals instead of the inflammation, which lands on our gout theme. Then a date on the calendar for Capricor, which is the cleanest live test of the endpoint argument we keep having. And a short, deliberately skeptical note on one of the weekend's cancer immunotherapy presentations.

Start with the fund, because this is a structure question and those are the ones that matter most to this organization.

On Wednesday the Penn-BioNTech Innovative Therapeutics Seed Fund announced its first investment. The fund is fifty million dollars, formed by the University of Pennsylvania together with BioNTech and Osage University Partners, and it invests at seed and Series A in companies built on Penn intellectual property or founded by Penn faculty — therapeutics, platforms, diagnostics and research tools. The first company is called Emvena. It is developing lipid nanoparticles engineered to deliver messenger RNA preferentially to the placenta, with a lead program in preeclampsia, a condition affecting somewhere between two and eight percent of pregnancies worldwide, with no approved therapy that touches the underlying disease — the options today are managing symptoms or delivering the baby early. The science comes from Michael Mitchell's bioengineering lab at Penn, published in Nature, and the seed money takes it toward investigational-new-drug-enabling studies.

Three features of this deal are worth the team's attention, and I would rank them in this order.

The first and most important is the quote from the founding scientist. Mitchell says the fund made it happen "in a matter of months" — saw the technology, assembled the team, launched the company. That is the claim we make about ourselves, and it is the one durable advantage a studio has over a conventional seed investor. Somebody else is now making it, with a university whose tech-transfer office is one of the two or three most commercially sophisticated in the country. I do not think this is a threat to deal flow in Baltimore. I think it is a benchmark. Esther, Yixuan — it would be worth reconstructing the actual timeline from the Nature publication date to incorporation to first close, because if theirs is genuinely shorter than ours, the difference is process, not science, and process is fixable.

The second is the capital structure, and this is the one for Matt and Eddie. Three kinds of money are sitting in the same vehicle: the university, which contributes the intellectual property and the faculty relationships; a commercial-stage pharmaceutical company, which contributes technical judgment and an eventual acquirer's eye; and a venture firm built specifically to invest alongside university tech-transfer offices. We have the first and the third in our own structure — the nonprofit does the de-risking, BioVentures writes the check. We do not have the second. The question is not whether we should sell a piece of ourselves to a pharmaceutical company; the answer to that is obviously no. The question is whether there is a version of a standing technical partnership — a company that reads our programs early and tells us honestly what would make them buyable — that we could have without giving up control of what we build. The Penn fund is evidence that at least one serious pharmaceutical company thinks the proposition is worth fifty million dollars of shared exposure.

The third is the choice of first asset, and it is a good one to argue with. They did not lead with a platform claim. They led with a single indication where the standard of care is to deliver the baby prematurely — enormous unmet need, an endpoint that is unambiguous, and a target organ that is temporary and physically distinct, which is the thing that makes the tropism claim credible. That is the same structural argument we make for the cochlea: a small, defined, locally accessible compartment where a delivery advantage is provable rather than asserted. Avi, Hugh — the placenta data are worth reading for the tropism methodology alone, because the question of how convincingly you demonstrate preferential delivery to one organ against conventional particles is a question we will be answering for ourselves.

Second item, and it lands squarely on the gout theme. A company called Anaheim Bio launched on Thursday out of Leiden with an oversubscribed forty-million-euro Series A. The lead program, ANA-one, is a small molecule for calcium pyrophosphate deposition disease — pseudogout — expected to enter the clinic in 2027, with cash into 2028.

The mechanism is the reason I am raising it. ANA-one is an inhibitor designed to lower extracellular pyrophosphate, which is the raw material for the crystals. It is not an anti-inflammatory. Crystal arthropathies have historically been treated by suppressing the inflammatory response to the crystal — colchicine, steroids, interleukin-one blockade — which is symptomatic by construction. The company's claim is that they are addressing the deposits themselves. For the gout program, this is a useful competitive marker and a useful framing argument: the field is moving from "calm the joint down" to "remove the substrate," and a program that can only do the former is going to be benchmarked against one that claims the latter. Jon, Hemaka — worth a look at whether the underlying patent position is composition of matter on the inhibitor or method of use on lowering extracellular pyrophosphate, because those two have very different implications for anything we build in the adjacent space.

There is a second thing here, and it is the fourth time in three weeks this pattern has come up. Anaheim was spun out of a company called Alesta Therapeutics shortly before BioMarin acquired Alesta, and the Series A was funded by Alesta's own former investors — Droia, Frazier, RTW, the Novartis Venture Fund, and two others — with the Alesta management team and chairman carried across. We saw the same shape when Jazz carved programs out into Setara with ninety million dollars and a retained stake. Assets that do not fit the acquisition get a new entity and the old syndicate re-ups. Eddie, this is now a reliable enough pattern to screen on rather than notice: when a Maryland-relevant company is acquired, the question to ask within the week is what did not go with it, and who is funding whatever is left.

Third, a date. Capricor's Duchenne cell therapy deramiocel has an agency decision scheduled for November twenty-second. That is two weeks from today.

We have followed this one for months and it is worth restating why it matters to us rather than restating the history. The company's first attempt was rejected in July of last year. It came back with the Phase Three data, and at the advisory committee in July the reviewers spent most of their attention on the trial's *secondary* endpoint — cardiac function — which experts on the panel called very fragile, and the committee voted nine to three against approval. Capricor's response was to narrow the proposed indication to upper-limb function, which is what the trial's primary endpoint actually measured.

That sequence is the argument we have been having about Aletira's functional measure and NuGate's, compressed into one program. A secondary endpoint you put in the package because it supports a bigger commercial story becomes the thing the panel interrogates, and a fragile secondary can sink a clean primary. The discipline that follows is unglamorous: put in the primary slot the measure you are confident separates, and be willing to take the narrower label. The twenty-second will tell us whether narrowing after an adverse vote is a recoverable position or whether the panel's verdict was really about the product. Either answer is informative, and I would rather we form a view before the news than after it.

Last, briefly, and with the skepticism turned up. At the cancer immunotherapy meeting in Phoenix this weekend, a San Diego company called Onchilles presented the first clinical data on a molecule called N-one-seven-three-five-zero. It is an engineered version of an enzyme from human neutrophils, injected directly into a tumor, designed to kill cancer cells selectively — the selectivity is said to come from altered histone H-one biology — and then to release the tumor's own antigens so the immune system is trained on them. The pitch is a personalized cancer vaccine with no sequencing and no custom manufacturing, which is a genuinely attractive proposition against the Merck-and-Moderna model.

The data are three patients at the starting dose, with no dose-limiting toxicities, and one anecdote: a patient with Merkel cell carcinoma, progressing through prior immunotherapy, had one of six lesions injected, and at week eight the investigator read all five uninjected lesions as undetectable. That is a striking observation and it is also a single patient in a tumor type with a documented history of dramatic immune responses. Even the presenting investigator framed the context correctly — intratumoral agents, including the toll-like receptor agonists and the oncolytic viruses, have a long record of impressive preclinical and early-anecdotal activity that does not survive a randomized trial.

So the read is not the efficacy. The read for our oncology surface-targets theme is that this is a selectivity claim with no surface target at all — the discrimination comes from a cancer-associated change in chromatin biology that the enzyme exploits, which is a different axis entirely from antigen expression level. That is the second time in two days we have hit a non-protein, non-expression-level selectivity argument, after the glycan-targeting antibody-drug conjugate we covered yesterday. Two is not a trend, but if our entire surface-target thesis is organized around how much more of a protein a tumor cell displays than a healthy one, it is worth asking Avi and Virginia whether we are systematically under-sampling the mechanisms that get their specificity somewhere else.

That is the watch. A fifty-million-dollar university-pharma fund just did, in months, the thing we say we are fast at; crystal arthritis is moving from treating the inflammation to removing the substrate; Capricor's verdict lands on the twenty-second; and there is a selectivity mechanism out there that has nothing to do with how much antigen a cell displays. The Sourcing Radar is in your feed alongside this one, and its lead is a Hopkins blood test that finds cancer in autoimmune patients a year before anyone can see it. Have a good Sunday.
