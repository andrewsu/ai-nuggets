## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Tuesday, November twenty-fourth, and the day's most important development for the portfolio is that Sanofi is paying forty-one billion dollars for Insmed — a company whose value rests on a neutrophil drug and an inhaled antibiotic. Those happen to be the two mechanisms sitting closest to our COPD and chronic-infections themes.

Start with the deal itself, then the two reads.

Early this morning Sanofi announced it will acquire Insmed for one hundred seventy-five dollars a share in cash, about forty-one billion dollars in total. That is Sanofi's largest acquisition since the Aventis merger, roughly twice what it paid for Genzyme. The price is about seventeen percent over Monday's close, and about forty-six percent over where Insmed traded before Bloomberg reported the talks last week — but it is still below the two-hundred-dollar-plus level the stock held a year ago. Closing is guided for the first half of twenty twenty-seven.

The motive is no secret. Dupixent is about two-fifths of Sanofi's revenue, and its core U.S. patent runs out in twenty thirty-one. Belén Garijo, who became CEO in May, has said repeatedly that she will buy approved and late-stage drugs to fill that hole. In January her finance chief put the ceiling for any deal at fifteen billion euros; by April it was twenty-five billion. This deal blows through both, and Sanofi still says it keeps room for early-stage deals.

So what is Sanofi buying? Three things. Brinsupri, which is brensocatib — a once-daily oral inhibitor of dipeptidyl peptidase one, or D-P-P-one, the enzyme that switches on the protein-cutting enzymes neutrophils carry. It was approved in August twenty twenty-five as the first drug for non-cystic fibrosis bronchiectasis, and Fierce reports it is on course for one point three five to one point four billion dollars in its first full year. Second, Arikayce, a liposomal inhaled form of amikacin for refractory Mycobacterium avium complex lung disease — a chronic, hard-to-clear mycobacterial infection. It did four hundred thirty-four million dollars last year, up nineteen percent, and it has a priority-review decision pending on moving into first-line treatment. Third, T-P-I-P, an inhaled once-daily prostanoid in Phase 3 for pulmonary hypertension and fibrotic lung disease. Guggenheim puts combined peak sales above fifteen billion dollars; Sanofi says above ten.

Virginia, this first read is for the COPD theme. For the last month we have been tracking the non-type-two lane: COPD patients with low eosinophils who keep having flare-ups on standard therapy. On the second of November we logged Boehringer Ingelheim's Phase 3 of verducatib, its own oral D-P-P-one inhibitor, enrolling exactly those patients — eosinophils under three hundred, still flaring. We noted then that the class already had an approved first-in-class drug, and that it was Insmed's. As of this morning, that drug belongs to the company that also sells Dupixent for type-two, eosinophil-high COPD. Sanofi now owns the leading drug on both sides of the eosinophil line.

Two consequences. First, the lane we called white space now has a strategic buyer with an obvious motive, which is good for an exit and bad for a me-too. Second, it changes who we would be competing against. There is no brensocatib COPD trial on the registry today — its studies are in bronchiectasis, cystic fibrosis, sinus disease and a terminated hidradenitis trial. If Sanofi starts a COPD trial, the neutrophilic lane goes from one big competitor to two, both with approved drugs to anchor them. Any COPD program we take forward has to answer one question in its first slide: why does it beat an approved oral D-P-P-one inhibitor in the eosinophil-low patient? A different mechanism alone is not enough. Watch Sanofi's investor materials and clinical-trial registrations for a brensocatib COPD study. That registration is the trigger.

Avi, Hemaka — the second read is for chronic infections, and it is the one I would put in front of the team. Arikayce is not a new antibiotic. It is a sixty-year-old drug class in a liposome, inhaled, so it actually reaches the lung and the macrophages where the bacteria sit. Its whole value is a delivery and penetration fix. That is the same problem our chronic-infections sourcing keeps coming back to. In August we flagged the Hopkins public-health paper showing that fibrin barriers keep vancomycin out of staph abscesses. The Chatterjee lab at U-M-B works on antibiotic tolerance. Their argument is that the drug never gets there, or gets there and does not kill. Today a big pharma paid for exactly that thesis, applied to an old drug with a refractory label, at roughly half a billion dollars a year in sales and growing. The usual objection to chronic-infection programs is that no one buys them. That objection got weaker this morning. The caveat is that Sanofi is buying the respiratory sales force, not anti-infectives as a category; Arikayce comes along with Brinsupri. Still, the comparable is now public, and it is a delivery play, not a discovery play.

Eddie, Emily — one line on exits. The buyer paid a seventeen percent premium on a stock already down from its high, for drugs with proven sales. The money is still going to commercial evidence. For our positions, the price gets set at proof of demand, not at proof of mechanism.

Second item, and it closes yesterday's flag.

Kymera reported Phase 2b results for K-T six twenty-one, a once-daily oral degrader of S-T-A-T-six. S-T-A-T-six is the transcription factor that carries the I-L-four and I-L-thirteen signal — the same pathway Dupixent blocks with an injection. In two hundred forty-four adults and adolescents with moderate-to-severe atopic dermatitis, a third of whom had already been on a biologic, every dose hit every primary and secondary endpoint at week sixteen. At the mid dose, sixty-two percent of patients reached a seventy-five percent improvement in skin score, versus twenty-two percent on placebo. Forty-three percent reached a ninety percent improvement, versus ten. Fifty-two percent had a meaningful drop in itch, versus thirteen. The only signal above placebo was conjunctivitis, the known effect of this pathway, all mild or moderate, with no discontinuations. Phase 3 starts by mid-twenty twenty-seven, an asthma Phase 2b reads out late next year, and Kymera went straight to a public offering.

Put that next to the lead item. On Monday an oral pill posted numbers in Dupixent's range in Dupixent's lead indication. On Tuesday morning Dupixent's owner spent forty-one billion dollars on drugs that do not depend on Dupixent. Those two facts are not unrelated.

Jon, Anthony — the read for Winnow is the same one we have now made three times in a week, and it keeps getting sharper. The convenience bar in immunology is moving fast: on the twentieth it was a quarterly siRNA in myasthenia gravis; today it is a daily pill matching a biologic in a type-two disease. Winnow's argument is selectivity plus the ability to interrupt the drug. The class it competes with keeps getting more convenient. "Better than a weekly injection" is no longer a differentiator anywhere in immunology. The pitch has to say what our selectivity buys that a pill or a quarterly shot cannot: depth of remission, or drug-free intervals. That is the sentence for the Series A materials. The usual caveat applies: these are week-sixteen numbers on about sixty patients per arm, with wide error bars on the placebo-adjusted gaps.

Third item, short, for the non-opioid pain theme.

Latigo Biotherapeutics started its Phase 3 of onzotrigine, an oral sodium-channel one point eight inhibitor, in pain after bunion surgery. About four hundred eighty patients, with topline results in the second half of twenty twenty-seven. The design detail is what matters: alongside placebo, there is an eighty-patient arm on Percocet — oxycodone plus acetaminophen. In its earlier abdominoplasty trial, Latigo beat placebo and was numerically better than Vicodin. Now it is choosing to test against a stronger opioid.

Here is the read. Our working rule on this theme has been that the binding constraint is the endpoint and the placebo response, not the mechanism. And from the Viatris–Pacira deal in October: buyers of post-surgical pain drugs pay for a pharmacoeconomic case, not novelty. An active opioid arm is how you build that case inside a pivotal trial. You show a hospital formulary committee a head-to-head against the drug it actually uses. It also means the sodium-channel one point eight class now has an approved leader in Vertex and a second entrant generating comparative data. Any pain program we look at should budget an active comparator from Phase 2 on. Placebo-only data will look thin by twenty twenty-eight.

Quickly, two loose ends. The Lilly purchase of the U-K Huntington's startup LoQus twenty-three that we could only see behind a paywall yesterday is now confirmed by multiple outlets, at up to seven hundred twenty-five million dollars. That is another early-stage C-N-S acquisition for the divested-asset screen. Note the shape: a startup bought whole, not licensed. And BioArctic closed its Phase 2a of exidavnemab, an alpha-synuclein antibody for Parkinson's, on safety and spinal-fluid exposure only. That tells us nothing about efficacy, so it stays a watch item for the Parkinson's theme.

That is the watch. Sanofi's forty-one billion dollars puts the approved D-P-P-one inhibitor in the same hands as type-two COPD, which gives the eosinophil-low lane a buyer and a heavier competitor at once, and it prices a delivery fix for a chronic lung infection at half a billion dollars a year. Kymera's pill in Dupixent territory raises the convenience bar Winnow has to clear again. And Latigo's opioid comparator arm is the pain-trial design we should expect to need ourselves. Talk tomorrow.
