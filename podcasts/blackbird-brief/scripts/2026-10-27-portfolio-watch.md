Bristol Myers Squibb disclosed on Friday that a patient with highly active relapsing multiple sclerosis died after receiving zolacabtagene autoleucel, its C-D-nineteen cell therapy. The sequence matters. After infusion the patient developed late-onset motor symptoms the company classified as a neurotoxicity syndrome, which did not resolve on corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin. Septic pneumonia followed, and the patient died the next day — forty-four days after the infusion. Bristol Myers says no other deaths have occurred across all autoimmune programs for this product, which now total a hundred and ninety patients treated in Phase One. In the Breakfree-Two study itself, sixty-six patients, forty-two percent had cytokine release syndrome, all of it grade one or two and all resolving in a median of two days.

Now the part that is ours. On October fifth we closed a standing open item. Two cell-therapy chief executives had gone on the record attributing the Novartis and Bristol Myers safety events to accelerated manufacturing protocols that over-stimulate T cells, and Kyverna's clean twelve-month safety came with a conventional process attached. We wrote the defensible read as "these two products' manufacturing, not cell therapy in autoimmunity." I am reopening that item, and here is the specific reason. The August pause came out of an increase in neurotoxicity events in the pivotal lupus study. Bristol Myers told BioSpace it did not see the same break in trend in the multiple sclerosis study — and the death happened in the multiple sclerosis study, after the pause decision, on day forty-four. A manufacturing-intensity explanation has to account for a fatal event arriving six weeks out, in the arm where the signal that triggered the pause was absent. It may still be the right explanation. It is no longer the parsimonious one.

So what for Winnow, and this is for Jon and Anthony. Do not read this as a competitor stumble for a slide — read it as the chronic-disease benefit-risk argument getting its hardest data point, and notice that the efficacy here is real. Every patient at the higher dose level remained off disease-modifying therapy, nine of them past twelve months. A single administration buying a year or more of drug-free remission in multiple sclerosis is what our engager has to be measured against. The argument that survives is not "cell therapy is dangerous." It is that the thing you cannot do with a cell is stop it. An interruptible engager has an answer on day forty-four that an infused cell product does not. Put that in the target product profile and retire the generic safety framing, which we already agreed to stop arguing.

Hold that against what we put in this show eight days ago.
