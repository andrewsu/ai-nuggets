## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Friday, November sixth. The most important development for the portfolio is a safety disclosure that did not come from a press release, a filing, or a regulator. It reached families through neurologist appointments and LinkedIn posts, and Novartis confirmed it to a trade reporter on Thursday morning. Nine patients treated with Zolgensma, the spinal muscular atrophy gene therapy, have developed tumors. In two of them, the company says there is evidence that the viral vector integrated into the patient's own DNA. That is the A-A-V integration risk, observed in humans, in the most widely dosed systemic gene therapy on the market, and it lands directly on Aletira.

Here is what Novartis actually said. More than six thousand patients have received Zolgensma since the twenty nineteen approval, across clinical trials, managed access programs, and commercial treatment. Nine tumors have been reported in patients previously treated. Of those nine, two have shown evidence of vector integration into patient DNA. Both of those patients were treated and continue to be monitored. The other seven have not been causally linked to the therapy. The company points out, correctly, that the risk of tumors from vector integration is already in the prescribing information — the label has carried a tumorigenicity warning tied to the A-A-V vector since approval. One parent's account, relayed through a patient advocate, says the two confirmed cases occurred somewhere between eighteen and sixty months after dosing.

Three things matter here, and only one of them is about the numbers.

The first is that the theoretical risk is no longer theoretical. Every A-A-V program in the world, including ours, has written some version of the sentence "integration is rare and the vector is predominantly episomal" into a preclinical package. That sentence is still defensible. But the conversation has changed from whether integration-driven tumorigenesis happens in humans to how often, in whom, and after how long. For Avi and Hugh, the practical consequence is that the long-term follow-up section of the Aletira pre-I-N-D stops being boilerplate. Fifteen-year follow-up for integrating vectors is already the agency's stated expectation. Expect the reviewer to ask what you will measure, in which tissue, and on what schedule, and expect that question earlier in the conversation than it would have come a week ago.

The second is a point in our favor, and we should be careful to state it without overclaiming. Zolgensma is delivered systemically to infants, at a dose high enough to reach motor neurons throughout the central nervous system, with wide biodistribution to the liver and everywhere else. The cochlea is a small, enclosed, locally injected compartment. Local delivery to a confined sensory organ is a smaller integration surface than systemic infusion by a large factor, and it is the same argument the Estuary launch made for the kidney back in October. That is a real differentiator. The discipline it imposes is that we have to hold the biodistribution data that supports it. An argument about compartment size is only as good as the number of vector genomes you can show in liver, gonad, and spleen after a cochlear dose.

The third is about communication, and it is for Matt and Maisha. Novartis is taking damage this week not primarily for the nine cases but for how the information traveled. Families learned at appointments. A patient advocate was told the company is withholding details on some cases and doing a tiered rollout rather than publishing. S-M-A Europe put out a statement saying it had been informed through confidential channels and that the community now needs timely public communication. Whatever the medical facts turn out to be, the company has lost the ability to narrate them. Blackbird spinouts will eventually be in a position to make a disclosure decision about a rare, serious, possibly-related event in a pediatric population. The lesson is cheap to learn now and expensive to learn later: the disclosure posture is part of the development plan, not a crisis-communications problem you pick up when the event occurs. It belongs in the target product profile discussion.

One more piece of context, because it is the second Novartis safety item this quarter. Over the summer the company paused multiple studies of its C-A-R T candidate after three patients died of immune effector cell-associated hemophagocytic syndrome. Different modality, different mechanism, same read on the environment: advanced-therapy safety signals are getting attention right now, and a program that is early has the luxury of designing its monitoring plan before it needs one.

Second item, and this one is about structure rather than science. It is for Eddie, and it is a sourcing channel we should know about.

The Orphan Therapeutics Accelerator — a nonprofit biotech, which is the part that should make you sit up — has in-licensed its first gene therapy. The asset is an A-A-V gene therapy for ornithine transcarbamylase deficiency, an X-linked urea cycle disorder where ammonia builds up in the blood and causes brain injury and coma. The therapy was developed by Paul Gissen's group at University College London together with Ian Alexander's team at the Children's Medical Research Institute in Australia. It carries orphan drug designations in both the U-S and E-U plus a U-S rare pediatric disease designation. The in-licensing came from U-C-L Ventures and C-M-R-I.

Now the interesting part. This program was already outlicensed once, to a company called Bloomsbury Genetic Therapies, which ran a Phase One/Two trial and dosed its first patient — a ten-year-old boy with late-onset disease — at Great Ormond Street in December twenty twenty-three. The plan was twelve patients. Bloomsbury shut down in twenty twenty-five, and the program went back to the universities. That first patient came off his nitrogen-scavenging drug and off his protein-restricted diet and has continued to do well, now more than two and a half years out.

So a clinically de-risked asset with two and a half years of single-patient efficacy data sat on a shelf because its commercial vehicle died. The accelerator's model is to pick those up. Their C-E-O, Craig Martin, says they screened more than eighty programs before this one. They run a network of C-D-M-Os and C-R-Os who have agreed to work under reduced-cost, success-based contracts. They have a commercial subsidiary that can either sell an approved product or out-license it. And they built a marketplace for shelved therapies in a joint venture with the American Society of Gene and Cell Therapy — which is, by Martin's own account, where this asset was first identified.

Two reads. The first is competitive, and it is mild. There is now another nonprofit with a de-risking mandate and a dealmaking apparatus operating in exactly our conceptual space. The difference is real and worth naming: they pick up assets that have already been through a company and failed commercially, while we pick up academic I-P that has never been in one. Those are different inventories, and ours is earlier. But the vocabulary is the same, and the next time a university tech transfer office describes Blackbird to its faculty, it may describe us alongside them.

The second read is the actionable one. That marketplace is an inventory of shelved cell and gene therapy programs with clinical data attached. Some of those assets are shelved for reasons that have nothing to do with the science — a company ran out of money, a strategy changed, a pipeline was cut. We are in a position to evaluate that kind of asset quickly, and a clinical-stage program with human data and a rare pediatric designation is a different risk profile from a mouse result. It is worth an hour to understand what is listed and on what terms. It is not a thesis, but it is a channel, and it costs very little to look.

Third and last, briefly, for the autoimmune theme and for Jon on Winnow. Evommune is paying Hinova Pharmaceuticals thirty million dollars upfront — twenty in cash, ten in pre-funded warrants — for worldwide rights outside Greater China to an oral molecular glue degrader aimed at autoimmune disease. Up to one point one billion in milestones on top, plus royalties. The target is V-A-V-1, a signaling protein found mainly in immune cells that drives both T and B cell activation. The pitch is that degrading it gives you oral control of lymphocyte activation with better tolerability than J-A-K inhibitors, which are the only oral option in rheumatoid arthritis today and carry boxed warnings for mortality and thrombosis. A mid-stage rheumatoid arthritis trial is slated for twenty twenty-seven; Phase One starts imminently. Oppenheimer called it a low-cost, high-probability-of-success deal and noted the preclinical claim that the molecule is twenty times more potent than Novartis's oral degrader in the same general space, the one Novartis bought from Monte Rosa for a hundred and fifty million upfront.

The read is about the shape of the autoimmune lane, and it follows directly from Wednesday. On Wednesday we talked about Sanofi walking away from the Nurix S-T-A-T-6 degrader, and the point then was that the target survived the molecule. Here is the complement. Thirty million dollars upfront buys a clinical-ready oral degrader against a validated immune signaling node, from China, with the big money deferred into milestones. That is now the market-clearing price for an autoimmune asset of this description, and Evommune bought it specifically because its own lead molecule failed twice this year and it needed a catalyst. For Winnow, the strategic point is not the target — V-A-V-1 is broad immune suppression and Winnow's whole claim is selectivity — it is the competitive arithmetic. A pharma partner weighing a selective T-cell engager against a thirty-million-dollar oral degrader is comparing a differentiated mechanism against a cheap, fast, oral one. Selectivity has to be worth the premium in a sentence that a business development person can repeat. That sentence should be ready before the conversation, not during it.

That is the watch for today. The through-line, if there is one: a decade-old gene therapy is teaching the field what its long-term risk actually looks like, a nonprofit is building a secondary market for assets that outlived their companies, and oral degraders keep getting cheaper. Have a good weekend, everyone.
