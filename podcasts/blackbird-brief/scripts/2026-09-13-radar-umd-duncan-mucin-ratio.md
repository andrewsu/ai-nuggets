## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Sourcing Radar for Sunday, September thirteenth. Three leads this week — a Maryland bioengineering paper that lands directly on the hole we identified in the COPD theme eight days ago, a Hopkins immunology program that is Winnow's thesis approached from the opposite end, and a Hopkins gastroenterology preprint that I am going to tell you not to build.

Start with the one that changes a theme position.

Gregg Duncan's lab, in the Fischell Department of Bioengineering at the University of Maryland at College Park, with Sahana Kumar and Allison Boboltz as the two hands on the bench, posted a preprint on Thursday about the two gel-forming mucins in airway mucus. Healthy airways run mostly on MUC five B. In asthma, and in much of COPD and cystic fibrosis, the balance shifts toward MUC five A-C. Everyone in the field knows that. What nobody had done cleanly is ask whether the ratio itself — not the total amount of mucus, not the inflammation upstream of it — is what breaks clearance.

The experiment is elegant and it is the reason to read this rather than the abstract. They took an immortalized human airway basal cell line and used CRISPR to knock out one mucin or the other, grew both at air-liquid interface until they made real muco-ciliated epithelium, and harvested mucus that is essentially pure in one mucin. Then they mixed those two mucus stocks back together at fixed total protein and at three ratios — three quarters MUC five B, even, and three quarters MUC five A-C — and transplanted the resulting gels onto washed primary human bronchial epithelial cultures from healthy donors. So the cilia are held constant, the total mucus load is held constant, and the only thing that varies is composition.

Transport falls monotonically as MUC five A-C goes up. About thirty microns a second at three-quarters MUC five B, about twenty-three at even, about nineteen at three-quarters MUC five A-C. Roughly a one-and-a-half-fold spread. Particle-tracking microrheology explains why: more MUC five A-C means smaller network pore size and higher microviscosity, from about two point six up to nearly four millipascal-seconds. And critically, ciliary beat frequency is identical across every condition. The cilia are working exactly as hard. The cargo got worse.

Here is the result that makes it a therapeutic hypothesis rather than a materials observation. Pure MUC five B gels and pure MUC five A-C gels transport at the same rate. MUC five A-C is not innately untransportable. The deficit only appears in mixtures, and it appears most sharply when you layer MUC five A-C onto a surface that is already MUC five B-predominant — which is exactly what happens in a diseased airway. The authors read that as tethering, MUC five A-C sticking to the epithelium underneath rather than releasing into the transported layer. And then they ran the correction: in cultures stimulated with interleukin thirteen for a week to produce an asthma-like MUC five A-C phenotype, supplementing with MUC five B improved transport, while supplementing with more MUC five A-C did nothing — and that improvement happened despite adding substantially more total mucus, which should have made things worse.

Be candid about the limits, because there are real ones. The direct head-to-head comparison in the interleukin thirteen cultures — MUC five B versus MUC five A-C — was not statistically significant; the authors say so in their own text, and the significant comparison is against untreated controls. Three biological replicates throughout. Everything is in vitro. And the declared competing interests are none, which in this context is the polite way of saying no patent has been filed.

So what is the commercial angle, honestly. It is not an inhaled MUC five B product. Gel-forming mucins are enormous, heavily O-glycosylated polymers that nobody manufactures recombinantly at therapeutic scale, and pretending otherwise wastes a diligence cycle. And the other direction is taken: Arrowhead has had an inhaled RNA-interference drug that silences MUC five A-C in Phase one-two-a in asthma and COPD since twenty twenty-two. So the lower-the-bad-mucin lane has a funded clinical incumbent, and this paper is supportive evidence for their thesis, not a challenge to it.

What is actually valuable here is two things, and I want to separate them.

The first is the thesis correction, and this is why it leads. Last week at the European Respiratory Society meeting we mapped the alarmin layer and found it contested at four separate points — ligand and receptor for both interleukin thirty-three and T-S-L-P — and we concluded that the only unowned position left was the residual one: the eosinophil-low COPD patient who now gets treated with AstraZeneca's antibody and gets about half the benefit the eosinophil-high patient gets. We did not say what that patient's disease actually is. This paper is the strongest available argument that at least some of that residual is a mucus-mechanics problem rather than an inflammation problem — obstruction produced by a material property downstream of where all four of those drugs converge. AstraZeneca's own poster at that same meeting reported fewer lung segments with mucus plugs. Nobody is selling the mechanism.

The second is the instrument. What Duncan's group has is a quantitative, human, tissue-level readout of mucociliary transport that is sensitive to mucin composition and controlled for ciliary beat. That is a diligence capability, and it is the same shape as the call we made on the Wirtz and Wu three-dimensional drug-response platform two weeks ago: not a therapeutics newco, a measuring device that a BioHub program in the airway would otherwise have to build.

So the verdict, and it is qualified. Virginia and Avi should take this meeting, and the agenda is not "can we license something," because today there is nothing to license. The agenda is two questions. Does the mucin ratio predict who is a mucus-obstruction COPD patient in real sputum — because if it does, that is a stratification asset and the residual population we identified stops being rhetorical. And second, is there any tractable way to move the ratio that is not a recombinant mucin — a secretion-biology target, a glycosylation target, something small. If the answer to the second question is no, this is a watch item and a capability, and we should say so plainly rather than funding a manufacturing problem.

Second lead, and this one is a company if it holds.

Johns Hopkins published a feature on Tuesday on work from Abdel-Rahim Hamad's lab in the Department of Pathology at the School of Medicine, with research fellow Rafid Al-Hallaf and Thomas Donner from the Hopkins Comprehensive Diabetes Center. The subject is type one diabetes, and the pitch is antigen-specific removal of the autoreactive T cells that destroy beta cells — roughly two percent of the T-cell repertoire — while leaving the rest of the immune system intact.

The mechanism is unusual enough to be worth stating carefully. Seven years ago Hamad's group described a lymphocyte that expresses both a T-cell receptor and a B-cell receptor, which they call the X cell. From those cells they derived a germline-encoded IgM autoantibody they call x-mab. In the nonobese diabetic mouse, according to the published abstract, x-mab prevents disease onset, induces durable remission, and preserves functional islets without disrupting systemic immune homeostasis. Mechanistically it binds islet-reactive CD four and CD eight tissue-resident memory T cells in pancreas and pancreatic lymph nodes, and engagement downregulates C-D sixty-nine — the molecule that holds tissue-resident memory cells in the tissue — so the pathogenic cells stop being retained where they do damage. They report the same antibody recognizes the analogous cells from human type one diabetes patients and downregulates C-D sixty-nine on them ex vivo. And structural modelling puts the binding on a conserved motif in the third complementarity-determining region of the T-cell receptor alpha chain, shared by diabetogenic clonotypes in both species.

If that is right, it is not an antigen-specific therapy in the usual sense. It is a key cut to a conserved shape in the receptor of the pathogenic clone. Which is a different and in some ways more powerful bet than the one Winnow is making — Winnow restricts which antigen the engager can see; this restricts by the receptor itself. It is also correspondingly more dangerous, because if that motif is less private than the modelling suggests, the selectivity argument collapses in one experiment.

Now the two things a partner will find in the first hour of diligence, and we should say them first. One: the underlying dual-expresser cell biology was publicly contested. Another group reported in twenty twenty-one that they could not reproduce the enrichment of these cells or the associated public clonotype in type one diabetes patients, and Hamad's group published a formal reply arguing the methods were not followed. That exchange is in the literature under both names and it will come up. Two — and this is a limitation on me, not on them — the twenty twenty-six paper is behind a paywall with no open-access version and no preprint, so everything I have just told you about the mouse and human results is from the published abstract and the university's own feature. I could not read the paper. Reading it is diligence step one, and if the data are as the abstract describes, the dispute over the source cell matters less than it looks, because the asset is an antibody with a defined binding mode, not a cell type.

The funding disclosure is the interesting part for us. The work carries the Keck Foundation, the Norman Raab Foundation, a Breakthrough T1D innovative award, N-I-H money, and the Maryland Innovation Initiative. That last one means the state translational ladder is already engaged and someone at Hopkins Technology Ventures has thought about this. No licensing status is public. That is question one for J-H-T-V, and it should be asked this week, because the landscape around it moved twice in the last five days. On Tuesday Kura carved out Caspian Therapeutics with fifty million dollars and Lilly's participation to expand beta-cell mass with a menin inhibitor. Last week Sernova and Seraxis merged into BetaNova, twenty-five miles from the BioHub, to replace beta cells entirely. Those are both downstream bets. Nobody in that set is stopping the attack. Hamad is.

Third lead, briefly, and the honest answer is do not build it.

Janet Staab and Nicholas Zachos, in the Division of Gastroenterology and Hepatology at the Hopkins School of Medicine, with Julian Naglik at King's College London, posted a preprint on Friday on Candida albicans and colorectal cancer. The observation that makes it interesting is a selectivity result: the fungus invades colorectal cancer cell lines and does not invade healthy patient-derived colon organoid monolayers, and healthy colonic epithelium expresses almost no transglutaminase two — they could not detect the protein at all in healthy organoid lysates, while the enzyme is elevated in colorectal tumors.

In the transglutaminase-high tumor line, fungal invasion triggers an injury response that activates the enzyme, which then covalently crosslinks a fungal hyphal adhesin onto the cancer cell surface. That requires the fungal toxin candidalysin — a toxin-deleted strain fails to activate the enzyme even after sixteen hours of contact. So it is damage, not adhesion, that flips the switch, and the result is a feed-forward loop where the invading fungus chemically functionalizes the tumor surface to hold more fungus.

Separately, and independent of that enzyme, infection pushes all three tumor lines toward an epithelial-to-mesenchymal phenotype and raises the activity of the branching enzyme M-GAT five along with branched N-glycans on the cell surface, and it increases cell migration. Blocking that glycan pathway pharmacologically with swainsonine returned migration to control levels in the transglutaminase-high line — but not in another line, and it did not block the epithelial-to-mesenchymal markers at all. So motility and phenotype are two separable arms, and only one of them is drugged by that tool compound.

Why this is not a company: there is no declared intellectual property, the tool compound is an old alpha-mannosidase inhibitor with a long and unhappy clinical history, and an antifungal adjunct in colorectal cancer is a trial nobody is going to fund on cell-line data. Why it is still on the radar: it is the cleanest statement yet of the chronic-infections thesis we keep articulating — the organism that changes the outcome is not the thing anyone is measuring, and in colorectal cancer care the mycobiome is measured exactly never. And it points at a stratification hypothesis, transglutaminase-high tumors as the colonizable ones, that somebody could test in existing tissue.

The practical action is small and cheap. The isolates in this paper came from the clinical mycology laboratory at Hopkins Hospital. We already have a standing item to borrow rather than build against Mary Ann Jabra-Rizk's Candida collection at U-M-B. That is now two Candida capabilities in Baltimore, sitting under a theme we fund and a tumor type Clasp treats. Esther should make one call and combine them into a single capability note rather than two separate maybes.

That is the radar. One theme correction, one company, and one watch item that earns a phone call. Portfolio Watch follows separately this morning with the Biohaven partial clinical hold, which is the most instructive licensing story of the week. Have a good Sunday.

**LEAD 3 — WATCH, not a build: *Candida albicans* functionalises the colorectal tumour surface (Johns Hopkins School of Medicine).**
Janet F. Staab and Nicholas C. Zachos, Division of Gastroenterology and Hepatology, JHU SOM, with Julian R. Naglik (King's College London). Preprint posted 2026-09-11.
*C. albicans* invades CRC lines (SW480, LoVo, HCT 116) but **not healthy patient-derived colon organoid monolayers**; healthy colonic epithelium expresses almost no TG2 (undetectable in healthy organoid lysates) while TG2 is elevated in CRC. In TG2-high SW480, hyphal invasion triggers an injury response activating TG2, which covalently crosslinks the hyphal adhesin Hwp1 to the tumour cell — **candidalysin-dependent** (an *ece1* deletion strain fails to activate TG2 even at 16 h, so cytotoxicity not contact is the trigger), producing a feed-forward loop that holds more fungus at the tumour niche. **Independently of TG2**, infection drives EMT-like change (E-cadherin loss, altered N-cadherin glycosylation, variable Snail1) and raises **MGAT5** activity and β(1-6)-branched N-glycans, increasing migration. **Swainsonine restored migration to control in infected SW480 but not HCT 116, and did NOT block the EMT markers** — motility and phenotype are separable arms.
*Why not a company:* no competing interests declared (no IP); swainsonine is an old α-mannosidase II inhibitor with a poor clinical history; an antifungal adjunct trial in CRC will not be funded off cell-line data. Funded by NIH R21DE021972, the Sherlock Hibbs Endowment, JHU GI/Hepatology, and BBSRC (Naglik).
