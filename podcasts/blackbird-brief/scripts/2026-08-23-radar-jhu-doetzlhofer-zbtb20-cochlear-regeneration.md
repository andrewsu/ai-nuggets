## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Sourcing Radar for Sunday, August twenty-third. One real lead this week, and it lands uncomfortably close to home: a Johns Hopkins preprint that names the molecular brake holding cochlear supporting cells in their mature, non-regenerating state, shows that brake is still being actively applied in postnatal tissue, and hands you a secreted ligand and its receptor as the druggable node underneath it. Clean intellectual property, no company on it — and the targeting problem it creates is the exact problem Aletira is built to solve. Then a second item out of Kennedy Krieger that I am closing as a watch, not a build. Then the sweep.

The lead is from Angelika Doetzlhofer's lab in the Solomon Snyder Department of Neuroscience at Johns Hopkins, with a joint appointment in Otolaryngology and the Center for Hearing and Balance. First author Charles Morgan.

Here is the setup. Sensorineural hearing loss — age-related, noise-induced — is fundamentally a hair cell problem, and mammals do not replace hair cells. Birds and fish do: their supporting cells, which sit right beside the hair cells and come from the same progenitor pool, convert into new hair cells after damage. Newborn mice manage a little of this, and then, at the end of the first week after birth, exactly as those supporting cells mature, the capacity switches off and never returns. Everyone agrees that closing window is the thing to reopen. Nobody has known what closes it.

This paper puts a name on it. The transcription factor Z-B-T-B twenty is highly expressed in cochlear supporting cells precisely as they mature. Delete it in the developing cochlea and the supporting cells fail to exit the cell cycle on schedule, fail to differentiate on schedule, and — the part that matters — fail to switch off their progenitor gene program. Two stemness genes this lab has worked on for years stay switched on days after they should be gone. These are supporting cells that never fully grew up, and in culture they behave like it. Purified knockout supporting cells formed organoids at about three times the rate of controls, formed larger ones, proliferated more, and when pushed to differentiate produced significantly more hair cells per organoid. In intact tissue explants, using a genetic trick that kills the hair cells on demand, knockout supporting cells made more new hair cells in both damaged and undamaged tissue.

That is a developmental story, and by itself it is not a company — you cannot go back in time and delete a transcription factor from a fifty-year-old's cochlea. Here is the move that makes it a lead. They asked whether the brake is a one-time developmental setting or something the cell keeps actively applying, deleted the gene acutely in already-formed postnatal tissue, and it still worked. Smaller effect, but bigger organoids formed at a higher rate. The repression is ongoing. It is a maintained state, not a shut door. That is the difference between a biology paper and a target.

Then they went looking for how. Profiling the acutely deleted cells turned up a receptor called P-T-P-R-Z one, upregulated. Its ligand is midkine, a secreted growth factor. So they added midkine to normal supporting cell cultures and reproduced the phenotype — larger organoids at every dose, and higher formation rates at ten and fifty nanograms per millilitre but not at a hundred, so there is a real dose-response with a ceiling. That is the commercial hinge. The transcription factor is a repressor and you will not drug it. Midkine and its receptor are a secreted ligand and a cell-surface receptor sitting downstream of the brake. That is a node you can build a molecule against.

Now the commercial read, and be careful, because this field has a body count. Frequency Therapeutics ran precisely this thesis — small molecules to wake up cochlear supporting cells and make new hair cells. Their Phase 2b missed on speech perception in twenty twenty-three, missed every secondary, and the company discontinued both hearing programs, cut fifty-five percent of staff, and pivoted to remyelination in multiple sclerosis — which, I will note without further comment, is what I led with last week. So "regenerate hair cells" is not a fresh pitch; it is a pitch with scar tissue. The difference is that Frequency pushed on generic stemness pathways without knowing what was enforcing the mature state. This paper names the enforcer and hands over a specific downstream axis. That is fundable if you frame it as "the field failed because it pushed the wrong node," not as "hair cell regeneration, take four."

The intellectual property is as clean as we ever see. No competing interests declared by any author. Funding is the National Institute on Deafness and Other Communication Disorders plus the David Rubenstein Fund for Hearing Research. No pharma sponsor, no existing company, no equity. A straight Johns Hopkins tech transfer path.

And here is why this is a Blackbird lead rather than a paper somebody else should build on. If your therapeutic is turning up midkine signalling in cochlear supporting cells, you have a targeting problem immediately. That ligand and receptor are a proliferation axis with real oncology history — the receptor is a glioma target. You do not want to broadcast a proliferative signal into the ear. You want it restricted to supporting cells, at a controlled level, inside a small semi-enclosed compartment. That is Aletira's entire premise, and supporting cells are already the cell type the field aims at for the connexin twenty-six programs. So the framing for Avi and Hemaka is not "should Blackbird build a midkine company." It is: does this become a second payload for SELEXON, delivered locally into the cochlea, where restricting expression to the right cell type at the right level is the safety story rather than a nice-to-have.

Candid limits, and there are three. Everything is organoid and explant — no regeneration in a living animal, no functional hearing recovery. Second, all of it is postnatal day five mouse tissue, a permissive window that does not exist in an adult human cochlea. Third, the acute deletion produced new hair cells only in damaged explants — arguably the right patient population anyway, but the effect needs damage signalling as a partner. So the diligence ask is narrow and cheap: show that activating this pathway increases hair cell counts in an adult mouse cochlea after noise or drug damage, with a hearing readout. One experiment, and it is the whole investment case. Esther, worth a conversation this week. Eddie, this is a provisional-filing moment on the midkine axis specifically, not on the transcription factor.

Second item, and I am closing this as a watch. From Maya Opendak's lab at Kennedy Krieger, with Norman Haughey at Johns Hopkins on the metabolomics: adverse early caregiving reshapes social behaviour through bile acid signalling. Two rat models, one of which cleanly separates social adversity from ordinary stress — and it is the social adversity, not the stress, that reorganises peripheral bile acid and tryptophan metabolism and the gene networks in the basolateral amygdala. Then the intervention: oral supplementation with one primary bile acid, chenodeoxycholic acid, during the adversity period rescued the infant social deficit. The closely related cholic acid did not, and the authors tie that to potency at the farnesoid X receptor, where chenodeoxycholic acid is the most potent endogenous agonist and cholic acid among the weakest.

Scientifically that is lovely. Commercially, here is the problem. The rescuing compound is a generic drug approved for decades; you cannot own it. What you could own is a method of use, a proprietary receptor agonist, or the serum bile acid panel as a stratification biomarker — and that receptor is a crowded, bruised field with known itch and lipid liabilities. Add that the endpoint is infant rat social approach, the effect was sex-specific, and the responding sex flipped between the two models. Interests are clean, funding is N-I-H and Kennedy Krieger. Real biology, no asset yet. Esther, keep Opendak on the watch list — a blood-measurable handle on a neurodevelopmental phenotype becomes a company once the biomarker matures.

The sweep, quickly. From U-M-B, a single-molecule protein sequencing platform out of Bryan Krantz's lab using the anthrax toxin translocase as a dynamic nanopore, reporting ninety-eight percent accuracy on native clinical peptides including a K-RAS G-twelve-D fragment. Potentially a real platform in a hot space, but bioRxiv has not rendered the body and I will not pitch you a paper I could only read the abstract of. First in the queue next week. The Lieber Institute had no corresponding-author preprints in the window.

So that is the radar. One lead worth a meeting this week: the brake on cochlear supporting cell regeneration now has a name, it is still being actively applied in postnatal tissue, and the tractable node under it is a ligand and a receptor — with clean Johns Hopkins intellectual property and a targeting problem we already own the answer to.
