So I want to use the quiet to do something we haven't done in the daily run for a while: a positioning read on the flagship. Aletira has been the program every one of these episodes talked around while leading on something else, and the landscape it lives in has genuinely moved. This is context, not a catalyst — but it's context the team should be carrying into the next board conversation.

Here's the frame. Aletira is our proof point — the first fully incubated Blackbird spinout, the one that ran the whole model, nonprofit grant to seed round to a bench in the Blackbird BioHub. The science is the SELEXON platform, licensed out of Johns Hopkins: it uses alternative RNA splicing to restrict where a transgene actually gets expressed, so you can bolt it onto a vector like an AAV and shut off expression in the cell types you don't want to hit. The lead indication signal is hereditary hearing loss, where cell-type selectivity inside the cochlea is a natural fit. Now hold that value proposition — controlling where a gene therapy expresses — against what has happened to the AAV field this year.

The defining gene-therapy story of 2026 has been the safety reckoning around Sarepta's Elevidys, the AAV micro-dystrophin therapy for Duchenne. Patient deaths from acute liver failure, a boxed warning for acute liver injury, the FDA pulling eligibility for non-ambulatory patients, the platform-technology designation revoked, a two-hundred-patient postmarketing safety study required. And the through-line of that whole episode is systemic, off-target AAV exposure and the liver toxicity that comes with a high systemic dose. Now, I want to be candid about what that does and does not say about Aletira, because it would be easy to over-claim here. SELEXON governs transgene expression selectivity — it doesn't retarget where the capsid physically goes, and a lot of the Elevidys liver signal is driven by capsid dose and biodistribution, which is a different axis. So this is not "SELEXON would have prevented Elevidys." What it is, is validation of the core premise Aletira is built on: that untargeted expression is a real and now FDA-underlined liability of the modality, and that platforms which add a layer of expression control are solving a problem the field has just paid for in the most public way possible. That's a tailwind for the thesis and a talking point for the Series A — the safety narrative has shifted the field toward exactly the kind of precision Aletira sells.

The other half of the landscape is the lead indication itself, and here the read is more double-edged. Hereditary hearing loss has gone from open frontier to a lane with a validated first target and real competition. Regeneron's otoferlin gene therapy, Otarmeni, was approved this spring as the first-and-only gene therapy for genetic hearing loss — and they're providing it free in the US, which resets the commercial reference point for anyone entering behind them. Eli Lilly, through Akouos, is advancing its own dual-vector otoferlin program with striking early hearing restoration. And Sensorion, one of the three original otoferlin players, discontinued its program earlier this summer. So the otoferlin sub-lane is consolidating fast around a couple of well-funded dual-AAV replacement strategies. The implication for Aletira is not that hearing loss is closed — it's that the easy, small-gene, single-cell-type target is now spoken for, and Aletira's real differentiation shows up in the harder monogenic hearing-loss forms, the ones where expressing the transgene in the wrong cochlear cell type is itself the problem, or where you need the selectivity to make a broadly-distributing vector behave. That's the conversation to have with Geoff Lynn's team: where is SELEXON's expression control genuinely decisive versus the now-crowded otoferlin-replacement approach, and does the seed-stage roadmap point at those differentiated targets rather than a me-too swing at the target Regeneron and Lilly already own.

Net-net on Aletira: the modality's safety reckoning validates the platform premise and sharpens the pitch, while the lead indication has gotten more competitive and demands a clear answer on where selectivity actually wins. Both point the same direction — Aletira should be leaning into the targets where cell-type control is the whole game, and the financing story should ride the field's pivot toward precision. Worth a Hemaka or Eddie touchpoint before the next raise.
