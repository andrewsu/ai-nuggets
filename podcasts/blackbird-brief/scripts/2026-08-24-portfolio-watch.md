## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Monday, August twenty-fourth. The day's most important development is a quiet one, and it sharpens the instruction I left you with yesterday: the office that will review Aletira's investigational new drug application has had no permanent director since June of last year, and as of this morning still doesn't. That fact decides how much "agree it with the agency in writing" is actually worth.

Start with what prompted it. Exactly one thing published this morning across the trade press — a BioSpace analysis by Tristan Manalac arguing that F-D-A under Kyle Diamantas is restoring reliability, integrity and transparency after the Makary and Prasad period, and that companies should not read that as leniency. It is worth walking through the evidence, because three of the four cases are gene therapy and they are the closest precedents we have.

Replimune is the one that actually landed. Its oncolytic melanoma therapy, now branded Tudriqev, got accelerated approval on August sixth in combination with nivolumab, in patients who progressed on a P-D-1 blocker. That was the third submission after two rejections, and it printed four days after the target date. The efficacy basis is an objective response rate of about twenty-four percent with a median duration of response of fourteen months, in ninety-one evaluable patients out of a hundred and forty in the pivotal trial. The advisory committee voted ten to three in favor in late July even though the agency's own characterization of the dataset was that it was messy. Then uniQure — F-D-A reversed itself and agreed that the Phase 1/2 data on the Huntington's gene therapy can support an accelerated approval filing, with a confirmatory Phase 3 as the condition; submission is guided to this quarter. Then REGENXBIO — a February complete response letter on the Hunter syndrome gene therapy, reversed in June, no additional studies and no new control arm required, resubmission also this quarter. And Disc Medicine reached a resubmission agreement on bitopertin after a February rejection.

Two quotes to hold onto. Mizuho's Uy Ear, on whether this is a permissive agency: regulatory decisions remain highly data-driven, reconsiderations are likely to remain highly case-specific, and the key lesson is to minimize areas open to interpretation. And Donald Fink, formerly of C-B-E-R, making the point that matters most for us — agreement reached with the agency about study design does not ensure regulatory approval.

Now Capricor, and I want to be precise because I gave you a strong reading yesterday. As of this morning there is still no announcement from the company or the agency. The August twenty-second action date passed in silence. There are two live readings in the public record and neither has been confirmed: STAT reported on the twentieth, from sources, that the therapy is heading for a rejection; and Linda Marbán said on the August thirteenth call that F-D-A is receptive to adding the open-label extension data to the current submission, which mechanically means an extension rather than a decision. Today's piece leans toward the second. I am not going to pick between them for you. What I will say is that the lesson I gave you yesterday does not depend on which way it lands. The analysis plan for that pivotal was finalized one day before unblinding and was never agreed with the agency, and that is true whether the outcome is a rejection or another six months of review.

Which brings me to the part I went and did the work on this morning, because it changes the shape of the action item. Those gene-therapy reversals all ran through one place — the Office of Therapeutic Products inside C-B-E-R. That is the office that will see the SELEXON package. It has not had a permanent director since June of last year. Its acting director, Vijay Kumar, stepped down at the end of June this year. Karim Mikhail, who is himself the acting director of C-B-E-R, took the Office of Therapeutic Products on top of running the center, and the agency said it would advertise the permanent job internally and externally. Nobody has been named. So the whole review chain above our future application is currently held by one person in two acting roles.

Hugh and Avi, the so-what is not "wait for stability." It is the opposite. Everything I said yesterday about agreeing the endpoint and the analysis with the division early gets more important, not less, and it changes what "agree" has to mean. A productive conversation with an acting official is worth very little after that official is replaced. Written minutes from a formal meeting survive a leadership change; a good phone call does not. So when the pre-investigational-new-drug package goes in, ask for the written response, get the endpoint and the statistical analysis plan into the official record, and treat any verbal alignment as unrecorded until you have it back on paper. Fink's sentence is the whole point — agreement on design is not a promise of approval, and it is worth even less if it isn't written down.

There is a second-order read for the raise. Geoff should use the reversals, but carefully. A regulator that reversed itself on two adeno-associated virus programs inside four months, and cleared Ultragenyx on a burden-of-care surrogate last week, is a regulator with appetite for this modality. That is a real climate argument. But the analysts covering it are all saying case-specific, and the agency's own leadership is temporary. So the framing in the deck is "the path exists and it is precedented," not "the agency has gotten easier." The second version will be argued with in the room.

Second item, and it is a week old — I missed it, and it only matters now because of what it makes into a pattern. On the seventeenth, Fulcrum Therapeutics and Slate Medicines announced an all-stock reverse merger with an oversubscribed two-hundred-and-forty-five-million-dollar concurrent private placement. Fulcrum holders end up with five percent, Slate investors ninety-five, the combined company operates as Slate, and it is funded into 2029. The lead asset is a monoclonal antibody that binds both PAY-cap and V-I-P, two neuropeptides in migraine biology, with the company arguing that hitting both beats the PAY-cap-only agents. It is licensed from a China-based company, DartsBio. And here is the stage: it goes into a Phase 1 in healthy volunteers in Australia, with preliminary data in mid-2027.

Put that next to Thursday's item. Ambros, a hundred and fifty million dollars into a Nasdaq shell for neridronate, licensed from Abiogen in Italy, a molecule Grünenthal already failed with. Slate, two hundred and forty-five million into a Nasdaq shell for an antibody licensed from China, not yet in a patient. That is close to four hundred million dollars into pain assets in five days, through the same structure, and neither company discovered its own molecule.

Hemaka, for the gut-restricted GCPII program with Barbara Slusher's team, this hardens what I said Saturday rather than repeating it. What the money is buying in pain is not a mechanism. Ambros bought an enrichment package — five entry gates on a failed molecule. Slate bought a pharmacological differentiation on a target class somebody else already de-risked. Both are answers to the same question, which is "which patients, identified by what, before you dose." We do not have our version of that answer yet, and the two live templates for producing one are an objective enrollment criterion or a receptor-selectivity argument. Pick which one we are running and write it into the preclinical plan, not the Phase 2 protocol.

Eddie, two notes. The first amends what I told you Saturday about the reverse-merger window: it is not restricted to Phase-2-ready assets. Slate did it with a molecule that has not been in a human. A large committed financing and a clean story is apparently sufficient, which widens the option set for a Baltimore asset considerably. The second is a diligence question that did not exist a year ago. Both of these assets were in-licensed from outside the United States, one of them from China — in the same week two Republican members of Congress formally asked the commissioner to discount Chinese clinical data absent recent F-D-A site audits. That is not a reason to avoid in-licensing. It is a reason to know, before we look at any such deal, exactly which data package came from where and whether the sites have been inspected.

Third, the calendar item, and it closes today. The Inspector General's request for information on safe harbors for trial participant remuneration closes at five p.m. Eastern this afternoon. I re-pulled the docket this morning: still eight comments, most recent still August fourteenth, Massive Bio on it, 1104health not. Rose Wang's team has a few hours. And the scope correction from the thirteenth stands — this is about paying patients, not paying physicians for coordination, which is the actual thesis and runs on the slower reimbursement track.

The rest is quiet, which is what a Monday after a weekend looks like. Nothing from Clasp, Adventris or aSKY. Nothing from Skylark or Sensorion. No G-P-R fifty-two news of any kind and still no named buyer for the Nxera asset. Nothing from CAMP4. Zero mentions of Blackbird anywhere in the window. Ahead of us: Ultragenyx's Sanfilippo decision on September nineteenth, CAMP4's analyst day on September twenty-eighth, the Nxera out-license that management says closes this half, and the Clasp monotherapy readout still guided to this year.

To close. The agency is being flexible with gene therapy sponsors right now, and three of the four cases in today's piece prove it — but the office granting that flexibility is being run by one person wearing two acting hats, with no permanent director in more than a year. So get it in writing. And in pain, four hundred million dollars moved in five days, none of it to a novel mechanism and all of it to a stratification story. I'll see you tomorrow.
