## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Saturday, November twenty-first. Today's most important development is one of our own: 1104health has grown its clinician network from about a hundred to more than seven hundred this year, and the state of Maryland has written it up as a startup success story, with Blackbird named as the funder of the original pilot. Behind that, the F-D-A's biologics center had a busy two days. It created a new body to watch cell and gene therapies after approval, and it turned down a Duchenne cell therapy for the second time, even though the company says both of its randomized trials hit their primary endpoints. And a short follow-up on yesterday's China story, because the market answered the congressional report within a day. Three items.

Start with 1104health.

On Friday the Maryland Department of Commerce ran a profile of the company in its Maryland Startup Series, and citybiz picked it up the same day. The headline number: 1104health began twenty twenty-six with roughly a hundred cancer care clinicians in its network and now has more than seven hundred. The state piece lays out the chain plainly. The technology was co-developed and licensed through Johns Hopkins Technology Ventures. Support from Blackbird Laboratories funded the initial pilot. TEDCO followed, through the Maryland Innovation Initiative and a two hundred and twenty-five thousand dollar investment from its Social Impact Funds.

Notice how the company describes itself now. The product is called the Shared Care Network: a secure collaboration platform that sits inside the electronic health records clinicians already use, so a community oncologist can bring in a specialist at an academic center, decide treatment together, and find trials and newer therapies the patient might otherwise miss. Trial access is one thing the network delivers, not the headline. And the state piece says the company is "developing" a business model that pays physicians for the time they spend coordinating care. Developing, not running.

So what for Blackbird? Three reads, mostly for Emily and Eddie, since this is a BioVentures position.

First, seven hundred clinicians counts the supply side, not the business. citybiz said it directly: the company has not disclosed revenue, patient enrollment, or clinical outcomes alongside the network number. That's normal at this stage. But the next investor will ask what happens after the sign-up. How many of those seven hundred are active in a given month? How many cases did they share? How many trial referrals, and how many patients actually enrolled? Ask Rose Wang's team for that funnel now, while it's a conversation and not a diligence request.

Second, the story has moved from "trial-enrollment marketplace" to "shared care." That's probably a smarter pitch to a health system, because it sells better decisions rather than recruitment. But it changes who the customer is. When we last mapped this, the revenue came from pharma sponsors funding the coordination work. A collaboration platform could also be paid for by health systems, academic centers that want referrals, or eventually payers. Those buyers have different sales cycles, so it's worth knowing which one the next raise is built on.

Third, compliance. Paying physicians for time spent coordinating care is the part of the model that touches the anti-kickback rules, and we've flagged before that the federal clinical-trial initiative names the missing reimbursement as a barrier without yet saying how it should be fixed. With seven hundred clinicians instead of a hundred, the compensation design has to be clean before payments start, not after. This isn't a sign that anything is wrong. It's the thing to have nailed down before scaling the payments.

And one small thing for Matt and Maisha. A state economic-development page now tells the story in our order: Hopkins invention, Blackbird pilot, state money, a growing company. That's a citation worth keeping for the next annual report or funder conversation.

Now the F-D-A, which matters for Aletira and NuGate.

On Thursday the F-D-A set up what it calls the C-B-E-R Safety Council, effective immediately. It covers everything the biologics center regulates, including cell and gene therapies, vaccines and blood products, and it replaces an older internal safety working group. The structure is what matters. The council sits outside the product offices that review and approve these products, and reports directly to the center director. Its job is to review safety signals after approval, run structured benefit-risk assessments, and recommend actions: safety label changes, risk-management programs, required post-approval studies, and market withdrawals. It is also told to publish public safety communications promptly after significant findings, along with the evidence behind them. Membership and procedures haven't been posted yet.

On Friday evening, two days before its action date, Capricor said it had received a second complete response letter for deramiocel. That's its cell therapy for Duchenne muscular dystrophy, made from donor heart cells and given by infusion every three months. According to the company, the letter says the data are not enough to establish substantial evidence of effectiveness, and it cites other clinical deficiencies that Capricor didn't describe. Capricor's chief executive says both randomized, placebo-controlled trials met their primary endpoints and that the company stands behind the integrity of its data. Its phase three enrolled a hundred and six patients with advanced disease. On the upper-limb function scale it showed a difference of about four and a half percentage points at twelve months, with a p-value of point zero three. But NeurologyLive reports that F-D-A reviewers disputed whether the trial met its prespecified endpoints under the original analysis plan. They questioned how missing data were handled and the changes made to the analysis plan after randomization. Last July an advisory committee voted nine to three that the data didn't show effectiveness in the heart disease Capricor first sought approval for. Several outlets report further concerns about how the trial was run. The letter isn't public, so we're keeping to what the company and the reviewers have said on the record.

Here's how the two fit together. In one week, the biologics center split post-approval safety away from the people who approved the product, and it applied the substantial-evidence standard strictly to a rare pediatric cell therapy that had every expedited designation available. Neither move lowers the bar for rare disease.

The read for Anthony, Jon and Hemaka, on how NuGate and Aletira design their first controlled studies: Capricor's problem, as the reviewers describe it, isn't only the size of the effect. It's that the analysis moved after randomization, and that missing data were handled in a way the agency didn't accept. Both are fully in our control and cheap to fix early. Lock the statistical analysis plan before the first patient is randomized. Prespecify how dropouts and deaths will be handled, because in A-L-S, and in any progressive disease, missing data are informative, not random. And choose a primary endpoint you're prepared to win or lose on. BioPharma Dive notes that the F-D-A's rejection of Summit's lung cancer drug made the same point this month: a survival effect seen only in after-the-fact analyses wasn't enough. Two rejections, one lesson. Post-hoc doesn't count.

The read for Avi on Aletira concerns the product after approval. Two weeks ago we covered Zolgensma's integration-linked tumors and how much of Novartis's damage came from how families heard the news. Now there's a dedicated F-D-A body, separate from the reviewers who know the product best, that is told to publish its safety findings quickly and show its evidence. In practice the agency can go public before a sponsor's carefully sequenced rollout. So the disclosure plan belongs in the target product profile from the start. And the long-term follow-up data this council will read, including vector levels in liver, gonad and spleen after a dose to the cochlea, need to be data we already hold, not data we promise to collect.

Last, a short one for Eddie, Emily and Matt.

Yesterday we covered the congressional commission's warning that NewCos built on Chinese assets pull U.S. venture money away from U.S. innovation. On Friday BioPharma Dive's weekly charts showed why investors aren't deterred. Six startups built on medicines from China have been acquired or gone public this year. Three were bought in billion-dollar deals: Ouro by Gilead, Candid by U-C-B, and Ollin by Johnson and Johnson on Monday. Ollin and Ouro were each acquired just over a year after launching. The appeal is time. Investors skip years of discovery by starting with an asset that's already in or close to human testing.

So, a candid correction to our own framing. Policy risk is real, but it won't win us a syndicate partner by itself, because the exits are real and fast. Our pitch has to beat the China NewCo on what it can't offer. That means a first-in-class mechanism rather than a fast follower, clean ownership of the I-P, with no licensor holding equity and keeping the China rights, and a credible, specific timeline to our own first human data. That last part is where we're weakest against them, so it's what we should tighten first.

That's the watch for this week. Have a good weekend, Blackbird team.
