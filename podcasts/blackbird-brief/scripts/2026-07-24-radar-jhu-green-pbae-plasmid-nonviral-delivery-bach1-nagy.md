## Script

This is the Blackbird Brief Sourcing Radar for Friday, July twenty-fourth, and I'll give you the honest headline first: it was a thin day, and in fact a thin week. The four-day sweep of the Johns Hopkins, U-M-B, and Lieber preprint servers turned up nothing with a new, ownable asset attached — that's the fourth straight lean preprint week — and the peer-reviewed journals this week are heavy on clinical trials and reviews rather than the founding-shaped biology we're hunting for. So today is a short, two-item radar, and I want to be clear up front that neither item is a clean spin-out. Both are watch-and-relationship items, and I'll tell you exactly why for each.

The one I could read cover to cover, and the anchor for today, is a fresh Johns Hopkins paper out of Jordan Green and Stephany Tzeng's lab in Biomedical Engineering, published this week in PLoS One. On its face it's a nuts-and-bolts engineering study: they built fourteen different versions of the DNA cargo you package inside their poly-beta-amino-ester nanoparticles — the non-viral gene-delivery system Green's lab is known for — and systematically asked which design gives the best gene expression in liver-cancer and brain-cancer cells. They varied the promoter, the plasmid backbone, the length, and the antibiotic-resistance marker. Two results matter. First, a minimal plasmid they call Nanoplasmid won on expression across almost every cell line. And second — this is the part with commercial teeth — that minimal plasmid is the only one carrying no antibiotic-resistance gene at all, which is exactly the kind of thing the FDA wants gone before a gene therapy reaches the clinic. So the real content here isn't a discovery, it's manufacturing readiness: they're closing the chemistry-and-controls gap between a benchtop non-viral delivery platform and something you could actually file on.

Here's my candid so-what for Blackbird. This is a genuinely strong platform — non-viral delivery is a hot lane, and it sits right next to the Mao-lab anionic-lipid-nanoparticle work we've already been tracking as a delivery chassis. But this particular paper is incremental optimization of a platform that has already been spun out, repeatedly. The conflict-of-interest disclosure is the tell: Green holds positions at three companies — Cove Therapeutics, Dome Therapeutics, and OncoSwitch Therapeutics — and Tzeng co-founded OncoSwitch. So the value here has largely been captured already; this isn't a fresh sourcing opportunity so much as a signal about where the Green ecosystem is heading. My action for the team is ecosystem intelligence, not a term sheet: Eddie, it's worth knowing where OncoSwitch, Dome, and Cove each are on financing and which indications they've carved between them, because that's a cluster of Baltimore gene-therapy companies coming out of one lab, and we should understand the map. And the antibiotic-free minimal-plasmid detail is a small but real de-risking data point to file away for any non-viral program we look at, theirs or anyone else's.

The second item I'll flag more briefly, and I want to be upfront that this one I've read only at the abstract level — the paper is behind an Elsevier paywall, so treat it as a target-biology flag, not a finished read. It's from László Nagy's group, published this week in the journal Immunity. Nagy is a heavyweight in immunometabolism who's now at Johns Hopkins, and the paper is about a heme-sensing transcriptional repressor called BACH1. What they show is that BACH1 does something unusual — it acts as what they call a pioneer repressor, simultaneously opening up regions of the genome to make them accessible while actively holding transcription down, and then rapidly redistributing when an inflammatory signal arrives. In animals, deleting BACH1 impaired the ability of macrophages to polarize and adapt, and hurt the tissue's resilience during inflammation and regeneration.

The reason I mention it at all: BACH1 is already an active drug-discovery target in other contexts, and this work sharpens the case for tuning it to control macrophage-driven inflammation and repair — which touches fibrosis, regeneration, and inflammatory disease broadly. But candidly, this is fundamental mechanism. There's no asset, no disclosed IP, no platform to license — it's target validation, not a company. So my read is: log it as a target-biology watch and, more importantly, as a relationship. Nagy is exactly the caliber of principal investigator now sitting inside our primary partner institution that we should have a line to, and if his group's macrophage-reprogramming biology ever crystallizes into a discrete therapeutic hypothesis, we want to be the first call.

So that's a deliberately short Friday radar, and an honest one. The fully-read anchor is a Johns Hopkins non-viral gene-delivery paper whose real value to us is a read on the Green-lab company cluster — Cove, Dome, and OncoSwitch — rather than a new deal. The watch item is a strong BACH1 macrophage-biology paper from Nagy's lab that's a relationship to build, not an asset to chase. No new sourcing opportunity cleared the founding bar this week, and I'd rather tell you that plainly than dress up filler. I'm back tomorrow.
