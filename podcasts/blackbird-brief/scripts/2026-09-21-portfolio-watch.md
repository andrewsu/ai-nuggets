Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Monday, September twenty-first, and the most important development for the portfolio came over the wire this morning: a subretinal A-A-V gene therapy hit its primary endpoint in a randomized, controlled pivotal trial in an inherited sensory disease — the first one ever to do that in this indication — and the endpoint it hit is the one we have been circling on Aletira since the twelfth.

Five items. Aletira twice, the autoimmune theme, the buyer set, and thirty seconds on a pot of federal money with a deadline attached.

Start with Beacon.

Beacon Therapeutics reported positive topline data from VISTA, the pivotal trial of laru-zova — laruparetigene zovaparvovec — in X-linked retinitis pigmentosa. That disease is caused by mutations in the R-P-G-R gene, it destroys photoreceptors starting in childhood, it affects roughly one in twenty-five thousand males, and there is no approved treatment. The trial enrolled eighty-five male subjects aged twelve to forty-eight, across two dose groups and an untreated control arm, over twelve months.

The primary endpoint, and this is the part that matters, was endorsed by the F-D-A in advance: the proportion of participants gaining at least fifteen letters of low-luminance visual acuity at month twelve. High dose, thirty-one percent responders, p-value zero point zero zero one nine. Low dose, twenty-four percent, p-value zero point zero one zero six. In the untreated control arm, zero responders. Beacon plans to start a rolling Biologics License Application later this year, and the program already carries Regenerative Medicine Advanced Therapy and Fast Track from F-D-A, P-R-I-M-E in Europe, and orphan designation on both sides.

Here is why this outranks the read we filed on the twelfth. On September twelfth we logged Opus Genetics' Type C alignment on the B-E-S-T-one program — microperimetry at prespecified loci plus a patient-reported outcome, in a randomized controlled trial — as the endpoint template for a compartmentalized sensory organ. But that was an agency conversation, accepted off five open-label patients. This is the trial actually run. Eighty-five patients, a real untreated control, a responder threshold on a machine-measured scale, and an endpoint the agency signed off on before anyone saw the data.

Now the contrast that turns this from a data point into a lesson, and I want everyone to hold on to it. Same gene. Same disease. Same modality. In April, MeiraGTx bought bota-vec — botaretigene sparoparvovec, also an A-A-V carrying R-P-G-R — back from Johnson and Johnson for twenty-five million dollars upfront. Johnson and Johnson's Phase three trial in that program, LUMEOS, ninety-five patients, missed its primary endpoint. The primary was a visual mobility assessment — a navigation maze. And here is the part to sit with: LUMEOS also showed ten- and fifteen-letter gains in low-luminance visual acuity. The same signal Beacon has just banked as a win. MeiraGTx is filing anyway, off the secondaries.

Two sponsors, one gene, one disease, one modality. The one that agreed a responder threshold on an instrument that reads out in letters hit its endpoint. The one that chose a functional task measuring how a person walks through a room missed it. The biology was almost certainly never the difference.

Geoff, this sharpens the September fourteenth instrument action rather than replacing it. The action was to find the hearing-loss equivalent of a validated functional scale. The refinement is a preference order: a threshold-and-responder endpoint on a quantitative, machine-measured scale beats a naturalistic functional task, and you get it endorsed before you run, not after. Audiometric thresholds are machine-measured and quantitative. Speech-in-noise performance and real-world auditory function are the maze. And notice the tension that creates with our own thesis, because we have been arguing for months that the commercially interesting deficit in treated hearing-loss patients is precisely speech perception in noise. That is probably right, and it is also the endpoint most likely to behave like LUMEOS. Both can be true. The pre-I-N-D ask has to hold them apart and say plainly which one is the primary and which one is the label story.

Two candid limits before we get comfortable. First, thirty-one percent responders means sixty-nine percent of treated eyes did not clear the bar. A responder analysis against a zero-responder control is a legitimate and rigorous design; it is not the same sentence as "the drug works." Second, and Avi should pull on this one: the dose response is inverted in the secondaries. On the ten-letter threshold, the low dose did better — fifty-nine percent against forty-eight. On microperimetry mean sensitivity, the low dose reached significance at p equals zero point zero four, and the high dose did not, at zero point zero six. Drug-related adverse events also ran higher in the low-dose group, thirty-eight percent against twenty-five. Higher dose wins the primary, lower dose wins two secondaries. There is a dose-selection story inside VISTA that the topline does not tell, and for a company whose entire pitch is about controlling where and how much a transgene is expressed, that is the question we should want answered before we ever cite this trial in a deck.

Which lands naturally on the second item, because the secondaries are the whole story there too.

BioSpace published a piece this morning on where rare-disease regulation is heading under the new permanent leadership. Karim Mikhail at C-B-E-R and Michael Davis at C-D-E-R were made permanent on September eighth. R-B-C reads Mikhail as a proponent of flexible trial design for cell and gene therapy in rare indications, and Davis as favorable for C-N-S, neuropsychiatry, rare disease and novel modalities. The evidence is already on the record: under Mikhail, the agency reversed the prior demand that uniQure run a whole new Huntington's trial with a sham-surgery control, and uniQure filed its Biologics License Application this month on the data it already had.

The vocabulary in that article is worth borrowing. John Crowley, who runs B-I-O and founded rare-disease companies to save his own children, says he hates the word flexibility in rare disease because it implies a lower standard. What he wants is an adapted framework. Mikhail agreed, and said the sentence I would put on the wall: if you have a lot of flexibility, it is difficult to be predictable, but with an adapted regulatory framework for rare diseases, you can be very predictable.

Now the case study, and this is the one to brief. Capricor's cell therapy for Duchenne muscular dystrophy met its Phase three primary endpoint — a statistically significant benefit on upper-limb function. The advisory committee voted nine to three against it. The discussion, and the question the advisers were actually asked to vote on, centered on a secondary endpoint: decline in left ventricular ejection fraction. A former cancer-drug regulator quoted in the piece put it exactly right — the functional outcomes from that trial were really meaningful, it just was not the topic of discussion.

Read that next to Beacon. One company hits its primary and has an inverted dose response sitting in its secondaries. Another company hits its primary and loses the room on a secondary. So here is the addition to the Aletira pre-I-N-D file, and it is a sentence, not a project: every secondary endpoint you agree to collect is a question you have agreed to be asked in public. Choose them as deliberately as the primary, and know in advance what an unfavorable result on each one does to the primary's standing. That is a Chief Medical Officer and Chief Development Officer problem — the same conclusion we reached Friday off the Serapha hiring — and it needs an owner before the meeting, not a hire after it.

One watch-item on that thread. Heidi Overton is the nominee for commissioner and has stated no position on any of this. The health-policy read is that continuity is the thing to watch, and B-M-O names a specific test balloon: Taysha's Rett syndrome gene therapy, a single-arm design with a natural-history control, aligned with the agency under the prior leadership, with feedback expected in the first half of twenty twenty-seven. Put Taysha on the watch list. Whether that alignment survives new leadership is the cleanest available signal on whether our own external-control and adapted-endpoint assumptions are safe to underwrite in the Series A.

Third item, and it belongs to the autoimmune theme.

Cue Biopharma reported Phase two topline on Saturday for CUE-two-twenty-one in chronic spontaneous urticaria — chronic hives that stay uncontrolled on antihistamines. A hundred forty-five patients, run in China, randomized and double-blind, with both a placebo arm and an active comparator: omalizumab, the drug sold as Xolair, at three hundred milligrams.

The molecule is an anti-IgE antibody engineered for a deliberate dual mechanism. Like Xolair, it stops IgE from binding its high-affinity receptor. Unlike Xolair, it leaves IgE able to bind C-D-twenty-three on B cells, which feeds back and reduces the synthesis of new IgE. Neutralize the pool, and turn down the tap.

The primary endpoint, complete resolution of hives at week twelve, came in at fifty-four percent on the top dose against eleven percent on placebo, dose-responsive and significant at all three doses. Xolair in the same trial was forty-one percent. But the number that matters is the late one. The last dose went in at week sixteen. At week twenty-eight — twelve weeks after anyone took anything — the top dose still showed sixty percent complete resolution, against twenty-four percent for Xolair. A thirty-six-point gap, off drug.

Jon, Virginia: this is a Winnow item, not an allergy item. On the seventeenth we concluded that Winnow cannot win on convenience and has to compete on depth and durability. On Friday, ninety-three million dollars into Georgiamune and sixty-six into RegCell capitalized both ends of that argument. This is the third instance in five days and it is the cleanest of the three, because it comes with a head-to-head against the actual standard of care and a measurement taken with the drug switched off. The claim being underwritten across all three is one claim: that a mechanism which changes the disease process beats one that suppresses the symptom, and that the way you prove it is to stop dosing and keep measuring. If that is the axis Winnow intends to win on, then an off-treatment durability arm is not a nice-to-have in the development plan. It is the product claim, and it has to be designed in from the start rather than discovered at the end.

Candid limits, and they are real ones. The week twenty-eight comparison against Xolair is post hoc, and the protocol explicitly included the active comparator without planned statistical testing. The omalizumab arm is seventeen patients. And the trial was run in China by a company related to the licensor. That is a hypothesis-generating result being presented as a comparative one. The mechanism is genuinely interesting; the superiority claim is not yet earned.
