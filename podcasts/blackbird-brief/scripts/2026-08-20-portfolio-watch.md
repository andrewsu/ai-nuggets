## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Portfolio Watch for Thursday, August twentieth. Yesterday afternoon the F-D-A approved a systemically delivered gene therapy whose central design choice is letting the target gene's own promoter decide how much protein gets made — and the label that came with it is the most useful document Aletira will read this quarter, in both directions.

Here's what happened. Ultragenyx received accelerated approval for Genglycos, generic name pariglasgene brecaparvovec, in glycogen storage disease type one-A, for patients eight years and older. Single intravenous infusion, A-A-V-eight capsid, carrying the glucose-six-phosphatase gene under its own native promoter. It is the first therapy ever approved at the cause of that disease, and it printed Wednesday, four days ahead of the Sunday action date. I told you Friday or Monday. It came early.

Four things in that approval bear on us, and they do not all point the same direction.

The first is the one that goes in the deck. Approved A-A-V products already use restricted promoters — Hemgenix and Roctavian run liver-specific promoters, Elevidys runs a muscle one. What is different here is that Ultragenyx did not build a synthetic tissue-specific promoter. They used the target gene's own native promoter, and they did it for a regulatory reason: so the treated hepatocyte still answers to insulin and cortisol the way a normal cell does. That is not cell-type restriction. That is physiological, dynamic control of how much protein gets made and when.

Line that up with what we established yesterday. Skylark restricts at the three-prime untranslated region, using the target gene's own native sequence. Ultragenyx restricts at the promoter, using the target gene's own native sequence. Aletira restricts at the splice. Three companies, three different elements on the same molecule, one shared premise — that the endogenous regulatory grammar of the gene should govern the transgene, rather than a viral promoter running flat out in every cell it reaches. As of yesterday that premise has an F-D-A approval behind it. This is the single best citation we have that expression control is a design axis the agency has now seen and cleared, and Geoff should be using it inside a week. It is a tailwind, and it is specific enough to be quoted.

The second thing is the correction to that story, and I want it on the record before someone else says it to us. Native-promoter physiological control did not make the product safe. Read the label. Seventy-one percent of treated patients had liver enzyme elevations. Every patient gets corticosteroids after infusion — not optionally, as standard mitigation. Twenty-four percent developed adrenal insufficiency, including serious events; fourteen percent developed Cushingoid features. Those are toxicities of the mitigation, not of the transgene. Ten percent had anaphylaxis. The product is contraindicated outright in severe hepatic fibrosis or cirrhosis, and the label carries the usual language about vector integration and a theoretical tumorigenicity risk.

So the honest read is this: expression control is not an immunology answer. The immune problem in systemic A-A-V is the capsid and the dose, not the cassette, and a perfectly regulated cassette does not touch it. If we pitch SELEXON as a safety platform in general terms, a gene-therapy-literate investor will put this label in front of us and we will lose the room. The defensible pitch is narrower and stronger: SELEXON solves off-target expression, which is a different problem from capsid immunogenicity, and it is the problem that determines whether the therapeutic works in the cell you meant. Hugh and Avi should tighten that sentence, because the loose version is now falsifiable in public.

The third thing is small and elegant and it argues for our beachhead. The post-marketing commitment Ultragenyx agreed to includes a control group, and the control group is patients who sought commercial treatment but cannot be dosed because they carry anti-A-A-V-eight antibodies. Pre-existing seroprevalence now excludes a population reliable enough that the agency will let it stand in as a comparator arm. That is a systemic-delivery tax. In a small semi-enclosed compartment, dosed locally at micro-dose levels, it is a much smaller tax — a real, non-promotional argument for the ear as the first indication, and one we can make without saying anything about Skylark.

The fourth thing is money, and it changes a strategic question rather than a scientific one. Ultragenyx received a priority review voucher with this approval. Congress revived the rare pediatric disease voucher program in the February appropriations act after it had lapsed, and the program now sunsets on September thirtieth, 2029 — the agency cannot award vouchers under it after that date. There is no separate deadline on getting the designation, so the binding constraint is the approval date. And these things are trading: Rocket sold one for a hundred and eighty million dollars, Denali for a hundred and ninety-five, Immedica and Jazz for two hundred each, all this year.

So a rare pediatric approval now carries roughly a two-hundred-million-dollar bearer instrument attached to it, and the window closes in about three years. Say the uncomfortable part plainly. Aletira is preclinical. A wholly owned program starting first-in-human from here does not reach approval before September of 2029 — that is not a pessimistic read, it is arithmetic. Skylark, dosing since May, plausibly can. Sensorion, with applications filed in Canada and France, plausibly can.

That is not a reason for gloom, it is a reason to be deliberate. The voucher is not our asset on a go-it-alone path — but there is now a partner already in the clinic in a pediatric genetic disease, on a clock, for whom a bolt-on expression layer is worth more today than it will be worth in 2030. That argues for licensing SELEXON into somebody else's investigational new drug application on a defined timeline, in parallel with our own program rather than instead of it. Matt and Eddie — I'd like that as an explicit second monetization path in the Series A narrative rather than left implicit, because right now the story reads as a single-track race we are behind in, and it does not have to.

One more calendar marker on this thread: Ultragenyx has a second decision coming, on its A-A-V therapy for Sanfilippo syndrome type A, with an action date of September nineteenth. Another read on the temperature, and it comes with another voucher if it clears.

Second item, and it lands on the cancer side of the board. Also yesterday, Merck and Moderna announced that the Phase 3 INTerpath-001 trial met its primary endpoint of recurrence-free survival and its key secondary endpoint of distant metastasis-free survival. Eleven hundred and thirty-seven patients with completely resected stage two-B through four melanoma, randomized to their individualized neoantigen therapy plus Keytruda, or Keytruda alone. Statistically significant, described as clinically meaningful, no new safety signals. Moderna's stock nearly doubled on it.

This is the first positive Phase 3 for an individualized neoantigen therapy and the first for an m-R-N-A cancer therapy, and the reason it belongs in this briefing is the premise underneath it. The claim being tested was that if you teach T cells to see the specific mutations in a tumor, patients do better. That claim now has a Phase 3 behind it. It is the same claim sitting underneath Adventris, the Hopkins mutant-K-R-A-S peptide vaccine working out of the BioHub, and underneath Clasp, where BioVentures is in the Series A and the entire pitch is that a mutant peptide presented on H-L-A is a sufficient and specific address for a T-cell engager.

Now the candid part, which is the more valuable half. The Moderna construct encodes up to thirty-four neoantigens, built bespoke for each individual patient, given over nine doses. That is the most operationally expensive way to be right. So this readout does two things at once — it validates the biology and it prices the individualized route, in public, in front of every pharma partner. Which makes the shared-neoantigen, off-the-shelf version of the same idea more valuable, not less: six K-R-A-S mutations in one peptide vaccine you manufacture once, or an engager you make once and give to anyone with the mutation and the right H-L-A type. That sentence should be in Adventris's and Clasp's partnering conversations this quarter, and as of yesterday it has a Phase 3 standing behind it rather than a hypothesis.

Two things to hold. No hazard ratios were disclosed — the effect sizes go to a medical meeting before submission, and this was an interim analysis with overall survival still running, so don't let anyone in our orbit quote numbers we do not have. And adjuvant melanoma on top of pembrolizumab is the friendliest setting in oncology for this modality. The other eight trials in that program — lung, bladder, kidney, pancreatic, gastric — are the ones that tell us whether it travels.

Third, the Inspector General docket. I pulled it directly again this morning. Still eight comments, most recent still August fourteenth, still not one from a trade association or a major sponsor. Comments close Monday at five p.m. Eastern. Two business days after today. Nothing has changed except the number of days left, and that is the whole point of mentioning it again.

The rest is short. On the gut program, the full Phase 2b results for Teva's anti-T-L-one-A antibody duvakitug published Tuesday — remission in ulcerative colitis, endoscopic response in Crohn's. The topline has been known since 2024, so this is not news, it is one more brick in a wall that was already built. It reinforces yesterday's line rather than changing it: inflammation is spoken for, fibrosis was claimed last week, pain is the open axis. No G-P-R fifty-two news; Nxera still has not named a buyer. CAMP4 has nothing since the quarter on the thirteenth; September twenty-eighth remains the event. No fresh K-R-A-S catalyst for aSKY, and no Clasp update. Nothing new from Skylark or Sensorion since our own work yesterday — the docket watch on the Skylark assignee stands. And zero mentions of Blackbird anywhere in the window.

So, to close. Yesterday the agency approved a gene therapy that regulates its transgene with the gene's own promoter, which is the best external endorsement our flagship's whole thesis has ever gotten — attached to a label that shows exactly how much of the safety problem expression control does not solve, and a voucher worth two hundred million dollars on a clock that runs out before we could reach it alone. Take the endorsement, tighten the claim, and treat the voucher as a reason to sell the layer to someone already in the clinic. I'll see you tomorrow.
