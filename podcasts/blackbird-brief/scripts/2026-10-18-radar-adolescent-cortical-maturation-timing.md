Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Sourcing Radar for Sunday, October eighteenth, and I want to be straight with you about the week before I start. Two thousand one hundred and seventy-seven preprints came off bioRxiv and medRxiv in the past eight days. Thirty of them have a Johns Hopkins, Lieber, Kennedy Krieger or Maryland author. Nothing from Lieber, nothing from U-M-B-C, nothing from Wilmer. And of the handful worth reading in full, exactly one has a readable body today — the rest are still waiting on bioRxiv to render. So this is a short radar: one lead I read cover to cover and am recommending we pass on, for reasons that are useful; one published paper that is the most portfolio-relevant thing that appeared all week and that I could not get the full text of; and one deferral I want someone to pick up on Monday.

Start with the lead. The paper is called "Inflections in adolescent cortical maturation signal psychopathology risk." It is led out of Mass General and Harvard by Joshua Roffman, with the Department of Psychiatry at the University of Maryland School of Medicine and the Maryland Psychiatric Research Center in Baltimore among the contributing institutions, alongside Cambridge and Washington University.

Here is the science in plain terms. They took twenty-nine thousand four hundred and eighty-two structural brain scans from eleven thousand seven hundred and twenty adolescents in the big federally funded A-B-C-D cohort, imaged every two years from age nine or ten through fifteen or sixteen. The cortex thins during adolescence — that is normal, and it is thought to reflect synaptic pruning. What nobody had resolved is whether that thinning follows one curve or many. Their answer is that every region follows the same S-shaped curve, but each region starts and finishes at a different time, so whether a given region looks linear or curved depends entirely on when you happen to image it. That single observation reconciles a decade of contradictory papers, and it is the genuinely good part of this work.

Then they located, for each region, the age at which thinning decelerates — the break point. Those break points run from thirteen and a half to just under sixteen, and they fall in order along what the field calls the sensorimotor-to-association axis. Sensory cortex finishes first, higher-order association cortex last. Fifty-five percent of the variance, and it survives a spatial permutation test.

The clinical claim is the part that matters to us, and it is carefully done. They tested three competing models: does current thickness go with current symptoms, do earlier symptoms predict later thickness, or does earlier thickness predict later symptoms. Only the third one was significant. A thicker cortex at nine or ten predicted more symptoms at thirteen or fourteen, up to four years ahead, and the predictive regions were only ever regions that were actively maturing — never the slow ones. The effect was driven by internalizing symptoms, anxiety and depression, rather than by rule-breaking and aggression. And the predictive regions overlap with a gene co-expression network whose peak expression is before birth, which implies the risk is scaffolded prenatally and only becomes visible a decade later.

So, is it a company. My read is no, and the reason is one number they report honestly. They ran a clinical sanity check — nine hundred and thirty-four adolescents with clinically significant symptoms against nine hundred and thirty-four propensity-matched controls — and the odds ratios on the predictive regions come in between one point one two and one point one seven. That is a real population-level effect and it is nowhere near an individual-level test. You cannot look at one fourteen-year-old's scan and say anything. The second problem is reproducibility: in their lower-quality replication sample, only forty-six of sixty-eight regions kept the same trajectory class, with a kappa of zero point five three. The third problem is ownership. This is open federal cohort data, the analysis is Harvard-led, Maryland is a contributing site rather than the originator, and there is no patent declared anywhere in the paper. There is nothing here to license.

But I am not raising it to say no. I am raising it because of what it does to two live questions. The first is for the Lieber G-P-R fifty-two program. We have been going back and forth on which instrument measures the cognitive and negative symptom claim, and we took a hard lesson this week from Vanda's alpha-seven failure on a self-rated scale. This paper is an argument that in adolescent psychiatry the useful variable is not the magnitude of a brain measure but its timing — the same thickness means opposite things at twelve and at sixteen. If anyone pitches us a structural imaging biomarker for a neuropsychiatric asset, the first question is now what age window it was calibrated in. The second is about prevention trials generally. The reason early-intervention psychiatry is almost unfundable is that you have to enroll thousands of healthy adolescents to catch a few hundred who convert. An enrichment tool is what makes that arithmetic work — and this paper is the clearest statement yet of how far short the current best attempt falls. An odds ratio of one point one five does not enrich anything. That is a well-specified gap, and well-specified gaps are where we work. I would not take a meeting on this paper. I would take a meeting with anyone who can get that number to three.

Second item, and it is the one that frustrates me. On Friday, Science Advances published work from the Solomon Snyder Department of Neuroscience and the Neurosurgery Pain Research Institute at Johns Hopkins — Michael Caterina and Mollie Meffert's groups, with Xinbei Li as first author. The finding is that after peripheral nerve injury, an R-N-A binding protein called Lin-twenty-eight goes up in pain-sensing neurons, and goes up higher and for longer in the injured axon itself than in the cell body. Lin-twenty-eight suppresses a family of small R-N-As called let-seven, and those let-seven molecules turn out to be sitting on metabolic transcripts inside healthy sensory axons. Injury removes that brake, the nerve's energy metabolism near the injury falls apart, and the neuron becomes hyperexcitable. Two interventions reverse it: restoring let-seven locally fixes the respiration defect, and knocking out Lin-twenty-eight specifically in pain-sensing neurons fixes both the metabolism and the excess firing, in the peripheral nerve and in the spinal cord dorsal horn.

Why I care: non-opioid pain is one of our named early-stage themes, our thesis on it is that entrants compete on a property or a population and never on the target, and this is a genuinely new target layer — axonal energy metabolism as the thing that makes injured nerves fire. Caterina is the person who cloned the capsaicin receptor; this is not a speculative group. It is funded in part by the Neurosurgery Pain Research Institute and the Blaustein Pain Research Award, both Hopkins. Here is the honest limit, and I am flagging it rather than talking around it: Science Advances is behind a Cloudflare wall that blocks every route we have, the paper is too new for the open repositories, and there is no preprint version. I have read the abstract and nothing more, so everything I just said is the authors' summary and not something I verified in the results. I am not making a recommendation on it today. What I want is for someone with Hopkins library access to pull the P-D-F this week, because if the axonal-metabolism mechanism holds up in the figures, this is the strongest pain lead we have seen since the Guan M-R-G-P-R-X-one work.

Last, a deferral for Monday. A preprint posted Tuesday from Sunny Shin's lab at Penn shows that the human enzymes caspase-four and caspase-five each assemble into distinct signalling complexes in response to bacterial endotoxin, and that they respond to a broader and partly different set of endotoxin structures than the mouse enzyme everybody models sepsis with. Transgenic mice carrying the human enzyme control one pathogen better and die of sepsis more readily from another. Our interest is the author list: Robert Ernst at the U-M-B School of Dentistry is on it, and the lipid A structural chemistry that makes the whole experiment possible is his. Ernst is already an active sourcing lead for us on his synthetic adjuvant platform. The commercial shape here is two separately druggable human targets in gram-negative sepsis plus an argument that the standard mouse model systematically mispredicts, which is a field that has buried a lot of capital on exactly that mistake. The body has not rendered yet — six days and counting — so it is not eligible this week. It is first in the queue when it does.

That is the radar. Thin week, honestly reported, and two things to chase rather than three things to admire. Today's Portfolio Watch is in your feed alongside this one, and the lead there is AstraZeneca putting a published price on A-I target discovery.
