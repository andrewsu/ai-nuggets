## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Sourcing Radar for Sunday, September sixth. Three leads this week, and the first one is a Maryland cancer-target paper that happens to sit on top of an open question about a company we already own.

Start with the science. Achuth Padmanabhan's lab, in Biological Sciences at U-M-B-C with a joint appointment at the University of Maryland Greenebaum Comprehensive Cancer Center, posted a preprint on Monday on ovarian cancer.

The protein at the center is Z-N-F two seventeen, a zinc finger transcription factor. It has been a known oncogene since a Cancer Discovery paper in twenty-twelve established it as a prognostic marker in breast cancer, and this same lab published the definitive ovarian characterization in npj Precision Oncology last December, showing it drives proliferation, metastasis and chemoresistance. So the target is established. The problem is that it is a transcription factor, which is the canonical undruggable class — there is no pocket, and nobody has ever regulated its protein levels on purpose because nobody knew what controlled them.

This paper answers that. They identify a deubiquitinase called U-S-P fifteen as the enzyme that protects Z-N-F two seventeen from destruction. Knock down the deubiquitinase and the transcription factor disappears — and here is the detail that makes it real, the messenger RNA for the transcription factor actually goes up while the protein goes down, and a proteasome inhibitor rescues the protein completely. So this is degradation, not transcription. Then the loop closes the other way: overexpressing the transcription factor raises the deubiquitinase protein without touching its own messenger RNA. Two proteins holding each other up post-transcriptionally, a positive feedback that keeps the oncogene abundant.

The functional package is respectable for a preprint. Knocking down the deubiquitinase in transcription-factor-high ovarian cells suppressed proliferation, dropped the mesenchymal markers and the matrix-remodeling enzymes, and killed migration, invasion, matrix adhesion, anoikis resistance and spheroid formation. In vivo, intraperitoneal xenografts in nude mice, six animals a group — reduced tumor burden by imaging and significantly prolonged survival. And knockdown re-sensitized the cells to carboplatin, paclitaxel and doxorubicin.

The commercial thesis writes itself, and it is a good one. Instead of recruiting a ubiquitin ligase to destroy a protein, which is what the entire targeted-degradation field does, you remove the enzyme that has been protecting it. Same destination, opposite direction, and the target you reach is one nobody can drug directly. Deubiquitinase inhibition is a real modality with real chemical matter — there are clinical or near-clinical programs against U-S-P one, U-S-P seven and U-S-P thirty. There is no clinical inhibitor of U-S-P fifteen.

Now be candid about what is missing, because it is a lot. First, they never show the transcription factor is a direct substrate — no binding, no ubiquitin chain work. They say so themselves in the discussion. Without that, the deubiquitinase could be acting through any number of intermediates. Second, everything here is short hairpin knockdown. Deleting a deubiquitinase is not the same as inhibiting its catalytic site — plenty of these enzymes work as scaffolds, and until somebody runs a catalytically dead rescue or a tool inhibitor, "target this enzyme" is a hypothesis and not a result. Third, one cell background, engineered overexpression rather than an endogenously high line or a patient-derived model. Fourth, and this is the one a partner will raise: this enzyme is pleiotropic. It sits on transforming growth factor beta signaling, on innate immune signaling, and it has T-cell-intrinsic functions. A systemic inhibitor carries on-target immunological liabilities you would need to characterize very early.

So on intellectual property, be precise about what exists. The work is Department of Defense funded, which means Bayh-Dole and U-M-B-C ownership. What is licensable today is a biology position and a patient-selection hypothesis, not composition of matter. Which means the Blackbird instrument here is not a license — it is a grant that buys the three experiments that make it licensable: direct substrate demonstration, a catalytic-dead rescue, and one patient-derived model with endogenously high transcription factor.

And now the reason this is our paper rather than somebody's paper. In twenty-eighteen, in Nature Communications, this same investigator established that the very same deubiquitinase is the selective upstream regulator of the turnover of p fifty-three R one seventy-five H — and identified a small molecule that drives that mutant to degradation through it. Clasp Therapeutics, which Blackbird BioVentures is invested in, has as its lead asset a T-cell-receptor-mimic engager against the p fifty-three R one seventy-five H peptide displayed on H-L-A A star oh two oh one, with a monotherapy readout expected this year. An engager's potency scales with how many peptide-H-L-A copies sit on the tumor cell surface, and surface copy number is downstream of how the mutant protein is degraded and by which route. Padmanabhan's answer in that paper was a lysosome-mediated route — which is the route that does not feed class one presentation.

I want to be careful here: that is a question, not a finding. Nobody has measured whether modulating this enzyme changes R one seventy-five H peptide presentation. But it is exactly the question our own standing item on Clasp target density is asking, and there is an investigator four miles from the BioHub who has spent eight years on the degradation biology of that specific mutant protein. Avi and Jon should want the answer whichever way it comes out.

So the verdict, ranked honestly. The ovarian oncology thesis is early and needs those three experiments before it is a company. The Clasp-adjacent question justifies the meeting by itself. Take the meeting for the second reason and evaluate the first while you are in the room.

Second lead, out of Johns Hopkins. Denis Wirtz and Pei-Hsun Wu, in Chemical and Biomolecular Engineering with the NanoBioTechnology institute and joint Oncology and Pathology appointments, posted a preprint on Wednesday describing a ninety-six-well imaging assay that counts live and dead cells one at a time through the full thickness of a three-dimensional collagen gel. A two-times objective captures an entire well in one field, two dyes separate live from dead, a z-stack covers the gel, and the whole plate runs in under two hours at better than ninety-five percent counting accuracy.

The finding is the interesting part. Across five breast cancer lines and five approved drugs, the half-maximal effective concentration was essentially the same in two dimensions and in three — and it was measuring the wrong thing. At that concentration, one triple-negative line grew five-fold in flat culture and fell to twenty percent of its starting population in gel. Another line expanded nine-fold flat and sat exactly at growth arrest in gel. The concentration actually required to kill cells rather than slow them was five-fold higher in three dimensions for one line at five days, and across the whole panel the killing concentration showed essentially no correlation with the potency number. Potency tells you where growth inhibition starts. It does not tell you whether anything dies.

Then the validation a tools platform needs. In flat culture, tamoxifen did not distinguish the estrogen-receptor-positive line from the triple-negative ones at all. In gel, at five days, it did. Olaparib picked out the B-R-C-A-one mutant line in gel and not flat. The clinically correct answers only appeared in the physiologically relevant format.

Candid read: this is not a venture-scale therapeutics company and I would not pretend otherwise. It is a method and a metric on a standard plate with off-the-shelf dyes; the defensible position is the analysis software, and the exit is a tools or services acquisition. Five immortalized lines in collagen, no stroma, no immune compartment — the authors list that limitation themselves.

But do two things with it anyway. It is a diligence instrument for our own portfolio: every oncology package we read quotes a potency number from a monolayer, and this paper's central result is that the number is real and largely irrelevant, so ask each program explicitly for the cytostatic-versus-cytotoxic answer. And it is a BioHub shared-capability candidate on the EPOCH template — a bench that runs ninety-six three-dimensional conditions in two hours is a plausible shared instrument, which is a conversation for Yixuan and Esther and costs a conversation rather than a grant. Wirtz is Vice Provost for Research and a serial founder, so assume Hopkins Technology Ventures has already triaged this. Ask them what they concluded.

Third, and this one I am deliberately not calling a build. Mary Ann Jabra-Rizk's group at the U-M-B School of Medicine posted a preprint on Monday on Candida auris, the multidrug-resistant hospital fungus. Fifty-three isolates from thirty-eight patients at their own institution, whole-genome sequenced. Every single isolate resistant to fluconazole, fifteen percent resistant to the echinocandins, seven and a half percent resistant to all four antifungals tested. They find the expected resistance mutations plus one in a transcription factor gene never previously reported, and one substitution in the glucan synthase gene never associated with echinocandin resistance in this organism. In one resistant isolate the resistance allele was present at thirty-one percent frequency — below the calling threshold — which is a mixed population, a resistant subclone emerging under therapy in real time.

The half I care about is the phenotype. Isolates vary enormously in whether they clump and whether they build biofilm, and it is not clade — it varies within a single patient over time. The clumpers and strong biofilm formers overexpress two cell-wall adhesin genes, one by more than eight-fold and the other by more than two hundred and fifty-fold. And buried in the discussion, seven closely related isolates came from three transplant patients in the same surgical intensive care unit at the same time, all of whom died within days of each other — a transmission event visible only because somebody sequenced.

There is no molecule here and I would not build an anti-adhesin company against this organism; the addressable population is small and the commercial case is hard even with terrible mortality. What this is, is a strategic asset for our chronic-infections theme. It is a characterized, sequenced, phenotyped clinical strain bank at U-M-B with matched serial isolates from the same patient, in a lab that can read a phenotype the standard hospital susceptibility test cannot see. That is the identical shape as the Chatterjee tolerance lead we ran on August thirtieth — a phenotype the clinical assay reports as treatable when it is not. And a two-hundred-fifty-fold adhesin swing inside one patient's isolate series is a genotype-blind phenotype, which is exactly what a rapid phenotypic susceptibility platform like Astek's, four miles from us, exists to catch.

So Yixuan, when we next talk to Chatterjee, the ask is whether Jabra-Rizk's collection and assays are available to that program. The value is capability and cohort access, not a license.

That is the radar. One lead worth a meeting for a reason that has nothing to do with its own indication, one platform worth a conversation and not a grant, and one collection worth borrowing. Portfolio Watch follows this morning with the Lp-little-a result.
