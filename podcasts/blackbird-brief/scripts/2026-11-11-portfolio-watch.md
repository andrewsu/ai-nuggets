## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Wednesday, November eleventh. The most important development for the portfolio today is Freenome's first quarter as a public company, because it answers the question we have had open on DELFI since late September. Freenome has now shown the whole playbook for a blood-based screening business: take one test through F-D-A approval, collect a hundred-million-dollar partner milestone for it, and use that money to launch the next test as a lab-developed test while carrying a regulated version behind it. That next test points at the same patients as DELFI's FirstLook Lung. After that, two gout programs presented on Monday that land on opposite sides of the question we opened Sunday — whether the category is moving from suppressing inflammation to removing the substrate. Then the real terms of Third Rock's China deal, which we flagged yesterday as a headline only. And last, a room full of ear surgeons in Seoul stating the conditions under which they would adopt hearing gene therapy, which is a message to Aletira about who actually decides.

Start with Freenome.

On Tuesday Freenome reported third-quarter revenue of a hundred eighty-three and a half million dollars, against one million in the same quarter last year. Almost all of it — a hundred eighty million — is revenue recognized under its collaboration and license agreement after the F-D-A approved SimpleScreen C-R-C in July. That produced net income of sixty million dollars against a sixty-four and a half million dollar loss a year ago, and left four hundred thirty million in cash. Full-year guidance is a hundred ninety-two to a hundred ninety-four million, which tells you the quarter essentially was the year. Abbott, which inherited the agreement when it bought Exact Sciences, launched the approved colon test at the end of September.

Here is why this is a DELFI item. Since late September we have had three questions open with DELFI, and Eddie and Emily own them: is there a regulated approval path for FirstLook Lung, is there a timeline, and if the answer is to stay a lab-developed test, what happens in 2028 when the Medicare benefit category exists and DELFI is not inside it. Freenome has now published its own answers.

On the first question, the answer is to run both at once. SimpleScreen Lung, launched October twentieth, is a lab-developed test — not F-D-A reviewed, by the company's own statement — for adults fifty to eighty with at least twenty pack-years who are not currently being screened. In an independent cohort of a hundred seventy-two lung cancers and four hundred thirty-six controls it reported eighty-five percent sensitivity at fifty percent specificity, with a negative predictive value of ninety-nine point eight percent. And in August the F-D-A granted Breakthrough Device Designation to the multiomics version of that same test, still in development. So: sell today's version as a lab-developed test, carry the next version toward a regulated submission with a designation that speeds review, and do not choose.

On the second, the approval pays for the next test. A hundred eighty million dollars of partner money arrived because one test cleared the agency. DELFI has no equivalent milestone because it has no approved product, and that asymmetry belongs in front of the board.

The third answer is distribution, and that is the part I would not have predicted. Freenome sells the lung and colon tests in a single blood draw. The colon test is approved, sits in the American Cancer Society guideline, and meets Medicare coverage criteria. The lung test does neither. So the covered test is the entry point and the unreimbursed test rides along. Note the number they lead with: fewer than twenty percent of people eligible for lung screening are up to date with low-dose C-T. Both companies chase that same non-adherent population, and FirstLook Lung's own headline is a ninety-nine point eight percent negative predictive value — the identical figure Freenome just put on its lung test.

So, for Eddie: DELFI's advantage in lung was being first and being alone, and as of three weeks ago it is neither. Ask whether DELFI has or could get a Breakthrough Device Designation for a next-generation FirstLook — the cheapest piece of this template to copy, and the piece that answers 2028 without committing to a submission now. And the uncomfortable one: Freenome's economics came from a partner paying for an approval, while DELFI's channel partnerships are its weakest disclosed area. The board question is not whether the fragmentomics works — it plainly does — it is who writes DELFI a hundred-million-dollar check for clearing a regulatory bar, and what that partner needs to see first.

One candid note. This is milestone recognition, not product revenue; service revenue was nine hundred thousand dollars. Nobody has yet shown these tests sell at scale, and the up-versioned colon data in October took the stock down about eleven percent on what the test still misses. The template is real. The commercial proof is not in yet.

Second item, for the gout theme, and it is a pairing. On Sunday we opened the read that crystal arthropathy is moving from suppressing the response to the crystal toward removing the substrate that makes it. On Monday in Orlando both sides presented within two hours of each other.

On the substrate side, Protalix presented Phase One data on P-R-X one-fifteen, a PEGylated uricase — an enzyme that destroys urate in the blood. The new analysis covers the eighteen, twenty-four and thirty-six milligram cohorts, six participants each, a single infusion, followed eighty-five days, with drug still measurable in plasma at day eighty-five. In the original Phase One a single dose held urate below six milligrams per deciliter for up to twelve weeks. The company is explicit about why the program exists: existing uricase therapy carries infusion burden, immunogenicity with loss of efficacy, and safety concerns. A Phase Two in uncontrolled gout is enrolling, topline in the second half of 2027.

On the inflammation side, Monte Rosa presented preclinical data on M-R-T eighty-one-oh-two, an oral molecular glue degrader that destroys NEK7, a protein the NLRP3 inflammasome needs in order to assemble. Degrade it and the crystal cannot trigger interleukin-one-beta release. In a rabbit model of crystal arthritis, oral dosing cut joint swelling and improved ultrasound joint scores, comparing favorably with prednisolone, colchicine and an NLRP3 inhibitor, and it lowered C-reactive protein in joint fluid, which is the target-engagement evidence. A Phase Two testing prevention of flare recurrence starts this quarter.

The read for Jon and Hemaka is that these two are not competing, and that is the useful part. Protalix lowers the stock of urate and does nothing for a patient mid-flare. Monte Rosa blocks the flare and leaves every crystal where it is. Look at the clinical question each chose: durability of urate lowering in uncontrolled gout, versus prevention of the next flare. Two trials, two endpoints, two patients. So the sharper version of Sunday's read is this — decide which clinical question we are answering before choosing a mechanism, because the mechanism fixes the trial and the trial fixes the cost. One more thing worth a line in the theme file, Yixuan: Monte Rosa also showed activity against calcium pyrophosphate crystals, which is a bid for the same pseudogout indication as the Anaheim Bio program we covered Sunday, approached from the opposite direction.

Third item, yesterday's watch-item with actual terms. Third Rock, our co-founding partner on the Lieber company, has a deal with Funnel Biosciences worth up to two point one six billion dollars in total potential fees. Funnel is a Beijing company founded in February of this year by a former Merck cancer researcher and a computational biophysicist. Its platform is epitope-guided antibody design — generative models plus physics-based energy calculations, designing biologics from scratch rather than immunizing animals or screening phage libraries. Third Rock nominates the targets; Funnel runs discovery and takes an undisclosed platform access fee, with the rest in milestones. This is Third Rock's second deal of this kind; the first, with a Shanghai peptide company, reportedly produced a development candidate in under eighteen months.

For Matt and Eddie: the headline is almost entirely milestones against an undisclosed access fee, so the real commitment is modest. This is Third Rock buying discovery capacity, not making a two-billion-dollar bet, and it is now doing it for the second time. If the Lieber company ever needs an antibody, that route exists — ask them about it as a resource rather than reading it as bandwidth lost to psychiatry. For Avi and Biolinco, Funnel sells de-novo design and positions explicitly against immunization and phage display. That is the commoditizing-design wave behind our standing line, that value moves to the empirical throughput layer design models create demand for. A firm nominating targets and expecting candidates in eighteen months needs something to test those designs against.

Last item, for Aletira, and it concerns the people who decide whether a product gets used. At the Politzer Society meeting in Seoul last week — six hundred thirty-four ear specialists from forty-one countries — gene therapy dominated the program, and the clinicians were careful in a specific way. Professor Choi Byung-yoon of Seoul National University Bundang Hospital made three points. Otoferlin mutations account for only two or three of every hundred cases of congenital hearing loss. Treatment brings thresholds to around forty decibels on average, which he described as a level where patients hear well with hearing aids — not restored normal hearing. And the approval in hand is accelerated, with a little over two years of follow-up, in a field where vector-associated deaths have been reported. His conclusion: making this standard treatment in Korea would need at least ten years of safety data and standardized guidelines, because effective hearing aids and cochlear implants already exist.

The read for Geoff, Hugh and Avi is that the adoption bar is not the regulatory bar. The comparator is not placebo, it is a cochlear implant — reimbursed, trusted, decades of outcomes — so any narrative that does not say explicitly why a G-J-B-two patient should take a one-time injection instead of, or before, an implant is incomplete. Forty decibels is the number the field will anchor on for what gene therapy delivers, so if SELEXON's cell-type selectivity is meant to beat a gene-agnostic approach functionally, make that claim in those units. And these clinicians treat accelerated approval as provisional, which cuts both ways: the voucher path we have been studying gets a product to market fast, but that room in Seoul will keep calling it unproven until long-term data arrive. Plan for a slower adoption curve than the approval timeline suggests, and decide now what a ten-year safety commitment looks like.

That is the watch. Freenome's quarter is the full template for a screening business — approval, partner milestone, lab-developed launch, breakthrough designation for the regulated follow-on — now pointed at FirstLook Lung's patients with the same negative predictive value claim. Two gout programs that answer two different clinical questions, which is the choice to make before picking a mechanism. Third Rock's China deal is discovery capacity, mostly milestones, and the second of its kind. And the surgeons who decide whether hearing gene therapy gets used want ten years of safety data and a reason to prefer it to an implant. Have a good Wednesday.
