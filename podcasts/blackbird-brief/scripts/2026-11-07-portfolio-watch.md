## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Saturday, November seventh. The most important development for the portfolio is that Sarepta has given up on the controlled trial that was supposed to win back the expanded label for Elevidys, and has told investors it will go to the agency with single-arm safety data instead. That is a decision about what counts as confirmatory evidence in gene therapy, made by the company with more A-A-V regulatory experience than anyone, and it goes straight into the Aletira pre-I-N-D file. After that, the one remaining late-stage negative-symptom program in schizophrenia gave a timing update that sets a date on the calendar for the G-P-R fifty-two program. And we close with a small Bethesda company presenting at the cancer immunotherapy meeting today, for Eddie's first-read list.

Start with Sarepta. On Thursday's earnings call, the company said it will not resume enrollment in ENVISION. That is the Phase Three study in non-ambulatory patients and in older ambulatory patients aged eight through seventeen — the population whose access to Elevidys was stripped from the label last year after the safety events. Enrollment was paused over the summer with roughly half of the hundred and fifty patient target recruited, and it will now stay paused permanently. The half-enrolled study is not being completed.

What replaces it is the interesting part. The company says it will present the agency with twelve-week safety data in the first quarter of twenty twenty-seven from the non-ambulatory cohort of a different, ongoing study called ENDEAVOR. That cohort is not an efficacy comparison. It is testing whether giving sirolimus, an immunosuppressant, before or after the gene therapy reduces acute liver injury. Sarepta's chief scientist said the commitment to non-ambulatory patients remains and that this cohort is now central to it.

Three reads for Avi, Hugh and Anthony, and the first one is the structural one.

When a controlled trial in a gene therapy program is stopped for safety, the replacement is almost never another controlled trial. It is a single-arm safety cohort, and the conversation with the agency shifts from "does it work in this population" to "can it be given safely in this population." Those are different questions with different evidentiary bars, and the second one is the only one that remains askable once the randomized study is gone. For the Aletira pre-I-N-D, the practical consequence is to think now about which population expansion you would want later and to enroll it while the primary study is running, because the option to go back and generate controlled evidence in an adjacent group disappears the moment a safety signal lands. You cannot restart a study that half the field has already decided is unenrollable.

The second read is about the sirolimus cohort specifically, and it is the one I would put in front of the pre-I-N-D team this week. The most advanced systemically dosed A-A-V product on the market is now running a cohort whose purpose is to find the right prophylactic immunosuppression regimen. That is an admission that the immunosuppression plan is part of the product, not an ancillary detail in the protocol. We have been treating Aletira's steroid and immune-management plan as something to settle before first-in-human. The Sarepta data point says to go further: decide whether you are testing a regimen as a variable inside the first study, with prespecified liver and complement readouts, rather than fixing one regimen by convention and discovering later that it was the wrong one. The cochlear compartment and the dose are both far more favorable than Elevidys, and I am not suggesting we borrow their risk profile. I am suggesting we borrow their study design question.

The third read is commercial, and it is the context that explains the first two. Elevidys brought in eighty-three and a half million dollars in net revenue for the quarter. That is above one analyst estimate and below consensus, and it compares with a hundred and thirty-one and a half million in the same quarter last year and a hundred and eighty-one million the year before. About fifteen hundred patients have been treated since the twenty twenty-three approval. Analysts at William Blair read the quarter as stabilization, pointing to enrollment forms and leading indicators holding flat against the prior quarter and to physicians showing more willingness to treat. Meanwhile the competitive clock is running. Dyne has an exon-skipping drug with an agency decision date of January twenty-first, and REGENXBIO intends to file its own Duchenne gene therapy in the first quarter with a possible accelerated approval in the second half of next year.

New chief executive Michael Severino, ninety days into the job after senior roles at AbbVie and Amgen, was asked about the company's mixed record on clean trial wins. His answer is worth keeping: if you want a positive study, you need the most substantial effect size you can get. That is the correct lesson and it is also the expensive one, because the way you buy effect size is in patient selection and site quality, both of which cost enrollment time. It is the same trade we have been circling on NuGate's endpoint and on the Aletira functional measure. A company with a marketed product and eroding revenue is now saying out loud that it would rather have a smaller, cleaner study with a bigger separation than a larger one that reads ambiguously.

Second item, for Hemaka, Maisha and the Lieber program. Minerva Neurosciences reported its quarter on Friday, and the operational news is that the confirmatory Phase Three trial of roluperidone is enrolling, with topline efficacy data expected in the second half of twenty twenty-seven.

The reason this matters to us is that roluperidone is the only late-stage drug candidate aimed directly at the negative symptoms of schizophrenia, which is the differentiation claim the G-P-R fifty-two program rests on. Recall the history: Minerva filed on the strength of its earlier negative-symptom studies and received a complete response letter in early twenty twenty-four. The trial now running is a three hundred and eighty patient study that started in April, and its primary endpoint is change from baseline to week twelve in the Marder negative symptoms factor score — the same clinician-rated factor from the standard schizophrenia rating scale, on the same twelve-week timeline, as the study that preceded the rejection.

That tells you something useful and slightly reassuring. The agency's objection was not that the instrument was wrong. If it had been, the sponsor would be running a different endpoint now. The objection was about replication and about the quality of the evidence behind the effect. The clinician-rated factor score remains the regulatory currency for a negative-symptom claim. That is consistent with the cleared digital therapeutic we covered on October sixth, which was built on a different clinician-rated negative-symptom instrument, and it is inconsistent with any temptation to lead a G-P-R fifty-two package with a novel digital or self-reported measure. Carry the novel measure as a secondary. Put the conventional clinician-rated factor score in the primary slot.

Two practical notes. The second half of twenty twenty-seven is now a dated read-across event. If roluperidone confirms, the category gets a validated path, a named bar, and a far more interested set of acquirers for anything else aimed at negative symptoms — and if it does not, the field's view of whether negative symptoms are a registrable claim at all gets worse, which affects our development-candidate timing conversation more than it affects the biology. Either way it is a date to build around. And Minerva is hosting a key-opinion-leader event in New York on November twelfth focused on the negative-symptom opportunity. That is free market research on how the people who will one day be our investigators frame this population, and I would have Esther or Yixuan on the webcast with a notepad.

One candid note on the financials, because the headline is misleading. Minerva reported about a hundred million dollars of net income for the quarter. That is almost entirely a non-cash gain from revaluing warrant liabilities from last year's private placement. Research spending went from under a million dollars in the year-ago quarter to five point two million, which is the real signal: the confirmatory trial is actually consuming money now. Do not read the income line as strength.

Last item, brief, and it is a sourcing note rather than a portfolio read. Precision Biologics, a small private company in Bethesda, is presenting preclinical data today at the cancer immunotherapy meeting on an antibody-drug conjugate they call P-B two-twenty-three. The target is unusual: not a protein, but a truncated carbohydrate structure — a class of shortened sugar chains that tumors display on their surface and healthy tissue largely does not. They have conjugated a standard microtubule-poison payload to the antibody at a drug-to-antibody ratio of just under four, and in an ovarian cancer model grown directly from a patient's tumor they report complete and sustained regression at the higher of two doses, with no concerning blood or chemistry findings.

Two honest caveats before anyone gets excited. The treatment arms in that experiment have three animals each, and a patient-derived xenograft in a nude mouse says nothing about how a glycan target behaves in a human with an intact immune system and a normal gut. And the payload is the same microtubule class that is already widely used, which, by the payload-exhaustion logic we worked through on the Clasp file, means these patients may have seen it before.

What is interesting is the targeting axis. Our oncology-surface-targets theme has been looking almost entirely at protein antigens, where the specificity problem is that healthy tissue expresses some of the same protein at lower levels. A cancer-associated sugar structure is a different kind of claim — the specificity comes from aberrant processing rather than from expression level. Whether it holds up is an empirical question, but it is a question we have not been asking. The company is a forty-minute drive from the BioHub, it is small and private, and the cost of understanding its target and its I-P position is one conversation. That goes on Eddie's first-read list, not on anyone's diligence calendar.

That is the watch for today. Sarepta tells us what happens to a confirmatory trial after a safety event and what replaces it, Minerva puts a date in the second half of twenty twenty-seven on the negative-symptom question, and there is a glycan-targeting A-D-C company in Bethesda worth an hour. Have a good weekend.
