## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Saturday, November fourteenth. Today's most important development for the portfolio: Adventris is now on the record in Nature Reviews Drug Discovery, making the case for off-the-shelf cancer vaccines. In the same article, one of the most respected cancer immunologists in the field makes the case against them, and that is the argument a partner will bring to Adventris's next meeting. Behind that, a rare pediatric voucher sold for two hundred and five million dollars in the same week the F-D-A shelved its other voucher program. Kyverna has filed the first autoimmune CAR-T application and quietly dropped its cheaper next-generation process. And a COPD antibody company priced an IPO while the company scheduled behind it pulled its own.

Start with Adventris. Asher Mullard's news feature, published yesterday, asks what the field should build now that Merck and Moderna's personalized vaccine, intismeran, has the first Phase three win. We covered the trial itself on October twenty-third, including the early stretch where the curves don't separate, which we read as a manufacturing delay. What's new is who is speaking for which side.

Andrew Allen, Adventris's chairman, is the article's main voice for off-the-shelf. He makes two arguments: cost, and the ability to treat before surgery. A personalized vaccine takes six to eight weeks from blood draw to injection; an off-the-shelf product is ready on the day of diagnosis. He describes Adventris's candidate as thirty optimized versions of common K-RAS mutations, built to cover all the common class one and class two H-L-A types and designed to help more than ninety percent of pancreatic cancer patients, which is the setting for the first planned trial. He also says the neoantigen-selection algorithms have been commoditized, with everyone performing at about the same level.

The other side comes from Ira Mellman, president of research at the Parker Institute and formerly head of cancer immunology at Genentech. His view is that personalization is the better immunological choice, because tumors and immune systems vary too much for a fixed antigen set, and H-L-A variation in particular means some patients will never present a pre-selected peptide. That objection goes straight at Adventris's design, and Mellman's name carries weight with any pharma partner.

But read Mellman's next quote carefully, because it helps Adventris. He says that in cold, low-mutation tumors like pancreatic cancer, you can struggle to fill twenty or thirty slots even if you use every mutation, and he calls the preoccupation with algorithms misplaced. Put plainly, the strongest critic of off-the-shelf vaccines concedes that personalization runs short in exactly the indication Adventris chose. For Eddie and Virginia, that turns the pitch around. Adventris doesn't need to win the general off-the-shelf debate. It needs to show that pancreatic cancer is the tumor where a personalized vaccine has too little to work with and the shared driver mutation is the antigen. Everything then depends on the H-L-A coverage claim. Ninety percent coverage is a design statement. It is not yet a measured immune response, so it is the first thing to ask Adventris to back with data.

There are two more things in the piece. First, the National Cancer Institute and the Foundation for the N-I-H are about to launch a five-year, two-hundred-million-dollar Therapeutic Cancer Vaccines Initiative, a public-private partnership that will test several candidates in parallel. For a BioVentures position that needs clinical data without spending its own equity on it, that is a channel to ask about now, before the slots are allocated. Second, Endpoints reported yesterday that Moderna is ready to make thousands of doses commercially as it heads toward a filing, and the partners expect a possible approval in twenty twenty-seven. The bar Adventris will be measured against is about to become a commercial product, with a Phase three discontinuation rate of almost seventeen percent against nine and a half on pembrolizumab alone. An off-the-shelf product that is easier to tolerate and given earlier has room to compete on both counts.

Second item: vouchers. On Monday Scholar Rock agreed to sell its rare pediatric disease priority review voucher to BioNTech for two hundred and five million dollars in cash. The F-D-A awarded it in September with the approval of their spinal muscular atrophy drug. In October, Ultragenyx sold one for two hundred and ten million. Two sales in five weeks above two hundred million puts these vouchers well above the hundred-and-fifty-million level the trade press has used as a benchmark. Congress revived the program in February after it lapsed at the end of twenty twenty-four.

On Thursday, Reuters reported that the F-D-A has shelved the Commissioner's National Priority Voucher, the discretionary one- to two-month review that former Commissioner Makary created. No new vouchers have been awarded since he left in May. The agency says the pilot is ongoing for current holders, and RBC wrote that it isn't clear how holders will be affected. So the statutory voucher, written into law, is worth more than ever, and the discretionary one has effectively stopped.

That matters for Aletira. A congenital, childhood-onset hearing-loss gene therapy has exactly the profile the pediatric program was written for. A voucher on approval is non-dilutive money equal to a large Series B, and unlike the commissioner's voucher, it doesn't depend on who runs the agency. Two actions follow. First, have regulatory counsel confirm now whether a G-J-B two indication meets the rarity and seriousness tests for designation, because the designation has to be requested before the marketing application, not after. Second, put a voucher line into the Aletira financing model as a probability-weighted asset. If a partner values the company without it, they are leaving out two hundred million dollars.

Third: Kyverna has completed its biologics license application for miv-cel, an autologous CD-nineteen CAR-T, in stiff person syndrome, requesting priority review under its regenerative medicine designation. It is the first filing for a CAR-T in any autoimmune disease, with a possible launch in twenty twenty-seven. The detail that matters for Winnow is lower down in the release. Kyverna has deprioritized K-Y-V one-oh-two, the next-generation version with the same CAR but made without apheresis, with shorter culture and lower cost. Earlier this year the C-E-O called that process a real advance for patient access and the route into more common diseases. Now it has been shelved so the company can concentrate on the product it already has.

The read for Winnow: the leading autologous company has just conceded that manufacturing access is a problem it can't fund in parallel. That leaves an opening for any engager arguing on access and cost, and miv-cel's one-year profile, with most patients off chronic immunotherapy after a single dose and no high-grade cytokine release or neurotoxicity, is the efficacy and safety bar Winnow will be compared against. As we said on November ninth, the format alone no longer sets Winnow apart. Selectivity, stated in one sentence, still has to do that work.

Last, briefly: Windward Bio priced a one-hundred-seventy-five-million-dollar Nasdaq IPO on Thursday night, plus a fifty-million-dollar private placement from Sanofi, which makes about two hundred twenty-five million. Its lead is WIN three-seventy-eight, a long-acting anti-T-S-L-P antibody licensed from China. It's in a Phase two-three trial in asthma and a Phase two in COPD, aiming for dosing twice a year. That's the thirty-fifth biotech IPO of twenty twenty-six by BioSpace's count, against eight in all of twenty twenty-five. On the same day, Rezera postponed a planned two-hundred-million-dollar IPO shortly before its debut. For the COPD program theme, the read is that the late-stage COPD money is going to less frequent dosing of a validated mechanism, not to new biology. A Blackbird COPD program will need a reason why it isn't just a better-dosed T-S-L-P. For Emily and Matt, the window is open but selective. It is open to clinical assets with a pharma name on the cap table, and it can close a day before pricing.

One date for next week: the Senate committee vote on Heidi Overton as F-D-A commissioner is scheduled for Wednesday the eighteenth. If it fails, the commissioner's voucher question stays open into twenty twenty-seven.

That's the Portfolio Watch. Have a good weekend, everyone.
