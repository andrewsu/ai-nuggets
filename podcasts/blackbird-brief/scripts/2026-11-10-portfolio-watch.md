## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Portfolio Watch for Tuesday, November tenth. The most important development for the portfolio today is a financing rather than a readout: Johnson and Johnson's venture arm led a hundred-million-dollar round in Delix, a non-hallucinogenic neuroplastogen company, and the August term sheet behind it reportedly carried a right of first negotiation on both the drug and the company after Phase Two. That is a pharma buying an option on a psychiatric asset before the proof, the day after we argued buyers would wait for it, and it says something specific about who the G-P-R fifty-two company's Series A investors could be. After that, argenx pays five hundred and fifty million dollars in cash for a peptide discovery platform it had been partnering with for two years, the cleanest platform-exit template we have seen this quarter. Then an Australian ALS therapy moves into a randomized Phase Two, funded to that point by a patient charity, which is relevant to NuGate on both design and model. And a short note on where Third Rock is putting money this week.

Start with Delix, because it bears directly on how the Lieber company gets financed.

Delix Therapeutics, based outside Boston and spun out of UC Davis, closed an oversubscribed hundred-million-dollar Series C on Monday. It was led by Johnson and Johnson Innovation, the company's venture arm known as J-J-D-C, and co-led by the Dementia Discovery Fund at SV Health Investors, with RA Capital and Artis Ventures returning and Adage Capital among the new names. Both lead investors take board seats. The money takes the lead drug, zalsupindole, through a placebo-controlled Phase Two in recurrent major depression starting in the first half of next year; Bloomberg reports two hundred and forty patients dosed daily or twice weekly for a month, with data in the first half of 2028.

The drug is an oral compound inspired by the psychedelic five-methoxy-D-M-T, engineered to promote neuroplasticity without hallucinations, dissociation or sedation. The clinical support is thin and should be described that way: an eighteen-patient Phase One-b, with no placebo arm, in which depression scores fell about half after a week and stayed down through day thirty-six. The FDA has cleared a Phase Two in which patients take the drug at home. That is the commercial thesis in one line. Psychedelics and esketamine need a clinic, a monitoring period and a certified site. A take-home pill does not.

The structure is the part to study. In August, before the round closed, Johnson and Johnson signed a term sheet for eighty-five million dollars at a hundred-and-ninety-million-dollar pre-money valuation, and that term sheet reportedly gave it a right of first negotiation after the Phase Two data, covering both the asset and the company as a whole. The round then grew to a hundred million. One caution: Monday's release does not mention that clause, so we know what was in the term sheet, not what was in the final documents.

Three reads, for the Lieber company first.

The first is for Matt and Eddie. Yesterday's retrospective on the AbbVie and Bristol schizophrenia deals concluded that large buyers will now pay later, and pay for real-world tolerability. This is the other half of that behavior, and it is not a contradiction. Johnson and Johnson is not paying an acquisition price before proof. It is paying a venture price, under three hundred million dollars post-money if the August valuation held, for a seat, a board view and a first look at the moment the proof arrives. Neuropsychiatric pharma appetite has not vanished. It has moved into the venture arm and into option-shaped terms. For our Series A planning, a strategic venture investor with a negotiation right is a realistic lead investor, not a fallback.

The second is about who that investor is, and it is for Hemaka and Maisha. J-J-D-C co-founded Rapport Therapeutics with Third Rock on a Janssen licence. That is the region-selective psychiatry company we covered on October twenty-first, whose bipolar mania Phase Two bought it a Phase Three on adequate efficacy and clean tolerability. It now leads a round in a mechanism sold on tolerability and outpatient convenience. Johnson and Johnson's own commercial franchise is Spravato, which is exactly the in-clinic model Delix is built to make unnecessary. The pattern across both deals is selective mechanisms with a tolerability story, backed by a company that already sells into psychiatry. That describes the G-P-R fifty-two pitch, and J-J-D-C already sits next to Third Rock on one cap table.

The third read is the price of that structure, and the whole team should hear it. A right of first negotiation is cheaper than a right of first refusal for the company, because it only obliges Delix to talk first, not to accept a matching offer. It still has a cost. Every other buyer knows Johnson and Johnson goes first, and some will not spend diligence money on an asset they think is spoken for. If we take a strategic lead into the Lieber company, the clause to negotiate is the time window and the trigger. A short exclusive period tied to a defined data package costs much less than an open-ended first look.

A secondary line for the Parkinson's theme: the Dementia Discovery Fund co-led because zalsupindole increased synaptic density in preclinical models of Alzheimer's and Parkinson's disease. A neurodegeneration fund writing a depression check on a plasticity mechanism tells you how that capital pool is thinking about disease-modification bets. Note it for the Parkinson's diligence list.

Second item, and it is for Biolinco, Winnow, and anyone thinking about how a platform company exits. On Monday argenx agreed to acquire Unnatural Products, a Santa Cruz company that discovers oral macrocyclic peptides using parallel chemistry and machine learning, for five hundred and fifty million dollars in cash upfront and up to five hundred and ninety-five million more in development and regulatory milestones, about one point one five billion dollars in total. argenx already held a minority stake. The two companies have collaborated for more than two years under a deal struck in 2025 with a double-digit-million upfront and up to one and a half billion dollars in option and milestone payments. U-N-P keeps its team and its Santa Cruz site, which becomes argenx's home for peptide discovery, and keeps leading discovery within a framework argenx sets.

The read for Avi and Eddie is the sequence, not the number. A platform company with no disclosed clinical asset sold a research collaboration, took an equity investment from the same partner, ran two years of joint work in the open, and was then bought outright for more than half a billion dollars in cash. The buyer had diligenced the platform for two years before it paid. That is the lowest-risk acquisition a large company can make, and argenx's chief executive said almost exactly that, pointing to two years of working together. It is the path a Blackbird platform company should design for from the first partnership: a collaboration that includes an equity stake and lets the partner watch the platform deliver, so the acquisition is a formality by the time it comes.

There is a specific line for Winnow and the Intergalactic Center as well. argenx describes itself as hunting novel immunology wherever it emerges, co-creating with academic researchers through what it calls its Immunology Innovation Program. It now owns an oral-peptide engine to go with its antibody engineering. That makes it a natural counterparty for any autoimmune biology coming out of Hopkins that needs a modality partner. Virginia, it belongs on the Winnow partner map.

One candid note on value. Five hundred and fifty million upfront for a preclinical platform is a strong price, but the five hundred and ninety-five million in milestones is tied to development and regulatory events, so it will be realized slowly if at all. The founders' outcome depends mostly on the upfront, which is why the cash figure is the number to quote.

Third item, for NuGate. NuNerve, a Queensland company, announced a seventy-five-patient randomized Phase Two of NUN-004 in motor neurone disease, the name used in Australia for ALS. The drug is a long-acting protein that blocks a receptor called EphA4, whose excess signaling has been linked to motor neuron degeneration and to poor repair of nerve connections. The trial randomizes two to one against placebo for twenty-four weeks, followed by twenty-four weeks of open-label treatment, across six Australian sites. The primary measure is change on the standard ALS functional rating scale, with respiratory, neurophysiology and biomarker measures alongside. It follows a Phase One-b in twenty healthy volunteers and eight patients, where the company reports the disease stabilized during treatment.

Two reads. The first is for Anthony and Jon, and it is about design. Fifty treated patients against twenty-five on placebo, with a functional rating scale as the primary at twenty-four weeks, is a small trial for that instrument in a broad ALS population, where progression rates vary a lot between patients. The company's clinical lead frames the study as finding out whether the Phase One signal is real, which is the right framing. Read it as a signal-finding study, not a registrational one, and do not let eight open-label patients described as stabilizing set expectations. For NuGate the contrast matters. A target-defined antisense program can enrich for patients and anchor the readout on knockdown and neurofilament before it ever needs the rating scale to carry the argument. A broad-population repair mechanism like this one has only the rating scale.

The second read is about the model, for Matt and Esther. NuNerve was set up by a family trust and UniQuest, the University of Queensland's technology transfer company, around twenty-five years of one lab's work at the Queensland Brain Institute. The charity FightMND has funded it through three consecutive grants from early clinical work to Phase Two, and only now is the company saying it will seek international investment and partners for Phase Three. That is nondilutive money carrying an academic asset all the way to a randomized patient study before venture capital prices it, a much longer runway than ours. It is a useful reference point when we discuss how far Blackbird grant money should carry a program before the BioVentures check. It is also a likely in-licensing or partnering conversation in about a year if the Phase Two reads out cleanly, and the company has said publicly that it is looking.

Last item, and it is a watch-item, not an analysis. Endpoints reports that Third Rock Ventures, our co-founding partner on the Lieber company, has entered a partnership worth up to two point one six billion dollars with a little-known Chinese company called Funnel Biosciences, which describes itself as an AI-driven biologics company. The terms behind the paywall are not available to us this morning, and we found no company release, so treat the size as a headline. The reason to note it is that it is our partner's money and attention. A Third Rock commitment of that size to a Chinese AI-biologics platform adds to the October pattern of Western capital licensing out of China. Eddie, it is worth asking our Third Rock contacts whether this is a fund-level bet or a single new company, and whether it changes how much partner bandwidth sits on psychiatry.

That is the watch. Johnson and Johnson's venture arm reportedly bought a first-negotiation option on a psychiatric asset ahead of Phase Two, which tells us the strategic venture investor is a realistic lead for the G-P-R fifty-two Series A and that the window and trigger of any such clause are what to negotiate. argenx converted a two-year platform partnership into a five-hundred-and-fifty-million-dollar cash acquisition, the exit sequence our platform companies should plan for from their first deal. An Australian ALS repair therapy enters a small randomized Phase Two after a charity funded it the whole way there. And Third Rock is putting large money into a Chinese AI platform. Have a good Tuesday.
