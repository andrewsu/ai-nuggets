## Script

Good morning, Blackbird team. This is the Blackbird Brief Sourcing Radar for Sunday, November first. Three picks, all preprints, all read in full, and the second one is uncomfortable because it is the paper behind a Baltimore company we watched form without us.

The lead lands directly on Aletira's organ. It is a study of how the tip-link protein protocadherin fifteen gets put in the right place in a hair bundle and kept there, out of Xiaowei Lu's group at the University of Virginia with Ulrich Mueller at the Johns Hopkins School of Medicine as co-senior author. Mueller has defined most of what we know about hair-cell mechanotransduction, so this is a serious group on a problem the field has largely left alone.

The tip link is the molecular string that pulls open the channel turning sound into electricity. Protocadherin fifteen is half of that string, and of its isoforms the one called C-D-two is the one you cannot hear without. What nobody had was an account of how the cell holds it at the right level in the right sub-compartment for a lifetime. The answer here is that two scaffold proteins, N-HERF-one and N-HERF-two, bind a short docking motif on the C-D-two tail, and that binding is switched on and off by phosphorylation of the motif.

They test it two ways in mice: a knockout of N-HERF-one, and a knock-in where the four phosphorylatable residues of the docking motif are replaced with alanine, jamming the switch. Construction goes almost normally — the staircase forms, the bundle holds together, mechanotransduction turns on at the right time. The failure is later. Those adults have elevated hearing thresholds, significant at twenty-two and thirty-two kilohertz, at one month and still at two and a half and five. At postnatal day thirty there is no hair cell loss whatsoever. What has degenerated is the third row, the shortest row of stereocilia, whose height depends on ongoing mechanotransduction. So the interaction is dispensable for building a bundle and required for maintaining one. The authors draw the conclusion themselves: tip links are continuously repaired in adult hair cells, and if you could modulate the kinase controlling that switch, you might promote tip-link repair and prevent permanent hearing loss.

The commercial read, straight: there is nothing to license. The kinase is unidentified — they say so. No composition of matter, no declared patent, N-I-H funded, non-commercial licence. This is target identification, not an asset. I bring it anyway for two reasons. Strategically, everything we see in hearing is gene therapy for congenital deafness, a few thousand patients per gene. This sketches a different program — a small molecule acting on tip-link repair in acquired and age-related high-frequency loss, a population three orders of magnitude larger — and whoever finds that kinase owns the entry point. Hemaka, Avi: not a licensing meeting, a scientific one with Mueller.

For our own program, two things fall out. A lesion with normal development, no cell loss, and damage confined to the shortest row of stereocilia is visible as nothing except a threshold shift at the two highest frequencies tested. That is one more worked example that the per-ear machine-measured threshold is the sensitive instrument, and because it is mouse genetics it belongs in the preclinical narrative rather than the pre-I-N-D package. The harder one: if the tip-link machinery is under continuous turnover for the life of the animal, then a one-time cochlear gene therapy is supplying a protein the cell is continuously replacing. That is a durability-of-expression question stacked on the level-of-expression question SELEXON exists to solve, and I would rather we raise it than a partner.

Second pick. Denis Wirtz's lab at Hopkins posted a paper on engineering car-T cells to move faster. The field's solid-tumor work is almost all persistence, exhaustion and antigen specificity; almost nobody asks whether the cell can physically get through the collagen. They start from an observation — T cells at high density move faster, because they secrete more interleukin five, interleukin eight and T-N-F alpha and then sense their own output — and build what they call a synthetic velocity receptor: an antibody fragment against one of those cytokines fused to the signalling guts of the native receptors, co-expressed with a mesothelin-targeting car. The cell converts its own inflammatory output into a motility signal.

It works. Faster through collagen, better squeezing through confined channels, and in a triple-negative breast cancer mouse model the best construct cut lung metastatic volume and incidence more than fourfold against the same car without it, with smaller primary tumors. Though profiling shows the enhanced cells are also enriched for memory states, so some of the benefit may be differentiation rather than movement.

Here is the part that matters to us. The phrase velocity receptor is not new. The original preprint is from twenty twenty-three and its first author, Adrian Johnston, is a former member of this lab who turned his Hopkins thesis into a Baltimore company called DUA in twenty twenty-six. We logged DUA on September twelfth, not as a lead but as a pattern — a Hopkins thesis that became a Baltimore cell-therapy company without us. This is the data underneath it, and the lab kept going after the founder left.

Eddie, Esther, the question is not scientific, it is chain of title. The attractive thing is that the module is orthogonal to the antigen, so in principle it licenses alongside anybody's car rather than competing with one. That is the good kind of intellectual property. But I cannot tell from the paper what is already optioned to DUA and what this new in-vivo package leaves unencumbered, and that is one call to Johns Hopkins Technology Ventures. Make it this week, because the answer decides whether this is a lead or a courtesy note. Raise the limits on the same call: one tumor model, mouse only, and nobody here asks the safety question. They have built a receptor that makes a T cell respond to its own T-N-F alpha. Amplifying inflammatory signalling inside the cell type whose inflammatory signalling is the dose-limiting toxicity of the whole modality is not free, and somebody will ask.

Third pick is short, and it is not a company. It is an instrument for the diligence kit. A group led by Gen Shinozaki at U-C Irvine, with James Potash of Hopkins psychiatry among the authors, tested something the field has only assumed: does blood methylation actually reflect the brain? They assembled paired brain and peripheral samples from a hundred and forty-eight donors — living tissue from epilepsy resections, autopsy tissue, and pediatric samples — combined that with five published cohorts, and defined more than a hundred thousand sites per tissue where blood reliably predicts brain. Then they characterised those sites, which is the point. They sit at promoters active in both the brain and the periphery, in housekeeping genes — D-N-A repair, nucleic acid metabolism, the cell cycle. They are depleted at enhancers and in developmental and synaptic genes. Variants near them contribute nothing to the heritability of any of seven disorders. And sites reported by published disease studies are not enriched among them at all; blood-derived disease hits are consistently depleted. Plainly: blood reflects the brain exactly where the brain is least brain-like, and the disease signal mostly sits outside. One extra finding worth keeping — buccal swabs are not better than blood despite the shared embryonic origin, and blood was the more reproducible.

Virginia, Yixuan, this gives us a free published test for a pitch we see regularly. When somebody brings a peripheral epigenetic biomarker for a brain disease, ask whether their sites are inside the shared set or outside. Inside means you may claim it reflects the brain, but it is probably housekeeping biology with no disease specificity. Outside means it may be real disease signal, but you cannot claim it is telling you anything about the brain. Both answers are useful and neither is the one the pitch assumes. That matters for Artemyx on the methylation side and for anyone selling the Lieber programs a blood biomarker.

That is the radar. One target-identification idea worth a scientific conversation rather than a licensing one, one asset whose value turns entirely on a chain-of-title answer we can get in a day, and one paper that should change how we read a category of pitch. Today's Portfolio Watch is in your feed alongside this, and the lead there resolves Friday's open question on Gilead's clinical hold.

**PICK 3 — not a build; a diligence instrument. RECOMMENDATION: add the catalog to the diligence kit.**
"A genome-wide catalog of sites where peripheral DNA methylation reflects the brain" — bioRxiv **10.64898/2026.10.23.761276** v1, posted 2026-10-24. Full text read (125 KB).
- **Groups:** Gen Shinozaki (UC Irvine / Stanford, corresponding), with **James B. Potash (JHU Psychiatry)** and Jody E. Hooper among the authors; multi-site US + Japan.
- **Resources:** **IMAGE-CpG2** — paired brain + peripheral DNAm from **148 donors / 3 cohorts** (adult surgical biopsy, adult autopsy, pediatric), 4 brain regions, 3 peripheral tissues (blood, buccal, saliva); ~7× the original IMAGE-CpG. **COMPASS** — meta-analysis with 5 published cohorts. **TRACE-CpG** — defines **BrESS (brain epigenome shared sites)**, **>100,000 CpGs per peripheral tissue** by cross-cohort out-of-sample prediction.
- **THE LOAD-BEARING RESULT:** BrESS sit at **CpG-island promoters active in BOTH brain and periphery, in housekeeping genes** (DNA repair, nucleic-acid metabolism, mitotic cell cycle); **depleted at enhancers, bivalent/Polycomb regions, and at developmental/synaptic/growth-factor genes**. SNPs near BrESS contribute **no heritability** for any of **seven disorders**. EWAS-reported CpGs are **neither enriched nor depleted** overall, and **blood-derived disease hits are consistently DEPLETED**. **Most BrESS are not explained by mQTLs**, and they are not enriched for blood eQTM sites.
- **⇒ PLAIN VERSION: peripheral DNAm reflects the brain exactly where the brain is least brain-specific, and the disease signal mostly lies outside that compartment.**
- **⇒ SECONDARY:** **buccal is NOT superior to blood** despite shared ectodermal origin; blood was **more reproducible** across cohorts. Retires a common pitch talking point.
- **⇒ READ (Virginia Burger + Yixuan Qiu) — THE DILIGENCE TEST.** For any peripheral-epigenetic biomarker pitch: **are the sites inside BrESS or outside?** Inside ⇒ you may claim it reflects the brain, but it is probably cross-tissue housekeeping biology with no disease specificity. Outside ⇒ it may be real disease signal, but **it cannot be attributed to the brain**. Correlation-with-brain and association-with-disease are **distinct properties of a CpG** — the pitch almost always conflates them. Relevant to **Artemyx** (methylation side) and to anyone selling the **Lieber** programs a blood biomarker.
- **⇒ AUTHORS' STATED LIMITS:** array covers a small, promoter-biased fraction of CpGs; **bulk tissue, no cell-type-composition adjustment**; brain from epilepsy resections + autopsy, neither a neutral window on the healthy living brain.
