Yesterday the F-D-A's Molecular and Clinical Genetics Panel of the Medical Devices Advisory Committee spent the day on Grail's Galleri test and then voted three times. Unanimously in favor of safety. Seven to two, with one abstention, that the benefits outweigh the risks. And six to four — the narrowest of the three — in support of effectiveness. Four of ten panelists voted no on whether the thing works.

Galleri is a prescription-only, next-generation-sequencing test that reads cancer-specific methylation patterns in cell-free D-N-A from a routine blood draw, intended for adults fifty and over, and positioned explicitly as an addition to guideline screening, not a replacement. Grail filed its premarket approval application on January twenty-ninth. The test is already on the market as a lab-developed test at nine hundred forty-nine dollars out of pocket. No multi-cancer test has ever been F-D-A approved.

The split vote is in the numbers. In PATHFINDER 2, thirty-five thousand eight hundred seventy-eight participants aged fifty and over with no clinical suspicion of cancer. Specificity ninety-nine point six percent, so a false-positive rate under four-tenths of one percent. Positive predictive value sixty point three percent. Cancer-signal-origin accuracy ninety-one point three percent. Median time to diagnostic resolution forty-eight days. Of the cancers it found, fifty-three percent were stage one or stage two, and seventy-one percent had no recommended screening test available at all. Those are genuinely good numbers.

And then the sensitivity. Thirty-nine point three percent across all cancers. Sixty-nine point eight percent for the twelve deadliest ones. A negative result is not an all-clear, and the panel said so out loud.

The harder fact is the other trial. NHS-Galleri randomized roughly a hundred forty thousand people in England, and its primary endpoint was a reduction in the combined number of stage three and stage four cancers. It did not meet it. There was no reduction in overall mortality. Grail's later update reported that stage four diagnoses alone fell across the three-year period, which is a stage-shift signal rather than a met endpoint. Phil Castle, who directs the National Cancer Institute's Division of Cancer Prevention, told the panel the data were too premature to prove clinical benefit, and that routine approval and broad population rollout would risk harm and waste resources. Michelle Tarver, who runs the F-D-A's device center, called the concept genuinely promising and then said that technologies with this kind of reach and novelty demand careful, rigorous evaluation. The panel's own recommendation was that the marketing materials tell people to keep getting their colonoscopies and their mammograms — and stop calling this early detection.
