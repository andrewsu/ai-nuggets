# DeepQuest — an Alzheimer's AI scientist that audits its own novelty, and a novelty verdict that depends on how much literature the auditor reads

Paper link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26343

## Script

Most AI-scientist papers measure whether the agent finished the job. Did it form a hypothesis, write the code, run the analysis, produce a report. DeepQuest, an arXiv preprint posted yesterday from Hangzhou Dianzi University and Tsinghua, starts from a better complaint. Automating experiments, the authors say in their first sentence, does not necessarily advance scientific understanding. An agent can run flawless statistics on a question nobody needed answered, or on one the field answered five years ago. So DeepQuest tries to score knowledge gained, not tasks completed. I think that is the right target. But the paper also ends up showing, without quite saying so, how fragile "novel" is when an agent is the one judging it.

The setting is Alzheimer's disease, with the OASIS-3 cohort as the working dataset: clinical assessments, cognitive scores, and amyloid and tau PET over years of follow-up. The pipeline has four stages. First it reads ten Alzheimer's review articles and pulls out their stated unknowns, the "it remains unclear whether" sentences, and turns each into a set of testable hypotheses linked back to the review that raised it. Second, a data agent checks every hypothesis against what OASIS-3 actually contains, meaning the variables, the visit pairings and the follow-up windows. Anything the data can't answer gets dropped before it is ranked. The survivors are ranked in tournaments of five with an Elo score, borrowed from Google's co-scientist. Third, a coding agent runs up to three rounds of analysis per hypothesis, and an audit agent checks the code and whether the conclusion follows from the output. Fourth, and this is the new part, a separate audit compares each final claim against a frozen bank of published clinical findings. It labels the claim a rediscovery, an extension, or a candidate new discovery.

The cleanest result is the one that's easiest to overlook. The single most important component is the boring one, the data-feasibility gate. Remove it and nineteen of the sixty tasks never finish, because the agent commits to questions the cohort cannot answer. That matches what anyone who has handed a coding agent a real clinical dataset has seen. The expensive failure is choosing a question your data can't settle, not writing bad code. Separating "was the experiment done correctly" from "does the result add anything" is also the right decomposition, and few agent papers make it.

Now the headline. DeepQuest reports a scientific finding rate of ninety-five percent: forty of forty-two hypotheses produced an extension or a candidate new discovery. Claude Code and Codex, running the same hypotheses, land around seventy-six to seventy-nine percent, and Stanford's Biomni lands at eighty-six. But look at how that rate is built. It counts rediscoveries, extensions and new discoveries alike. In practice it measures whether the run produced a stable, evidence-backed claim at all. That's a completion metric, and the paper's own framing says completion is not the point.

The novelty labels themselves move with the reference corpus. The main comparison uses a frozen bank of two hundred clinical papers, all published after February 2026 so the language models couldn't have seen them in training. Against those two hundred papers, twenty of DeepQuest's forty-two claims are candidate new discoveries and zero are rediscoveries. Expand the bank to about forty-six hundred papers from 2024 to 2026 and new discoveries drop from twenty to thirteen, while rediscoveries appear. The finding rate doesn't change at all, because the metric can't tell those categories apart. Swap the judge from a GPT model to Claude and DeepQuest's new discoveries jump from twenty to thirty-three. So "new" here means new relative to whichever papers were retrieved, as read by whichever model did the reading. The authors do say this, carefully, in the methods. The abstract doesn't. And nobody checked the older literature, which is where Alzheimer's knowledge mostly lives.

The three showcase findings make the point concretely, and the authors deserve credit for replicating each one in an independent cohort, ADNI or AIBL. One: newly appearing neuropsychiatric symptoms predict dementia two years later, a hazard ratio around two and a half in OASIS-3 and three in ADNI. Two: higher neocortical tau means faster decline on the Clinical Dementia Rating and earlier first impairment. Three: each person's regional pattern of amyloid stays relatively stable over time, once you adjust for total amyloid load. All three hold up. But the paper's own appendix concedes that the first two refine relationships Zahinoor Ismail's mild behavioral impairment work and Rik Ossenkoppele's tau work had already established. The refinements are a time-lag window and a continuous tau measure instead of a positive-or-negative cutoff. Those are good analyses and real extensions. They aren't what most listeners hear when a system reports a ninety-five percent discovery rate. The MMSE slope result for tau also failed to replicate in ADNI, which the paper reports honestly.

One more thing, for those of us who build knowledge resources. DeepQuest's novelty audit works on a repository where each published finding is broken into a claim, the scope it holds within, and a supporting excerpt. That's the same unit SPIDER argued for yesterday: a finding with its conditions, not a bare triple. Once again the quality of the verdict is capped by the coverage and structure of that evidence store, not by the agent's reasoning. A novelty auditor over two hundred papers is a retrieval system with a small index.

So here's my take. DeepQuest asks the right question, whether an agent's output adds to what we know, and its data-feasibility gate and its split between execution and contribution audits are worth copying. But a novelty verdict that falls by a third when you index more literature, and rises by two thirds when you change the judge, is a property of the audit setup, not the discovery. If agent-scientist papers are going to report discovery rates, the reference corpus and the judge need to be fixed in advance and reported next to the number. And at least a sample of the "new" calls should go to a human who has read the field.
