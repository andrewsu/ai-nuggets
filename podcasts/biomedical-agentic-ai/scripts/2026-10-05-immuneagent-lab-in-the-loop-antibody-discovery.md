# ImmuneAgent — lab-in-the-loop antibody discovery, and the case where the model was dead weight

Paper link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03160

## Script

Today's pick is a system called ImmuneAgent, out of Nankai University and the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences, and it's the rare agentic biology paper where the agent produced molecules that worked in an animal.

The problem it attacks is a data-to-therapy gap. We have billions of sequenced B cell receptors sitting in public single-cell datasets, and fewer than one in ten thousand has an experimentally validated antigen. The obvious move — train a predictor to score receptor-antigen binding — runs straight into what the field now calls the memorization trap. Across a benchmark of fifty recent models, accuracy on receptor-antigen pairs seen during training looks good, and accuracy on genuinely novel epitopes collapses to roughly coin-flipping. That isn't a tuning problem. It's what happens when you have almost no hard negatives: sequences that look exactly like binders and aren't.

ImmuneAgent's answer is to stop treating this as a single prediction task. An orchestration agent keeps a to-do list and a knowledge graph, routes context to sub-domain experts spanning repertoire analysis, structural biology and molecular dynamics, and those experts run a dual loop — reason-and-act for strategy, code-as-action for sandboxed execution over roughly two hundred tools. That architecture is familiar by now. What isn't familiar is the piece they call lab-in-the-loop: cloned antibodies that failed to neutralize get labeled and fed back as negatives through a low-rank fine-tune of the predictor. Every cloning round becomes training data, and the failures — the expensive, usually discarded half of the experiment — are the whole point.

Does it work? On two fully held-out datasets the predictor started at an F-one of point eight one in validation and fell below point six on unseen data, the memorization trap exactly. After the experimental-feedback fine-tune it came back to point eight five on an influenza cohort and point seven one on a SARS-CoV-2 one. Notice that second number: still below its own validation score. The loop recovers some generalization. It does not abolish the gap.

Then the wet lab. From unsorted single-cell receptor data — no antigen sorting, no sorting panels, just sequencing that already existed — the system nominated candidates and the team cloned a hundred and ten. Sixty neutralized something. Twelve were broadly neutralizing. Five went into mice against a lethal influenza challenge, and all five gave complete protection, with lung viral titers statistically indistinguishable from a clinical-stage benchmark antibody; two of them beat that benchmark against an H5N1 strain.

Credit where it's due on the statistics. The authors explicitly decline to compare their eleven percent broad-neutralizer yield against the published rates from antigen-sorted screens, on the grounds that the denominators aren't matched. They also say outright that the mouse experiment wasn't powered to establish equivalence with the comparator. In a literature where agentic systems get benchmarked against whatever makes them look best, that restraint is worth noticing.

But here's the result I'd actually put on the slide. They pushed the system onto human papillomavirus — a target with no HPV antibodies and no HPV antigens anywhere in the predictor's training data. The deep-research stage read the primary literature, worked out that the L1 capsid surface loops are the neutralization target, assembled a small antigen and epitope database from public sources, and then reported honestly that there wasn't enough there to fine-tune on. Fifty candidates were cloned off a combined ranking. Nine neutralized at least one HPV type, two of them cross-neutralizing a pair of high-risk types.

And when they asked which signal in that ranking had done the work, the learned predictor had no relationship to neutralization at all. Neither did somatic hypermutation. What predicted it was the cell-of-origin marker, clonal expansion, and the structural co-folding score — three pieces of immunological prior knowledge the agent had assembled by reading papers, not by learning from data.

That's the claim buried in this paper, and I don't think the authors quite foreground it. When you step fully outside the training distribution, the machine-learning component of your agentic system contributes nothing, and what carries you is the agent's ability to retrieve domain knowledge and combine orthogonal weak signals. Which reframes what the agentic part is for. It isn't orchestration overhead wrapped around a model. On the hardest case it is the entire source of signal, and the model is dead weight.

The biology holds up too. F-C-R-L-five positive, C-D-twenty-seven positive atypical memory B cells show up as a reservoir of broad neutralizers across four independent pathogen settings, and hydrophobic contacts at the antibody-antigen interface track breadth. Those are portable, testable hypotheses, and they came out of an evidence chain you can read rather than a score you have to trust.

The honest limits: every validation here is in vitro neutralization plus prophylactic mouse protection, which is a long way from a therapeutic, and the loop was closed by a large human team doing the cloning, not by a robot. But as a demonstration that feeding experimental failure back into an agent is the thing actually worth engineering, this is the strongest version I've seen.
