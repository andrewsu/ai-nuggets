# PolyGraph — receipts for an agent-built knowledge graph, and an audit of who wrote them

Paper link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17719

## Script

Today's pick is a preprint out of the Technical University of Munich, headed for a NeurIPS workshop on verifying agents. It's called PolyGraph, and it's about the part of agentic knowledge graphs that nobody designs for: deletion.

The setup is this. Agents increasingly build knowledge graphs as memory, and building them has gotten easy — point a model at a corpus and out come extracted claims, entity merges, schema-entailed edges, community summaries. Then a source gets retracted. Now everything downstream has to be re-examined: the merge that used it, the edges entailed from that merge, the summary written over that community. What deployed pipelines actually do is delete the directly extracted facts and stop there. On this paper's corpus — three hundred PubMed oncology abstracts, sixty-one of them genuinely retracted — that takes the fraction of searches returning invalidated content from thirty-two percent down to twenty-eight. About an eighth of the problem. And when the deletion cuts along the schema instead of along documents, revoking every assertion that uses a predicate you've judged unsound, deleting the extracted facts changes nothing whatsoever: thirty-seven percent before, thirty-seven percent after. All of the damage is living in the derived edges and the summaries, not in the facts.

So rebuild the graph without the retracted source and diff it against the old one. That's the obvious move, and the measurement that kills it is the number I'd want every builder of agentic memory to carry around. Build the same unchanged corpus twice, with no deletion at all, and the two graphs differ in twenty-nine thousand artifacts. Now remove the sixty-one retracted documents, and the graph changes by six thousand. The resampling noise is five times the size of the signal you are trying to read. At the summary layer it's not even five to one — two runs over identical input share not a single summary in common. A constructor that samples cannot be verified by replay, and no later process can recover what a particular draw of the model was leaning on.

Which forces the actual design. The constructor has to emit its justification in the same call that creates the artifact — not afterward, not on request. PolyGraph calls that a receipt: a set of alternative evidence bundles, each claimed to be sufficient on its own. Then cleanup is pure set arithmetic. An artifact survives a deletion exactly when one of its bundles is untouched by it. Milliseconds over a twenty-four-thousand-artifact graph, zero model calls.

But a receipt is the model's own self-report, so the second half is an audit that grades it. Delete the cited evidence, hand the constructor only what's left, and ask five times whether it still arrives at the same artifact. The paper's own framing is a student who writes that wolves live in Duisburg and cites book A or book B, either one sufficient. Book A gets pulled. You don't argue with the citation — you take away book A and have the passage rewritten five times from book B. Whether the wolves come back is the verdict.

And the verdict splits hard by layer. Merge receipts — each one backing a single identity claim, that these two spellings are the same entity — agree with re-derivation sixty-eight and eighty-five percent of the time. Summary receipts agree a third of the time. Same agent, same pipeline, same corpus. Worse, the errors run in one direction almost exclusively: leakage. Over-deletion is at or near zero in every cell. A wrong receipt nearly always errs toward keeping the artifact, which is the silent failure — the graph goes on serving content it can no longer justify, and nothing in the system raises its hand.

The diagnosis is the part worth carrying away, because it isn't about summaries. A merge is one narrow claim and its receipt has the same shape. A summary is several claims — one per sentence — and its receipt was a flat list over the whole text, so a bundle that genuinely supports one surviving sentence kept the entire summary alive after every other sentence had lost its evidence. Re-elicit with one receipt per sentence and the same constructor writes receipts that are a hundred percent and eighty-eight percent faithful — the merge layer's range — and cleanup can drop the dead sentences and keep thirty-three of the thirty-five touched summaries instead of deleting all of them. A receipt is trustworthy when it's shaped like the claim it backs. The author is honest about the catch, too: asked for sentences with receipts, the model wrote eight hundred and fifty-seven seven-word sentences where it had written a hundred and eighty-one sixteen-word ones, more than half of them resting on a single assertion. The receipts got honest partly because the claims got narrow enough to be trivially checkable, and whether that's still a summary a human wants to read is a separate question.

Two limits I'd keep in front of this. Re-derivation measures whether the constructor would still produce the artifact — not whether the surviving sources actually support it. If the model can supply the missing evidence from parametric memory, the artifact grades alive while being completely ungrounded, which is exactly the failure a right-to-erasure obligation cares about, and this audit cannot see it. And the one incident in the paper that no audit of reasoning would have caught: the constructor's command-line tool printed a provider session-limit banner to standard output, the cache stored it as a valid model answer, and it was served for eleven hundred prompts. Two builds silently extracted nothing from a non-empty corpus while reporting well-formed statistics, and one of them was about to become the independent-rerun arm of the replay experiment, where an empty graph reads as total divergence. A structural guard caught it; thirteen content-filter refusals cached the same way slipped past that guard. Whoever verifies the agents has to verify the plumbing too.
