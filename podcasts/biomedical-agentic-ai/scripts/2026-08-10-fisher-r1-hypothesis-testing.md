# Fisher-R1 and P-Bench — LLM agents run the statistics correctly and still reach the wrong conclusion

Paper link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.07437

## Script

Here's the uncomfortable premise. We keep saying the hard part of automating science is the doing — writing the code, running the pipeline, wrangling the data. A new paper out of James Zou's group at Stanford, with biostatisticians at Wisconsin, argues the hard part is somewhere else entirely: the reasoning about what the numbers actually mean. And it shows that today's best agents fail at that in a way that never surfaces as an error message.

The setup is the everyday scientific workflow. You have a question and a dataset, you pick a statistical test, you compute a p-value, you draw a conclusion. Coding agents can now run that whole loop end to end — inspect the data, write the analysis, report the result. The claim here is that they execute the code correctly and still reach the wrong conclusion, because they choose the wrong test, or ignore the assumptions the test depends on.

Their motivating example is one every genomicist will recognize. The task: is tumor mutation burden associated with tumor purity? The dataset has a couple of high-leverage outliers. Run an ordinary linear regression and you get a highly significant association — driven entirely by those outliers. Run a rank-based test instead and it vanishes. And here's the sharp part: a frontier agent, GPT-5.4, actually notices the outliers in its own reasoning, flags them out loud, and then goes ahead and fits the linear model anyway. A false discovery, produced by a system that saw the warning sign and ignored it. That's not a bug you catch by checking whether the code ran.

To measure this at scale they built P-Bench — four hundred twenty-five open-ended hypothesis-testing tasks on real scientific data, spanning economics, biology, and medicine. What makes it trustworthy is how the answer keys were made. They didn't transcribe conclusions out of paper abstracts. They took published claims, re-ran the canonical reference analysis on a clean machine, logged the execution, and read the p-value and the decision straight off that run — then had domain experts audit every one. So each task is reproducible and traceable to an actual executed analysis. That reproducibility-first construction is worth noting in its own right; it's the open-science version of building a benchmark.

The headline numbers are sobering. Split the benchmark into an easy half and a hard half. On the hard half, the best model in the whole study lands at thirty-three percent. GPT-5.4 gets about thirty. DeepSeek-V4-Pro, around twenty-six. These are nowhere near solved — the frontier is getting two out of three hard hypothesis tests wrong. And the failures cluster exactly where a trained statistician earns their keep: outliers, unequal variances, clustered observations. Textbook assumption violations, the kind that hand you a confident, precise, wrong answer.

So what's the fix they propose? Fisher-R1, an open-weight agent — and I'll flag the open-weight part, because for a finding this cautionary it matters that the tool is inspectable, not a closed API behind a paywall. The training idea is clever. You can't get a verified reward signal from real published data at scale — you'd need trustworthy ground truth for thousands of analyses. So instead they generate synthetic tasks where the data-generating process is code they wrote themselves. The simulation runs the correct statistical method on its own simulated data, and the resulting p-value and decision become the answer key — automatically, and verifiably correct. They vary each task along six axes: the method, the domain scenario, the sample size, the effect size, the way the prompt is phrased, and a random seed. Then warm-start with supervised fine-tuning on expert trajectories, and finish with reinforcement learning against that verified reward.

One detail in the reward is worth lingering on. They don't score the raw distance between p-values. They map each p-value onto a z-score scale first, because raw p-values are crushed together near zero — the gap between p equals point-one and p equals ten-to-the-minus-ten is enormous in evidence but almost invisible on the raw scale. Scoring in z-space puts the reward where the information actually lives. It's a small choice, but it tells you they thought carefully about what "close to right" even means for a p-value.

Does it work? On the hard tasks, the fine-tuned fourteen-billion-parameter model beats its own untrained backbone by a wide margin and edges past both GPT-5.4 and DeepSeek — a twenty-one percent relative improvement over DeepSeek on average, up to twenty-six percent on the hardest cases. So training on a verified statistical reward genuinely buys reliability.

But I'd hold two things in tension here. The improvement is real, and the recipe is elegant. And the absolute ceiling is still thirty-three percent on the hard tasks. This isn't a solved problem; it's a well-measured one. For anyone building agents that touch real analyses — and that's most of us now — the takeaway is blunt: a green checkmark on "the code ran" tells you almost nothing about whether the conclusion is sound. Statistical reasoning is a separate capability, it's largely missing today, and it has to be trained and tested on its own terms. P-Bench is a good place to start doing exactly that.
