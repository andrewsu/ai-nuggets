# Uncertainty as a flag — a clinical LLM agent acts on a model's doubt only when it's pre-thresholded into a yes/no, not raw scores

Paper link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.20582

## Script

Today's paper is small, tidy, and carries a lesson that's much bigger than its clinical setting. It's really about how you wire a machine-learning model's uncertainty into a language-model agent, and it lands on something counterintuitive.

Start with the setup. A German radiology and digital-health group trained a fairly ordinary multi-task classifier to read chest radiographs — eight thoracic findings, about a hundred and forty thousand training images. Nothing exotic there. What they added is Monte Carlo dropout: you leave dropout switched on at inference time and run the same image through the network many times, and the spread of those predictions gives you an epistemic uncertainty — a measure of how unsure the model itself is, as opposed to just how confident its single point prediction happens to sound.

And that uncertainty is genuinely informative. When they ask whether the model can catch its own mistakes, ranking predictions by the confidence of the point output alone already gives a decent error detector; adding the Monte Carlo uncertainty makes it meaningfully better. The improvement is real but modest — the error-detection score goes from 0.74 to 0.77. If the paper stopped there, it would be one more "uncertainty helps" result, and there are a lot of those.

The interesting part is what they do next, and it's the part worth your attention if you build agentic systems. They set up a controlled experiment where a language-model agent — GPT-5.1 — plays a clinical-decision-support role. For each case it gets a realistic clinician request note and the model's read, and for every finding it has to make a call: commit — accept the model's answer and fold it into management — or escalate, meaning order a specific confirmatory step, a radiologist second read, a point-of-care ultrasound, a repeat scan. A confident misdiagnosis is when the agent commits to a call that turns out to be wrong.

Now the clever bit. It's a two-by-two design. One axis is what information the agent gets: the prediction alone, or the prediction plus the uncertainty. The other axis is how that information is represented: as raw numbers — the actual uncertainty values, with some calibration context — or as a single pre-computed binary flag that just says elevated risk or normal. The four prompts are byte-for-byte identical except for that one data block. So the experiment isolates exactly one variable: not whether the agent has the uncertainty, but how it's handed over.

And here's the result. When the uncertainty was handed to the agent as raw numbers, the agent basically couldn't use it. Its error-catching sensitivity sat at 0.63 — no better than making optimal use of the plain prediction, and well short of what the uncertainty signal actually makes possible. The information was right there in the prompt, and the agent left it on the table. But when they handed over the exact same information pre-digested into a binary flag — a little logistic-regression detector, fit on the validation set, decides the cutoff and just tells the agent risky or not — the agent acted on it, and confident misdiagnoses on the unreliable findings dropped from eight and a half percent to under three.

Same information. Same model. The only thing that changed was the representation, and it was the difference between the signal being ignored and the signal cutting dangerous errors by two-thirds.

I like this because it names something a lot of us have half-noticed and never measured. We tend to assume that if you give a capable agent all the relevant numbers, it'll integrate them the way a statistician would. This says: no — for a decision like this, the agent won't reliably threshold a continuous uncertainty score on its own. It needs the judgment call already made — the calibration, the cutoff — delivered as an action-shaped signal. Availability isn't enough; representation is the thing that determines whether the agent can use it at all.

The caveats are honest and worth keeping in view. This is a simulated decision-support setting, not a deployed one; the clinician notes are themselves generated by a language model; it's chest radiographs, eight findings, one agent. And the underlying error-detection gain from the uncertainty is modest — the drama is all in the agent's use of it, not in the raw signal. So don't read this as Monte Carlo dropout being a breakthrough. Read it as a design principle: when you feed a model's uncertainty into an agent, pre-threshold it. Do the calibration upstream, in a component you can validate, and give the agent a flag, not a distribution to interpret.

That's the transferable payload, and it generalizes well past radiology. Any time you're piping a probabilistic model into an agent that has to act, where you put the decision boundary — inside the agent's head, or in a small calibrated classifier sitting in front of it — is itself a design decision. This is a clean little demonstration that it's often the one that matters most.
