# AutoScreen — a multi-agent co-scientist that designs CRISPR libraries and re-ranks the hits

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.09.06.749678v1

## Script

Today's pick is a bioRxiv preprint from Stanford called AutoScreen, a multi-agent system that decides which genes are worth putting in your CRISPR screen, and which hits from a finished screen are worth chasing. It comes out of Le Cong's lab, with Jure Leskovec, Russ Altman and Aviv Regev on the author list, and it posted this week.

The framing is the right one. Functional genomics no longer has a data-generation problem; it has a prioritization problem. A genome-scale screen hands you a few hundred statistically significant genes with no mechanistic context, and then a postdoc spends two weeks reading papers to decide which five to follow up. And when you can't screen everything — in vivo work, scarce primary cells, combinatorial designs — somebody has to hand-build a focused library, which the authors clock at about three days of expert curation.

AutoScreen puts five specialist agents at this. One does deep literature research. One restructures those findings into ontology-aligned search terms. One fans out across twenty-six biomedical databases in parallel and normalizes everything into a common identifier space. One synthesizes. And one annotates the survivors with expression, dependency and druggability flags. It runs in two directions: design a library from nothing but a free-text study description, or take a finished hit list and re-rank it by fusing the statistical signal with everything the knowledge bases know.

The genuinely new idea, though, is their fix for a failure mode everyone has noticed when language models do target design. Ask one which genes matter for autophagy and it hands you mTOR and AKT, because those are the famous ones. The authors call it fame bias, and the usual dodge is a static blacklist of hub genes. AutoScreen instead generates a per-screen anti-hub anchor — the specific textbook genes that this screen's own retrieved evidence says the model will over-predict, but which lack mechanistic relevance to this particular phenotype — and inserts that list as a soft "avoid" constraint at generation time. Because the anchor is derived from the retrieval rather than hardcoded, what the agent is told to steer away from changes with the biology. And since anchor length is arbitrary, they run it at five, ten and twenty anchors and fuse the three rankings. In that autophagy screen, the effect is to push the famous non-hits out and pull the actual autophagy machinery into the top fifteen. It's a small, transferable trick, and it's the kind of thing that outlives the system it shipped in.

The numbers deserve honesty. Across three hundred and twenty genome-scale screens from BioGRID ORCS, AutoScreen recovers six point four percent of ground-truth hits in its top hundred predictions, against five point four percent for the strongest agent baseline and three point eight for a frontier general-purpose model. Those are real margins but small absolute ones. The advertised nineteen percent improvement is nineteen percent of six percent — this is a hard task and nobody is close to solving it.

The more persuasive result is the prospective one. The team designed two libraries from study descriptions alone, then went and ran the corresponding T-cell killing screens fresh in the wet lab. The designed libraries caught seventy-five and eighty-two percent of the ground-truth hits, beating both language-model baselines, on screens that did not exist at the time the design was made. That is a much harder test than retrospective recall, and it is the test most agentic discovery papers skip. In the other direction, re-ranking a leukemia surface-protein screen pulled three genes back up out of the statistical noise — mucin one from rank one hundred eighteen to five, podoplanin from eighty-one to forty-four, and L-R-R-C-fifteen from one thousand three hundred eighty-four to six hundred fifty-nine — and all three then validated individually as resistance genes in a killing assay with primary human natural killer cells.

Two things make this worth more than its benchmark. First, auditability is treated as a design requirement rather than a nice-to-have. Every ranked gene carries its supporting sources, its fusion provenance, and the anchor variant that produced it, and the authors say outright that they regard this, not predictive accuracy, as the prerequisite for letting an AI system into experimental design. Second, the Resource Hub: they ran the whole pipeline over more than five hundred public CRISPR, expression-atlas and UK Biobank datasets and cached the resulting evidence layer keyed by canonical identifiers — Reactome, Gene Ontology, standard disease ontology codes. That turns the expensive part, retrieval, into a reusable substrate, and it doubles as a benchmark that regenerates itself as new datasets land, which is a genuine answer to benchmark rot.

The caveat the authors flag is the one that matters. Ground truth is defined as the top hundred genes from each original analysis, so the benchmark rewards agreeing with what humans already found. And the entire evidence layer sits on curated knowledge bases that are themselves skewed toward well-studied genes. So fame bias is being fought at the generation step while being fed back in at the retrieval step. About a quarter of AutoScreen's recovered hits were unique to it, missed by both baselines — and those are precisely the ones that have not been validated at scale. That's the experiment I'd want to see next.
