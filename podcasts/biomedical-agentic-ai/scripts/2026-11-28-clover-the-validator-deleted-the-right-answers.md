# CLOVER — LLMs curating chemical-gene interactions for CTD, and the second-opinion model that made things worse

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.11.10.765703v1

## Script

Today's paper is about curation, and specifically about a design choice nearly every agentic pipeline makes without testing: put a second model behind the first to check its work. The preprint is called CLOVER. It was posted to bioRxiv on Tuesday by computer scientists at North Carolina State, working with the team behind the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database, CTD. Their CTD co-authors include Carolyn Mattingly and Allan Peter Davis, who has run CTD's biocuration for twenty years. If you've ever pulled a chemical-gene edge into a knowledge graph, there's a decent chance it came from CTD, and every one of its millions of interactions was put there by a human reading a paper.

The task is to read a full-text article and emit the interactions the way a CTD curator would write them. That means a chemical, a gene, and one of forty-seven interaction types, each with an increase, decrease or affects prefix. They had LLMs read text paragraphs, tables, figure images and spreadsheet supplements section by section, with PubTator tagging the chemicals and genes up front. The answer key is what CTD's curators actually recorded for fifty randomly chosen open-access papers.

The baseline says a lot. Hand a model the whole PDF with a minimal prompt and it recovers thirteen percent of what the curators found. The full pipeline with Claude Sonnet 4.5 gets to about seventy-two percent recall at sixty-one percent precision. That's useful for suggesting candidates, nowhere near replacing anyone, and the authors don't pretend otherwise.

The interesting part is where that improvement comes from, because it isn't really the LLMs. The raw extractions were poor across the board for every model. What moved the numbers was a deterministic refinement stage. First, it maps every chemical, gene and interaction term onto CTD's own controlled vocabularies. That pushed recall up to around eighty percent, because the model often had the right fact under the wrong name. Then a set of filters throws out anything citing prior work, anything that's common knowledge, null results and non-significant findings. That's the precision gate. Text sections benefited most, because papers are full of sentences like "X is known to induce Y" that a model happily extracts and a curator never would. So the model's job is to read. The rules decide what a curated fact is.

Then comes the validator experiment, the part I'd want every agent builder to see. They ran every combination of four models as extractor and validator, a second LLM checking the first one's output against the source passage. Validation helped in three combinations out of the whole grid. Nothing beat Claude extracting alone. And the failure modes differ by model. When a model checked its own output, DeepSeek threw away three hundred sixteen correct interactions and recovered fifty-four. GPT-4.1 threw away two hundred thirty-five and recovered two. The authors' read is that an uncertain validator defaults to rejecting, and DeepSeek as a validator was strict and literal about evidence, flagging things as unsupported and flipping prefixes. Claude went the opposite way. Checking itself, it deleted zero correct answers, but it let through three hundred eight new false positives, the biggest precision drop in the study. So one validator deletes, another rubber-stamps and adds. Neither is a second opinion in any useful sense. A checker is a model with its own bias, and you have to measure which way it leans before you put it in the loop.

Second, the error cases are curation lessons. Some mismatches were the model reading promoter induction as increased activity when CTD records increased expression, a level-of-description call that curators make by convention. Others were entity resolution. A table said "three-alpha-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase", and without species information the lookup landed on the wrong family member. A figure said "NTE" where CTD records the gene symbol PNPLA6. And some weren't errors at all. Correct interactions that CTD simply doesn't curate, because the chemical wasn't the paper's focus. In a manual review of two hundred of Claude's text extractions, Davis found a handful of valid interactions CTD had missed. So precision against CTD is a floor, not a measurement of truth. For calibration, CTD's own earlier study of agreement between curators came in at about ninety-one percent precision and seventy-one percent recall. A second human doesn't hit a hundred either.

The same pattern turned up this week in an FDA paper in Drug Safety, AskDrugTox, which classifies liver and cardiac toxicity from drug labels. About a third of its disagreements with the expert reference lists came from the reference being out of date. The labels had changed since the humans curated them. In both cases, the gold standard is a snapshot of curation policy at a moment in time.

Now the limits, and they matter for how far to read this. Fifty papers. Of a hundred thirty-six possible interaction classes, only seventeen even appear in the answer key, and ninety-five percent of the reference interactions are expression changes. So this is mostly a benchmark for "chemical raises or lowers gene expression." Only single-step interactions, while CTD's real value is in nested multi-step statements with gene forms and species. Only papers in the open-access subset, which the authors put at roughly a third of what CTD curates. And it isn't deployed in CTD. The closing observation is the one I'd underline. The ceiling on all of this is set less by models than by publishing. Gene names left to the authors' discretion, and supplements in whatever format a journal felt like. Standardize those, and the rules layer that's doing the real work gets much easier.

The take: for knowledge-base curation, put your effort into the controlled vocabulary and the filters, keep the human as the final gate, and don't assume a second LLM is a safety check until you've measured whether it deletes or rubber-stamps.
