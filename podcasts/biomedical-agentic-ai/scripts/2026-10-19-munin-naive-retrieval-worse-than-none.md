# Munin — a self-hosted literature agent for one lab, and the finding that naive retrieval is worse than none

Paper link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.23480

## Script

Today's pick is an arXiv preprint from the Institute for Drug Discovery at Leipzig University Medical School, describing Munin, an open-source framework for running a scientific literature assistant entirely on your own hardware, over your own group's corpus.

The setup will be familiar to anyone who runs a lab. You have years of accumulated literature — licensed articles you can't redistribute, preprints collaborators sent you, unpublished drafts, internal reports. A hosted service can read all of it, but you have to send it your unpublished work to get that. A self-hosted chat frontend keeps everything local, but the model behind it has no way to actually retrieve from the collection. Munin's pitch is that you shouldn't have to choose, and its central claim is deliberately unflattering to itself: the thing that makes the system work is not the model inside it. It's the harness. They ship the evaluation suite that tests that claim alongside the code, so you can check it on your own corpus rather than taking their number for it.

But the result I want you to take away from this paper isn't the headline accuracy. It's the ablation.

They run three arms on the same two hundred literature questions. A bare model with no retrieval at all. Naive retrieval — pull the top five documents from a production vector store, stuff them into the prompt, make one completion. And the full agentic loop. Naive retrieval came in below the bare model on every single backbone they tested. Not marginally — down eighteen points on one, twenty-one on another, thirty-one on a third, all with p-values under a thousandth and confidence intervals nowhere near zero.

The mechanism is what makes this worth five minutes of your attention. When the top-five retrieval misses the relevant document, the model treats the abstracts it did get as authoritative. It reads the absence of the number it was asked for as evidence that the number is unavailable, and declines to answer. Abstention rates go from about four percent bare to fifty percent under retrieval on one Qwen backbone, and from twenty-three percent to seventy-five percent on the other. The bare model, handed only the question, has no such anchor — so it answers from its own weights, and gets a decent fraction of them right. Fixed-context retrieval doesn't just fail to help. It actively suppresses a response channel the model already had. On the third backbone, gpt-oss-20b, the failure mode was uglier still: a hundred and sixty-two of a hundred and ninety-nine turns simply ended without the model emitting a final answer.

Now, that top-five-into-the-prompt design is exactly what the widely deployed self-hosted chat frontends do. Open WebUI, LibreChat, AnythingLLM — all keep your data on-premises, all treat the model as a black box behind a chat window, none read full text before answering or route a query to a not-in-corpus path. This paper is a direct measurement of that design, and the measurement says it is worse than nothing.

The fix isn't a better prompt, and they're explicit that it's architectural. Retrieval and answering interleave, so the loop can notice its evidence is insufficient and go get more. The reading agent is bound by what they call a quote-or-abstain contract — it has to produce a supporting sentence before it's allowed to emit an answer. Under that harness, accuracy roughly doubles, from thirty-nine percent to eighty-seven. Roughly twice as many claims in the answer trace back to a passage the model actually read. And with all outbound network access turned off, consulting only the local corpus, both Qwen backbones still beat the strongest published result on this benchmark.

The piece I'd actually steal, though, is how they test whether the system knows when its corpus doesn't contain the answer — because they built ground truth that requires no human annotation and no model judge at all. One set is a hundred fabricated papers: eighty DOIs verified nonexistent against the registry, twenty described only by title and claim. Any identifier the system returns is a fabricated citation by construction, so scoring is a pattern match and a membership test. Zero annotation cost, and across three backbones and three hundred fabricated references, it produced not one invented local citation. The second set is a paired shadow corpus — fifty questions asked twice, once against production and once against an identical index with the forty-nine source papers and their two thousand chunks surgically removed. Only one thing differs between the arms, so the paired difference is exactly what you want to measure.

And there's a lovely confession buried in the methods. They validate the shadow in both directions before every run, because a paper that silently survives removal quietly converts a negative question into an answerable one. They learned that the hard way: a retrieval path added after the recipe was written — the chunk index — wasn't being shadowed, and it invalidated the test until somebody noticed.

The honest caveats are theirs, mostly. The harness gain on gpt-oss is about a third of the Qwen figure. Model-agnostic turns out to mean a handful of documented per-family patches, and their first gpt-oss run scored ten points low purely from a tool-name parsing defect and a scorer that didn't recognize bolded answers — they kept the pre-repair scorecard in the repository so you can see what that cost. The questions are multiple choice, and a real fraction are answerable from parametric knowledge, which is precisely why the corpus-only control exists.

The framing I like is their treatment of network egress as a first-class experimental variable rather than a deployment detail, and re-certification as routine: when you swap the model, you re-run the suite, because a stronger model inside a specialized harness is not reliably a better system. That's a claim about tool contracts, not about intelligence. It's Apache-licensed, the whole evaluation is one command, and for once the useful contribution to a group like ours is the measurement apparatus rather than the assistant.
