# LABEL — the knowledge graph knows who must not take a drug; Wikipedia, PubMed and the models trained on them mostly don't

Paper link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.19166

## Script

The usual story about biomedical knowledge graphs is that they're the incomplete, lagging copy of what's in the literature. Text is where the knowledge lives, and the graph is a lossy summary somebody curated from it. A new preprint from Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute turns that around for one kind of fact, the kind where getting it wrong hurts people. It shows that a curated graph records who must not take a drug, and general prose mostly doesn't. Then it shows that language models inherit the prose, not the graph.

The setup is clean, and it's worth a minute because the design makes the result believable. They start from PrimeKG, the Zitnik lab's precision-medicine graph, whose drug–disease edges come from DrugCentral. They take every drug with at least three indications and three contraindications and sample exactly three of each. That gives 574 drugs and 1,722 facts per relation. Then they ask a simple question of three text sources. For each fact, does the drug's text actually name the disease? The sources are the drug's English Wikipedia article, forty PubMed abstracts per drug, and the official FDA label. Disease names are matched through MONDO, so exact labels and synonyms count. A bare parent term like "diabetes" doesn't count for "type 1 diabetes."

The important design choice is that every comparison is within a drug. Famous drugs have long Wikipedia pages and thousands of papers, so comparing indications of popular drugs against contraindications of obscure ones would just measure fame. Instead they compare each drug's benefits against its own safety limits and pool the results with a Mantel–Haenszel odds ratio. They also control for the fact that PrimeKG lists about three times as many contraindications as indications. And they run a negative control on Hetionet, where both drug–disease relations, treats and palliates, describe benefit. There, as expected, no gap shows up.

Here's the result. Wikipedia names about twenty percent of a drug's indications and under ten percent of its contraindications. PubMed abstracts are worse, twenty-six versus eleven. The pooled odds ratio is above two in both. Half the PubMed search budget was deliberately spent on adverse-effect and contraindication queries, and that barely moved anything. Toxicity papers rarely list the pre-existing conditions that rule a drug out. A model judge, checked against four human annotators with a kappa above point nine, confirms the matched sentences really state the relationship. Now look at the FDA label as a whole. It names indications and contraindications at the same rate, about twenty-three percent each, an odds ratio of one. So the safety facts aren't obscure. They just aren't in the prose.

The models follow the prose. Scaling Qwen 2.5 from half a billion to fourteen billion parameters lifts closed-book accuracy on indications by about eighteen points. Contraindication accuracy sits between twenty-two and twenty-seven percent at every size, against a chance rate of twenty. With harder distractors, sibling diseases from the MONDO tree plus an option to say "not enough information," the gap is large in all four models tested. The bigger models don't learn the safety facts. They just shift from confidently wrong guesses to abstaining.

Then comes the part that tells you what kind of problem this is. Give the models a retrieved passage that actually names the contraindication, and accuracy jumps by fourteen points, slightly more than the gain for a stated indication. So the models aren't bad at reasoning about harm. They read a safety fact as well as a benefit when it's in front of them. The problem is upstream, in what got written down. Routing safety questions to the whole FDA label lifts contraindication accuracy from forty to about fifty-six percent, but only when the label names the condition. One practical detail matters for anyone building these systems. The label's own Contraindications section names under three percent of PrimeKG's contraindications. Most of them sit in the boxed warning, warnings and precautions, and specific-populations sections. So if you index only the section with the obvious name, you miss most of the safety facts.

For this audience, I think the bigger point isn't about clinical chatbots. It's about how we build knowledge graphs now. Much new graph construction is text mining with language models: read the literature, emit triples. If the literature underwrites safety limits by half, an LLM-extracted drug graph will have the same skew. And a "language model as knowledge base" benchmark built only on what-does-this-drug-treat questions will flatter the model in exactly the place it's weakest. This paper is a quantitative argument that curated, regulatory-sourced resources like DrugCentral and openFDA aren't legacy scaffolding to be replaced by reading. For some relation types, they're the only place the fact reliably lives.

There are caveats, and the authors state most of them. The models are small and from an older generation. Qwen 2.5 tops out at fourteen billion parameters, and no frontier model or medical model was tested, so "scale doesn't help" is shown only up to there. Everything is multiple choice. The link to pretraining is a correlation, though they point out that OLMo releases its training data, so it could be traced token by token. And absolute coverage is low everywhere. Even the full FDA label names only about a quarter of PrimeKG's contraindications, partly because DrugCentral records specific subtypes the label states more broadly. So the within-drug ratio is the robust finding here, not the raw percentages. Finally, the agent in their design figure, with a planner that routes by relation type and a verifier that checks whether evidence names the exact condition, is a proposal. Only the routing step was tested.

Still, the take is sharp. The model is fine at reading a safety warning; it just never saw most of them. For medication safety, the curated graph and the regulatory label aren't a fallback for when retrieval fails. They should be the first place the agent looks.
