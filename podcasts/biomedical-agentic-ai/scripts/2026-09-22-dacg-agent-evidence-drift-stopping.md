# DACG-agent — a stopping rule for literature-retrieving agents, because more evidence makes true nulls look beneficial

Paper link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.24101

## Script

Today's pick went up on arXiv yesterday, out of the Center for Brain-Inspired Computing Research at Tsinghua, and it makes a claim that should bother anyone building a deep-research agent over the biomedical literature. The claim is that retrieval depth is not a performance dial. It is a dose.

Here is the mechanism, and it is almost embarrassingly simple once you see it. The published literature is skewed toward positive findings. Every retrieval step samples from that skew. So an agent that accumulates evidence step by step, with no model of the bias in its source, does not converge on the truth — it converges on the skew, and it does so with rising confidence. The authors formalize this as evidence drift, and they prove that for a query whose true answer is no effect, the probability of misclassifying it as beneficial follows a strictly increasing envelope in retrieval depth that approaches one. More reading, more certainty, wrong answer.

The proof is for a plain vote-counting aggregator, which is a strong simplification, and they are upfront about that. So they simulate the version they actually deploy, a noisy-OR confidence update, and the two curves are statistically indistinguishable. Then they stress the assumptions. Correlated evidence slows the drift but keeps it monotone. Query-varying bias reproduces the same curve. And the realistic case — where null findings get extracted at lower confidence than positive ones, because nulls are both underpublished and underreported within the abstracts that do exist — drives the false positive rate to nearly a hundred percent by step twenty. The theorem, in other words, is a floor.

Now the empirical half. The benchmark is derived from Cochrane systematic reviews: intervention-outcome pairs with a consensus label, held out by review so nothing leaks across splits, and the reported numbers come from a class-balanced test set of a hundred forty queries, seventy beneficial and seventy no-effect. Run a fixed retrieval budget of three steps and the drift rate is seven point nine percent. Run twenty and it is fifteen point seven. Accuracy on the null class falls from forty-seven percent to forty. The detail that lands hardest is the timing: the median time to the correct answer is one step. The agent typically finds the right answer after a single retrieval and then spends nineteen more steps losing it.

The baselines bracket the failure nicely. A zero-shot language model with no retrieval at all gets ninety-one percent on beneficial queries and forty-three percent on nulls — which is not reasoning, that is publication bias absorbed during pretraining and played back. Bolt retrieval onto it and the null accuracy drops to thirty-one percent. Retrieval made it worse. And then there is KGARevion, a real published biomedical knowledge-graph agent, run end to end and unmodified: ninety-seven percent on beneficial, fifteen percent on nulls. It called fifty-nine of seventy genuine nulls beneficial. The reason is a schema problem that anyone who builds knowledge graphs should feel in their teeth — its curated graph has relation types for indication, contraindication and association, and no representation whatsoever for the absence of an effect. So a confirmed link reads as benefit. There is no vocabulary in which the system can say nothing happened.

The fix is not better ranking. They rebuilt the pipeline on a different model with a cross-encoder reranker and got forty percent on nulls, exactly the level of unstopped full-budget retrieval. The fix is knowing when to stop, and their agent layers two signals. The first watches the KL divergence between successive label posteriors — not the posterior's magnitude, which is badly calibrated, but whether it is still moving. The second is a process reward model trained on preference pairs from recorded trajectories, which halts when the graph state starts drifting away from configurations that historically preceded correct answers. The ablation is honest about the division of labor: the KL layer does essentially all of the accuracy work, lifting null accuracy from forty to sixty percent and halving drift, while the reward model buys efficiency, holding that accuracy with fourteen percent fewer steps. Mean retrieval ends up at under four steps instead of eleven.

The result I keep turning over is the ontology audit, because it inverts the intuition. They grounded all three hundred two benchmark entities against Medical Subject Headings — only twenty-two percent match a descriptor exactly, forty-three percent after light normalization, and the rest are non-atomic clinical phrases dragged out of review titles. Then they split the queries by whether both endpoints ground cleanly. The well-grounded ones drift more, not less: thirty-six percent against seventeen. And the reason is mechanical rather than mysterious. Entities that map cleanly to a controlled vocabulary are the well-studied ones, they carry roughly twice the retrieved evidence, and more evidence is precisely what the theorem says pushes a bias-agnostic aggregator toward benefit. The best-characterized concepts in medicine, where a decision-support tool is most likely to be trusted, are exactly where this failure concentrates.

Be clear about what the paper does and doesn't win. Overall accuracy goes from sixty-one to sixty-nine percent against full-budget retrieval, but the zero-shot model is at sixty-seven and the confidence intervals overlap heavily — the defensible gains are on the null class and on producing a traceable evidence chain at all. The oracle ceiling is seventy-seven percent, which is a limit of abstract-level synthesis. It is one benchmark family, and Cochrane's own labels inherit the very bias being studied, though that cuts in the authors' favor, since a mislabeled null would only understate the drift they measure. And they tried the intuitive alternative — stopping when the graph stops growing, by density, by path discovery rate, by evidence plateau — and every one of those failed, because a structural signal cannot distinguish "not enough evidence yet" from "genuinely no effect." Only belief monitoring can.

The transferable lesson is short. Every agentic literature system on the market right now advertises retrieval depth as a feature, and every one of them is exposed to this, because the skew lives in the corpus rather than in the retriever. Deciding when to stop reading is not an optimization detail. It is a correctness requirement.
