# SABLE — an open-source agentic framework for hit-to-lead drug optimization that keeps the language model out of the quantitative decisions

Paper link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.11483

## Script

Today's pick is a drug-discovery agent called SABLE, out of Alexander Tropsha's group at UNC Chapel Hill. What I like about it is not that it's an agent — we've had plenty of those — but that it's an agent built around a deliberate act of self-restraint.

Here's the problem it's solving. Hit-to-lead optimization in medicinal chemistry is the slog where you take a molecule that weakly hits your target and iterate it into something that's potent, selective, safe, drug-like, and actually synthesizable — all at once, with those objectives constantly fighting each other. Making one part more potent can wreck solubility or flip on a toxicity flag. There's a natural temptation to point a large language model at this and just let it drive: read the goals, propose molecules, score them, pick the winner. And a lot of recent agentic chemistry tools do roughly that, with the language model sitting at the center of the scientific judgment.

SABLE's authors think that's exactly the wrong place to put the language model, and they cite a pretty damning piece of evidence. In the ChemCrow paper's own evaluation, GPT-four, when it was asked to judge chemical outputs, couldn't reliably tell clearly wrong answers from tool-grounded correct ones. If your model can't distinguish right from wrong on the quantitative task, you shouldn't be handing it the quantitative decisions.

So SABLE draws a hard line. The language model is the interface and the router. It reads your natural-language request, extracts the campaign parameters — the seed molecule, the target, the objectives, how many iterations — and calls the right tools. But it never scores a molecule, and it never picks the winner. Those jobs go to explicit, domain-specific machinery: a reaction-templated enumerator called HEALER that only builds analogs you could actually synthesize from real building blocks; predictors for the physical properties and the absorption, metabolism, and safety profile; the Boltz-two structure model as the affinity oracle; and classic Bayesian optimization to decide which candidates are worth evaluating next. The whole thing runs as a stateful workflow in LangGraph, and every number that comes out is traceable back to the tool that produced it. It's a computational twin of the analyze-and-prioritize half of the design-make-test-analyze loop, with the provenance kept honest.

Two things make this more than a plumbing exercise. First, the synthesizability constraint is baked in from the start — you're never optimizing toward a beautiful molecule nobody can make, which is a classic failure mode of generative design. Second, the Bayesian optimizer means it converges on good candidates while evaluating only a slice of the enumerated space, so you're not paying for a Boltz-two inference on every possible analog.

They put it through real case studies. A retrospective run on a published lead-optimization series against the RNA-methylation enzyme METTL3; a campaign against a kinase called CAMKK2; and a validation set of four targets spanning a protease, a lyase, a kinase, and a G-protein-coupled receptor — the classes where Boltz-two actually reports decent affinity ranking. In every case, starting from a weak seed compound, SABLE pulled out analogs with improved predicted affinity, and in the multi-objective runs it traced out a front of trade-offs rather than collapsing to a single answer.

And here's the part I respect most: they're honest about the ceiling. Boltz-two is a computational oracle, not ground truth. Its predicted potencies don't correlate perfectly with measured ones, and in the METTL3 case SABLE didn't rediscover the exact published best compound. The authors say plainly that it moves in the right direction and enriches your candidate set, but it is not a substitute for a real biochemical assay or for a medicinal chemist's review. This is decision support, a co-pilot, not an autonomous chemist. After a stretch of papers showing language models confidently reaching wrong conclusions, it's refreshing to see a system designed from the ground up to not overclaim.

For this audience the meta-point is the interesting one. The maturity move in scientific agents right now isn't giving the model more authority — it's the opposite. It's figuring out precisely which decisions the language model is genuinely good at, which are routing and interpretation and translation, and then walling off the quantitative judgments behind explicit, auditable, swappable tools. SABLE is open source, it's bring-your-own-tools — you redefine your predictors in a config file without touching the orchestration logic — and that modularity is really a bet that today's components, Boltz-two included, will be superseded, and the framework should outlive them. That's the right bet, and it's a clean design pattern for anyone building agents over their own computational stack.
