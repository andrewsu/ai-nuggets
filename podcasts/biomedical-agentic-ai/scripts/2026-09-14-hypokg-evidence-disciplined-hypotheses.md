# HypoKG — knowledge graph paths don't make better biomedical hypotheses, they make more honest ones

Paper link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.12260

## Script

Today's pick is a benchmark paper from the School of Computing at the University of Georgia, posted to arXiv this week, called HypoKG. It asks a question that a lot of us have been building on top of without ever testing: when you hand a language model a knowledge graph path and ask it for a hypothesis, does the path actually do anything, or is the model just reading the two ends and writing a story?

Here's the setup. They merge three databases — KEGG, Rhea and UniProt — into a single human biochemistry graph, about seventeen thousand nodes and thirty-two thousand edges, restricted to human pathways and human proteins so that model-organism enzymes sharing a name can't sneak in. Out of that graph they sample five hundred and fifty multi-hop paths that run from an enzyme-kinetics entity to a rare disease endpoint, crossing between three and nine different domain types along the way. And the thing that makes the benchmark interesting is that ninety-four and a half percent of those source-disease pairs have zero co-citation in PubMed. Nobody has written the sentence connecting these two things. So right away the graph is doing one job that has nothing to do with reasoning — it's surfacing pairs that the literature does not contain.

Then six models generate a hypothesis for each path under four conditions, thirteen thousand two hundred hypotheses total. The conditions are the whole experiment. One: source enzyme only. Two and three: the full path, with slightly different instructions. And four, the critical one: just the source and the disease, no intermediate structure at all. That fourth condition is the ablation nobody in this literature has run, because it tests the right counterfactual. It's not "knowledge graph versus no knowledge graph" — the endpoint pair in condition four was itself discovered by the graph. It's "does the mechanism in the middle add anything once you've been told which two things to connect?"

Everything gets scored on a five-part rubric built from sessions with eight PhD biochemists: relevance, mechanistic specificity, testability, novelty, and a fifth criterion they call evidence proportionality — is the strength of the claim justified by the evidence the model was actually given.

And the result is an inversion. Endpoint-only wins the aggregate score, and wins it clearly: about sixteen point seven out of twenty-five, against fifteen point one and fifteen for the two full-path conditions. Give a good model an enzyme and a rare disease and it will write you something more relevant, more novel and more testable than if you also hand it the mechanism. But on evidence proportionality, the full-path conditions win, and the endpoint-only condition loses. The path doesn't make the output better. It makes the output more disciplined. They call this evidence-disciplined reasoning, and it's the right name, because what the graph buys you is a constraint, not a capability.

Now, that could easily be an artifact of LLM judges preferring text that cites entities it was shown. Two controls rule that out, and they're the reason this paper is worth your time rather than just your citation.

The first is a shuffled-path control. They permute the intermediate nodes while holding both endpoints fixed — same entities, scrambled mechanism. Evidence proportionality drops by about eight tenths of a point, highly significant. Novelty doesn't move at all. So the models really are reading the ordered structure, and what the structure constrains is specifically the grounding of the mechanistic claim, not the creativity of the idea. That's a clean dissociation.

The second is the human study. Three PhD-level experts scored a stratified sample of fifty-five paths, blinded, seeing all four conditions for the same path in randomized order — so path difficulty and disease familiarity are controlled within subject. They reproduced the machine ordering exactly. And on fifty of the fifty-five paths, all three experts unanimously flagged the endpoint-only hypothesis as making a speculative mechanistic leap. Ninety-one percent. The fluent one is the one the biochemists don't believe.

Two more findings that matter if you're building this kind of system. The source-only baseline has a relevance score of one point zero one — the floor — while its testability stays at four out of five. Read that again: given just an enzyme, the models write crisply falsifiable, well-structured hypotheses about the wrong disease. They drift to NAFLD, Alzheimer's and type 2 diabetes, the diseases the training data is full of, and they do it in impeccable scientific prose. A well-formed hypothesis about something you didn't ask about is a failure mode that no readability metric will ever catch.

And the benefit is capability-gated. The domain-specialized MedGemma is the only model in the suite where the full path actually beats endpoint-only. BioMistral shows essentially no gain from the path, and its shuffle control drop is not significant — meaning it was never using the path in the first place. Llama at seventy billion parameters is barely better. So "add a knowledge graph" is not a free upgrade you can bolt onto whatever model is cheap. Below some threshold the graph is decoration. There's a related result on path length: the benefit peaks around four domain crossings and collapses by eight, because long paths invite what they call local subgraph collapse — the model finds the single strongest local transition and quietly forgets the endpoints.

The limitation the authors own is the right one. Their judges agree least on precisely the criterion the paper rests on, about point six seven, which they say straight out. And evidence proportionality is a proxy for grounded reasoning — nothing here checks whether a single one of these thirteen thousand hypotheses is true.

But the takeaway is sharp, and it generalizes past this benchmark. If you are optimizing a hypothesis-generation agent against an aggregate quality score with a model in the judge seat, you are actively selecting against grounding, because the confident story outscores the careful one on four criteria out of five. The knowledge graph is not there to raise your score. It's there to stop the model from writing checks the evidence can't cash.
