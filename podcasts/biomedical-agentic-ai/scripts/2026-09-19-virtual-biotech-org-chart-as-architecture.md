# The Virtual Biotech — thirty-seven thousand agents curate every trial outcome, and the org chart is the architecture

Paper link: https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aeg6779
Open preprint: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.02.23.707551v1

## Script

Today's pick landed in Science on Thursday, and it is the most ambitious agentic system anyone has pointed at drug discovery so far. It's called the Virtual Biotech, and it comes out of James Zou's group at Stanford — the same lab whose Paper2Agent work was on this show yesterday. This one is a good deal bigger.

The premise is worth stating carefully, because it is a claim about organizations rather than about models. Drug programs fail, the authors argue, not mainly because any single analysis is hard, but because the evidence is fragmented. The human geneticist, the single-cell person, the pharmacologist and the clinical trialist all work from different data in different formats, and they rarely cross-reference each other before a decision gets made. So instead of building one very smart agent, the team built an org chart. A Chief Scientific Officer agent takes your question, interviews you to clarify what you actually want, has a chief-of-staff agent prepare a briefing on the state of the field, and then routes subtasks to four divisions — target identification, target safety, modality selection, and clinical officers. Eleven specialized agents in all, each wired to its own slice of more than a hundred custom Model Context Protocol tools that sit in front of roughly seventy-eight thousand targets, thirty-nine thousand diseases, fourteen million protein interactions, and over a hundred million single-cell profiles. A scientific reviewer agent then critiques what comes back, and the CSO re-delegates wherever the evidence is thin. The design rationale I find most persuasive is the quiet one: if the machine mirrors the human org chart, then every agent has a corresponding human expert who knows how to audit it.

The headline number is thirty-seven thousand agents, and it is worth being precise about what they did, because it is the most durable thing in this paper. The system spun up thirty-seven thousand clinical-trialist agents in parallel, one per trial, to annotate outcomes for fifty-six thousand trials in the Open Targets set. That dataset has targets but no endpoint results, and the registry records are frequently incomplete or free text only. So each agent ran a three-tier cascade — query the registry, then search the literature when fields were missing, then go to press releases and regulatory announcements — and recorded the source for every single annotation. Against a human reviewer on a hundred randomly drawn trials, primary-endpoint agreement was about ninety percent, and against the manually curated Therapeutic Data Commons set, eighty-five percent across seven thousand overlapping trials. The disagreements cluster almost entirely in genuinely ambiguous cases: multi-arm studies where some arms hit, a p-value of point-oh-five-one with supportive secondary outcomes, single-arm designs that don't collapse to a binary. That is the result I'd hang onto. A source-tracked, publicly released map of clinical outcomes linked to drug targets at that scale simply did not exist, and it did not exist because manual curation of it is unaffordable.

What they found with it is a new prior for target selection. Drugs aimed at genes with cell-type-specific expression were forty percent more likely to advance from phase one to phase two, forty-eight percent more likely to reach market, and carried thirty-two percent lower adverse-event rates. Crucially, that signal is orthogonal to human genetic evidence — it replicates in trials with no genetic support at all. Genetics has been the gold-standard prior for a decade; this says single-cell specificity is a second, independent one.

The case study everyone will quote is B7-H3 in lung cancer. The agents ran with web search disabled and a January 2025 knowledge cutoff. They found B7-H3 upregulated not in tumor cells but in cancer-associated fibroblasts, inferred an immunosuppressive signaling program from ligand-receptor analysis, had the reviewer agent object that dissociated data can't speak to spatial context, went back and confirmed immune exclusion in spatial transcriptomics, then tied high expression to worse survival in TCGA, and recommended an antibody-drug conjugate. In August 2025 — after the cutoff — the FDA granted breakthrough designation to exactly such a conjugate. Forty-six dollars of API credit, under a day.

I'd temper that story slightly. The conjugate modality for B7-H3 was already an obvious bet; a naked antibody against it was in phase two before the cutoff. The genuinely novel piece is the fibroblast-centric mechanism, and a breakthrough designation doesn't validate that. What the case really demonstrates is speed and coherence — the reviewer-to-spatial-to-survival handoff is the kind of cross-division move that siloed teams routinely fail to make.

The limitation the authors name is the right one. A single mistake propagates through a virtual organization the same way it does through a real one, and one reviewer agent is thin insurance. But their answer is the honest one too: unlike a real company, this one leaves a complete, downloadable audit trail of why it believed what it believed.
