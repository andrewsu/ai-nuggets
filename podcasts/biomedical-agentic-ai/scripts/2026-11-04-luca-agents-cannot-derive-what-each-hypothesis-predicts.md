# LUCA — scientific agents rescue organisms they cannot diagnose, and the break is in deriving what each hypothesis predicts

Paper link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04473

## Script

Today's pick is a short arXiv paper from Relation Therapeutics in London, written for the NeurIPS workshop with the wonderful name "I Can't Believe It's Not Better." It's called "Treating the Symptom, Missing the Cause," and it does something this show has been asking for for months: instead of reporting that a scientific agent reasons badly, it hunts down exactly where the reasoning breaks.

The setup is a synthetic world called LUCA, released by the same group alongside it. Each organism is a twenty-gene regulatory network with three external signals and three observable phenotypes. Gene names are invented, so no model can lean on what it remembers about biology. The group plants one hidden fault, a gene stuck high or low or a signal stuck on, and the agent gets a budget of perturbations: push a gene up, push it down, block it, switch on a signal, watch the phenotypes. Then it has to commit to a diagnosis, and only after that propose a rescue that brings the phenotypes back to healthy. Because the authors wrote the network, they can simulate every experiment the agent could have run, including the ones it didn't.

The headline result is the one in the title. Every one of seven models rescues more organisms than it diagnoses. Claude Opus 4.6 rescues all twenty medium-difficulty organisms and diagnoses eleven. Sonnet 4.6 rescues all twenty and diagnoses seven. The reason this is possible at all is obvious once you see it: a gene downstream of the fault is a perfectly good place to intervene. Push on it and the phenotype comes back, while the real cause sits there untouched. That's a treatment that works for the wrong reason, which in drug discovery is a very expensive kind of success.

But the authors say up front that the gap isn't their main finding, and the investigation is what makes the paper worth your time. They walk through suspects in order, and the elimination is clean.

First suspect: the agents just didn't explore enough. Across models, the ones that run more experiments do diagnose better. Within any one model, though, sessions with more experiments diagnose no better than sessions with fewer. Exploration is a model-level trait, not the lever.

Second suspect: the evidence wasn't available. Here's where ground truth pays off. In fifty-three of the sixty organisms, some available experiment cleanly separates the true fault from every competing explanation. Only seven are genuinely unresolvable.

Third suspect: the agents didn't look for that evidence. They mostly didn't. About twenty-four percent of their experiments discriminate between competing causes, against nineteen percent if you picked at random. Barely above chance, and the authors call it what it looks like, confirmation bias. And yet it barely matters, because in ninety-one percent of sessions the agent runs a discriminating experiment anyway, usually a single clean perturbation, with a median of sixteen more experiments still to come. Diagnosis accuracy in those sessions is thirty percent. The answer is sitting in the agent's own lab notebook, and it commits against it.

There's one telling worked example. An agent suspects a gene, tests it up, down and blocked, and all three readouts match the true fault rather than its suspect. Fourteen experiments later it diagnoses the suspect. Its rescue works.

So where does it break? The probe experiments answer that, and this is the part I'd carry into any agent design. They take real session logs, cut just before the agent committed, and hand them to all seven models with less and less help. Give a model one result, two candidate causes, and what each cause predicts, and it picks correctly essentially every time. Tell it what it currently believes and it revises when it should. Hand it a full log, typically twenty-two experiments, with the two causes and their predicted readouts for every experiment, and it still finds the decisive one, at eighty-eight to a hundred percent. Now take away only the predictions, so the model has to work out for itself what each cause would have produced, and accuracy drops to between fifty and seventy-four percent, for every model, with no overlap in the intervals. Reading evidence is fine. Updating is fine. Finding the needle in the log is fine. What fails is the counterfactual step: if this gene were the culprit, what would this experiment have shown?

Two more details sharpen that. A baseline with privileged access to the network's response function, one that perturbs every gene in both directions, predicts unseen experiments almost perfectly and diagnoses at near chance. Exhaustive, well-calibrated data does not produce causal identification by itself. And the one behavioural signature of success is contrastive. When an agent perturbs the true cause in only one direction, it diagnoses correctly four percent of the time. When it pushes the same gene both up and down, seventy-one percent, in every model. That's the logic of pairing CRISPR interference with CRISPR activation, rediscovered by watching agents.

The caveats are real and the authors state them. Twenty seeds per model, so the confidence intervals are wide. The contrastive signature is a correlation; agents that already suspect the right gene may simply test it more carefully. The probe logs come almost entirely from Opus sessions. And this is a toy world, which tells you where reasoning can fail, not how often it does in a real wet lab.

Here's my take. Outcome-based evaluation is going to keep rewarding exactly this agent: it fixes the phenotype, it ran the right experiment, the log looks diligent, and its causal model is wrong. For anyone building agents on top of knowledge graphs, the fix this points to is structural, not motivational. Don't ask the model to hold competing hypotheses in its head. Make it write down, per hypothesis, the predicted readout of each experiment before running it, and let the tooling do the comparison. The paper's own probes show models are near perfect once those predictions exist on the page. The missing piece is a table, and tables are something we know how to build.
