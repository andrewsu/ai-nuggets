# SPOKE on the EHR — when a biomedical knowledge graph helps clinical prediction, and when the graph only re-expresses what the record already said

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.10.04.756605v1

## Script

If you build biomedical knowledge graphs, you have probably made this argument, or at least nodded along to it. Electronic health records capture what was observed about a patient, but not why those observations belong together. A diagnosis, a drug and a lab value sit in the record as separate entries, and a graph that links genes, pathways, compounds and diseases ought to supply the missing biology. A preprint posted this weekend from Rancho Biosciences puts that argument to the most careful test I have seen it get, and the answer is more useful than a yes.

The setup reuses an established method. Patient records from UCSF are mapped through standard vocabularies onto SPOKE, the precision-medicine knowledge graph out of UCSF, and each patient's concepts are diffused across the graph with personalized PageRank to produce a patient signature. Earlier work did this one disease at a time and reported that it helped. This paper runs three diseases chosen to differ on purpose: Parkinson's, multiple sclerosis and chronic pain. And it varies the graph itself, from no graph at all, to only the edges between a patient's own recorded concepts, to a graph scoped to the cohort, to one anchored on the disease node, to the whole of SPOKE.

The first result is the practical one. A graph built from every concept that actually appears in the cohort, plus one hop of neighbors with the big hubs removed, keeps about forty-five percent of SPOKE's nodes and a fifth of its edges. It matches the full graph in all three diseases and runs three to five times faster. The opposite intuition fails badly. Anchor the graph on the disease node and its neighborhood, which sounds like the biologically principled choice, and accuracy collapses, from about 0.94 to 0.69 in multiple sclerosis, because only two or three percent of the patients' recorded concepts fall inside that neighborhood. The lesson is to scope the graph to the data you have, not to the disease you are thinking about.

The second result is the one the abstract leads with, and the numbers deserve a closer look than the abstract gives them. The graph helps in Parkinson's, does roughly nothing in multiple sclerosis, and hurts in chronic pain. But helps means a gain of about six thousandths of AUROC, on a baseline already near 0.95. The large effect sizes come from a paired design over the same twenty-five splits, which makes tiny, consistent offsets look dramatic. One more caveat that the authors do not dwell on: those splits are described as independent, but they are all 80/20 cuts of the same cohort, so the p-values are on the optimistic side.

The comparison that should get your attention is a different one. In Parkinson's and MS, the minimal graph, just the edges among a patient's own recorded concepts with no expansion into genes or pathways, performs the same as the full cohort graph. Whatever value the graph adds is already in the relationships among clinical concepts the record contains — drugs, side effects, comorbidities — and reaching out into molecular biology adds nothing measurable. The authors' own feature analysis agrees: the Parkinson's gain came mostly from graph-derived comorbidity features, while MS showed the biggest shift toward molecular features and gained nothing at all.

Chronic pain is the cautionary case, and it is a lesson about the graph, not the model. SPOKE has no real disease node for it, only two peripheral symptom and side-effect nodes with thirteen neighbors between them, and the diagnosis codes do not even map onto those. So diffusion spreads non-specific clinical signal, and every additional bit of graph makes the prediction a little worse. Coverage bias in knowledge graphs is well documented; this is what it looks like downstream. And you can measure it before you train anything, by asking how densely the graph represents the disease and how many of the cohort's concepts actually land in it.

The third result explains the other two, and it is the part I would take away. Without the graph, the model's top features are nearly uncorrelated, with a median absolute correlation around 0.04 in Parkinson's. With the cohort graph it is 0.98. Diffusion gives neighboring nodes similar scores, so one piece of disease signal gets smeared across dozens of correlated features. You can remove features one by one, most important first, and accuracy barely moves until almost nothing is left. The graph is not adding new information. It is re-expressing information the record already had, and it helps only when the diffusion happens to carry something the raw codes did not.

That has an uncomfortable implication for the interpretability argument, which is usually the fallback when accuracy gains are small. The authors pitch the graph as a biological translation layer whose value lies partly in interpretability. But they also note, correctly, that random-forest importance is least reliable precisely when features are this correlated, so a top-ranked gene or pathway is not evidence that it drives the prediction. You cannot have it both ways. If the explanation rests on which biological nodes rank highest, it rests on the least trustworthy number in the analysis.

The limits are real. This is one site, an early SPOKE release of about forty-seven thousand nodes where current versions run to millions, cases and controls matched on how much healthcare they used rather than on demographics, and classification of patients who already carry the code rather than prospective prediction. There is also a buried methods detail worth knowing: under a forty-iteration cap, more than ninety-nine percent of the PageRank vectors on the cohort graph never converged, compared with about half on the full graph. That does not overturn the equivalence, but it means the cheap graph is being compared in a partially converged state.

Even so, this is the kind of paper the knowledge-graph community needs more of. It reports integration against a no-graph baseline and a concept-only baseline, across diseases that differ on purpose, and it shows the result can go negative. For anyone wiring a knowledge graph into a clinical model, or handing one to an agent as background knowledge, the takeaway is concrete. Check how well the graph covers your disease before you start. Scope it to your data. And treat it as a transformation of the signal you already have, not as a source of new signal, until an experiment shows otherwise.
