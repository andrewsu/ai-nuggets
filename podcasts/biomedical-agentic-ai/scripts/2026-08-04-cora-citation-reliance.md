# CORA — an agentic clinical retrieval assistant shows a citation persuades physicians even when the model is wrong

Paper link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.00817

## Script

Today's pick lands squarely on a question anyone building grounded biomedical agents should worry about: does showing an LLM's sources make clinicians safer, or just more persuadable? The system is called CORA, a Citation-Oriented Retrieval Assistant, and it's a textbook agentic RAG loop. For each clinical question it plans a search, pulls evidence from a vector database of guidelines, textbooks, and case reports, judges whether what it retrieved is actually sufficient, reformulates and searches again if there's a gap, and only then answers with linked citations. Nothing exotic in the architecture. What makes this worth five minutes is that they didn't stop at benchmark numbers. They put it in front of forty-six practicing dermatologists from twenty-one countries and watched what the citations did to human decisions.

First the benchmark story, because it sets up the twist. Across five backbone models, adding retrieval never hurt accuracy and usually helped, but the size of the help scaled inversely with how good the base model already was. GPT-5, already near ceiling, barely moved. The weakest model, Gemma 3, jumped almost nine points on standard questions, and twenty-three points on a contamination-resistant set of case reports published after every model's training cutoff. So retrieval is mostly rescuing weaker models on rare and unseen cases. Genuinely useful, but not the headline.

The headline is the reader study. Each physician answered a question cold, then saw CORA's answer with its cited sources, rated whether those sources actually supported the answer, and could revise. Average accuracy climbed from about seventy-one percent unaided to eighty-three percent with CORA. Good. But that average hides two behaviors with opposite safety meaning. Assistance corrected a hundred and ten wrong answers, and it also flipped twenty-three initially correct answers into wrong ones. The net is positive; the harmful reversals are real.

Now the finding that should make you sit up. They separated adoption of correct advice from resistance to incorrect advice, and stratified both by whether the physician perceived the citation as supporting the answer. When CORA was right, a citation that looked supportive raised adoption of its correct advice from thirty-four percent to seventy-seven percent. Great. But when CORA was wrong, a citation that looked supportive dropped physician resistance from ninety-two percent all the way to thirty-five percent. Read that again. When a doctor judged that a source backed an incorrect answer, they abandoned their own correct answer in fifteen of twenty-three cases. The exact same grounding signal that drives good reliance also drives deference to wrong answers.

That's the whole point, and it's why this matters well beyond dermatology. We tend to sell source-linking and grounding as the safety layer for medical LLMs, the antidote to fluent hallucination. This study shows the citation is persuasive roughly independent of whether the answer is correct. And crucially, the cited sources often didn't hold up: only about sixty percent of individual citations were rated as genuinely supportive. So the model retrieves something plausible, attaches it to a wrong answer, the citation reads as support, and the human's skepticism collapses at exactly the wrong moment.

The lesson for agentic biomedical systems is sharp. Citation presence is not calibration. An agent that can retrieve and attach a source is not the same as an agent whose answer is actually entailed by that source, and the gap between those two is where the danger lives. If you're building grounded RAG or knowledge-graph-backed agents for research or the clinic, the retrieval loop is the easy half. The hard, unshipped half is answer-citation faithfulness: automatically checking that the claim is supported by the evidence it cites, not merely accompanied by it. Until that's solved, more grounding can mean more confident errors, not fewer.

One caveat worth naming. This is dermatology question-answering with a within-subjects design, and the CORA answers in the reader study came from an open-weight model, Llama-4 Scout, not the strongest backbone. A better generator might produce fewer wrong-but-cited answers. But that cuts both ways, because the failure mode is about how humans read citations, and that doesn't get safer just because the model gets better. If anything, a more fluent, better-cited wrong answer is more dangerous, not less.

So: agentic retrieval works, grounding helps on average, and the citation is a double-edged instrument. Build the faithfulness check, or you're shipping persuasion, not evidence.
