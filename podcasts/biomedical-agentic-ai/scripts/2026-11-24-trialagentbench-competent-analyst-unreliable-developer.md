# TrialAgentBench — agents now analyse a messy clinical trial about as well as a statistician, then lose most of a drug programme's value before the final decision

Paper link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.30916

## Script

Here is a problem every clinical-trial benchmark quietly has. A real trial never tells you the true effect of the drug. Each patient is seen on one arm only, so the best you can do is grade an agent against the published analysis, flaws included, or against a list of methods a statistician would accept. Today's paper, from David and Lei Clifton's groups at Oxford, takes the approach statisticians have always used to test their own methods: simulate the world, so the right answer is known before any data exist. Then hand the agents everything a real sponsor's statistician would get, messy records included, and score them against the truth.

The benchmark is called TrialAgentBench, and it has two halves. In the first, the agent plays trial statistician. It gets the protocol, a data dictionary and raw, linked site tables with dropout, treatment switching, unit changes, re-sent data and recording errors that only show up when you check site records against an audit. It has to build the analysis dataset, pick a method without being told which assumptions hold, and save its code. And here is the clever part: the score isn't the one number it produced. The authors rerun the saved script on thirty fresh replication trials from the same simulated population, so a lucky estimate can't pass for a good analysis.

In the second half, the agent runs a drug company. It gets three or five candidates it knows nothing about, a budget up to about three hundred million dollars, six years, and a brief that spells out study costs, the approval rule, and what each unit of benefit and harm is worth. It buys safety, exploration and confirmation studies out of a fixed simulated pool of sixty-four thousand participants, watches results come in, and eventually stops or advances one drug at one dose. Because every participant has an outcome under every drug, you can replay any programme and ask what a different choice would have earned.

The first result is good news, and it came fast. Models from August 2025 were about twenty percent worse than a reference analysis that knows how each trial was set up, and they badly missed about one task in six. The top five models released since July, Anthropic's and OpenAI's current flagships among them, are now within three and a half to six percent of that reference, working from raw records, with serious misses on almost no tasks and ninety-five percent intervals that cover the truth about ninety-five percent of the time. Nearly all the remaining gap comes down to one habit. When site records under-count the outcome more in one arm than the other, you need an audit-based correction. When the errors are equal, the correction only adds noise. The top models correct anyway, on eighty-three percent of the tasks where it isn't needed. That's an over-cautious default, and a sensible one, since skipping a needed correction costs far more.

There's a side result here that matters for how we grade agents. When the authors scored the same answers by whether the method was on an accepted list, twenty-eight percent of answers marked wrong were as accurate as the reference analysis. One model ranked fourteenth by that rubric and second against the truth. A rubric of approved methods rewards doing the expected thing, not getting the answer right.

Then the agents try to develop a drug, and that's where it falls apart. A simple rule-based developer, using only public information and a prior built from Cochrane effect sizes, captures fifty-eight percent of what an all-knowing oracle earns. The best agent gets forty-eight percent. The rest land twenty-four to sixty-six points below the rule. Three models that are statistically tied as analysts span fourteen to forty-eight percent as developers. Reading a trial well and deciding well are different skills.

Because every programme can be replayed, the authors can say where the value goes, and most of it goes early. Replace the agents' final call with a fixed rule and they recover half a point. Most of the loss happens during exploration and confirmation, in which studies they buy and which drugs they drop. Agents spend about forty percent more on studies than the rule, yet ninety-three of the hundred sixty-five failed approvals had fewer than three hundred treated participants completing the trial, a minimum printed in every brief. The costliest mistake is abandoning a drug that would have worked, because a stop is final while a shaky advance still has to get through review. And the best agent's characteristic failure is subtle. It favors the intensified dose, reads the short study almost correctly, and then assumes the benefit and harm stay where they were at the end of follow-up. They don't. It estimated a hundred seventy-nine million dollars a year in net value for those regimens. The truth at the horizon in the brief was seventy-six million. Seventeen of its ninety-one approved drugs lost money, and for sixteen of those its own estimate had predicted a profit.

Two caveats. This is a simulator with its own disease models, and the rule-based developer was built with that world's structure in mind, so treat the fifty-eight percent as a strong reference, not a human baseline. And the hundred-turn limit leaves some programmes unfinished, which the authors count separately.

My take: this is the right way to evaluate agents for drug development. Score against a known truth, not a published answer, and score the decision, not just the analysis. The finding is a useful map of where people should stay in the loop: study design, harm valued at the right time horizon, and a second look before anyone kills a programme. Clinical reasoning on a single trial looks close to solved. Planning across a programme isn't. The tasks, harness, simulator and an independent verifier are all public, so the next agent that claims to run a drug programme has a test it can't argue with.
