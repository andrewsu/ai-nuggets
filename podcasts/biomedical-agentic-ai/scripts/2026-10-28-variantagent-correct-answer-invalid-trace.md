# VariantAgent — post-GWAS interpretation where the trace is part of the evidence

Source: bioRxiv 10.64898/2026.10.23.761278v1, posted 27 Oct 2026. Guangzhou Medical University / Guangzhou National Laboratory / Shanghai AI Laboratory.

## Script

Today's pick went up on bioRxiv yesterday, from a collaboration between Guangzhou Medical University, the Guangzhou National Laboratory, and the Shanghai AI Laboratory. The system is called Post-GWAS Intelligence, and the agent inside it is VariantAgent. The framing is one that should land hard on anyone who thinks about biomedical data integration, because it names an asymmetry we have all been living with and mostly stopped noticing.

Genetic association discovery became cumulative twenty-five years ago. A shared coordinate system, shared summary statistics, a catalogue now holding more than a million curated variant-trait associations. Interpretation of those associations never became cumulative at all. Fine-mapping, molecular QTLs, chromatin maps, sequence models, perturbation screens — the work that turns a locus into a mechanism — gets done once, per study, by specialists, and then compressed into prose and scattered across supplementary files. Every group rebuilds it. Nothing accrues.

So the proposal here is not another pipeline. It's a representation. Every tool-derived result the agent produces gets written into a standardized, variant-centred evidence unit, and each unit carries its evidential status explicitly: is this a statistical association, a source-backed molecular relationship, a computational prediction, or a candidate nominated by a knowledge graph? Those four categories are never collapsed into each other. The trait-level report is linked to the units but kept structurally separate from them, so a fluent narrative can't quietly launder a prediction into a measurement. That separation is the whole idea, and I think it's the right one.

Now the result I actually want you to take away, because it's the sharpest thing in the paper and it generalizes well beyond genetics. They built a benchmark of twenty-six questions that require real analysis of supplied GWAS summary statistics, and then they scored it twice. First the ordinary way: did you get the right answer. VariantAgent got sixty-five percent, Biomni fifty-four, a bare Claude Code setup forty-six. A respectable but unremarkable spread. Then they audited the execution traces and asked a second question: was the answer actually produced by a valid analysis? Correct genome build, correct allele orientation, ancestry-matched linkage disequilibrium, the required method actually run on the intended variant set. Process-validated accuracy: sixty-one percent for VariantAgent, thirty-one for Biomni, under eight percent for bare Claude Code. Look at it from the other direction and it's even starker — among the answers that were correct, ninety-four percent of VariantAgent's had a valid trace, fifty-seven percent of Biomni's, and seventeen percent of the bare agent's.

Sit with that last number. Five out of six times the bare harness got the right answer, it got there by a route that a blinded geneticist would not accept. In genetics specifically this is not a hypothetical hazard, because the published answer is in the training data and the agent can simply recall it while performing analysis theatre on the side. But the general lesson is that for a computational claim, the trace isn't documentation of the evidence. It is part of the evidence. An agent benchmark that only scores final answers cannot see the difference between a method and a plausible guess, and the gap it's blind to here is a factor of eight.

The second finding is about where automated reanalysis actually adds anything. They re-ran thirty-four published GWAS and compared domain by domain. Independent signals went up three and a half fold, prioritized variants under five fold — modest, as you'd expect, since association mapping is the part the field already does uniformly. But prioritized genes went up fifty-eight fold, mechanistic hypotheses thirty-five fold, perturbation evidence eighty fold. The authors are careful, and I'll be careful with them: that is not eighty times more validated biology. It's eighty times more interrogation of an evidence space that published studies sample thinly and selectively, because no one has the person-hours to do it exhaustively per locus. The expansion is the diagnosis, not the achievement.

And then they close the loop, which is the part I found genuinely new. Run it over a thousand and forty-one GWAS, get half a million evidence units across three hundred and seventy traits, and now query the accumulated catalogue for regions that look shared across traits but resolve to different fine-mapped variants. That surfaced twenty-seven hundred candidates, filtered to fifty-nine worth formal evaluation. One of them, on the long arm of chromosome one, carried associations for both type 1 diabetes and arm fat-free mass. The agent went back to the original summary statistics, ran colocalization across twenty-one sensitivity configurations, and every one of them said distinct signals, not shared — posterior probability for two independent signals above point nine nine nine. So the accumulated interpretations generated a statistical question that the raw associations alone never posed, and then the same system answered it against the primary data. That's the cumulative logic working.

Two honest caveats. Recovery of published findings averaged eighty-two percent, which means roughly one finding in five from the source papers was not reproduced — and in the type 1 diabetes deep dive, the agent recovered only three of twelve manually encoded evidence paths while generating six hundred and eight of its own. Different coverage, not strictly better coverage. And the runtime here is Claude Code with forty-plus hand-written domain skills and a scenario library on top; ablating the scenario guidance and the workflow-level audit drops the score from eighty-eight to eighty. The agent isn't the model. It's the methodology someone encoded around it.
