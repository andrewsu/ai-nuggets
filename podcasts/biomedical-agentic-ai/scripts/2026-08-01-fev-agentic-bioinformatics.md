# FEV — grade agentic bioinformatics on the workflow trace, not the final answer

Paper link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.27556

## Script

Today's paper is a survey with an argument, and the argument is the interesting part. It's called FEV — Function, Evidence, Validation — from a group at the University of Alabama at Birmingham, and it's a systematic map of the whole agentic bioinformatics landscape: 109 agent or agent-adjacent systems plus 28 benchmarks, 128 publications in all, spanning genomics, single-cell and spatial omics, protein science, drug discovery, and computational pathology. If you build or use language-model agents for biological analysis, this is a mirror held up to the field.

The core move is a reframing. When we evaluate one of these agents today, we tend to look at two things: does the final answer look right, and does it pass a benchmark. The authors argue both are the wrong unit of analysis. Two systems with identical architectures can offer completely different levels of scientific assurance, and two systems with totally different architectures can expose the same quality of workflow trace. So the thing worth grading is not the model, not the agent count, not the final answer — it's the inspectable trajectory from the biological question to the evidence-supported conclusion. Grade the trace, not the answer.

FEV breaks that trace into three independent axes. The first is Function: which workflow operations the system actually demonstrates — planning and task decomposition, role specialization, tool selection, capturing and maintaining workflow state, self-evaluation and repair, and knowing when to escalate to a human. The second is Evidence: what traceable sources actually back its actions and claims — the scientific literature, structured biological knowledge like knowledge graphs and ontologies, primary biological data, software and statistical outputs, model predictions, and real experimental or clinical observations. The third, and the one with teeth, is Validation: five cumulative stages of assurance. Stage zero is an illustrative demo. Stage one means it runs. Stage two means the computation is actually replayable — you have the inputs, parameters, dependencies, intermediate artifacts, and traces to reconstruct it. Stage three means it's been scientifically evaluated, usually a benchmark plus some robustness analysis. And stage four means prospective empirical testing — the agent's output was actually taken into the lab or the clinic and checked against reality.

Here's the finding that should sting a little. When they place all 128 systems on this scale, the field bunches up hard at stage three. Most systems have run a benchmark and called it a day. Only seven of them reach stage four — actual prospective validation — and most of those seven are in protein engineering, where you can synthesize a designed sequence and just measure whether it folds and works. Closed-loop refinement, where a wet-lab result feeds back to improve the agent, is described as exceptional. And the evidence axis has the same hole: agents lean on the literature, structured knowledge, and retrospective data, but grounding a claim in a fresh experimental or clinical observation is rare.

The sharpest line in the paper is that broader operational scope does not imply stronger scientific assurance. Bolting on more agents, more tools, more databases, more memory modules makes the system do more — it moves you along the Function axis — but it does nothing for whether the workflow is replayable, verified, or empirically true. Capability has been racing ahead of accountability. Planning and tool execution are mature; provenance, replayability, external validation, and prospective testing are lagging badly.

Why does this matter beyond scoring other people's papers? Because a bioinformatics workflow can execute flawlessly and still be scientifically wrong — the wrong normalization, the wrong sample grouping, an authoritative but irrelevant reference pulled in with total confidence. A green checkmark on "the code ran" tells you nothing about whether the biology is right. FEV is, in effect, an argument that we've been building agents that optimize for the part that's easy to measure — fluent output, successful tool calls, benchmark numbers — and neglecting the part that actually establishes scientific credibility, which is the auditable trail underneath.

My take: as a survey, this is most useful as a checklist. If you're building an agentic system, the honest question isn't "did it get the answer" — it's "what's my validation stage, and can someone else replay this?" The uncomfortable answer, for almost the entire field right now, is stage three — we ran a benchmark. The paper doesn't offer a magic fix; its contribution is a shared vocabulary that makes the gap legible, so that "we tested it prospectively and the wet lab agreed" stops being a rare footnote and starts being the bar. For a field moving as fast as agentic bioinformatics, a common language for what counts as evidence, and what counts as merely running, is worth more than another leaderboard.
