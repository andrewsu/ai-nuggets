# PFArena — the leaderboard inverts, and the agent advantage turns out to be arithmetic

Paper link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.28921

## Script

Almost every benchmark for language models in protein science asks the same question: can the model score a list of mutations somebody already gave it? That's a measurement. It isn't a decision. A benchmark that went up on arXiv on Friday, from Shanghai AI Laboratory with three groups at Tsinghua — their generative symbolic intelligence lab, the Institute for AI Industry Research, and the School of Pharmaceutical Sciences — reorganizes the whole evaluation around the decision a protein engineer actually faces, and it is the most useful thing I've read this month about when to reach for an agent.

The benchmark is called PFArena, and the design move is to vary exactly one thing: how much of your own measured data you get to hand the model. There are four task interfaces. The first is open-ended single-mutant generation — nothing but the wild type and the assay description, now propose forty substitutions. The other three are ranking a supplied library of multi-mutants, with progressively more evidence: no measurements at all, then one measured anchor mutant, then the full table of measured single-mutant effects. Two hundred and two assays, two hundred ninety-three task instances, assembled from ProteinGym, MegaScale, MaveDB, FLAb, the Human Domainome and CombinGym. Against that they ran six protein language models, six frontier general-purpose models, and five agent configurations — the Biomni framework plus a lightweight in-house toolkit, each on a couple of frontier backbones, with up to forty tool-use rounds.

The headline is that the leaderboard inverts end to end. On open-ended generation, protein language models win: VenusREM on top, ProSST second, and the bare frontier models sit at the very bottom, below ESM-two. On the task where measured single-mutant effects are supplied, that is exactly reversed. Agents take the top four slots; the family mean for agents is zero point six three, for bare language models zero point five four, and for protein language models zero point three five. VenusREM, which won the first task, lands twelfth.

Stop there and you'd conclude that agents beat specialist models once there's data in the room. The authors don't stop there, and this is the part that earns the paper its place. They asked a purely mechanical question: how much of a multi-mutant's fitness is just the sum of its component single-mutant fitnesses? For every assay they correlated that sum against the true combination fitness — a transferability diagnostic with no model in it at all. Then they correlated that diagnostic against each family's performance. For the general-purpose language models the correlation is zero point seven three. For agents, zero point six five. For protein language models, zero point three eight.

So the frontier models' dominance tracks, nearly one to one, how well plain addition would have worked. And on the seven assays out of twenty-nine where additivity genuinely fails — where component effects don't transfer — bare language model performance collapses to zero point zero one five. Essentially nothing. Protein language models hold at zero point two four, agents at zero point three one. The win isn't protein understanding. It's arithmetic, executed fluently over evidence you supplied, and it evaporates in precisely the epistatic regime that was the reason you wanted a model in the first place.

The agent result deserves a separate note, because it's a different kind of win. Agents also beat bare language models on the open-ended task — and the reason is that their tool belt contains protein language models. The agent's edge there is routing: it knows to call the specialist. And in the non-additive regime it degrades more gracefully than its own backbone does, presumably for the same reason. Which suggests the honest way to read "agent" in this paper is not as a capability tier sitting above language models, but as a mechanism for deciding which of the other two families to trust for the question in front of you.

Now the sobering number, and it applies to everyone. A typical assay in the generation task has around three thousand legal single substitutions; the model proposes forty, about one and a third percent of the space. The average number of verified hits inside those forty is one point two one. The best single case recovered thirteen. The authors say plainly that this only marginally beats random sampling, and they're right. And across all three ranking tasks everything falls apart past two substitutions — roughly zero point three to zero point four on doubles, down to zero point one to zero point two beyond that. Nobody here can search sequence space, and nobody here handles depth.

Two things partially help, and both are about the loop rather than the model. Splitting that same forty-mutation budget into four rounds and feeding the measured fitness back between rounds improved every model monotonically — and three of the four were below random sampling in single-shot mode, while all of them cleared it by round four. Sampling sixteen times and fusing the rankings also helped, but a large oracle gap remains: the best of those sixteen is about twice as good as any aggregation of them, so good answers are being generated and then not recognized.

One detail worth flagging if you benchmark in this area. Several proprietary models refused a subset of assays on safety grounds, and rather than quietly dropping those cases the authors substituted a random baseline. That's the right call for measuring deployed usability, but it means a frontier model's protein-engineering score now carries its provider's biosecurity policy as a term inside it.

Their own caveats: everything is retrospective, every configuration was run once, and the assays are public, so contamination can't be excluded. Hold the absolute numbers loosely. Hold the crossover, and that additivity diagnostic, tightly.
