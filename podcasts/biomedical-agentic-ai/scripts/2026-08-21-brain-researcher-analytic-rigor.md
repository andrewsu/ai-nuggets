# Brain Researcher — an agentic harness that makes a neuroimaging claim earn its scope

Paper link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19902

## Script

Today's pick comes out of Russ Poldrack's group at Stanford, with collaborators at Georgia State, UT Austin, and Queensland, and there's a useful irony in the authorship. Poldrack co-authored the study where seventy independent teams analyzed the same functional MRI dataset, no two of them used the same workflow, and their conclusions diverged. Now his lab is shipping an agent that runs neuroimaging analyses. The premise of the paper is that those two facts are the same problem. If seventy trained humans can't converge on one dataset, then an agent that executes a pipeline flawlessly has established nothing. Running an analysis and establishing a claim are different acts, and almost every scientific-agent paper conflates them.

The system is called Brain Researcher, and the unit it operates on is not an answer. It's a governed research trajectory.

Here's the shape of it. Before anything runs, the researcher writes a commitment card: the question, the analyses considered admissible, the checks that must pass, the criteria for success and for failure. That card is sealed with a content hash, so any later edit to the plan is detectable. It's preregistration with a tamper seal, enforced by the same software that will do the work. Then the agent operates inside the researcher's own computational environment, calling version-pinned neuroimaging containers through a Model Context Protocol server, and every proposed tool call gets tested against machine-readable validation rules before it executes. Afterward, a review layer writes a claim card and assigns the result one of six states: accepted, qualified, revised, blocked, rejected, or deferred.

Underneath sits a piece that will interest anyone who builds biomedical knowledge graphs. Their graph is about seven hundred forty-six thousand nodes and two and a half million edges, pulling together neuroimaging ontologies, data repositories, and literature, all normalized to the OpenNeuro Vocabulary so that a query resolves consistently across sources. The design choice worth stealing is this: source-backed facts carry explicit provenance — the source, and where available, the verbatim supporting quote and a grounding label. Coverage is partial, and the partiality is tracked rather than papered over. That's the difference between retrieval you can audit and retrieval you take on trust.

The benchmarks are strong, and the authors are clear-eyed about which one actually matters. Across seven frontier models and sixty tasks, first-action correct tool selection went from twenty-three percent without the harness to ninety-four percent with it. Seventy points, and every one of the seven models improved. But that's a routing result, and routing is the easy part. The harder number is evidence grounding — a cited source that an independent judge could locate and confirm actually supports the claim. That went from under five percent to twenty-two percent. Nearly a five-fold gain, and still a seventy-eight percent failure rate. They report it that way. Most of the misses were real but off-topic citations rather than fabrications, which is a meaningfully better failure mode, but grounding is not solved and the paper says so.

The part I'd put in front of every lab is the schizophrenia audit. A collaborator brought three prespecified hypotheses about functional network connectivity in a cohort of three hundred sixty-three people. Brain Researcher expanded that into a four-hundred-eighty-specification multiverse spanning connectivity estimators, confound choices, dimensionality reduction, classifiers, and how finely you carve up brain domains. The headline hypothesis — that between-domain connections show larger group differences than within-domain ones — came out favorable in twelve of twenty-four contrasts. Fifty percent, which sounds like noise. It isn't. Support was one hundred percent under Pearson and Spearman correlation and zero percent under partial correlation and mutual information. A clean partition by estimator family. The effect is real inside a measurement regime and absent outside it, and no single-specification analysis could have told you that. All three hypotheses were logged as qualified — supported within identifiable analytic subfamilies, not in general.

Then the governance lesson, which the authors volunteer rather than bury. Partway through, a server fault caused execution to fall back to a general-purpose coding agent, and that agent scored any specification with a permutation p-value under point-oh-five as favorable, ignoring sign, for a directional hypothesis. Apparent support went to nearly universal. The automated review layer did not catch it. A human did, by reading the code and the specification curve. Two checks were then added permanently: a directionality test requiring the statistic to match the hypothesized sign, and a warning whenever execution falls back to a general-purpose agent. That's the honest version of what this buys you. Not the elimination of expert inspection — the conversion of one caught error into an enforced rule every future run inherits.

Two other episodes ended with no claim at all. A cocaine-use-disorder study rejected all five prespecified brain-behavior associations, an exploratory sweep over seventy more combinations surfaced nothing surviving correction, and the system blocked promotion to confirmatory status and converted the null into a replication plan. A cross-cultural meta-analysis produced a tidy prefrontal story from subgroups of six to eight studies, against a recommended minimum of seventeen, and got blocked as exploratory. An agent whose most valuable output is "you don't get to say that yet" is close to a new product category.

The caveats are real. Most of this is same-dataset multiverse rather than independent replication, the public datasets may sit in the models' training data, and the review layer's error calibration is internal to the team. But the framing is the contribution. Once decisions and claim states persist as records, a qualified or negative result stops being a dead end and becomes the thing that narrows the next question. That's an infrastructure argument rather than a model argument, and it's the right one.
