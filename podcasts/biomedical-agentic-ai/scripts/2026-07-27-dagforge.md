# DAGForge — an agent that turns biomedical literature into auditable, evidence-linked causal graphs

Paper link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.21859

## Script

Almost every causal analysis in biomedicine starts with someone drawing a graph. A directed acyclic graph — nodes for your variables, arrows for what causes what. That diagram is where you commit your causal assumptions, and everything downstream, every adjustment for confounding, every effect estimate, rides on it. And it's almost always built by hand, which is slow, and — this is the part that actually bites — the finished graph gets divorced from the evidence that motivated it. Six months later nobody can tell you why there's an arrow from this exposure to that outcome. Was it a paper? A hunch? A reviewer's suggestion? The assumptions are frozen into the picture, but the reasoning is gone.

Today's pick is a system called DAGForge, out of a group at Mass General and Harvard, and it goes after exactly that gap. It's a browser-based agent that authors causal graphs as auditable, evidence-linked artifacts — meaning every single arrow carries its receipts.

Here's the design choice that makes it worth your attention, because it's the opposite of what you'd expect. DAGForge does not let the language model draw the graph. That's the whole trick. If you ask a general-purpose model to "give me a causal DAG for these variables," you get a confident answer you can't check and can't reproduce. DAGForge refuses to work that way. The model's only job is to make structured pairwise judgments — take two concepts at a time and answer a narrow question: is there a causal relationship here, which direction, what sign, how confident, and — critically — quote me the verbatim passage that supports it. Nothing else. The model reasons in small, checkable atoms.

Then a deterministic solver — plain code, not the model — assembles those atoms into a graph. It enforces the hard structural guarantees: no cycles, respect for temporal ordering, exogeneity flags, and any edges the user marked required or forbidden. Only after all of that does a tightly bounded model step in to resolve whatever ambiguity is left over. So the things a language model is bad at — global consistency, never contradicting itself, respecting that a cause can't come after its effect — are handled by machinery that literally cannot hallucinate. And the thing the model is genuinely good at — reading a sentence and judging what it claims — is fenced into exactly that role.

The evidence layer is just as deliberate. For each run, DAGForge freezes its own literature snapshot — it pulls a PubMed corpus, deduplicates it, and pins it, so the same run reproduces. Every edge comes with a card: the confidence, a provenance tag saying whether the claim came from the literature, from the UMLS ontology, from a temporal constraint, from the user, or from the solver, plus the citation and the exact quoted passage. You can also ask it, on demand, for the backdoor adjustment set — the variables you'd actually condition on. It's provenance-first from top to bottom.

Now the results, and there's a genuinely interesting wrinkle. On compact benchmark graphs it's near ceiling — pairwise skeleton F-one around ninety-four percent, directed F-one around ninety. On the harder cohort, graphs reconstructed from real published literature, directed F-one drops to about sixty-six percent, with recall around eighty-seven and precision around fifty-eight. So recall beats precision — it draws more arrows than the reference graph has. And the naive baseline, just asking the same model to draw the whole graph directly, actually matches those terse reference graphs a bit better on raw structure.

But look at what those "extra" edges are. Eighty-seven percent of DAGForge's so-called false positives — the arrows not in the reference graph — carry a verified verbatim excerpt from the literature. They're not hallucinations. They're real, cited relationships that the human-drawn reference graph left out for parsimony. The precision metric is punishing the system for finding genuine, sourced causal claims. That completely reframes the scoreboard.

And that's the take. The win here isn't a higher number — the baseline wins on the number. The win is a different contract with the user. An LLM that hands you a graph gives you an answer you have to trust. DAGForge hands you an argument you can audit, arrow by arrow, down to the sentence and the citation, with the graph's shape guaranteed by code instead of vibes. For anyone doing biomedical causal work, that's the difference between an oracle and a collaborator. Feed it a one-line study context and recall climbs to ninety percent — but the real product was never the graph. It's the evidence trail underneath it.
