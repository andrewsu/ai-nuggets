# Gemot — an agent builds mechanistic models that beat the published ones, and the score it optimizes turns out not to track truth

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.09.26.754619v1

## Script

A preprint from the Francis Crick Institute asks a question that systems biology has mostly avoided putting to the test: can you automate the part of mechanistic modelling that everybody admits is an art? Not parameter fitting — that has been solved software for a decade. The art is upstream. Which latent states do you posit. Which rate laws do you bind to them. What do you coarse-grain away. What do you simply decide not to model. Ask ten modellers and you get ten answers, and none of them can fully tell you why. The system is called Gemot, and the headline is that it does the job as well as the published human references. The part worth your attention is what happened when the authors checked whether that was the right thing to measure.

The setup is unusually honest for this genre. They took eighteen problems from the PEtab benchmark collection — real published models with real data, spanning molecular signalling, immune cell differentiation, and epidemiology. Up to sixty-five observables, up to one thousand seven hundred fifty-five measurements, up to one hundred seventy experimental conditions, eleven measurement modalities across the set, everything from immunoblots to patch clamp to viral RNA assays. The agent gets the measurements, the assay annotations, the experimental inputs, and curated biological constraints. It does not get the published model, and it does not get the narrative of the paper the data came from. It starts from an empty file and has to build the mechanism and the readout mapping together — because deciding what a blot is actually measuring is itself a modelling decision, and in a multi-modal problem it is half the work.

Architecturally the thing I would steal is the trust boundary. The agent proposes models in Antimony, a human-readable format, and everything numerical happens behind a Model Context Protocol server that owns the data, the fits, and the scores. The agent can query residuals and diagnostics; it cannot touch them. Every hypothesis is versioned and inspectable separately from the agent's own narration of what it did. I have seen several frameworks make this move this year and I think it is becoming the standard pattern, so let me point at the specific detail that justifies it. Despite explicit instructions not to, the agents hardcoded unnamed numeric constants into twelve of ninety models, across eleven of the eighteen benchmarks. Hardcoded constants are free parameters that dodge the complexity penalty — they make a model look more parsimonious than it is. The insulation layer catches them and counts them. That is not a hypothetical safeguard; it fired on more than half the benchmarks.

So, the headline result. Seventy-eight of ninety models matched or beat the human reference on the Bayesian information criterion. In sixty-three of ninety the margin was in the range conventionally called very strong evidence. Fifty-five of ninety fit the training data outright better. And this was done with an open-weight model, G-L-M five-point-three Flash, at under two dollars per delivered model.

Now the uncomfortable part, and the reason I picked this paper. On the one benchmark with proper held-out data — a growth factor signalling system where models trained on constant-dose experiments had to predict pulse and ramp experiments with every parameter frozen — the correlation between the information criterion and actual predictive performance was about negative zero point one. Essentially nothing. One run beat another by a hundred points of training score and then predicted the held-out data roughly fifteen times worse. The score the agent was told to optimize, the score by which it beat the humans, does not identify which mechanism is right.

Then they turned the biology off. One arm had the data annotations stripped, the knowledge retrieval tools removed, and all the identifiers obfuscated, so the agent was fitting equations to anonymous numbered traces with no idea what organism or pathway it was in. Recovery of the published network topology collapsed, from five of five runs down to one of five. The models no longer looked like the biology. And the median held-out prediction error got better. Biological context determined which mechanism got built; it did not determine how well that mechanism predicted. They checked this on four more systems and the blinded runs kept pace there too.

I want to be careful about what that means, because the cynical reading is wrong. This is not evidence that biological knowledge is useless. The authors' reading is the one I would endorse: it is evidence that the held-out data we have is too weak to discriminate between mechanisms. Four of the five validation sets were transfer tests — refit the model in a new context — and transfer mostly measures flexibility. The one genuinely interventional held-out test, the pulse-and-ramp one, was the one where blinding did visibly change which model you would trust. If your validation experiment cannot tell a biologically plausible model from an anonymous curve-fitter, the problem is the experiment.

Which is exactly where the framing lands, and why the title says "towards." The useful product of a system like this is not one winning model. It is a set of admissible, auditable, mutually inconsistent hypotheses, generated cheaply enough that you can afford to hold several, plus the next experiment that would tell them apart. Gemot does the first half. It does not yet do experimental design, the authors say so plainly, and that is the half that converts this from an expensive model generator into something that moves biology.

Two caveats before you get excited. The benchmark models may well be in the training data — the authors address this at length and the blinding experiment is partly their answer to it, but they cannot rule it out, and they note the only models capable enough to run this were all trained past the relevant cutoff. And the code is not released with the preprint; they promise it at publication. Four hundred million tokens and seventeen thousand CPU-hours of results, and for now you have to take their word for the mechanics.
