# ANCHOR-RE — a training-free neuro-symbolic agent that stops LLMs from inventing biomedical relations

Paper link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.03154

## Script

If you build biomedical knowledge graphs, you already know the dirtiest problem in the pipeline: relation extraction. You point a system at the literature, ask it which entities are actually related, and it hands you edges. The catch is that most candidate pairs in real text aren't related to each other at all. In the datasets used here, unrelated pairs make up sixty-eight to eighty-three percent of the candidates. So the whole game is precision — knowing when to say "no relation" — and that is exactly where large language models fall down. Give an LLM two biomedical entities in the same sentence and it will confidently narrate a relationship between them, whether or not the sentence supports one. That's over-prediction, and it's poison for a knowledge graph, because a single hallucinated edge propagates into every downstream analysis that trusts the graph.

Today's paper is called ANCHOR-RE, out of Illinois and Minnesota, from a group with deep roots in SemRep-style symbolic biomedical text mining — which makes the framing pointed, because this is that same community trying to fuse their old symbolic tools with modern LLMs. It's an agentic, neuro-symbolic framework, and the thing I like most about it is that it requires no fine-tuning at all. Everything happens at inference time.

Here's the intuition. They split the work into two roles. The first is a Decider — an LLM that proposes candidate relations, but not naked. Its prompt is grounded: it gets structured evidence pulled from real knowledge bases — UMLS for general biomedical relations, DrugBank for drug-drug interactions — plus semantic-type constraints from the UMLS Semantic Network that rule out biologically implausible pairings before the model ever reasons. So the Decider is doing LLM-style contextual reasoning, but fenced in by ontology.

The second role is where it gets genuinely clever. It's a Verifier, and it only wakes up when the Decider says "yes, there's a relation here." Its job is to second-guess that yes. But it isn't checking against hand-written rules — the way classic symbolic systems like SemRep do, with curated triggers and constraints. Instead, offline, they run the frozen Decider over training data and collect its systematic false positives — the cases where it claimed a relation that the gold labels say doesn't exist. Then they hand those failures to another LLM and ask it to induce reusable verification rules, phrased as structured questions, that capture the recurring ways the Decider fools itself. That becomes an Error Pattern Knowledge Bank, one set of rules per relation type. At inference, when the Decider fires, the Verifier pulls up the rules for that relation and asks: does any of these rejection patterns apply? If one triggers, the prediction gets flipped back to "no relation."

So the model learns from its own error distribution instead of from a human writing constraints by hand. That's the move worth sitting with. The false positives it makes are structured and repeatable — things like a causal trigger word that superficially matches a relation definition, even though the actual causal effect is mediated by a third entity. The Verifier learns to recognize those shapes and veto them.

Does it work? The gains are driven almost entirely by cutting false positives, exactly as designed. On drug-drug interactions, micro F-one jumps from about 0.77 to 0.87. On SemRepGS it's a more modest bump, from 0.65 to about 0.68 — and the honest reason is instructive. The verification helps everywhere, but the knowledge-base grounding only helps where the knowledge base actually has coverage. For drug interactions, DrugBank gives direct evidence for over half the test pairs. For the broader UMLS relations, only about seven percent of pairs have explicit pair-level evidence in the knowledge base, so grounding has little to grab onto and the Verifier is doing most of the work. That's a clean, useful finding: symbolic grounding is not a magic additive, it pays off in proportion to how well your ontology covers the relations you're actually extracting.

Two more things earn my trust. First, it isn't a GPT trick — they reproduce the same pattern of gains on open-weight Qwen models, so the benefit lives in the framework, not the backbone. Second, they ran a contamination-controlled test: a hundred biomedical articles published in 2026, after the model's training cutoff, so the LLM couldn't be leaking memorized benchmark answers. On five hundred sampled predictions from that set, experts judged sixty-nine percent correct — precision holding up on genuinely unseen literature.

The limitations are real and the authors are upfront about them. The Verifier is one-sided: it only rejects, so it can shave false positives but it can't tell treats from prevents, or coexists-with from associated-with — the hard job of disambiguating similar positive relations is untouched. And training-free doesn't mean cheap — running a Decider plus a Verifier at inference costs more than a supervised model that's already been trained, though you save the annotation and fine-tuning entirely.

For anyone constructing a biomedical knowledge graph from the literature, the takeaway is that reliability here didn't come from a bigger model or more labels. It came from architecture — letting an agent watch where the LLM systematically over-commits, distilling that into soft symbolic constraints, and firing a verifier only at the moment of risk. That's a template that generalizes well beyond relation extraction.
