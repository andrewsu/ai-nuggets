# GUIA — an agent-to-agent network that lets rival biomedical agents work the same case

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.08.13.744542v1

## Script

Count the specialized biomedical research agents this field has shipped. CRISPR-GPT for gene editing design. ChemCrow for organic synthesis. Pathology-CoT for whole-slide images. GeneAgent, Eubiota for the gut microbiome, Biomni, and a dozen more. Every one of them is a separate system with its own framework, its own model, its own execution environment, and no way to talk to any of the others. We have built a field of silos and called it progress.

A preprint posted to bioRxiv this week, from Edwin Cheung's cancer group at the University of Macau, is the first serious attempt I've seen to treat that fragmentation as the problem rather than shipping agent number eleven. The system is called GUIA, and the interesting thing about it is not what it can do. It's what it lets other people's agents do together.

The mechanism is deliberately unglamorous. Each specialized agent sits behind its own HTTP endpoint and speaks Google's Agent-to-Agent protocol, exchanging JSON. A central orchestrator decomposes a research question into subtasks and delegates each one to whichever agent claims the relevant expertise. There's no tight integration — no shared codebase, no shared model, no shared runtime. If you've been watching the tooling layer, this is the move that the Model Context Protocol made for tools, pushed up one level to agents. MCP standardized how one agent reaches a database. Agent-to-Agent standardizes how one agent hands work to another agent it didn't write and can't inspect.

So does the interoperability actually buy anything? Here's the number that carries the paper. On microbiome tasks about how drugs affect microbial growth, GUIA on its own scored forty-five percent. The bare underlying model scored thirty-five. A competing multi-agent system with a fixed roster scored thirty-four. Then they plugged in Eubiota — somebody else's four-agent microbiome platform, unmodified — and accuracy went to ninety-three percent. Not a few points. Double. That's capability accumulating across a system boundary, and it's the cleanest demonstration of the thesis anywhere in the paper.

Now hold that against the weakest result, because the pair is where the real lesson lives. They also tested cross-server collaboration by giving GUIA access to a clinical agent running on a physically separate server, and measured medical question answering. The improvement was, in their own word, subtle. Why the gap? Because GUIA was already strong at medical questions and hopeless at microbiome analysis. The payoff from interoperability is proportional to how non-overlapping the expertise is. And the authors say plainly that they chose agents with non-overlapping domains in order to keep task allocation stable — which is a quiet admission that when two agents both plausibly claim a task, routing gets worse. Nobody has solved agent selection under overlap. In any real open network, overlap is the normal case, not the exception.

The validation work is genuinely good, and I say that as somebody tired of benchmark-only agent papers. They handed GUIA in-house mass spectrometry data from androgen receptor pulldowns across three prostate cancer cell lines and asked it to find actionable coregulators. It designed and ran the whole pipeline itself: filter the proteomics for hits enriched across cell lines, pull prostate transcriptomes from the Cancer Genome Atlas, keep genes up in both layers, check disease-free survival, mine PubMed, and prioritize. It came back with CASZ1. Then the humans went to the bench. Knocking down CASZ1 in prostate cancer cells suppressed androgen-receptor-driven genes, chromatin immunoprecipitation put CASZ1 directly on androgen receptor binding sites, and proliferation dropped. That's a hypothesis generated by the network and confirmed experimentally. Separately, the borrowed pathology agent looked at a prostate specimen and called small-cell neuroendocrine carcinoma — under one percent of prostate cancers — and synaptophysin staining confirmed it.

Two things bother me here, and they sit at different levels.

Start with the paper itself, which is a manuscript whose entire argument is that fragmentation and gatekeeping hold science back. Every third-party agent they integrated is public on GitHub, which is precisely why they were able to integrate them at all. GUIA itself is available by filling out a registration form at a link-shortened URL. The protocol underneath is open, so the idea travels regardless. But you cannot join the network this paper describes, and that makes it an argument for exactly the thing it declines to do.

The deeper problem belongs to the field. Agents on different servers in GUIA can exchange structured messages but cannot share files. That isn't a footnote. The hard part of distributed, cross-institutional science has always been moving and governing the data, not moving the instructions. A network that can pass a question across an institutional boundary but not a dataset has solved the easy half. And the architecture is a star — one orchestrator, spokes radiating out — which is fine for one lab running two servers, and is precisely the topology that fails when you try to federate across institutions with no willing central hub.

Even so, I'd rather have this paper than another domain agent. The right unit of progress here is the interface, not the agent, and somebody finally built the interface and showed that it pays.
