# Pyrite — a pose-ranking benchmark where expert chemists land below chance, and the language model does best as a feature engineer rather than a reasoner

Paper link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14511

## Script

Today's paper is a quiet demolition of two assumptions at once, and it comes from a collaboration between Stanford and Genesis Molecular AI. The task is pose ranking. A cofolding model takes a protein and a small molecule and predicts how the molecule sits in the pocket. Modern ones will happily hand you a hundred plausible binding modes. Only one of them is right, and at the moment you need to choose, you don't have the crystal structure. Everything downstream — which compound you make, which series you abandon — rides on picking well.

So the authors built a benchmark. They sampled up to a hundred and twenty poses per complex from their own cofolding model, kept the top twenty-five by a binding-energy score, and paired them up. Every pair contains at least one genuinely good pose, where good means within one angstrom of the crystal with high local interaction agreement. The opponent is either a near-miss or a clear miss. After filtering, fifteen hundred pairs across two hundred and eighty-five structures. The question for any ranker is simply: which of these two is closer to the truth?

Then they ran five expert chemists at it. Each had more than a decade in computer-aided drug design. They got interactive three-D views, two-D contact maps, downloadable structure files they could open in PyMOL, the protein family and function, a binding-energy estimate and a count of strained torsions. Two independent annotations per pair, fifteen hundred annotations in total.

The experts scored forty-two and forty-five percent. That is below chance. When you throw out the pairs where they said the two poses looked about the same and score only their confident calls, they climb to fifty-one and fifty-seven percent — which is to say, roughly a coin flip. And here is the detail that makes this a result rather than an accident: the annotators agreed with each other seventy-seven percent of the time. They were not guessing. They were consistently applying a shared body of expertise that was pointing the wrong way.

Why? The authors traced it. The experts leaned on the binding free energy estimate, the MM-GBSA score. On this benchmark, picking the pose with the better MM-GBSA score gets you thirty-two to thirty-five percent accuracy. The score is not weak. It is anti-correlated. And that is not an artifact of adversarial curation — nothing in the filtering selected for pairs where the score misleads. It falls out of the regime. Once you have already used the energy score to keep the top twenty-five candidates, every survivor is predicted to be thermodynamically comfortable, and the residual differences in that number stop tracking correctness and start tracking something else. The experts' accuracy was lowest exactly when their written reasoning cited that score, and significantly higher when they talked about interactions and torsion geometry instead.

I want to pause on what that means for agents, because it's the whole point. The standard story about tool-using agents is that the hard part is knowing which tool to call. This paper is a clean counterexample. The tool is right there, it runs, it returns a number with a real physical interpretation, and in this operating regime that number should be read backwards. Calling the tool is trivial. Knowing how much to believe its output in the particular regime you're standing in is the entire job, and a decade of domain experience did not supply it.

The agents did better. The authors built a structured representation of each pose — about thirty fields covering geometry, the interaction list, strain measures, validity checks — and then tried two ways of using a language model on it. The first simply reasons over the pose pair in text and picks one. That gets to sixty-seven percent at best. Notably, when you give the model only raw atomic coordinates it collapses back to chance; it cannot extract geometry from numbers on its own, and the structured features do the lifting. The second approach doesn't ask the model to judge anything. It asks the model to write Python that computes features, and then fits a plain logistic regression on those features. That version reaches seventy-four percent, the best number in the paper.

Two things about that gap are worth carrying away. The first is that a vanilla logistic regression on the precomputed numbers alone already hits sixty-four percent — beating two of the four frontier models' prose reasoning. The second is what the model actually contributes when you use it as a feature engineer. It bounds the outlier-prone energy score through a squashing function instead of trusting its magnitude. It writes conditional features, like weighting close-contact differences more heavily when the target is a G-protein-coupled receptor. That is chemical knowledge, expressed as code, fitted on data, and therefore auditable and correctable. The prose reasoning inherits the same heuristics the humans had. The code gets to be checked against outcomes.

They also trained an open-weight model with reinforcement learning on the older half of the structures, rewarding it on cross-validated accuracy. The twenty-seven-billion-parameter model gained fifteen to nineteen points as a feature writer and overtook the frontier models. In the reasoning traces you can watch it learn to stop deferring to the energy score.

My caveats are real. The benchmark is built on one company's proprietary cofolding model, deliberately chosen for diverse poses, so the anti-correlation may be specific to that pose distribution. Five annotators is five annotators. And pairwise ranking is not the same as ranking a full set. The authors resist the obvious shortcut — just flip the sign on MM-GBSA — on the grounds that a rule contradicting the physics shouldn't be deployed on the strength of one benchmark, which I think is the right instinct.

The takeaway I'd hold onto is this. If you are building agents that drive scientific software, the thing to measure is not tool coverage. It's calibration: does the system know the regime in which each tool's output inverts? And when the answer is no, the fix may not be better reasoning. It may be letting the model propose features and letting the data fit the weights.
