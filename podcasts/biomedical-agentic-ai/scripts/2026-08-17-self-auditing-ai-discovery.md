# Self-auditing AI discovery — an agent that puts its own scoring rule on trial for immune rejuvenation

## Script

Today's pick is a bioRxiv preprint with a deceptively modest title — "Virtual-cell verification enables self-auditing AI discovery for immune rejuvenation" — that quietly does something most AI-scientist papers don't. It builds an agent that doesn't just generate and rank hypotheses. It puts its own scoring function on trial.

Here's the setup. Almost every AI drug-discovery loop you've seen — Co-Scientist, Robin, Biomni — proposes candidates and ranks them against some objective. But that objective, the rule that decides what a good candidate looks like, is written once by a human and then frozen. The AI optimizes hard against a target it never questions. This paper's whole argument is that for messy, distributed phenotypes, the objective itself is the thing most likely to be wrong — and it should be a falsifiable experimental claim, revised whenever the data disagree.

The phenotype they chose to prove this on is immune aging, and it's a good stress test precisely because there's no single signature to reconstruct. Aging is smeared across cell types and gene programs. So they build three verifiers instead of one predictor.

The first is called PACE, a phenotype verifier: ten immune-aging gene modules, each scored with a direction — up or down with age — and resolved by cell type, chosen because they stay stable across four independent blood cohorts. On an in-house cohort of 434 elderly donors it beats five established aging clocks. The point isn't the leaderboard win; it's that they refuse to collapse aging into one scalar number, because you can't audit a scalar.

The second piece is the one I found most elegant. It's called CellQ, a virtual-cell model that predicts how a cell's transcriptome shifts under a drug. The trick: it compresses each single-cell transcriptome into just eight discrete tokens, using residual vector quantization — think of it as a coarse-to-fine code, where the first token captures broad context, the next cell identity, then activation state, then finer programs. And those eight tokens get dropped directly into a reasoning language model's vocabulary. There's no separate projection network bolting cells onto text — the cell literally becomes eight words the model already knows. That's eight tokens versus two hundred-plus for gene-list methods like Cell2Sentence, which means you can pack many cells into context and reason over a whole population. Trained on over a hundred million cells, it hits state-of-the-art perturbation prediction on the Tahoe atlas, and an ablation shows it's the quantization doing the work, not just the language model underneath.

Now the payoff — the third verifier, the Analyzer–Planner–Auditor agent. They screened 110 compounds in primary immune cells from elderly donors. The agent started cold, from the literature, with the obvious scoring rule: average how much a drug restores all the aging modules, equal weight. Then the Analyzer looked at the actual wet-lab response matrix and caught something. The modules that fall with age — the aged-down programs — were far more reversible than the ones that climb with age. The asymmetry was structural, across the whole panel. And it meant the equal-weight rule was direction-blind: it would happily reward a drug that only fixes the easy direction. Concretely, metformin restored both directions, as you'd hope, while rapamycin only pushed the aged-down modules — and the naive rule scored them as if they were equal.

So the Planner proposed a new objective — instead of averaging the two directions, take the minimum across them, which penalizes one-sided specialists and rewards drugs that move both. The Auditor approved it. And then, the part that matters most, they locked that revised rule and tested it cold on a separate 13-compound functional assay measuring real T-cell aging markers. The revised rule tracked the functional readouts better than the one it started with. The self-correction generalized.

Here's why this belongs on your radar. Strip away immune aging and what's left is a template: an agent that surfaces a quantitative mismatch between its own objective and the experimental evidence, and then rewrites the objective — not the candidates, the objective. The authors frame it as one turn of a generate-verify-update loop, the same shape as AlphaEvolve or The AI Scientist, but pointed at the scoring function rather than the answer. For anyone building agentic discovery systems, that's the interesting frontier: not better search over a fixed target, but agents that treat "what counts as good" as a hypothesis to be tested.

The honest caveat, which they state plainly, is that the agent's self-critique is only as expressive as the scoring functions it can write down — it found a better rule inside a small space of rules, not an open-ended one. It swapped an average for a minimum; it didn't invent a wholly new kind of objective. Still, it's a clean, wet-lab-grounded demonstration that the objective can be the thing you learn, not just the thing you're handed. And that's a more honest picture of how science actually moves.
