# CircuitATLAS — the edges they deleted on purpose

Paper link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.09643

## Script

Almost every biomedical knowledge graph you have ever used is organized around a disease-to-gene edge. That edge is the spine of the thing. It is how you get from a condition to a target, and it is why these graphs are useful. A preprint out of a small San Francisco company called Exin Therapeutics, posted to arXiv yesterday, builds a three-point-eight million node systems neuroscience graph and then deliberately deletes that edge type. No disease-to-gene. No disease-to-protein. Not missing — removed by design, and excluded at the query layer so the agent reasoning over the graph cannot route around it.

The argument for doing that is better than it first sounds. If you ask which molecule is broken in epilepsy, you get the sodium channel. If you ask which molecule you could push on to make a hyperexcitable cortex behave, you might get something that is not broken at all, and is merely well positioned. Deep brain stimulation does not fix the causal lesion. Neither do most antiseizure drugs. So the graph is built to force a longer path: from a measurable activity phenotype, to the circuit producing it, to the cell population, to a molecular effector expressed in that cell population in humans — and only then to a target. Delete the shortcut and the system has to walk.

The construction is worth describing because it inverts the usual LLM pipeline. They did the narrowing deterministically first. A hand-curated lexicon of seventeen hundred neuroscience terms screened fifteen point nine million papers. Candidate edges were only generated between allowed pairs of node types, and only when two entities co-occurred within a four hundred character window. That produced fifty-seven million candidates. Language models entered only after that, as verifiers — asked to say supported, unsupported, or uncertain for a relation they were handed, with instructions to judge only what the passage says and not to fill gaps from their own knowledge. A cheap model did the first pass, Haiku did a second pass over what survived, and then they spot-checked four thousand edges with Opus.

Opus disagreed on seventeen point eight percent of them. It judged that the relationship was not clearly stated in the supporting text. And here is the part I respect: they published that number and kept the edges anyway, moving a second verification to inference time, on only the handful of edges that a given reasoning chain actually depends on. That is the honest trade. A five million edge graph with a known one-in-six noise rate, plus a rule that you re-verify the specific edges you lean on, is a more defensible object than a smaller graph that pretends to be clean.

The agent workflow on top is a specialist stack — a coordinating investigator, a circuit agent, a molecular agent, feasibility and clinical agents, and an adversarial skeptic that can challenge any stage but explicitly holds no veto. Three invariants are enforced in code rather than in prompts, which is the right instinct. Disease association can never serve as a reward signal. The molecular stage queries a masked view of the graph with the disease and its models stripped out. And the sign of the required intervention is computed as a product of three signs and checked deterministically, so a hypothesis that wants to decrease excitability by increasing something that increases excitability gets rejected rather than argued about.

They also stress-tested the mask, which almost nobody does. They handed the masked payload to a model with no other context and asked it to name the disease. It could not. But a stronger multi-agent attack did recover the disease class — it got epilepsy, though not Dravet syndrome — from the circuit phenotype alone. Rather than quietly dropping that, they state the weaker claim precisely: not that the agent is blind to the disease class, but that every candidate's evidence chain is disease-free and reconstructible from the masked subgraph. That is what careful looks like.

So does it work? They ran three diseases, got about sixteen distinct hypotheses each, and the structural gates killed five outright, including sodium channel blockade in a seizure disorder — a real error, caught. For epilepsy the agent nominated the neuronal alpha-three sodium potassium pump, arguing it generates a hyperpolarizing current recruited in proportion to how hard a neuron has been firing. They expressed it in cortical interneurons in mice and hit the cortex with a convulsant. In the control animals the beta and gamma band response jumped roughly eight to eleven fold. In the treated animals, essentially not at all. Clean separation — on five animals versus six, which is where I would put the asterisk.

Then they took the validated target into a structure-guided chemistry campaign, and this is the most instructive failure in the paper. Two hundred fifty predicted binders, seventy-four with a verified synthetic route, fifty orderable today. Sounds like a win. Except: of two hundred three pockets they derived, one hundred seventy-five contain no meaningful difference between the alpha-three pump and the alpha-one pump that keeps the rest of your cells alive. The cavities are small enough to fit only fragments, and synthetic tractability collapses from seventy-six percent to six percent across that size range. And most of all, docking predicts that a molecule binds. It does not predict whether binding turns the pump up or down. The entire therapeutic thesis is potentiation. The pipeline cannot tell potentiation from inhibition.

So the campaign does not end in a lead compound. It ends in a request for a thallium flux assay to resolve the direction of the effect — an assay this lab does not own, returned through their lab interface as a capability request. I think that is the right place for an agentic discovery system to stop, and it is a more useful result than a ranked list of molecules would have been. The system reasoned its way to the one measurement that decides everything, and then said: I need an instrument I do not have. Every other number in the paper is downstream of someone going and buying it.
