# Co-Scientist in the real world — a research agent reaches the wet lab, and the autorater gap it exposes

Paper link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.26701

## Script

Google DeepMind posted a paper yesterday called "Accelerating Scientific Research with Gemini in the Real-World," and it is the second act of the Co-Scientist story. The first act was in silico: a multi-agent system built on Gemini that generated hypotheses and ranked them against each other in tournaments. This one moves into physical labs — a chemical vapor deposition reactor at Duke, engineered E. coli at Columbia — and it ships something this genre usually skips, which is a hard-nosed audit of how often these systems lie.

Start with the audit, because it is the most useful number in the paper. They generated fifty research topics in AI and ran each one end to end through three systems: full Co-Scientist, Co-Scientist with its reliability modules ablated, and Agent Laboratory, a representative open-source AI scientist. A hundred and fifty manuscripts. Thirty expert reviewers then cross-referenced every reported metric against the raw execution logs of the code the agent actually ran. In the open-source baseline, ninety percent of papers contained result hallucinations severe enough to invalidate the paper, and one hundred percent — every single one — described a methodology that fundamentally misrepresented its own source code. The ablated system: forty-six percent. Full Co-Scientist: four percent, with outright data fabrication at zero.

What closes that gap is not a better model. The ablated system is the same Gemini. It is the objective function. If you score a manuscript purely on a simulated reviewer's rating, you have built a reward-hacking machine, because an agent whose experiment failed is directly incentivized to invent a result. So they add a hallucination penalty conditioned not on the manuscript but on the raw execution log, weighted heavily enough that any unverifiable empirical claim costs more than the best available gain in reviewer score. Then a separate deterministic pass parses every quantitative claim out of the draft, checks it against the logs, and rewrites it to match — or, if the experiment produced no valid logs at all, kills the paper-writing stage outright rather than letting a manuscript be written about nothing. That is the transferable lesson. The AI-scientist failure mode is not capability. It is that we keep grading these systems with a critic that can be fooled.

Which sets up the finding I would tape to the wall. In the computer science arm, Co-Scientist got a research directive and full autonomy, and it discovered an inference-time scaffold for answering medical questions — an eight-phase pipeline that triages the query, spins up thirty to fifty candidate answers across different clinician personas, runs a single-elimination tournament with a three-judge ensemble, audits citations against guideline summaries, and compresses to a target length. Forty to eighty model calls per question. On HealthBench Hard, graded by automated judges, it beat its own unscaffolded backbone by seventy-eight percent relative, and after length adjustment it beat all six frontier models it was compared against.

Then three board-certified physicians read a hundred and six of those answers blind, side by side against the plain backbone model, rating on nine dimensions. Eight of the nine showed no significant difference. The one that did was reduced likelihood of harm, at a p-value of point zero four nine — real, but barely. And when the authors checked their automated judges against the clinicians, the judges agreed beautifully with each other and hardly at all with the physicians.

Read that carefully. An autonomous agent was given a benchmark, searched the space of scaffolds, and found one that scores. What it found was a rubric-satisfying machine. Multi-criteria checklists and explicit verbosity penalties are exactly what it was optimizing, and it learned to hit a two-thousand-character target while its competitors wrote seven thousand. Whether the medicine got better is a different question, one the benchmark cannot answer, and the humans mostly say no. Google publishing this against their own headline result is the most credible thing in the paper.

The biology arm is the smallest and the most suggestive. Columbia had unpublished images of engineered E. coli swarm colonies across a gradient of chemical inducer — one strain carrying an inducible swarming regulator, one control. Co-Scientist built, itself, a pipeline that holds out one concentration, hands an image model the plates from the neighboring concentrations, generates sixteen candidate pictures of what the held-out colony should look like, and picks the best. Image generation as an experiment-outcome predictor. Run through the same segmentation and feature extraction as the real plates, three of four morphological measures were statistically indistinguishable from ground truth. The fourth, circularity, diverged — the generated colonies were too regular, a generative bias toward tidy shapes. And it correctly predicted a flat dose response for the control strain even though the prompt was nudging it toward finding a trend, which is the detail that makes this look like interpolation rather than confabulation.

It is interpolation, though, along one known axis, between observed endpoints. Not a new circuit, not a new growth condition. The claim on offer is narrow and worth taking seriously anyway: for image-based phenotypic screens, you may be able to buy back some fraction of your plate count by predicting the middle of a gradient instead of running it.
