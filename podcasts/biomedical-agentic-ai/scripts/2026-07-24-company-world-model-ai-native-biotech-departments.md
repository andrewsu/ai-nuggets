# Company World Model — should an AI-native biotech's agents mimic departments, or share one asset-to-value state?

Paper link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.18696

## Script

Today's paper asks a question that sounds like a management-consulting joke but turns out to be a real systems-design problem: does an AI-native biotech need departments?

Here's the setup. When people build a drug company out of AI agents, the default move is to copy a human org chart — a biology agent, a clinical agent, a regulatory agent, a commercial agent, a finance agent — and wire them together through a project-manager or investment-committee layer. Each agent owns a function, memos get passed around, a decision comes out the top. It feels natural because it's how human companies are organized. This single-author working paper argues that copying the org chart is the wrong abstraction, and then does something unusually disciplined: it builds a benchmark to test that claim instead of just asserting it.

The alternative it proposes is what it calls a Company World Model. Instead of departments passing notes, you keep one shared, live object — the paper calls it a Live Asset Value Record — that holds everything known about a drug program: the evidence, the counter-evidence, the uncertainties, the red flags, the regulatory path, the commercial assumptions. On top of that shared state you put operators — a Deal Room, an Approval Room, a Revenue Room — that each model how actions change one dimension of value, plus a planner it calls an Investment Arbiter that picks the next move. The key mental shift is that those rooms are not renamed departments. A Deal Room isn't the business-development team; it's a transition model over buyer demand and walk-away conditions. Departments are a view for humans; the shared predictive state is the computational primitive.

The benchmark is the best part. Forty-five retrospective decision cases, each a real biopharma moment — semaglutide in obesity, aducanumab in Alzheimer's, exa-cel in sickle cell disease, the Duchenne gene therapy Elevidys, deals like Pfizer buying Seagen and Gilead buying Forty Seven — each with a strict information cutoff date, a fixed list of allowed public sources, and the actual outcome hidden from the agents. The organization has to return a structured decision — go, no-go, watch, or partner — with its evidence and an audit trail, and it gets scored both automatically and by blinded pairwise judges that don't see which architecture produced which answer.

And the first result is striking. Under a success objective defined by business-value conversion — external dealmaking, regulatory approval and launch, and revenue discipline — the Company World Model architecture crushed the department-mimic baseline. Blinded judges preferred it something like forty-two to three. That's the headline you'd expect to see tweeted: AI-native beats departments.

Except the author then spends the rest of the paper trying to break their own result, and mostly succeeds. First they build a stronger human baseline — still departments, but with shared memory and real investment-committee conflict resolution — and the gap narrows to twenty-six to nineteen, which is no longer statistically convincing. Then comes the move I really respect: they swap the judge. Instead of asking "which output better converts this asset into value," they ask a neutral question — "which output is better reasoned, better evidenced, more auditable." Under that neutral judge, the Company World Model's advantage evaporates entirely. The department-mimic baseline actually edges it out.

So what's the real finding? The answer is objective-sensitive. If you're optimizing for turning a drug program into deals and revenue, organizing your agents around value-conversion operators over a shared state genuinely helps. If you're optimizing for neutral scientific diligence, it doesn't. In world-model language: the value function is doing the work, and an architecture tuned to one value function won't automatically win on another. The ablations back this up — remove the Revenue Room and revenue reasoning collapses, remove the Deal Room and dealmaking reasoning collapses — so it's not just a writing template; the operators carry specific, measurable weight.

Here's why this matters for anyone building agentic systems, biomedical or not. The lazy version of "agentic AI for science" is to take your existing human workflow, assign each step to an agent, and call it multi-agent. This paper is a careful argument that the org chart is a governance convenience, not the right decomposition for machines — and that the thing you actually have to specify, out loud, is the value function the whole system is optimizing. Hide it inside role prompts and you can't reason about it. Expose it, and organizational design becomes a testable variable instead of a metaphor. That generalizes well past biotech: any multi-agent system copied from a human team chart is smuggling in an implicit objective nobody wrote down.

The honest caveats, which the paper states plainly: this is dry-lab only — retrospective decisions, no real drugs discovered, no deals closed. The world model is a prompt-level structure, not a trained simulator; revenue is scored as reasoning discipline, not forecast accuracy. Everything ran through a single coding-agent harness on one model, and the neutral-judge replication on a second model got cut off by an API error partway through, so the cross-model check is incomplete. Take the numbers as directional.

But the framing is the payload, and it's a good one: stop cloning your org chart, start defining your shared state and your value function. It turns "AI-native organization" from a slogan into something you can actually measure — which is exactly the move this field keeps skipping.
