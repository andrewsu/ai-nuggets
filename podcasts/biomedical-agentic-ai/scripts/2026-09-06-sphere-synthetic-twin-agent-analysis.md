# SPHERE — a synthetic twin that lets a cloud AI agent write the analysis of patient data it never sees, with the algorithm withheld pending a patent

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.09.01.748580v1

## Script

Today's nugget is a preprint out of Stanford, posted yesterday, and it aims squarely at the thing that stops most biomedical AI work before it starts: you have the data, and you are not allowed to show it to the model.

The tool is called SPHERE. You point it at a sensitive tabular dataset and it emits what the authors call a synthetic twin — a file with the same shape and the same statistical structure, in which no row corresponds to any real participant. The original records never leave your institution. Then you hand the twin to whatever cloud model you like.

There are a hundred synthetic-data papers, so what makes this one worth five minutes is the guarantee. Most methods either add calibrated noise — that's differential privacy — or fit a generative model, a GAN or a diffusion model, and sample from it. Noise dilutes weak associations and collapses your power. Generative models give you plausible-looking data with no exact inferential correspondence to what you started with. SPHERE does neither. It transforms the data rather than perturbing it: no noise added, no model fitted, no per-dataset calibration. And because it is a transformation, every mean, every variance, every correlation comes out unchanged — delta exactly zero, by construction. Every ordinary-least-squares coefficient, every standard error, every p-value, every principal-component loading you compute on the twin is numerically identical to the one you would get on the real data. Not close. Identical to machine precision.

Now the part this show cares about. The reason you cannot point a frontier model at patient data is not capability — it is that the good models live behind cloud APIs that are not authorized to receive protected health information, while the local open-weight alternatives want serious GPU infrastructure and hand back weaker agentic reasoning. So the authors ran a four-agent virtual lab — a principal investigator, an epidemiologist, a biostatistician and a clinical expert — on national health survey data, in two scenarios. In the first, identical prompts on the real records and on the twin: same conclusions, same top three predictors of systolic blood pressure, same effect sizes. That is the sanity check. The second one is the actual result. The agents saw only the twin, never the real data, and wrote a four-hundred-eighty-six-line analysis pipeline. That pipeline was then run unchanged on the real records. Every coefficient, every standard error, every p-value came out identical across all nineteen predictors.

That is the workflow, and I think it is the right shape. The agent does not analyze your data — it writes the analysis, on a stand-in, and you run it. Because the transformation preserves second moments exactly, the code does not approximately transfer; it transfers exactly. And it holds at scale: on UK Biobank genotypes, four hundred sixty-one thousand participants and nine point seven million variants, per-variant effect sizes correlate at one point zero. Then they re-ran published papers, including organ-aging clocks from a Nature Medicine study — nonlinear models, formally outside the class SPHERE guarantees — and still reproduced the disease and mortality associations.

But here is the piece that makes actual news for anyone who cares about open data. The paper releases the Stanford Alzheimer's Disease Research Center cohort. Six hundred forty-four participants across nine modalities — cognitive testing, plasma biomarkers, amyloid and tau PET, genotypes, single-cell RNA from blood mononuclear cells, and proteomics from both cerebrospinal fluid and plasma — two hundred and six thousand features, eight diagnostic groups. Open portal, registration and a click-through data-use agreement, and explicitly no institutional review board gate. There are more than thirty of these centers in the United States, each behind its own protocols and secure-enclave requirements that take weeks to months per site. One of them just went open.

And now the caveat, which is not small. The algorithmic construction of SPHERE, and the formal derivations behind the privacy guarantee, are withheld from the preprint pending a patent application. They go to editors and referees on request, and into the peer-reviewed version. What you get today is a macOS desktop app and a description of what the method preserves.

Sit with what that means. The central claim is information-theoretic: without the random parameters, which are drawn fresh each time and never retained, each released row maps back to an uncountably infinite set of originals, and for one of their analyses the reconstruction set contains at least seven point nine billion discrete branches. That claim is the entire basis for releasing patient-level data with no review board in the loop. And right now nobody outside the review process can check the derivation. The empirical side — an adversarial attack suite scoring ninety-two and a half percent across singling-out, linkability and inference — is real work, but an attack suite certifies robustness against the attacks in the suite. This is not differential privacy: no epsilon, no composition accounting, no budget you can reason about across releases. That is not a reason to dismiss the work. It is a reason to notice that the most interesting open-data release of the month is gated on a method the community cannot yet audit, and to watch what appears in the published version.

The stated limitations are honest. Exactness covers only analyses determined by the first two moments, so generalized linear models, survival models, tree ensembles, interaction terms and subgroup-specific effects all fall outside — and they attenuate toward the null rather than manufacturing signal, which is the right direction to fail. Non-Gaussian continuous columns get smoothed toward Gaussian, so your histograms will look wrong even when your regressions are exact. And it assumes independent rows, so longitudinal repeated measures and graph-structured data do not come along for free.

If this survives review with the construction disclosed, it is a genuinely different answer to a problem the field has been solving badly for twenty years.
