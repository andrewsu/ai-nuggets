# The Findings Workflow — gated stages, deterministic hooks, and a findings graph for LLM-assisted analysis

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.10.28.762630v1

## Script

Today's pick is a bioRxiv preprint from the MacCoss lab in Genome Sciences at the University of Washington, describing something they call the Findings Workflow. It's a free, open-source Claude Code plugin for quantitative data analysis, and it is the most concrete answer I've seen to a question this show keeps circling: if you're going to let a language model do your statistics, what has to be true before you believe the result?

The framing is sharp. A scientist holding a protein abundance matrix has two problems that look separate and aren't. One is confidence — is that the right test, were the data loaded correctly, did some imputation sneak in. The other is provenance — six weeks and four thousand chat messages later, how exactly was this figure produced? Their argument is that conversational analysis doesn't solve the first problem, it conceals it, and it manufactures the second one from nothing. Fluent, well-formatted output makes a cross-validation leak harder to find, not easier.

So the plugin builds a harness around the conversation. Seven numbered stages with gates between them: state the science, understand the metadata, understand the data, build and verify tested data loaders, and only then explore. You cannot analyze before the loaders pass. Thirteen subagents across seven subsystems, each paired with an independent reviewer — a statistician and a stats reviewer, a coder and a code reviewer, a figure generator and a figure reviewer. And a findings knowledgebase: every substantive observation written to its own markdown file, with structured frontmatter carrying controlled vocabulary for genes, proteins and diseases, typed edges to other findings — supports, refines, contradicts, supersedes, closes — and provenance down to which script produced which panel.

The piece I'd steal outright is the distinction they draw between hooks and gates. The gates live in prompts, because whether a given request really constitutes data exploration is a judgment call no script can cleanly decide. The hooks are Python, and they are absolute: one makes the data directory read-only, one refuses to let a figure exist without provenance and a matching legend, one blocks a script from being promoted unless linting and strict type checking pass. Then there's a document called the enforcement map, which takes every convention the project claims to follow and names the specific mechanism that checks it — a hook, a reviewer agent, the orchestrator, or a human. The principle is stated outright: a convention is only real if something actually checks it. That sentence is worth more than most agent architecture diagrams.

The worked example is a reanalysis of Mag-Net, a plasma extracellular vesicle proteomics dataset — ten Alzheimer's patients, ten Parkinson's with dementia, ten Parkinson's cognitively normal, ten controls. And this is where it gets interesting in a way I don't think the authors fully lean into.

At the metadata stage, before touching the data at all, the model found that every Alzheimer's subject came from a single collection site. It recorded that as a finding, and then it kept dragging that finding back into the room — when differential abundance returned three hundred and sixty-three significant proteins, when the classifier hit an area under the curve of point nine six matching the published result, when the enrichment ran. It also proposed the right control unprompted: train a classifier to predict collection site among the thirty non-Alzheimer's samples. That came back at chance, which is genuine evidence the disease signal isn't just the site. Good practice — and good practice that a persistent record kept visible instead of burying in a transcript.

But look at what the workflow actually added past the original paper. Small GTPases and microtubule transport proteins down in Alzheimer's reproduces the published result. The new finding is complement and coagulation proteins up in Alzheimer's, which the original didn't report. And that is precisely the result that cannot be separated from collection site, as the paper says plainly. The one novel claim the system produced is the one its own provenance trail marks as unusable. I read that as a success — but a success of a different kind than better analysis. It's a success of bookkeeping honest enough to indict its own output.

Two more moments worth noting. The scientist is the one who pointed out that over-representation analysis counts proteins and ignores abundance — and the first enrichment run had returned zero significant terms, so without that intervention the analysis ends in a null rather than in thirty-nine gene sets. The scientist also caught that Boruta returns relevant features without direction. Both are exactly the catch a stats reviewer subagent exists to make. Neither one made it.

And the paper is candid about the thing I most wanted to know. Under Evaluation, the authors write that whether the workflow produces better results is a legitimate question, that they've had enthusiastic informal feedback from test users, and that measuring the impact properly is beyond the scope of the manuscript. No control arm, no comparison against unstructured use, no error-injection test. One worked example, on a dataset where the answer was already published.

So here's my take. I'd use this. The gates are right, the read-only hook on the data directory is right, and findings files with typed relationships are a better lab notebook than any lab notebook I have ever actually kept. But the claim on the tin is that structure makes model-assisted analysis trustworthy, and what's demonstrated is that structure makes model-assisted analysis auditable. Those aren't the same thing, and only one of them has evidence behind it yet. Auditable is still worth a great deal — it is the precondition for ever measuring the other one.

It's open source, it's a plugin, and installation is two commands. The cost of finding out is very low.
