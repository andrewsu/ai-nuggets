# Drug-centric knowledge graph — when the therapeutic, not the target, is the unit of identity

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.10.15.759051v1

## Script

Today's pick is a bioRxiv preprint from QuantHealth describing what they call a drug-centric knowledge graph. I want to use it for two separate arguments — one about schema design, and one about where language models have quietly ended up inside our data infrastructure.

Start with the schema, because it's the cleaner argument. Almost every biomedical knowledge graph you have ever queried is organized around the target. A compound is a node, it has an edge to a gene, and the gene is where all the interesting structure hangs off. That works fine right up until the therapeutic stops being a small molecule. Two antibodies can hit the same antigen and differ in sequence, format, isotype and half-life. Two antibody-drug conjugates can share the exact same antibody and differ completely in linker and payload — which is to say, they differ in nearly everything that determines whether the drug works or kills the patient. In a target-centered representation, those pairs collapse onto each other. The schema is throwing away precisely the thing that distinguishes the intervention.

Their fix is an identity model, not a bigger edge list, and that distinction is the whole paper. The complete intervention is the unit of identity: one node, one persistent identifier. Chemical structures, sequences and components link to that identity rather than defining it. And when a component is itself a therapeutic — the antibody inside a conjugate, say — the composite points at that component's own node, so you can compare two conjugates at the antibody level, at the linker level, or at the payload level without any of them losing their own clinical associations. On top of that sits a two-tier schema. Twenty-nine relation types that every compound has, covering targets, indications, adverse effects, classifications, pathways, Gene Ontology concepts, cells, anatomy and half-life — those give you a common basis for comparison across classes. Then nineteen modality-specific relation types that apply only to your drug type: chemistry for small molecules, sequence and format and isotype for antibodies, linker and payload for conjugates. And the relation semantics deliberately split three things most graphs merge into one: molecular recognition, functional action, and disposition. Binds is not Inhibits, and neither one is Metabolised by. Anyone who has tried to do something quantitative on a graph where drug-target interaction is a single undifferentiated predicate knows exactly why that matters. Forty sources, roughly one hundred sixty-six thousand nodes, eight point one million edges, about eighteen thousand compounds.

Now the second argument, which is the one I actually want to leave you with. Look at where the language models are in this pipeline. They are not an agent sitting on top answering questions. They are load-bearing inside construction. When a source identifier or a free-text entity name can't be resolved by cross-references, a model picks the right vocabulary term out of an embedding index. When a source uses its own relation vocabulary, a model maps that label onto the registered predicates for that node-type pair — six hundred and one rules, which is how an agonist assertion correctly becomes both Binds and Activates. And in confidence scoring, a model's assessment of biological plausibility is one of four weighted signals. But read the validity rule carefully, because this is the part that should make you sit up: a plausibility score above a threshold admits the assertion on its own, regardless of source support, graph connectivity, or literature co-occurrence. A model's guess about whether something is biologically reasonable can be decisive against three empirical signals.

To the authors' considerable credit, they say all of this out loud. The limitations paragraph states flatly that the language model outputs were never quantitatively validated, that the predicate-mapping table got manual review but no systematic validation, and that the validity rule lets plausibility alone carry an edge. They also disclose, rather than quietly fix, that five of those mapping rules take Gene Ontology annotations carrying a NOT qualifier and map them to positive predicates — meaning those edges assert the opposite of their own source. That is a bug, and they shipped it labeled.

Here is why that's the right call and not a cop-out. Every edge retains a reference back to the extracted triplet it came from, and the affected rules are flagged in the supplement. So the disclosure is actionable: you can find those edges and filter them. Provenance at the assertion level is what converts an admitted error from an apology into a correction you can apply yourself. Compare that to the alternative, which is a graph with the same error silently absorbed.

The catch is what you can actually take home. The graph is proprietary, the construction code is proprietary, and the execution records for this build were not retained, so it cannot be reproduced exactly even by them. What is published is the schema, the source inventory and the predicate-mapping rules. Which, honestly, is the part worth having. The three design principles — intervention-level identity, a two-tier shared-plus-modality schema, and relation semantics that separate recognition from action from disposition — are implementation-independent. You could adopt them tomorrow in BioCypher on open sources and get most of the value.

The real message is about trust boundaries. We have crossed from using models to query knowledge graphs into using them to construct the predicate layer, and almost nobody is measuring the error rate that introduces. This paper at least tells you where the model touched the data. That should be the floor, not a distinguishing feature.
