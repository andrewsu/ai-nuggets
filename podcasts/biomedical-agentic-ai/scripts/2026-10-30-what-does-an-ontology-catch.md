# What Does an Ontology Catch? — the schema is a judge, and it is wrong more often than it is right

Paper link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37809

## Script

There's a move that has quietly become standard practice in agents that query knowledge graphs. The agent writes a SPARQL query, runs it against an endpoint, and before you trust the answer, you check the query against the graph's ontology. If a property says its subject has to be a protein and the query asks it of a taxon, flag it and make the agent try again. The appeal is obvious: the schema is formal, it's deterministic, it costs no model call, and somebody curated it. Today's paper, from the Sano Centre for Computational Medicine and AGH Krakow, asks the question nobody has asked, which is whether that checker is any good.

They make the checker itself the object of study, and the headline is uncomfortable. On UniProt, the curated ontology — the one published by the people who built the graph, used exactly as published — raises more false alarms than either a schema mined straight from the data or one that Claude wrote from a summary of that data with the identifiers masked out. And on real agent answers, its flags are right only marginally more often than flags thrown at random.

Here's the setup, because the design is the reason I believe the result. Seven models — two Claudes and five open-weight — answer two hundred and ten generated questions against the Swiss-Prot subset of UniProt, three attempts each, about forty-four hundred answers, with a pre-defined correct answer for every question. Three ontology sources, each used three ways: as context in the prompt, as a check on the answer, or both. And because real agent errors are sparse and lumpy, they also build about thirteen hundred artificially faulted queries — invented identifiers, type swaps, and what they call changes of meaning, like replacing COUNT with COUNT DISTINCT or dropping a grouping key. The whole thing was pre-registered: the claims, the statistical tests, and the margins frozen in a timestamped commit before any test question was run.

The numbers. On real answers the curated check catches about nineteen percent of wrong answers, and flags about seventeen percent of correct ones. Precision one-point-six points better than random, confidence interval straddling zero. The data-derived ontology flags three and a half percent of correct answers; the masked LLM-written ones, under two percent. Both confirmatory claims clear their registered five-point margins. So the expensive artifact is the worst judge in the lineup.

Why? They audited it blind, two annotators, and the causes are mundane and devastating. Most false alarms come from a term the ontology simply doesn't declare — and since the check requires every class and property in a query to be declared, one missing property produces a false alarm on every correct answer that touches it, forever. The rest come from reading an open-world axiom as a closed-world constraint. And every single audited false alarm was, on inspection, a correct answer.

Then there's the table I'd put on a slide if I were teaching this. Each curated ontology they looked at disagrees with its own data. In UniProt, a property declared as a floating-point number whose one hundred and thirty-nine values are all integers; five properties declared functional — meaning one value per subject — that have two distinct values for thousands of subjects. In Rhea, four properties the data uses between fifteen thousand and ninety thousand times each, which the ontology never declares. In a private graph of nearly a billion triples, ninety-four percent of triples use an identifier the ontology doesn't contain, mostly renamings. These are not exotic graphs. These are the reference resources our field runs on, and the schema has drifted from the data underneath it.

Two more findings sharpen the point. First, the baseline. Just retry whenever the query comes back with no rows. On the artificial faults that catches seventy-nine percent with a zero percent false alarm rate — better than any ontology, for free. The authors' rule follows directly: a check earns its place only on errors that running the query doesn't already reveal, so compare it against retry-on-empty before you deploy it. Second, the ceiling. On changes of meaning — the fault class where the query is structurally impeccable and asks a subtly different question — the curated, data-derived and masked ontologies catch zero. Not few. Zero. Random flagging at the same rate catches forty-five percent. And when they hand-coded the real wrong answers nothing flagged, almost all of them were a wrong path through the graph or a wrong filter, which no structural check could catch even over a perfect schema. The errors that survive are semantic, and ontologies are not semantic checkers.

The obvious alternative gets tested too. An LLM judge, given the question and the query and the results, catches more errors than any ontology and is genuinely better than random — nearly sixteen points of precision. But acting on its flags corrupted more correct answers than it fixed, while the curated check's retries fixed over a quarter of the flagged wrong answers and corrupted about one in a hundred. A judge you can't act on isn't much of a judge either.

What I take from this is a reframing I'd want anyone building over Wikidata or UniProt or a disease KG to internalize. The ontology is not the ground truth of the graph. It's a second artifact, maintained on a different schedule by different people, and it drifts. If you publish a knowledge graph, the actionable recommendation here is cheap: generate a data summary and validate your ontology against it before every release, because that summary is enough to surface every one of these contradictions. If you build agents, treat your checker as a component with a measurable false-positive rate, not as an oracle. And notice the quiet result underneath all of this — the data-derived schema was also the better prompt context, beating the curated one on accuracy, and the LLM-written version cost under a dollar to produce.

The authors say their next step is turning these checks loose on an agent helping biologists query a large graph of genomes, proteins and computational predictions, where no correct answer is known in advance and a flag is the only automatic signal you get. That's the real setting. The numbers in this paper are what tell you which flags are worth listening to.
