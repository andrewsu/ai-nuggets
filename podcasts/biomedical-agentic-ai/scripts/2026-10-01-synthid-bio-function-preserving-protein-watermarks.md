# SynthID Bio — watermarking AI-designed proteins without breaking them

Paper link: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10965-y

## Script

Google DeepMind published a paper in Nature yesterday that I would file under research infrastructure rather than AI capability, and right now I think infrastructure is the more interesting of the two categories. The method is called Synth-I-D Bio, and it embeds a hidden, statistically detectable signature into AI-designed protein sequences and AI-predicted protein structures, without changing what the protein does.

The trick on the sequence side is a port. ProteinMPNN, the workhorse that writes an amino acid sequence onto a designed backbone, decodes residue by residue, which makes it formally a language model over a twenty-letter alphabet. So they took tournament sampling, the watermarking scheme DeepMind already ships on its text models, and pointed it at residues. At each position you draw several candidate residues, a secret key scores them, and the winner advances through a bracket. What comes out is a sequence that scores measurably high against that key and looks like nothing in particular to anyone who doesn't hold it. The structure side is a different and, I would argue, more consequential choice. They fine-tuned AlphaFold three's diffusion module with an extra loss term, co-training a small detector that reads atom-to-atom distances and torsion angles. The watermark lives in the weights.

Now the claim worth checking, because everyone in this area makes it and almost nobody tests it: function preservation. They went to the bench. Fifteen known binder backbones for each of three targets — the SARS-CoV-2 receptor binding domain, vascular endothelial growth factor A, and P-D-L-one — resequenced with and without the watermark, giving about two hundred twenty unwatermarked and two hundred sixty-seven watermarked designs per setting, with affinities measured by surface plasmon resonance. No significant population-level difference in binding affinity. No significant difference in hit rate at the tighter affinity cuts; the one gap showed up at the loosest cut, where unwatermarked designs did better. All nine negative controls were dead, as they should be. On the structure side, a true positive rate above ninety-nine point eight percent at a one-in-a-thousand false positive rate, with no measurable loss on AlphaFold's own accuracy metrics at the noise setting they recommend. That is the result, and it is the thing prior work had not managed. The function tax, as far as a few hundred affinity measurements can tell us, is zero.

Here is the part I would actually take away, and it generalizes well past proteins. Where you put a watermark decides whether anyone can remove it. The sequence watermark sits in the sampling loop, outside the model — easy to adopt, and trivially switched off, and worse, a single pass of the molecule back through the same publicly available ProteinMPNN strips the signal. They tested that on thirty-eight thousand binders and it works. The structure watermark is in the weights, so it survives the model being released openly, and a user running the published checkpoint cannot turn it off. If you are serious about provenance for open-weight models, the sampling layer is a courtesy and the weights are the only real control surface.

To their considerable credit, the authors say in print that the punitive version of this is a dead end. If detecting a watermark triggers extra scrutiny, a bad actor simply uses an un-watermarked tool, or resequences. What is left is the positive version: an intact watermark is cheap evidence that an order came from a vetted tool, which lets a synthesis provider skip the expensive part of screening and pass the savings back to the customer. The stated goal is the gene synthesis industry's historical one — raise the cost of misuse, not guarantee security.

But the application that should interest anyone working on biomedical data is the one the paper gives the least space to. Essentially every AI tool in this field stands on the Protein Data Bank, UniProt, GenBank, MGnify — public databases anyone can submit to, where submission is tied to publication credit. And the failure mode is not hypothetical. Improperly labelled synthetic DNA deposited in GenBank has already been shown to degrade BLAST-based pathogen identification. A watermark detector at the submission gate is a provenance check at the moment of ingest, carried by the one artifact that cannot be stripped out of the record. If you care what our reference databases look like after a decade of generative models writing into them, that is the version of this work that matters.

Two reasons you cannot do it yet. First, this is a zero-bit scheme. It tells you a watermark is present and nothing else — not which model, not which version, not who. For curation, that is most of what you would want to know. Second, detection needs the secret key, so a database maintainer would have to strike bilateral arrangements with every model developer, and public-key watermarking is, as the authors note, unsolved and known to be hard. One more practical crack: running a watermarked structure through a standard constrained relaxation in OpenMM destroys the signal, and that is something people do routinely and without any malice at all.

Code and the recommended structure model weights are on GitHub. Take this as the proof of concept the authors say it is. The engineering looks close to settled; what is left is almost entirely coordination.
