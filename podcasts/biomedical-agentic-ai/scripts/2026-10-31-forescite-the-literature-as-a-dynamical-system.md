# ForeSCITE — hypotheses from the literature's own motion, not from asking a model for an idea

Paper link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.38719

## Script

Almost every hypothesis-generation system of the last two years has the same shape. Retrieve some papers, hand them to a language model, ask for an idea, then critique and revise. Co-Scientist, Robin, the Virtual Lab — different orchestration, same primitive. A new preprint out of Yale's biomedical informatics department, posted Wednesday, does something structurally different, and the difference is worth sitting with: there is no agent loop in it at all.

The system is called ForeSCITE. The idea is to treat the published literature not as evidence to retrieve, but as a record of motion — and then to extrapolate that motion.

Here is how it works. Pull the conclusion sentences out of roughly three million PubMed abstracts published before a March 2023 cutoff. Embed each one. Cluster them into four hundred and seven thematic domains. Now, inside each cluster, train a linear classifier to tell the oldest quarter of conclusions from the newest quarter. The direction that classifier learns is the interesting object: it is a vector that points, in semantic space, the way that subfield has been moving. Take a recent conclusion, push its embedding along that vector by some step size, and decode the shifted coordinate back into English using a language model trained specifically to reconstruct conclusion text from embeddings.

The output is a full scientific claim — an intervention, a biological context, a direction of effect, a proposed consequence — rather than the concept pair that classical literature-based discovery gives you. That's the real advance over the temporal literature-based discovery line of work this descends from.

First question: is there actually a temporal signal to extrapolate along? Yes, and robustly. Across all four hundred and seven clusters, those early-versus-late classifiers hit a mean accuracy of about eighty-five percent. And when you decode an extrapolated vector to text and then re-embed that text with the original encoder, it lands further along the time axis than where it started. The displacement survives the round trip through language, which is the part I would have bet against.

Then the evaluation, which is a proper time-sliced holdout. Same seed conclusions for everybody; ForeSCITE against three prompting strategies and two retrieval-augmented ones. ForeSCITE covers more of the post-cutoff semantic space — at the larger step sizes, around ninety percent of the coverage achieved by the actual human-written conclusions from the held-out period. It produces more novel biomedical entity pairs. And more of those novel pairs turn out to be pairs that really do show up in the post-cutoff literature.

That last number deserves care, and to the authors' credit they give it care. At the best settings, about two percent of generated entity pairs were novel pairs later observed. Two percent sounds like nothing. But they built two chance baselines to make it legible. Sampling unseen pairs uniformly gives an expected rate of about one-thousandth of a percent. Sampling weighted by how well-connected each entity already is — which is the baseline that actually matters, because it captures "just name popular things together" — gives seven-tenths of a percent. So two percent is roughly three times the hard baseline, not three orders of magnitude above a straw man. And the holdout window is short relative to how long it takes to fund, run, and publish a biomedical study, so this is a floor under incomplete ascertainment rather than a hit rate.

There's a nice structural finding buried in the step size. Push harder and novelty goes up monotonically — but agreement with the future literature peaks at intermediate displacement and then falls, as the generated distribution collapses into a lower-rank region. The step size isn't a decoding hyperparameter. It's a search policy on a dial from conservative continuation to speculation, and the empirically best setting is neither end. If you want a quantitative handle on the adjacent possible, this is about the cleanest one I've seen.

Then the case study, which is where I have reservations. They generated one thousand four hundred sixty-five hypotheses about CAR-T therapy, had GPT-5 score them for novelty, sent the top two hundred forty to two immunology cell biologists, and got thirty-seven back judged both novel and biologically plausible. That's two and a half percent of everything generated. The authors say outright this is not a prevalence estimate, because the lower-scored bucket was never reviewed — and that's exactly the joint I'd worry about. A model screening another model's novelty, with no audit of what the screen discarded. We have no idea whether the good ideas were in the pile nobody opened.

The other limit is the one linear direction per cluster. Fields branch. They reverse after a negative result. They lurch when an assay gets cheap. A single first-order trend is stable and interpretable, and it is also going to be wrong exactly where things get interesting. And since the corpus is PubMed conclusions, the trajectory being extrapolated is the trajectory of what got published — positive findings, surviving the file drawer.

Why this belongs in an agentic-AI conversation despite having no agents in it: the authors position it, correctly, as something that goes in front of deliberation rather than in place of it. Every co-scientist system starts from a research direction a human typed into a box. This is a way to estimate that direction from data, before any agent starts reasoning. That's a more useful thing to add to the stack than another critique loop.
