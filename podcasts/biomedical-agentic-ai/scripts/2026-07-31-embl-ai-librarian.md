# EMBL AI Librarian — an agent-first knowledge layer that turns Europe PMC into citable evidence for LLM agents

Paper link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28229

## Script

Here's a premise worth sitting with. More than half the traffic hitting the web now comes from automated agents, not people. In the life sciences that shift is already inside our daily workflows — agents that summarize the literature, orchestrate bioinformatics tools, prioritize biomarkers, generate hypotheses. And almost all of them share one dependency: they have to anchor their reasoning in published evidence. Which means they're all pounding on the same door — resources like Europe PMC, the open literature engine run by EMBL-EBI, with roughly forty million abstracts and twelve million full-text articles behind it.

The problem, argued in a new paper out of Fabio Petroni's group at EMBL with EMBL-EBI and Julio Saez-Rodriguez's lab, is that Europe PMC was built for humans, not for agents. On the way in, you need keyword syntax and a fielded query language, and because a gene has a symbol and a protein name and a disease has three synonyms, you need several complementary searches to get good recall. On the way out, you get whole ranked documents, most of which are irrelevant — and for an agent with a fixed context window, reading full papers to find one sentence burns the token budget fast.

The reflexive fix these days is retrieval-augmented generation: chunk the entire literature, embed every passage into a giant vector database, and let the agent pull the nearest passages in natural language. Petroni, of all people, knows that playbook cold — this is the lab lineage behind KILT and a lot of the early RAG work. And their argument here is essentially that, for this problem, the dense-vector reflex is wrong. Three reasons. It's expensive — OpenScholar's datastore alone is about seven hundred forty gigabytes of embeddings. The margin is shrinking — a well-tuned keyword backbone driven by a capable model issuing multiple queries now matches or beats dense retrieval on scientific benchmarks. And embeddings flatten structure: they throw away exactly the curated fields Europe PMC spent years building — gene, protein, organism, chemical, disease annotations — and a nearest-neighbor lookup isn't inspectable the way a fielded query is.

So EMBL AI Librarian maintains no index of its own. None. It's a single LLM controller sitting on top of Europe PMC's live search, and it works in three stages. First, planning: it takes your natural-language question and expands it into a set of complementary subqueries in Europe PMC's own advanced syntax — some keyword, some fielded, targeting titles, MeSH terms, gene and protein fields, organism constraints. Each one passes a deterministic syntax validator before it runs, so malformed queries get dropped rather than silently returning garbage. Second, recall: it fires those subqueries in parallel against the live engine, pulls the full-text XML, breaks each paper into paragraphs, and ranks them with classic keyword scoring. Third, precision: one LLM call reads the candidate sentences, filters the irrelevant ones, re-ranks what's left, and extracts the specific supporting sentences — and cleverly, it emits sentence identifiers rather than regenerating text, so it decodes far fewer tokens. What comes back isn't a pile of papers. It's a ranked set of citable evidence snippets, each clustered by source, tagged with its metadata, and carrying the exact query that found it — so the retrieval stays auditable end to end.

And it works. On ScholarQABench literature synthesis, Librarian lifts citation F-one by more than sixteen points over strong, recently published agentic baselines — including the dense-retrieval systems it's arguing against. Drop it in as the evidence layer of an existing claim-verification pipeline, and agreement with expert consensus goes up five points. Give a frontier model — they use GPT five point four — Librarian instead of plain web search, and open-form question answering climbs about eight points. On foundational biology tasks it adds up to four points, and over eleven on manipulating biological sequences. And because it's model-agnostic — any LLM can be the engine — it inherits the next model's gains for free.

Why this one matters for us specifically. The headline result is a benchmark win, but the real argument is architectural, and it cuts against where a lot of money and infrastructure are currently flowing. The bet is that you should not build a private seven-hundred-gigabyte embedding moat around the literature. You should wrap the open, curated, fielded search engine that the community already funds and maintains, and let an increasingly capable LLM drive it. That keeps the structured biological metadata intact, keeps every retrieval inspectable, and rides model progress instead of fighting it. It's a thesis about open science infrastructure dressed up as a retrieval paper — the knowledge layer as a shared public good rather than a proprietary index. The code, prompts, and eval pipelines are all released. If your agents are grounding themselves in the biomedical literature, this is the design worth copying.
