# NIBench — neuroimaging agents reproduce every number and still miss a quarter of the conclusions

Paper link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.15831

## Script

Today's paper separates three things we usually lump together when we say an agent "did the analysis": running the steps, deciding what the steps should be, and deciding what the result means. It's NIBench, a neuroimaging benchmark from Yonggang Shi's group at the USC Stevens Neuroimaging and Informatics Institute, the same team that built the NeuroAgent framework on the Claude Agent SDK earlier this year. The headline finding is that the third thing, judgment, is where the trouble is.

Start with the part that looks like good news. There are fifty-six real workflow tasks across FSL, FreeSurfer, MRtrix, AFNI and friends. They cover structural, diffusion and functional MRI and PET, plus a dozen booby-trapped inputs like flipped or zeroed diffusion gradients that don't make the software crash. Each task runs twice. One version hands the agent the full protocol: exact commands, flags and atlases. The other states the same goal, inputs and required outputs, but deletes the recipe and says to use standard defaults. Grading is deterministic, checking Dice overlap and numerical tolerances against reference outputs, with no language-model judge anywhere.

With the recipe, the best closed models are essentially perfect. Claude Opus 5.5 fully solves fifty-five of fifty-six. Take the recipe away and every one of nineteen models drops. The closed models lose about nine points on average and the open-weight models lose thirty-eight, mostly on multistep pipelines. The number of tasks that every model solved fell from twenty-nine to two. So a benchmark that supplies the protocol is mostly measuring transcription. The real gap between models shows up when the agent has to rebuild the workflow itself, which is what a researcher actually wants to delegate.

The second experiment tries to close that gap without touching weights. Agents distill their own trajectories into editable skill files, with leakage probes to make sure no answers sneak in. It works on the tasks the skills were distilled from. One GPT model jumps from seventy-three to ninety-eight percent there, while its score on the held-out half actually slips. Across five models, gains on familiar tasks ranged up to twenty-five points, while held-out changes ran from slightly negative to thirteen points. The thirteen-point gain had largely evaporated by the third round of distillation. Two details I liked: a skill library written by a stronger model often helped a weaker model more than its own did, and for at least one model the expert-written library still beat the distilled one. The lesson is that a distilled skill library is a lab artifact you can read, edit and share, but you have to validate it on tasks it wasn't grown from.

Now the part that should change how we audit agents. The team took thirty-six claims from three published Alzheimer's studies, on tau trajectories, cognitive decline and progression in amyloid- and tau-positive people. They handed Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6-astra the raw, unfiltered ADNI tables, about five thousand participants. Each agent had to build its own cohort, run the analysis, and call every claim supported, contradicted or undetermined, with code that regenerates its numbers. On reproducibility, all eighteen runs got perfect marks: every quantity and every sample size regenerated from a clean directory, and every predefined data-processing trap was avoided. On conclusions, both models matched the expert answer key exactly seventy-three percent of the time.

The identical aggregate hides opposite personalities. Neither model ever flipped supported into contradicted. Every error was about certainty. Claude was too sure eighteen times and too cautious eleven. GPT was the mirror image, too cautious twenty-two times and too sure seven. The worked examples are worth reading. On progression to dementia, six of thirty-six double-positive people progressed against two of three hundred forty-two negatives. GPT called it undetermined because of the small event count, even though its own confidence interval on the hazard ratio sat entirely above one and the claim was only about direction. On the tau-versus-cognition claim, Claude computed a bootstrap interval that included zero. It then overrode that interval because ninety-four percent of its analytic variants came out significant. But those variants all reused the same participants, so they weren't independent votes. That's a classic multiverse-analysis mistake made by an agent that did every computation correctly.

One caveat changes how hard to lean on this. The answer key is itself a judgment. Undetermined means things like the effect surviving only sixty-eight percent of participant resamples, so "too sure" partly means "drew the line somewhere other than these experts did." It's also two models, three studies and one heavily published cohort. A no-data control shows that much of the accuracy comes from prior knowledge, and giving Claude the data on the tau-trajectory question made it worse, flipping three claims from right to wrong.

Here's the take. We have spent a year building provenance, reproducibility checks and fabrication audits for scientific agents, and this paper shows those checks can all pass green while a quarter of the conclusions are wrong. Reproducibility certifies the numbers, not the inference. If you're putting an agent into an analysis pipeline, the thing to log and review isn't just the code. It's the decision rule that turned an interval into a verdict, and which way your particular model tends to lean when the evidence is thin.
