# Distributed biological reasoning — one agent per perturbation, two thousand at once, across a whole-brain Perturb-seq atlas

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.10.15.759436v1

## Script

Today's pick comes from close to home: Xin Jin's lab in neuroscience at Scripps Research, working with OpenAI and PerturbAI, the company Jin co-founded. And the idea at its center is so simple it's almost embarrassing that it works this well.

Here's the problem. In vivo Perturb-seq now lets you knock out nearly two thousand genes across the entire mouse brain and read out the transcriptome of every cell type. The lab published exactly that atlas earlier this year. Measuring is no longer the bottleneck. Interpretation is. Tens of thousands of perturbation-by-cell-type responses, and every one of them needs someone to look at the expression changes, read the relevant literature, and decide whether it means anything. The authors say plainly that even a team of experts left a lot of the biology in that dataset under-interpreted.

The obvious agentic move is to point one capable agent at the whole atlas. What they did instead was mirror the structure of the experiment: one agent per perturbation. Nineteen hundred forty-six agents, run in parallel, each given the cell-type-resolved differential expression for its gene, the experimental context, a shared coding environment, and literature access. Each writes a structured report with findings, quantitative evidence, caveats and proposed follow-up experiments. Those reports go into a knowledge graph linking perturbations, findings, genes, pathways and cell types, and a second layer of synthesis agents reads across that graph looking for convergence, antagonism and context-dependence. The whole first pass took thirty-two minutes.

The test that makes this credible is a blinding trick. They used OpenAI's life-sciences model, GPT-Rosalind, whose stated knowledge cutoff predates the atlas paper, and withheld the manuscript. Of the fifty-five published findings that could in principle be derived from the expression data alone, the agents matched or refined forty-eight and partially recovered four more. The findings they missed were the ones that needed data the agents were never given.

But the result I'd actually take away is the scaling experiment, because it isolates the architecture from the model. They held the model, the prompt, the tools and the data fixed, and gave a single agent between one and eight hundred ninety-three perturbations. As the workload grew, the single agent's steps and tool calls plateaued. It simply stopped digging. At full load it cited eleven references in total; the distributed version kept seven hundred eleven times more. And "no supporting literature found" came to dominate the single agent's output, while the distributed agents held steady. That's the honest version of a familiar intuition: a single agent with a huge task doesn't fail loudly, it quietly gets shallow. Parallelism here isn't about speed. It's about each question getting its own attention budget.

They also checked whether the literature judgments were real or just vibes. Each finding gets flagged as agreeing with prior work, inferred from it, contradicting it, or having no precedent. Then they shuffled gene labels while keeping the expression profiles intact. Agreement fell from about twenty-nine percent to thirteen with labels shuffled within the screen, and to six with genes from outside it. So the agents are actually checking whether this phenotype fits this gene, not rubber-stamping. The disagreement flags turned out useful in their own right: one flagged a Wdr26 prediction extrapolated from a fibroblast study of a different protein complex, and human review found that the extrapolation itself was the error. A contradiction flag works as a pointer to a shaky inference.

Two prediction tasks show what the agents add beyond summarizing. Asked to predict which held-out perturbations would produce any transcriptional response in thalamic neurons, they hit balanced accuracy of about seventy percent. My favorite call was Nup93, correctly predicted to do nothing, on the reasoning that nuclear pore scaffolds are so long-lived in neurons that the old protein outlasts the three to four weeks between editing and harvest. Then the overcalls turned into a finding. Several genes the agents expected to have big effects showed none, and those were among the strongest cell-depletion hits. The affected neurons had died, so there was nothing left to measure. So the authors gave the agents a second task, ranking which perturbations deplete neurons using transcriptional evidence alone. Distributed agents beat the single agent, a DepMap essentiality baseline and Biomni.

Finally, prioritization. Synthesis produced about thirty-four hundred relational findings, each with a priority score. Thirty doctoral-level biologists, blinded and barred from using generative AI, rated sixty reports. Human scores tracked the agent ranking, most clearly on follow-up priority, and in head-to-head comparisons experts preferred the distributed interpretation seventy-five percent of the time. The well-established Apc–Wnt story scored high on evidence and low on novelty, which is exactly how a good postdoc would triage it.

Now the caveats. One dataset, analyzed retrospectively, with no prospective experiment yet. Human scores ran from about two point six to three point one on a five-point scale, so the ranking is directionally right rather than decisive. The months-of-human-effort comparison is an illustrative estimate, not a measured human baseline, and the authors label it that way. Most of the authors are OpenAI or PerturbAI employees, and the model is OpenAI's. To their credit, the pipeline and skills are on GitHub, the atlas is on Hugging Face, and every raw agent report is on Zenodo, so anyone can check the claims.

For anyone who builds knowledge graphs, this is the part worth stealing. The graph here isn't a curated resource you query. It's the shared memory that lets two thousand narrow agents become one study-wide interpretation, with every edge pointing back to a report, a fold change and a citation. The general lesson: when your data comes in thousands of independent units, give each unit its own agent, and let a graph do the integrating.
