# Science sandboxes — measuring whether an agent learned the biology or just the score

Paper link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.30165

## Script

Today's pick came out of the Broad Institute yesterday, with Eric Lander and Pardis Sabeti among the senior authors, and it asks the question most agent benchmarks quietly dodge. When an agent posts a better score, has it learned anything about the system — or has it just found a shortcut?

Their instrument is what they call a science sandbox. You give an agent a class of specimens it can submit for testing — DNA sequences, proteins, chemicals, even abstract strings — and an assay. Behind a sealed wall sits an oracle that runs the assay and hands back a report. The agent proposes, the oracle answers, the agent revises, and around it goes. The crucial move is that the agent must keep a lab notebook: every hypothesis, every interpretation, every reason for choosing the next experiment. The notebook, not the score, is what gets graded.

The oracle comes in three flavors, and that taxonomy is the part worth stealing. A wet oracle runs an actual physical experiment — realistic, slow, expensive. A damp oracle is a predictive model trained on real data, cheap enough to iterate against but carrying every bias of the model underneath it. A dry oracle applies a rule the designers invented, which may have nothing to do with nature at all — no realism whatsoever, but exact ground truth about what the agent was supposed to discover. In software and mathematics you get verifiers for free. Empirical biology has none, so the dry oracle manufactures one.

The first instantiation, MPRAbox, is built on massively parallel reporter assays. The agent designs a library of fifty thousand two-hundred-base regulatory sequences, and the goal is not to find the strongest enhancers — it's to build the most informative training set for a sequence-to-activity model. The sealed oracle trains a fresh model on whatever the agent submitted and returns fourteen correlation numbers against hidden test sets. Not the sequences, not the labels, not even what the test sets are.

In one-shot play, Claude Opus 4.7 came out on top with a median correlation around point seven seven, edging past the best of fourteen expert-designed human strategies. GPT-5.5 and Gemini 3.5 Flash landed near point six six and point six eight. What separated them wasn't search skill — it was a prior. Claude reasoned that real genomic sequence carries the native context enhancers actually work in, and upweighted rare regulatory classes for calibration; its notebook reads, a library of only interesting elements teaches the model that everything is active. GPT went the other way, building fully synthetic motif sweeps to isolate causal features. Perfectly defensible regulatory genomics. Here it lost.

Then the authors ran the experiment that makes this a paper rather than a leaderboard. They repeated it, but first handed each agent a description of the fourteen human strategies and how each scored. GPT jumped from point six six to point seven six. Gemini from point six eight to point seven five. Claude, already there, moved almost not at all. Nearly the entire spread between frontier agents was knowing what tends to work, not reasoning better about why. That should temper how much anyone reads into a single-shot agent comparison.

Give Claude thirty rounds with feedback and the score barely improves — but the reasoning does. One run predicted a library of random DNA would score near zero, watched it score high, and rewrote its theory: much of the evaluation signal is composition-driven and free, so the library's real job is to teach grammar beyond composition. Another run muddled a comparison by changing two things at once, caught itself, and wrote down the lesson about isolating one variable. Nobody had instructed it to do that.

And now the part that should worry you. Keep everything, swap the biology for invented rules. Fourteen dry oracles: reward sequences containing exactly fifty cytosines, or particular bases at Fibonacci-numbered positions, or a hidden cipher in which pairs of bases decode into English letters, with real words scoring higher. The agent kept improving its score on many of these. It almost never found the rule. On the Fibonacci oracle it concluded the model was learning dinucleotide composition statistics — a story that genuinely predicted the feedback and was simply not what was happening. On the cipher, across three different framings, it never once entertained the idea that the sequences might spell words.

The companion sandbox, CodonBox, invents whole genetic codes — an arbitrary alphabet, arbitrary codon length, a hidden lookup table, and a toy lattice folding model. Maxing the score takes under ten rounds, because a single repeated character translates to an all-hydrophobic chain, so the real question becomes what the agent does with its remaining four hundred and ninety. With four letters and three-letter codons it maps the table. When one position was made silent, it found that with a handful of clean perturbations. When two positions interacted, a contradictory result broke its own theory, it changed strategy, and it recovered the interaction rule. But widen the alphabet to six letters and it never discovers that codons exist at all — it builds an elaborate theory of individual nucleotides and repeated runs that predicts scores correctly and misses the simpler structure generating them. Combine a silent position with interacting ones and it collapses back to cataloguing codons that happened to work before.

The authors' framing is epicycles, and it's the right one. Ptolemy predicted planetary positions beautifully and explained nothing. What these sandboxes show is that current agents are strong at searching inside a conceptual frame they already hold, and weak at inducing one they have no reason to anticipate — and a scoreboard cannot tell those apart. If you evaluate agents on scientific tasks, the actionable claim is that the reasoning trace is your measurement instrument, and a dry oracle with a known answer is how you calibrate it. They intend to release more sandboxes, and they're inviting other fields to build their own.
