# Cleared-catalog radiology agent — what an orchestrator can report with only FDA-cleared tools, and why "not looked for" gets written as "not there"

Paper link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09836

## Script

Today's paper is an arXiv preprint from Raidium, a Paris radiology AI company. It asks a question that sounds like market research but turns out to be a clean experiment about agents and their tools. Suppose you build a radiology agent: an orchestrator that plans a read, calls specialized models for detection, segmentation and measurement, and writes the report. Suppose the only tools it may call are the ones regulators have actually cleared. How much of a real report can it write?

The answer is under a third. Planning from the clinical indication, the way a real system would, the agent recovers twenty-nine percent of the clinically actionable findings. That's with tools that never make a mistake.

That last detail is the design choice that makes the paper worth reading. Every tool output is simulated from the reference report. If the agent asks whether there's a lung nodule and the report mentions one, the tool says yes. So tool accuracy is out of the picture completely. All that's left is which tools exist and whether the agent thinks to call them. It's an upper bound by construction, and it still comes out low.

Building the tool catalog was itself a knowledge-engineering job. The authors took the FDA's list of AI-enabled radiology devices, about eleven hundred and sixty of them, and pulled the public decision summary for each one. One LLM pass extracted the finding classes each device outputs, a second pass checked them against the summary, and a consolidation model merged synonyms. The result is about two thousand finding classes, each tied to the imaging modalities it's cleared for. In effect it's a controlled vocabulary of what regulated radiology AI can currently see. Fifty-nine percent of those classes rest on a single device.

Then they compared that catalog against an unlimited one, where the agent can ask for any finding class it likes. They also crossed it with a cheat condition where the planner gets to read the target report before planning. The four numbers are worth hearing together. Cleared tools with a realistic plan give twenty-nine percent. Unlimited tools with a realistic plan give forty-four. Cleared tools with a plan written from the answer give forty-three. Unlimited tools with the answer give seventy-four. In this setup, knowing what to look for is worth more than having more tools.

The most useful part of the paper is the attribution. Every finding the realistic agent missed, almost ten thousand of them, was assigned a cause. About sixty-two percent were catalog gaps: no cleared tool covers that finding on that modality. And the gaps aren't exotic. Pleural effusion is cleared for X-ray and ultrasound but not for CT. Same for ascites, mediastinal lymph nodes and atelectasis on CT. These are findings a first-year resident is trained to check on every study.

About a third of the misses are the agent's fault. The right tool was on the menu and never called. Vascular stenosis, aortic calcification, atherosclerosis and pleural effusion where it was available. These are the things a radiologist surveys on every study whatever the indication. An agent planning from "staging of endometrial cancer" goes looking for metastases and skips the routine sweep.

Their worked example shows why this matters clinically, not just as a recall number. On a chest CT for cancer staging, the agent found the lung nodules. The reference report also had a pleural effusion, lymphadenopathy and ascites, none of which have a cleared CT class. The generated report doesn't say "I couldn't assess the pleura." Its impression says no pleural effusion identified. The framework does let the agent flag an unavailable tool, but here a missing tool turned into a confident negative. A tool-using agent tends to treat "I had nothing to look with" and "I looked and found nothing" as the same thing. Your system needs an explicit way to tell them apart.

Two more results sharpen the point. First, catalog gaps have a long tail, but the head is cheap. Mine the missing classes from about six hundred reports, add the ninety-nine that recur in at least five of them, and on a hundred held-out reports recall rises from about thirty-five to fifty-two percent. That closes roughly three quarters of the gap to the unlimited catalog. Adding four hundred tools barely does better, and precision drops. Second, the orchestrator decides what the catalog is worth. Swap in a small model, either GPT-five-point-four nano or a nine-billion-parameter Qwen run locally, and even with every tool available it scores where the big model scored with the cleared catalog. The unlimited catalog was worth twenty-three points with the strong orchestrator and one point with the small one.

The caveats are the ones the authors state. The reports are fictional, from an open multilingual corpus. The tools are perfect, so real gains would be smaller. The held-out set is a hundred reports. The scorer is itself an LLM-based finding matcher, though every match is stored for audit.

Here's my take. People usually argue about tools versus models as if that were the axis. This paper separates three things: what the vocabulary covers, what the agent thinks to ask, and what the agent says when it can't ask. All three show up anywhere an agent works through a fixed set of tools, from knowledge-graph APIs to lab instruments. The cheapest fix in the paper isn't a better model. It's two pieces of plumbing: a coverage checklist that forces the routine survey, and a report format that records "not assessable with available tools" as a separate state from "absent." Even with perfect tools and a plan written from the answer, a quarter of the findings stay out of reach. Radiology isn't just tool composition. But most of the distance between today's cleared tools and that ceiling comes down to a vocabulary and a habit, and both are fixable.
