# Gemma curation agent — an LLM pipeline that curates genomics metadata to ontologies at near-human accuracy, one-twentieth the cost

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.07.30.741874v1

## Script

Today's paper is a status report from the front line of biomedical data curation, and it's the kind of result that should make anyone who maintains a curated database sit up. The group behind Gemma — the transcriptomics re-analysis resource out of Paul Pavlidis's lab at the University of British Columbia — has built an agent that does their sample-level curation at close to human accuracy, roughly one-twentieth the cost, and at least a hundred times the speed. And they're moving it into production.

Some context on why this is hard. Gemma has, since 2005, hand-curated more than twenty-three thousand transcriptomics studies, mostly pulled from GEO. Curation here doesn't mean slapping on a couple of keywords. It means reconstructing the experimental design — what the factors are, which sample groups they define — and grounding every feature in terms drawn from about thirteen ontologies, expressed as structured subject-predicate-object statements. Think "bortezomib, delivered at a dose of ten milligrams per kilogram, for one week." The information is buried in messy free-text GEO fields, in cryptic sample names, and often only in the paper itself. It takes a new curator months to get proficient, and even experienced curators agree with each other only somewhere between sixty and ninety percent of the time.

So there are really two stories here. The first is that they built the whole system using Claude Code — over a hundred thousand lines of Python, iterated across dozens of pipeline versions in a matter of weeks. Their point is blunt: they would not have attempted a system this complex the old way, because the cost of building it before knowing whether it would even work was prohibitive. The coding agent removed that barrier. That's a meta-lesson worth sitting with — the agent's biggest contribution may have been to the engineering, not the inference.

The second story is the design of the pipeline itself, and this is where the paper earns its keep. The guiding principle is mechanical-first: lean on deterministic, non-LLM steps wherever possible, and only escalate to a language model when judgment is genuinely required. A typical study takes about twenty-five LLM calls, split across a tiered lineup — a cheap fast model for the easy stuff, a mid model, and the strongest reasoning model for the hard cases. And every single annotation carries provenance: the model is always required to emit supporting quotes and justifications, and any ontology term the LLM proposes is never trusted on its own — it gets validated by separate mechanical and LLM stages. Verifiability is baked in, not bolted on.

The evaluation is genuinely thoughtful, because the naive version is a trap. Two curations that disagree can both be correct. So a straight string match would punish the agent for using a synonym, or for being more complete than the humans were. They built an aligner to match up two annotation sets, then an arbiter — and a stronger "boss" model for the hard cases — to judge which side is actually right. And here's the kicker: the agent repeatedly caught genuine errors in Gemma's own human curation. Two genes mixed up, that sort of thing. The auditor cuts both ways.

On the numbers: the agent almost always finds that a factor exists — recall of ninety-eight percent — but pinning the exact factor value is harder, an F-score around point-six, and whole-experiment tags are lower still. Crucially, though, outright factual errors were rare — eight genuine ones across four hundred datasets. Most of the gap was the agent not perfectly following Gemma's idiosyncratic house rules, not getting the biology wrong.

A few honest findings I want to flag, because they cut against the current hype. First, the multi-agent back-and-forth adjudication — the thing everyone reaches for — was only partly effective. Their repeated experience was that improving the proposer directly beat adding another adjudication layer. Self-revision loops added almost nothing past the first pass. Second, when they built a triage classifier to predict which outputs need human review, they got a decent signal for factual errors — but the LLM's own stated confidence was actively anti-predictive, worse than a coin flip at flagging its own mistakes. If you build agent pipelines, that one should stick.

Where this points is the part I find most provocative. At about fifty cents a study, they think they could re-curate the entirety of Gemma in a year or two, and eventually curate all of GEO. And they float shifting curation upstream — to submission time, or even to the lab bench as the experiment is being run, if a group's coding agent just handles it as routine. With curated data increasingly consumed by other AI systems rather than by humans, the economics of getting metadata right are about to change.

The code, the benchmark of five hundred studies, and the evaluation framework are all open. Given how little the field agrees on what good curation even is, that benchmark may end up mattering as much as the agent.
