# LifeSciBench — 750 expert-written life-science tasks, and the best model finishes a third of them

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.08.13.744657v1

## Script

OpenAI put a benchmark on bioRxiv this past week, and the interesting thing about it is not the leaderboard. LifeSciBench is 750 tasks written by 173 scientists with doctorates and industry bench experience, independently reviewed by another 453 experts, spanning seven research workflows and seven biological domains. The best model tested, which they call GPT-Rosalind, earns a mean rubric score of 0.576 and passes 36.1% of tasks. On 171 of the 750, no model produced a single passing response.

Start with what makes the construction different, because that's the part worth stealing. Most life-science evaluations ask well-posed questions with checkable answers — retrieve this fact, run this analysis, get this number. LifeSciBench deliberately does the opposite. The prompts are written the way you'd hand a problem to a knowledgeable colleague, with the ambiguity left in, and the model is graded not on a final answer but against a rubric the task author wrote — nineteen thousand three hundred eighty-nine criteria across the benchmark. Some award points for stating an assumption or reaching for the right evidence. Some deduct points for a named failure mode. Partial credit is the default, and a response only counts as passing at seventy percent of available points.

That choice produces the paper's most useful number, which is the gap between the two metrics. A rubric score of 0.576 sitting next to a 36.1% pass rate means models routinely get most of the way there and can't close. For GPT-Rosalind specifically there are 109 tasks where it collects at least half the rubric points and still passes fewer than one time in five. It finds the right evidence, executes part of the reasoning, and then misses something the expert considered non-negotiable. If you're deciding whether to hand a model a real piece of work, that's the actual shape of the risk — not obvious wrongness, but competent-looking incompleteness.

Two failure modes show up in every model, and both should bother anyone building agents for bench science. The first is artifacts. A little over a thousand files are attached across these tasks — microscopy images, gel images, raw instrument outputs, sequence files, molecular structures, PDFs. GPT-Rosalind passes 44.6% of text-only tasks and 28.6% of tasks with a file attached. The authors anticipated the obvious objection, that artifact tasks are simply harder, and regressed the gap against workflow type, reasoning depth, answer format, rubric count, and prompt length. Adjustment shrinks it by somewhere between eighteen and thirty-eight percent, and it stays negative for every single model. Real research is almost entirely artifact-mediated. The hole is in exactly the place that matters.

The second shared weakness is producing things. Rubric criteria labeled generate-or-construct — emit an actual nucleotide sequence, a working construct, a chemical structure — score lowest of twelve operation categories for every model without exception, topping out at 0.418. Models discuss a design fluently and then can't hand you the object. For anyone thinking about agentic workflows, that's the whole difference between an assistant and a collaborator.

There's also a result that quietly undercuts leaderboard thinking. GPT-5.5 and Gemini 3.1 Pro are indistinguishable in aggregate, 0.519 against 0.515, confidence intervals overlapping. But Gemini was the single best model on 214 of the 750 tasks and GPT-5.5 on only 61. Task-level score correlation between the two GPT models is 0.93; between Gemini and GPT-Rosalind it's 0.64. The families are failing on different things, and one headline number hides all of it.

Now the part I'd flag hardest, and it's structural rather than a knock on the science. OpenAI built this benchmark. An OpenAI model wins it. GPT-5.5 is the judge grading every response. The expert authors are anonymous, coordinated through a vendor, unnamed at the vendor's request. And the data availability statement says public release of the tasks, rubrics, and artifacts may be limited by licensing, privacy, proprietary information, or biological safety considerations. Read plainly: don't count on seeing it.

Some of those constraints are legitimate. There is genuine dual-use content in a life-science benchmark and I'd rather they thought about it than not. But the net effect is that the most carefully constructed evaluation of AI on real life-science work is one nobody outside the lab that built it can run, contest, or verify. LAB-Bench is open. BixBench is open. The rubric-based methodology here is the real contribution and I want people to copy it — which is precisely what an unreleased benchmark makes hard.

One more limit, which the authors state and I'll amplify. Every evaluation is single-turn: one prompt, one response, no follow-up, no clarifying question. Models did get a full containerized scientific stack — BLAST, Scanpy, DESeq2, RDKit, GROMACS, PyMOL — and unrestricted internet access. So this is a well-tooled model, not an agent that plans, checks its own work, and revises. Thirty-six percent is a floor for agentic systems rather than a ceiling. But it also isn't an estimate of what any of this does to your research productivity, and to their credit the closing ask is that somebody correlate benchmark performance against real deployment studies in live labs. Almost nobody does that. Until somebody does, every number in this space — these included — is a proxy of unknown quality for the thing we actually care about.
