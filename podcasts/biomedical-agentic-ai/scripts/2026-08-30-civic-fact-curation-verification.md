# CIViC-Fact — an LLM checks a cancer variant knowledgebase, and the abstract turns out not to be enough

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.09.10.675443v3

## Script

If you have ever curated a biomedical knowledgebase, you know the shape of this problem. CIViC — the Clinical Interpretation of Variants in Cancer — is the open, crowdsourced one: anyone can submit a structured claim that some variant in some cancer predicts a response to some drug, backed by a citation. Crowdsourcing solved the writing problem, and in doing so it created a reviewing problem. Every submission has to be checked against the source paper by a small team of expert editors, and that check is now the rate-limiting step. A preprint updated this week out of Canada's Michael Smith Genome Sciences Centre and the CIViC group at Washington University asks whether a language model can help, and the interesting part is what they found along the way.

Start with the finding that should change how you read every biomedical language-model paper. They took eighty-five development entries where the claim was verifiable from text and the full article was available, and asked one question: could a curator have validated this from the abstract? Twenty-eight percent, yes. Forty percent required evidence found entirely outside the abstract. The rest were only partly supported. So roughly seven in ten real curation decisions cannot be made from the abstract at all. And these are long documents — averaging thirty-two thousand characters, more than five times the length of the documents in LongHealth, which is itself sold as a long-document biomedical benchmark. A claim needs two supporting passages on average, sometimes fifteen, scattered across distant parts of the paper. One example in the paper needs three: one passage to establish that the disease is high-grade glioma, a second describing the erlotinib response in preclinical experiments, and a third purely to disambiguate which variants were actually tested. Almost the entire biomedical fact-checking literature is built on abstracts. That literature has been grading itself on the easy third.

The second surprise is what AI-assisted review should even mean. They hand-audited ninety-four CIViC entries. Seventy needed no change at all. Five contained clear curation errors. The other nineteen are the interesting ones — nine were technically correct but could be stated more precisely, pick three C A overexpression rather than just expression, and ten needed updating to a data-model feature that did not exist when they were curated. Complex molecular profiles only arrived in CIViC in twenty twenty-three, and a lot of the knowledgebase predates them. So the dominant work here is not catching people being wrong. It is catching entries that have drifted out of alignment with a schema and a set of guidelines that keep moving. That reframes the tool. It is not a fact-checker. It is a drift detector for legacy content that nobody would otherwise ever revisit.

Then the part I would put in front of anyone building a curation assistant. They compared three systems: few-shot GPT five point two, few-shot Gemma four, and a fine-tuned three-billion-parameter Llama. On the static benchmark the small fine-tuned model is competitive, seventy-seven percent against eighty-two and seventy-nine. Fine-tuning works when your training and test data match. Then they ran the same three on genuinely new CIViC submissions collected months after the benchmark cutoff, with a human curator assigning reference labels in parallel. On claims where the retrieved text actually contained the answer, the few-shot models hit sixty-four percent and the fine-tuned model forty-five. But the real gap is elsewhere. On claims where the evidence was not sufficient — where the answer lived in a supplementary file or a figure — Gemma correctly flagged eighty-eight percent of them and GPT seventy-five. The fine-tuned model correctly flagged zero. None. It had learned to always produce a verdict.

That is the failure mode worth naming. The hard skill in an assistive curation system is not verification. It is abstention — recognizing that what you were handed does not settle the question. And the authors give a second, quieter reason the fine-tuned model lost: during the study, CIViC's own curation guidelines changed. Should a preclinical result in a breast cancer cell line be filed under breast cancer, or under the pan-cancer scope the study authors intended? Should a secondary resistance mutation carry its driver mutation along into the profile? Those were genuinely ambiguous, experienced editors disagreed, and the guidance was revised. A frozen fine-tuned model encodes last year's answer and cannot be told otherwise. A few-shot prompt is updated by editing a handful of examples. For a living knowledgebase, that is not a minor engineering preference. It is the difference between a system that ages with the standard and one that fights it.

The honest limitations land in the same place. Retrieval works — appropriate evidence surfaced for thirty-seven of forty new entries, good enough to point a curator at the right paragraphs. Labeling works well enough for triage. Proposing the actual corrected value does not: the models pick the right field to fix and then fill it with a deprecated disease ontology term. Semantically plausible, mechanically unusable. Which points exactly where you would expect it to — put the current ontology, its preferred terms and its hierarchy, into the context at inference time.

The authors are refreshingly clear that they are not shipping a model. They are shipping a benchmark with provenance attached, built to be rerun as the models turn over underneath it. Given how fast the model of the month changes, that is almost certainly the more durable artifact.
