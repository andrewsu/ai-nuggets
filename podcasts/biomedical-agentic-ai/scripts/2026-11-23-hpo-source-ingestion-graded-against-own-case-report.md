# Observed, Curated, Reused — rare-disease rankers score well when HPO has already read the patient's own case report

Paper link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27301

## Script

Here is a leak that no amount of patient-level deduplication will catch. A rare-disease benchmark takes a child described in a published case report, turns that child's symptoms into a query, and asks a diagnostic tool to rank the right disease. Meanwhile, Human Phenotype Ontology curators read the same case report and add those symptoms to the disease's annotation profile, with the paper cited as the source. The patient is nominally held out. Their phenotypes are not. Today's paper, from Richard Röttger's group at the University of Southern Denmark and accepted to the ML4H findings track, measures how much of rare-disease diagnostic accuracy comes from exactly that loop. The answer is most of it.

The design is clean, and it works because HPO keeps provenance. Every disease-phenotype annotation cites its source, and HPO publishes versioned releases. The authors took about sixty-five hundred cases from Phenopacket Store, the GA4GH corpus of literature-derived patient phenotypes, and lined them up against six HPO releases from October 2021 to June 2026. For each case, they found the first release in which HPO cited that case's publication. That gives a natural experiment: a before snapshot, an after snapshot, and the patient's query held fixed throughout. Then they added a counterfactual. In the after release, they deleted every annotation citing the patient's own paper and re-ran the ranking. They split by publication, not by patient, and held out about a thousand cases from a hundred fifty-four papers before looking at any results.

The primary ranker is Resnik semantic similarity, the classic phenotype-matching baseline, chosen because you can follow term by term how an annotation moves a disease up the list. Mean reciprocal rank was about a quarter before HPO ingested the paper. It jumped to point eight four after ingestion. And with the source-linked annotations removed from that same later release, it fell to point zero five. Of nine hundred ninety-one cases, nine hundred seventy-four improved because of their own paper, seventeen tied, and none got worse. LIRICAL, the likelihood-ratio tool many listeners will have used, shows the same effect at the same size, and an information-content cosine ranker shows it at about half the size.

What makes this convincing rather than just suggestive is the set of controls. Papers from the same period that HPO did not ingest moved the needle by essentially nothing, so this is not general ontology growth. Deleting evidence for the competing diseases did nothing; deleting evidence for the correct disease did everything. Synthetic phenotype additions matched for count, information content, depth and organ system came nowhere close to the real ones, so it is the content of the patient's own phenotypes that matters, not just profile size. And when they restricted removal to annotations whose only citation was the patient's paper, the effect shrank to roughly half but stayed large. That shrinkage is worth noting, because the strict removal also strips facts that other papers independently support, which is part of why the source-removed score falls below the before score.

The intuition is simple once you see it. These rankers don't learn from patients at all. Everything they know is in the disease profiles. So if the profile for a disease was partly written from this exact patient, the ranker is matching the patient against a description of themselves. That is retrieval, not diagnosis. The authors put it well: diagnosis is prospective inference under uncertainty, and a benchmark can't claim to measure it if the system has covert access to the publication that solved the case.

Two side audits reinforce the larger theme of what survives curation. Of about ninety-seven hundred disease-phenotype links in published case reports, only about thirty-five percent were in HPO's 2021 release, and which ones made it in is predictable rather than random. Downstream graphs like PrimeKG and OpenBioLink largely follow HPO: a link HPO captured was about eleven times more likely to show up there than one HPO missed. That's propagation, not independent confirmation. If you build a knowledge graph by stacking resources, you should remember that agreement among them is often one source repeated. In the electronic health record audits, fragmenting a patient's timeline destroyed the ability to tell which diagnosis came first far more than dropping the same amount of data as whole patients.

Why does this belong on an agentic AI show? Because the rare-disease benchmarks now used to grade language models and agents are built from these same Phenopacket cases, and the agents being built for differential diagnosis increasingly call HPO, Monarch or a phenotype-similarity tool as their grounding step. An agent that queries the current HPO at test time inherits this leak directly. A language model trained on the literature may have read the case report itself. That isn't a result of this paper, which only tested classical rankers, but the mechanism carries straight over, and it means a recent comparison between LLMs and traditional tools on these cases may be partly a comparison of how much access each had to the answer key.

The fix the authors propose is about bookkeeping, not modeling. They tried several scoring tweaks, including frequency weighting, penalties for missing expected phenotypes, and a small neural model on phenotype qualifiers, and none recovered performance after source removal. What they recommend instead is a reporting checklist: record where each test case came from, which knowledge-resource versions the system could see, whether test sources overlap with that knowledge, and whether the knowledge matches the intended decision date. In short, run historical evaluations against the ontology release that existed before the case was published.

My take: this is one of the most useful things you can do with a well-curated open resource, and it's possible only because HPO cites its sources and versions its releases. Provenance is what made the leak measurable. Anyone benchmarking a diagnostic agent on literature-derived cases should now report a time-correct, source-excluded score alongside the headline number. If the two disagree, the headline is measuring the curators, not the agent.
