# BioMem — auditing agentic memory on biomedical tasks, and finding that the notes an agent keeps rewriting are the ones that drift from their source

Paper link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13426

## Script

Today's paper takes something most agent builders treat as plumbing and audits it as a scientific instrument. The plumbing is memory. Agents now keep persistent stores of notes: what a patient said three sessions ago, what a past case looked like, which drug interacts with which protein. The paper is from Hong Yu's group at UMass, with collaborators at Minnesota and Florida. They ask a plain question that nobody had asked carefully in biomedicine. When an agent writes something into memory, retrieves it later and reasons from it, how much of the original information survives the trip?

The setup is a wide comparison. There are five memory strategies. Three are the popular agentic memory systems: A-MEM, which builds linked notes and keeps revising them, plus Mem0 and LightMem, which extract and consolidate. Next to those sit two dumb baselines: plain retrieval over the original records, and simply putting everything in the context window. Each strategy runs on four open-weight models. There are three kinds of task. Two are knowledge questions over pharmacology and Alzheimer's relational knowledge. Four are clinical predictions from MIMIC and TCGA records. And one is a set of long medical conversations spread over many sessions.

The headline result is that no memory system wins. Which one comes out on top flips with the task and with the backbone model. Across the traces, the elaborate memory systems retrieved the right fact no more often than plain retrieval did. All that structure bought no clear coverage advantage.

The finding I'd carry away is about fidelity. A-MEM doesn't just store notes; it evolves them. When a new record comes in, older related notes get their context rewritten to reflect it. The authors went back and used a model to check each stored note against the original record it came from. They asked whether the note now said things the source never said. For the opioid-overdose task, notes with unsupported content went from twelve percent at creation to forty-four percent after evolution. On the pharmacology knowledge set, it went from sixteen to thirty-five. The dose-response is the striking part. Among notes that were never rewritten, roughly one in ten carried unsupported content. Among notes rewritten five or more times, it was about eight in ten. Every rewrite is a chance to drift, and the notes rewritten most often are the hub notes that the rest of the memory links to.

That should sound familiar to anyone who curates knowledge. In Wikidata, or any well-run knowledge graph, a statement carries a reference to where it came from, and an edit doesn't quietly erase that link. A self-revising memory has no such discipline. The note keeps the authority of having been written down while slowly losing its connection to anything that was observed. Last month we covered PolyGraph, which showed that rebuilding the same agent-built graph twice gives you tens of thousands of differing artifacts. This is the same problem measured differently: each note is checked against its own source, and the error grows with every revision. And the checker here is itself a model, so treat the exact percentages as estimates. The rewrite trend is the robust part.

Two more results change how you'd design one of these systems. The first is that retrieving the right answer is not the same as using it. On the knowledge tasks, accuracy was in the high seventies to high eighties when an answer-bearing record was retrieved, and in the low to mid teens when none was. That part is expected. But about a fifth to a quarter of all questions got a wrong answer that matched something sitting in retrieved memory. When everything was in context, that rate was around ten percent. Retrieval puts plausible competing answers right next to the question, and the model takes the bait.

The second is that similarity is the wrong retrieval objective for prediction. On pathology reports, retrieving similar reports worked beautifully: the retrieved neighbors were more than fifteen times enriched for the right cancer type. On readmission, retrieved neighbors were often less likely than chance to share the patient's outcome. Two patients with similar charts can have very different futures. A memory store that finds look-alikes is answering a different question from the one a risk model asks.

The conversational results run the other way from the knowledge results. On the knowledge tasks, stuffing a thousand records into the context did worst. Across long patient conversations, the full history beat every memory system. The margin was ten points over A-MEM and thirty-nine over plain retrieval. So compressing a knowledge base into memory helps, and compressing one patient's own history into memory loses things you needed.

The authors' fix, which they call HMRU, is sensible and modest. It combines keyword and embedding retrieval, reranks the results, and adds instructions about how to use what came back, all without retraining. It adds four to five points of exact-match F1 on the knowledge tasks and closes about half the gap to full context in conversation. It doesn't touch the fidelity problem, and that problem is the real contribution here.

My take is that agentic memory in biomedicine should be treated as a curation problem, not a storage problem. Keep a pointer from every note back to its source record. Treat rewrites as edits with a history. And re-check the most-rewritten notes, since that's where the drift piles up. An agent that remembers fluently but can't say where a memory came from is not much better than a model that never remembered anything at all.
