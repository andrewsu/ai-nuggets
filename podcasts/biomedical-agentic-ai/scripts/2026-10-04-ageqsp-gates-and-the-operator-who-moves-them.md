# AgeQSP — a gated agentic modelling workflow whose most useful output is the record of what it refused to do

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.09.29.755460v1

## Script

Today's paper opens with a line that could be the thesis statement for this whole beat: generation is no longer the scarce step; checking is. An agentic workflow can propose combination therapies, build the mechanistic models to support them, and design the trial, faster than a team can verify any of it. So a group in Edinburgh built a workflow where a hypothesis can be generated freely but only reaches a report by clearing three gates — precedent and evidence, model integrity, and release — and where every failure exit is logged, so the effect of each gate on the final list can be counted rather than asserted. Then they ran it end to end on a real programme and published the audit trail, including the parts that make them look bad.

The domain is quantitative systems pharmacology for healthy ageing. The agent starts from a thirty-four-state model that includes semaglutide and metformin branches, calibrated to published weight, inflammation and insulin-resistance trajectories. The first gate it hits is external validation. The locked semaglutide model is tested, with nothing refitted, against an independent reanalysis of a thirty-two-week randomised trial that measured DNA-methylation clocks. All nine organ and system clocks moved in the direction the model predicted. The magnitudes were off by more than an order of magnitude: about minus a seventh of a year per year predicted, against observed reductions of one-point-six to five years per year. And the failure is structural, not a tuning problem — a global sensitivity sweep over three thousand parameter sets puts the entire plausible range of the model's whole-system effect nowhere near the observation. The workflow returned HOLD, refused to promote the validation tier, and every biological-clock claim stayed out of the programme from that point on.

Then it generates seven combination hypotheses on a gut-immune axis, encodes all of them into one SBML extension as switchable branches, and runs identifiability analysis. This is where the paper earns its keep. Forty-eight new parameters, one hundred and forty-seven design observations. Five parameters are identifiable, and all five are observation scales — bookkeeping, not biology. Six more are weak or confounded. The other thirty-seven are flatly non-identifiable, including every rate for the two lead agents, every short-chain-fatty-acid term, and every coupling into the systemic states. The worst parameter has a relative standard error of sixteen thousand percent. And the interaction itself, the thing the whole exercise exists to estimate, is invisible: swinging the interaction coefficient from minus a half to plus a half moves the primary model output by six parts in a thousand.

So the workflow throws almost all of it away. Seven hypotheses become one. Forty-eight parameters become six. The interaction term is locked at zero until randomised factorial data exist. From that reduced model it derives a two-by-two factorial trial with a single primary endpoint and an estimand that can actually be rejected, powered at three hundred and fifty-six participants. And a nine-thousand-person virtual trial then finds the design's weak point before anyone spends money: a stool assay floor, below which thirty-seven percent of simulated baselines fall. Substitute the floor for censored values, the standard thing a statistician does by reflex, and power drops from eighty-one percent to thirty-three, with the estimate pulled toward the null and coverage collapsing to thirty percent. That is cheap to fix now and ruinously expensive to discover after a protocol lock.

But the reason I picked this paper is the section where the authors turn the audit on the gates themselves, and four things fall out.

Formal model validation couldn't run, three times, because the validators wouldn't install in the environment. At the operator's request the policy was changed to accept any qualified validation route, and a substitute execution route passed. That substitute agreed with the reference model to eight decimal places — and still waved through a malformed time reference that left the released model unloadable in a standard simulator. The broken element was the event that starts the washout. And the washout, their own optimal design analysis says, is where fifteen of the twenty informative samples sit. So the gate that got redefined was guarding exactly the thing that carried the model's identifiability. Their conclusion is the right one: the audit trail made the change visible. It did not prevent it.

Second, detection of simulated evidence ran on keyword matching, and five real-looking fixtures passed as genuine in one batch. A label is not provenance. Third, the signed manifests are SHA-two-fifty-six digests, not signatures bound to an identity. And fourth, the one every agent framework should post on the wall: of the last seventeen pipeline stages, ten returned PASS while doing nothing on data at all, because they were generated as a batch from a shared template and built runbooks rather than running anything. A PASS means a stage met its own criteria. It does not mean the programme advanced. If you are scoring agentic pipelines by stage completion, you are measuring template expansion.

Now the caveats, and they are real. This is one group's single programme run over three days, from a company with a patent pending, which is why the two lead compounds appear only as Agent A and Agent B with their source studies withheld. You cannot check the biology. Nearly every combination result is explicitly labelled lowest tier — assumed kinetics, assumed noise, noise-free synthetic data. And the scripts behind the sample-size table and the virtual trial aren't in the archive, so those specific numbers can't be regenerated from it. A paper arguing for auditability that ships a partially unreproducible audit is an awkward object.

But the reporting checklist they propose at the end is portable, and it's the part worth stealing: report the number of proposals generated before gating and not only the survivors; report parameter provenance and identifiability for anything that drives a ranking; report the null model of non-interaction; and report every gate that was redefined, waived or overridden, and by whom. That last item is the one nobody currently reports, and this paper is evidence it's the one that matters.
