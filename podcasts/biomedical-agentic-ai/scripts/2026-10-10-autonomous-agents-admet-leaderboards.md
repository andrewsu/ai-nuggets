# Autonomous LLM agents on the ADMET leaderboards — fourth of 104 against human teams, in twenty-four hours on one GPU

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.10.01.755314v1

## Script

There is a version of the autonomous-scientist question that is easy to dismiss, and a version that is not. The easy version is whether an agent can write a paper. The hard version is whether an agent can do the part in the middle — the method development. Take a prediction problem someone actually cares about, decide on the representations, build the models, engineer the features, calibrate, ensemble, and come out with something that beats what the specialists built. A preprint out of Deep Origin this week takes a serious run at that, and the headline result is that current agents can.

The setup is clean and worth repeating. The agents got a fresh sandboxed account, one H100, sixteen virtual CPUs, and the official protocol for each benchmark — the prescribed splits, the prescribed metrics, the published scoring code. Humans set up the environment, wrote the task prompt, and audited the traces afterward. No scientific guidance, no code edits, no picking the winning model.

On the Therapeutics Data Commons ADMET benchmark group — twenty-one endpoints, everything from Caco-2 permeability to drug-induced liver injury — the agents were required to build one method that works across all twenty-one, not a bespoke pipeline per task. The references they were measured against are endpoint-specific, each one someone's best effort on that single problem. Taking the best score per endpoint across agent configurations, the agents beat the leaderboard reference on eighteen of twenty-one, and beat the tougher reference that folds in peer-reviewed publications on seventeen. A single Codex agent, running one method across all twenty-one tasks, beat that combined reference on thirteen.

Now, that is the weakest evidence in the paper, and the authors say so. These are public benchmarks with public solutions, and whether any of that sat in the model's pretraining data cannot be audited. So the part that moves me is the two OpenADMET challenge replays, where the agents were put up against a hundred-plus real human teams on data from actual lead-optimization campaigns.

On the ExpansionRx challenge — nine regression endpoints, trained on early lead-optimization compounds and tested on later ones, so a temporal split rather than a scaffold split — the five-agent Codex team landed an overall score that would place fourth out of a hundred and four entries, about five percent worse than the winner. It reached top-five placement on seven of the nine endpoints. It did this in a twenty-four hour replay with thirty submissions, against a field that had roughly eighty-five submission opportunities over the real schedule.

On the PXR induction challenge, which asks for potency values on close chemical analogues of screening hits and keeps the final analog set blinded, the five-agent team's mean absolute error came in just under the best published entry. First by that metric, out of a hundred and three final submissions — though the authors are careful that this is not a statistically meaningful separation from the leader. My favorite detail in the whole paper is a sentence in the limitations: in that PXR run, three of the five agents died partway through on model-service capacity errors. A crippled team still took the top line.

And on the Practical Molecular Optimization benchmark, which is a different capability — not predicting, but deciding which molecule to score next under a hard budget of ten thousand oracle calls — the agent-built optimizer scored well above the benchmark paper's best, and above the closest recent method evaluated under the same pretraining data and the same call budget. That last qualifier is doing real work; several higher published numbers come from runs with extra pretraining data or a different budget, and the paper walks through which is which. That kind of apples-to-apples bookkeeping is itself a decent signal about how carefully this was run.

What did the agents actually build? Nothing exotic. Ensembles of tabular models over frozen pretrained molecular embeddings — three-dimensional conformation embeddings, two-dimensional graph embeddings — plus fingerprints and ionization descriptors, with endpoint-specific selection. For the receptor-potency task they added per-residue protein-ligand features pulled out of frozen structure-prediction models. That is exactly the work a strong computational chemist does: not inventing an architecture, but knowing which seven things to combine and how to validate the combination. The agents are competitive at integration and engineering, which happens to be where most of the value in applied modeling lives.

Three things worth carrying away. First, more agents is not better. On the ADMET benchmark group, a single Claude Code agent beat its own five- and ten-agent versions on seventeen and fifteen of twenty-one tasks. On ExpansionRx, the five-agent team beat the single agent on eight of nine. Team size is a task-dependent setting, not a dial you turn up. And the mixed team — five Claude agents plus five Codex agents — was the worst configuration tested and produced no best score anywhere.

Second, transfer is directional. The method developed for the lead-optimization challenge, moved unchanged onto the ADMET benchmark group, still beat the strong reference on eight of twenty-one. The method developed on the benchmark group, moved onto the challenge, would have placed fifty-first of a hundred and four. Developing against live feedback produces something more portable than developing against a static leaderboard.

Third, and this is the authors' own closing line, the benchmarks here are retrospective, and retrospective wins are a test of capability, not of usefulness. The experiment that would settle this is prospective: a problem with no published solution, and new measurements at the end. Nobody has run that yet. But this is the most convincing evidence I have seen that when somebody does, the agent is going to show up competitive.
