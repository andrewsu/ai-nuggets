# Stress-testing LLM lab agents — a real robotic chemistry lab shows plausible plans, 3.3% that actually run, and no genuine replanning

Paper link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.23045

## Script

Today's paper is a reality check on the dream of the autonomous lab, and it's the most rigorous one I've seen. A team at the University of Science and Technology of China took a real, general-purpose robotic chemistry lab — forty-five automated workstations spanning the full research cycle, everything from liquid handling and centrifugation to electrochemical and photocatalysis reactors to X-ray diffraction and chromatography — and turned it into a benchmark for what they call scientific agency. Not whether a model can write a plausible protocol, but whether it can convert scientific intent into physical action that a robot will actually carry out.

Here's the setup, because the setup is the contribution. The lab's capabilities are exposed to the agent as machine-readable skills — sixty-two operations, and crucially, four hundred thirty-three hard constraints and hundreds of parameters. A single liquid-handling station will tell the agent it accepts at most sixteen source solutions, caps one transfer at a milliliter, enforces a fixed bottle order. Any chemically reasonable plan that violates those limits gets rejected before it ever reaches a robot. The lab keeps execution authority — a workflow only crosses the physical boundary if it passes schema, state, and compilation checks. So the agent has to reason inside the real physical envelope of the instruments, not in the abstract.

They ran this at scale: six agent harnesses — think Claude Code, Codex, and others — paired with nine frontier language models, forty-eight configurations, thirty-two expert-written catalysis tasks, three trials each. Forty-six hundred trials in all.

And the headline number is sobering. Only three point three percent of trials produced a workflow that domain experts judged actually executable on the robot. The single best system — Claude Code driving Claude Opus 4.7 — reached twenty-eight percent. Out of those forty-six hundred trials, only about six hundred even reached verified dispatch, and only a hundred fifty-one were truly executable. These agents are fluent at proposing experiments. They are not yet reliable at producing one the machine can run.

The first deep finding is that competence lives in the whole stack, not in the model. Plan quality and actual execution turned out to be almost decoupled. One pairing wrote beautiful, high-scoring structured plans and produced zero verified dispatches. Another, running a weaker-scoring model in the same slot, wrote mediocre plans but got forty-one experiments into the lab. High reasoning scores simply did not predict who could get a robot moving. That should make anyone quoting a plan-only leaderboard nervous.

But the finding that stuck with me is about adaptation. They took their strongest planner and put it in open-ended goal mode — a genuine closed loop: design an experiment, run it on the robot, read the data, replan. Pick your own catalyst, your own protocol, five rounds. And the agent did a lot right. It chose alkaline urea oxidation, designed a twenty-one-step workflow, held its goal and its context across all five rounds, and tuned parameters intelligently — concentrations, reaction times, spin speeds, how widely it spread its formulations.

What it never did was step back and rethink the plan. Its round-one design had flaws a chemist would catch in seconds. It loaded so much carbon powder that it would swamp the catalyst's signal in the very characterization step meant to read it out. It left the binder out of the electrode. It specified UV-Vis measurements without the analyte-specific color reagents you actually need to quantify the products. The authors deliberately left these errors in — they wanted to see whether feedback from failed experiments would make the agent recognize and correct its own design. It didn't. Across five rounds and repeated null results — round one recovered almost no material and gave no valid electrochemical readout — the agent kept turning knobs inside its original plan but never triggered workflow-level replanning, and never redesigned its analytical method. It optimized within the box. It never questioned the box.

That's the line between parameter search and scientific judgment, and it's exactly where these agents fall off. The authors' framing is that end-to-end autonomy isn't a model problem you solve by scaling — it's a co-design problem. The agent and the lab abstraction have to be optimized together: more precise constraint encoding, better skill descriptions, and above all a feedback channel where a rejected workflow or a null result reshapes the plan itself, not just its numbers.

And for anyone building agentic systems — on biomedical knowledge graphs, on automated analysis pipelines, anywhere — the transferable lesson is the same. It is easy to demo an agent that produces a coherent, plausible artifact. The hard, unglamorous, load-bearing part is the interface where the world pushes back: the constraints, the validation gates, the evidence that's supposed to force a rethink. That's where deployment readiness actually gets decided, and this paper is the first I've seen that makes it measurable.
