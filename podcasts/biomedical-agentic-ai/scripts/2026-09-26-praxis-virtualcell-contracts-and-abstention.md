# PRAXIS-VirtualCell — the virtual cell as a runtime with contracts, and the comparison that falls out of it

Paper link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.29221

## Script

Almost all the energy in virtual-cell research is going into better predictors. Bigger single-cell foundation models, better perturbation prediction, more pretraining data. A paper that went up on arXiv yesterday, from the National Glycoengineering Research Center at Shandong University, argues that the missing piece isn't a model at all. It's the plumbing. I'd normally be wary of an infrastructure paper making that argument, because infrastructure papers are easy to write and hard to evaluate. But this one is worth your time, because the infrastructure immediately coughed up a result that should bother the field.

The system is called PRAXIS-VirtualCell, and the design move is to stop treating a virtual cell as a network and start treating it as a runtime. Seven things get declared independently: the data, the cell state, the perturbation, the predictive model, the adapter that translates between them, the execution environment, and the validation evidence. A dataset declares its species, its assay, its conditions, its feature space, its controls, its provenance. A model declares which inputs, which perturbations, and which outputs it actually supports. Then explicit compatibility contracts decide whether a given question can be answered by a given pairing — before anything runs. Nothing is forced into a common biological representation, which is the trap every previous unification attempt fell into. The runtime standardizes how an experiment is defined, executed and recorded, not what a cell is.

The agent sits on top of that, and its role is deliberately small. A question goes through five stages — ask, review, route, run, results. The language model parses the natural-language question into a structured request, proposes candidate execution plans, and writes the final explanation. What it explicitly cannot do is create data, change feature mappings, generate model outputs, issue validation evidence, or overrule an execution decision. Those are permissions, enforced by the runtime, not instructions in a system prompt. Regular listeners will hear the echo of yesterday's episode, which found exactly this division of labour empirically, by measuring it. Here it's baked into the architecture as an authority boundary.

Now the part that earns the paper its place. Because every experiment is forced through one contract, models become directly comparable almost for free. On human K-five-six-two cells, using the Norman perturbation benchmark — thirty perturbation conditions, about five thousand genes read out — they ran GEARS, a well-known graph-based perturbation predictor, against a simple additive model. The additive model won in twenty-five of the thirty conditions. And it gets sharper when you split by type: additive wins ten of fourteen single-gene perturbations, but fifteen of sixteen combinatorial ones, and combinatorial perturbations account for roughly ninety percent of GEARS's excess error. Combinatorial prediction is the entire pitch for a model like that. It's supposed to be the place where a learned graph beats just adding two effects together.

Be fair about what that is: one benchmark, one configuration, no careful tuning of either side. It is not a takedown. But it lines up with published systematic evaluations showing single-cell foundation models failing to beat linear and nearest-neighbour baselines, and the interesting thing is how cheaply it appeared. Nobody set out to write a benchmarking paper. The comparison fell out as a byproduct of having a shared contract.

The feature I actually like most is abstention. The runtime separates two things we routinely conflate: whether the software ran, and whether the science is supported. Validation evidence is stored in its own right, in distinct states — development validation, external stress testing, technical reproducibility, scientific validation. So when the E. coli sucrose condition had no feasible wild-type solution, it was recorded as unsupported rather than being forced to emit a number. Same for the very-low-glucose condition in yeast. And crucially, a scientifically-validated-equals-false flag survives all the way into the final output. That matters enormously when a language model is the last stage of your pipeline, because the natural-language report is generated from the structured record. The model cannot quietly upgrade an abstention into an answer. It has nothing to upgrade from.

That same discipline makes the paper honest about its own weak numbers, and they are weak. In E. coli, all twenty-six conditions gave positive correlations with a median around zero point two two at the condition level — but drop to individual genes and the median correlation is about zero point zero four, with barely half the genes positive at all. Yeast is worse: the gene-level median correlation is exactly zero. Flux-balance prediction of gene-deletion fitness is close to useless gene by gene, and the framework reports that rather than finding a summary statistic that flatters it. That's not a flaw in the system. That is the system working.

So what's the take. The contribution here isn't the agent, and it isn't any model in the registry. It's a claim about where the leverage sits: we have hundreds of biomedical predictors and no shared way to say what each one actually supports. Contracts, provenance and evidence states are deeply unglamorous, and they're the thing that makes it safe to point an agent at a model zoo at all. The caveats are real — this is one group, there's no comparison against other provenance or workflow systems, and the contracts are written by hand, so an abstention is only as honest as whoever declared that model's scope. But treating "should a prediction be made here" as a first-class runtime decision, rather than something the language model decides on the fly, is the right primitive. I'd like to see it stolen.
