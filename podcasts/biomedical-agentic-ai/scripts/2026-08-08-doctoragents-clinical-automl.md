# DoctorAgents — agentic reasoning replaces brute-force search for machine learning on small, messy clinical data

Paper link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.05375

## Script

Today's paper reframes a workhorse of biomedical machine learning — automated pipeline building — as a reasoning problem instead of a search problem. It's called DoctorAgents, out of McGill and Mila with rheumatology collaborators, and it's aimed squarely at the kind of data most clinical machine learning actually runs on: small, messy, and temporal.

Here's the setup. Most automated machine learning tools work by brute force. You hand them a table, they search over a big predefined space of preprocessing steps, models, and hyperparameters, and they hill-climb toward the best cross-validation score. That works beautifully on large, clean, static datasets. It falls apart on a rare-disease registry or a single-center ICU cohort, where the data is high-dimensional, sparse, and irregular in time — patients measured at different frequencies over different durations, where the same lab value means something different depending on when it was taken. Brute-force search has no idea what any of that means. It just tries things.

DoctorAgents replaces the search with a team of language-model agents that reason about the data and then iterate. A consultant agent reads the dataset's metadata and a small representative sample and writes a structured task specification — what the prediction target is, what leakage traps to avoid, how the time windows should work. Then generation, validation, and refinement agents build an actual executable pipeline, run it, critique the results in plain language, and edit the code.

The clever mechanical piece is how they edit. Instead of regenerating the whole pipeline every iteration — which is what prior agentic systems do, and it's wasteful and unstable — they use an adapted form of textual gradient descent. The natural-language critique of what went wrong gets propagated, in words, into localized edits: this preprocessing step is leaking, replace it; this feature isn't helping, drop it. It's gradient descent where the gradient is a sentence and the update is a targeted code change. And a persistent memory log records every change and its outcome, so the system doesn't overwrite a good idea two steps later or re-try something it already knows fails.

They tested it on four tasks: three from the public MIMIC-four ICU database — in-ICU mortality from the first forty-eight hours, seven-day readmission, and whether a stay runs past three days — plus an in-house rheumatoid arthritis cohort, predicting who responds to anti-T-N-F therapy from one-to-nine years of sparse pre-treatment records. Crucially, they fed all of it in raw, with the original irregular timestamps, no fixed binning, no manual visit selection — the whole point is that the agents decide how to aggregate time.

Now, the honest part. Against strong baselines — a specialized small-data tabular model and two other agentic pipeline builders — the domain-specialized version of DoctorAgents comes out on top on all four tasks. But the margins are modest and the fold-to-fold variance is large: on mortality it's roughly zero-point-five-two versus zero-point-five-one for the next best, with error bars that overlap. So if you're grading purely on the accuracy leaderboard, this is a win by a nose, not a knockout.

That's not where I'd put the significance, though. Two things make this worth your attention. First, the ablations are the real result. Strip out the memory module and the optimization trajectory becomes unstable — it finds a good pipeline early and then thrashes and loses it. That's a concrete, generalizable lesson about agentic systems: persistent structured memory isn't a nice-to-have, it's what keeps iterative self-improvement from wandering off. Strip out the consultant, and it simply starts from a worse place and spends its budget catching up.

Second, the features it builds are clinically legible. For mortality it constructs things like the hourly rate of change in blood bicarbonate and mean arterial pressure on the second ICU day; for readmission, the respiratory-rate slope in the last six hours before discharge; for the arthritis cohort, shared-epitope alleles and anti-C-C-P antibodies alongside recent disease-activity scores. These aren't opaque embeddings — they're the features a clinician would recognize as sensible, generated autonomously from raw irregular data. That's the payoff: an agent doing the feature-engineering reasoning a human data scientist would do, on exactly the small, ugly datasets where the human data scientist is the current bottleneck and brute-force tools give up.

So: modest numbers, but a genuinely different philosophy — reasoning over search — and an interpretability-and-memory story that's the part I'd actually take away. Worth a read if you build clinical models on small cohorts, or if you care about what makes agentic iteration stable rather than merely plausible.
