# NLM Cell Knowledge Network — the Cell Ontology grounded in experimental evidence, and a graph motif that mostly generates bad drug-repurposing hypotheses

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.10.30.763199v1

## Script

Today's pick is a preprint from the National Library of Medicine's own intramural program describing something they have built and released: the Cell Knowledge Network, a semantic knowledge graph that connects data-derived cell phenotypes to diseases and drugs. It is a US government work under a CC0 license, with the filtered data on Zenodo and the code on GitHub. For a listener who cares about open biomedical knowledge infrastructure, that alone makes it worth five minutes.

Here is what is actually in version one. Reproducible computational workflows ran over eighty-eight single-cell datasets covering ten human organ systems, pulled from CELLxGENE, and produced two thousand five hundred eighty-five cell sets and five thousand one hundred thirty-nine selectively expressed genes. A cell set is the empirical object: a cluster someone actually found in a dataset, with the author's own label attached. That is deliberately not the same thing as a cell type. The cell types come from the Cell Ontology, anatomy comes from UBERON, gene-disease and drug-target links come from Open Targets, proteins from UniProt and NCBI Gene. Twenty-six assertion types tie it together, loaded as triples into a graph database with provenance on every node.

The piece I would not skip is the relation joining those two halves. Each cell set is mapped to a Cell Ontology term through a relation they call composed primarily of, and the mappings carry explicit semantics borrowed from the vocabulary world: some are exact matches, some are merely related matches, and when three different smooth muscle cell sets all collapse onto one ontology term, the graph says so rather than quietly pretending they are the same. Every cell set also carries quantitative evidence — a silhouette score for how well separated the cluster is, and an F-beta score for the minimal marker gene combination that distinguishes it, produced by a pipeline called N-S Forest.

That is the real contribution, and it runs in the direction most people don't build. Usually an ontology is a controlled vocabulary you annotate data with. Here the data pushes back on the ontology. The paper gives three worked cases. Nasal club cells and non-nasal club cells come out as reproducibly distinct cell sets but both map to the single term "club cell," which is an argument for splitting that term. Two dendritic cell sets, cleanly distinct in the lung atlas, map to terms that sit in a parent-child chain in the current hierarchy, which is an argument that the hierarchy is wrong. And when six independent lung datasets all nominate CFTR as a marker for ionocytes, that is reproducible experimental evidence for an ontology term's definition rather than one lab's claim. The Cell Ontology is apparently already pulling N-S Forest markers and their scores into its cell type descriptions. This is the loop that ontology people have wanted for twenty years and almost never get: a machine-generated, score-bearing evidence stream feeding term revision.

The second half of the paper is where I would push back a little. They notice a recurring seven-node motif in the graph — a gene selectively expressed in a cell set in an organ, genetically associated with a disease, encoding a protein that interacts with a compound that treats that disease — and because of its shape they name it the Big Dipper. One hundred eighty-nine genes form the complete motif. Four hundred forty-two form what they call a bottomless Big Dipper, where the compound interacts with the protein but is not linked to the disease. Those four hundred forty-two, they suggest, are drug-repurposing hypotheses.

The admirable thing is that the authors immediately demolish their own examples. CFTR is causal in both cystic fibrosis and hereditary chronic pancreatitis, so the modulators look transferable — except one of the compounds the graph returns is crofelemer, which inhibits CFTR-mediated chloride transport, exactly the wrong direction. In the pulmonary hypertension example, the gene is KCNK3, the channel is TASK-1, and the compounds the graph hands you are halothane, isoflurane, sevoflurane and a couple more volatile anesthetics, plus doxapram, which inhibits the channel when you need it opened. Their own verdict on that case: no suitable candidate. So the motif count is a count of topological shapes, not of hypotheses. Pharmacological direction is not in the graph, and without it four hundred forty-two is a number about the schema rather than about biology.

Two limits worth carrying. The expression layer stores only the top ten binary genes per cell set, so absence of a gene-cell link means essentially nothing, and version one is healthy tissue in ten organ systems. And for this show's usual beat: the agentic part is future work, not shipped. They describe a planned natural-language interface where a model that knows the schema writes graph queries, executed through a Model Context Protocol server, with an agent narrating the returned subgraph with citations — plus plans to textualize assertions into controlled English and to generate subgraph embeddings for retrieval. That is a sensible answer to making a knowledge graph legible to language models. It is also, today, a paragraph. What exists now is the curated graph, the provenance, and the browser — which is the part that is hard to do and that nobody can shortcut.
