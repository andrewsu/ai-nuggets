# AskChem — when the retrieval unit is the claim, not the paper, agents stop hallucinating citations

Paper link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28618

## Script

Today's pick is a systems paper called AskChem, out of NYU, and it's one of those papers where the specific domain — chemistry literature — is almost beside the point. The real argument is about what the right unit of retrieval should be when the thing doing the reading is an AI agent.

Here's the problem it starts from. When a chemist asks something like "what electrocatalysts have been reported for turning carbon dioxide into carbon monoxide, and at what efficiency," the answer isn't in any one paper. It's scattered across dozens, as individual findings about specific catalysts, conditions, and measurements. And what does every literature-search tool hand back? A ranked list of documents. You still have to open each paper, find the relevant sentence, check the number, and assemble the answer yourself. That interface was built for a human who reads. Point a language-model agent at it and the agent inherits exactly the same problem — or worse, it answers from memory and fabricates plausible-looking citations. In the paper's own example, a strong model asked to answer directly cites fourteen paper identifiers, and six of them don't resolve. They point to nothing.

AskChem's move is to change the unit of retrieval from the paper to the claim. They run every paper through a language model that segments it into atomic, typed assertions — the authors explicitly borrow the analogy from Segment Anything, which chopped images into reusable regions. Each claim is one finding, tagged with its type, and — this is the key part — grounded by a source identifier and a verbatim quote from the paper. So the smallest thing you retrieve is already a verifiable, provenance-carrying fact. And they've done this at scale: two point four million claims from a hundred and forty-seven thousand papers going back to 1925, and every single claim in the index carries its source and its quote.

On top of that claim store they build three structures, and this is where it gets interesting for anyone who cares about knowledge graphs. First, a faceted taxonomy — organizing claims by what they're about, induced from the corpus while reading it rather than imposed as a fixed ontology up front. Second, and most important, an evidence graph: typed, directed edges between claims — this one supports that one, this contradicts that, this one extends or derives from it. About a hundred and seventy thousand edges, and when a domain expert audited a sample, the edge types were right about ninety-eight percent of the time. That graph is what turns retrieval into synthesis — an agent can land on a finding and then walk to the results that back it up or contradict it, which is the thing plain document search can never do. Third, a more exploratory "living taxonomy" that hangs findings under scientific principles.

And crucially, all of this is exposed not just through a website but through a REST interface, a software development kit, and a Model Context Protocol server. It's built to be an agent's tool, not just a page for a human to browse. It's live, and it's open.

Does it work? They built a benchmark of thirty cross-paper chemistry questions. Take a frontier model answering alone: about eighty-eight percent of its cited identifiers actually resolve. Ground that same model in AskChem's claims and it goes to a hundred percent — the citation hallucination just disappears, because every reference the model can use is already a real, quoted claim. It also roughly doubles citation density and scores best on relevance.

Now the honest part, and the authors are honest about it. A closed, commercial deep-research agent in their comparison beats AskChem on the depth of quantitative detail per citation and on staying strictly on topic. So AskChem is not the most thorough system in the room. Its pitch is a different profile: it's claim-level, it's open-data, it's fast enough to browse interactively, it's directly usable as an agent tool, and it eliminates fabricated citations. And the traceability guarantee is narrow — they can prove every claim is grounded in a real quote from a real paper, but not that the extraction interpreted that quote correctly. The taxonomy placements aren't validated either.

But step back from chemistry, and here's why I think this matters for us. The default assumption in agentic literature work is that you bolt a retrieval tool onto a model and let it read papers. AskChem is a bet that that's the wrong abstraction — that agents need a substrate of atomic, provenance-carrying claims linked by a supports-and-contradicts graph, because that's the representation over which synthesis and verification are even possible. Whether or not the claim turns out to be the right primitive, the framing is the useful part: build the knowledge layer for the agent, don't just hand the agent the same document search we built for ourselves. That's a very different thing to build, and this is a concrete, open, running instance of it.
