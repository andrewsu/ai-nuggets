# EcoXAI — a knowledge-graph-grounded multi-agent system that autonomously repurposes drugs for Alzheimer's

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.07.08.737358v2

## Script

Today's pick is a preprint called EcoXAI, out of the group behind AlzKB, the Alzheimer's Knowledge Base. It's an autonomous multi-agent system for biomedical discovery, and it's one of the cleaner demonstrations I've seen of the idea that a knowledge graph is the right leash for an agentic loop.

Here's the problem it's built around. If you point a general-purpose language model at a biomedical dataset and ask it to find something, you get fluent, plausible, and frequently wrong. It hallucinates mechanisms, it proposes biologically implausible targets, and there's no thread you can pull to check its work. The usual fix is to bolt on retrieval, but retrieval over text still lets the model wander. EcoXAI's answer is to make a structured biomedical knowledge graph the substrate the agent actually reasons over — not a document it reads, but a database it queries with Cypher, whose schema it retrieves on its own and traverses to build hypotheses.

The architecture is deliberately un-monolithic. Instead of one big agent, EcoXAI is a planner-orchestrator that decomposes a research question into ordered, auditable stages: ingest the data, profile it with an exploratory-analysis agent, retrieve domain knowledge from the graph, generate candidate hypotheses grounded in both the dataset and the graph, evaluate each one with a method appropriate to its type, and store what survived in a discovery memory. That memory is a semantic vector store, so the next round of hypotheses is retrieval-augmented against everything the system already tried — which is how it avoids re-proposing the same idea forever. The whole stack is containerized, it runs on local models or API models interchangeably, and every stage persists its scripts and logs so a human can inspect or rerun any step mid-loop. If you care about reproducibility and open science — and this audience does — that auditability is the point, not a footnote.

Now the case study, which is where it earns its keep. They pointed EcoXAI at AlzKB and asked it to do drug repurposing for Alzheimer's, with two deliberately harsh constraints: don't use any drug already associated with Alzheimer's, and don't use any gene connected to Alzheimer's for feature selection. In other words, they forced it to find novel mechanism rather than rediscover annotations. Running a local Qwen model continuously for about two days — compute they note would have cost over three hundred dollars at frontier-API prices — it autonomously generated, executed, and evaluated a hundred and three independent drug-repurposing hypotheses. For each drug it queried the graph to pull targets, expanded outward through the interaction network with several traversal strategies, built competing gene-set feature panels, trained gradient-boosted models, and wrote its own analysis report. Seventy-nine of the hundred and three beat a randomized baseline of a thousand models trained on random gene sets.

But the number I'd actually highlight is a methodological one the agent surfaced on its own. Direct drug targets, by themselves, usually performed at or below the random baseline. The predictive signal lived in the expanded interaction networks — first- and second-order interactors, shared pathways, bridge genes. That pattern held across immune modulators, kinase inhibitors, metabolic drugs, calcium channel blockers, GPCR compounds. The system, without being told to, kept discovering that Alzheimer's risk is a network property, not an isolated-target property. That's a real biological take falling out of the search behavior, not a metric.

The headline hypothesis is Maraviroc — the CCR5 antagonist used as an HIV drug. Starting from Maraviroc's validated targets and explicitly excluding every Alzheimer's-associated gene, EcoXAI built a seventy-seven-gene network, got a model over baseline, and then — instead of stopping at a significant result — kept going, added second-order bridge genes, and pushed the model to an AUC comparable to the best human-curated models in prior published work. Here's the striking part: CCR5 sits three hops from Alzheimer's in the graph, and all the Alzheimer's genes were fenced off. Yet the literature independently backs the connection — elevated CCR5 in Alzheimer's brains, the CCL5-CCR5 axis regulating amyloid and tau through microglia, amyloid inducing CCR5 at the blood-brain barrier. So the agent recovered a genuine, evidence-supported mechanism through graph reasoning, not by leaking a known label.

My take: don't over-read the effect sizes — AUCs in the low point-six range on one disease, downsampled, are a proof of concept, and the authors are candid that agentic systems still hit planning errors and cascading failures, which is exactly why the per-stage isolation and logging matter. What's worth your attention is the pattern. This is agentic AI where the knowledge graph is the reasoning substrate that keeps the loop honest, the auditability is designed in rather than promised, and the whole thing runs locally on open weights. That's the template a lot of biomedical agentic work should be copying.
