# GEOMeta — LLM agents curate 600,000 GEO samples, and the labels immediately expose the foundation models

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.08.19.745739v1

## Script

Today's paper is about the least glamorous problem in computational biology, and the one that quietly caps everything else: metadata.

The Gene Expression Omnibus holds millions of samples. Almost none of it is reusable at scale, because what a sample actually is — which tissue, which disease, treated or control, what dose, how old the donor was — is scattered across free-text study descriptions and idiosyncratic per-sample fields, in whatever vocabulary the submitter felt like using that day. A group at Michigan State has now run a stack of language-model agents across that corpus and released standardized, sample-level annotations for just under six hundred thousand human bulk RNA-seq samples, linked to processed expression profiles. They call it GEOMeta.

Three weeks ago on this show we covered the Gemma curation agent out of UBC, doing essentially the same job on the same repository. Two independent groups landing on production-grade automated GEO curation within a month of each other is itself the story. But GEOMeta pushes on a different axis — raw scale, and what you learn once the labels actually exist.

Start with the architecture, because their central claim is a design claim: you cannot do this with one prompt, no matter how good the model is. So they split curation into stages that are separately inspectable — contextual extraction first, then field-level standardization, then controlled derivation of secondary labels under explicit rules, then ontology mapping, then deterministic post-processing. That's thirty-two prompt templates behind four extraction agents, twenty-one field-specific standardization agents, and a handful of mapping agents. And critically, annotation happens at the level of the individual sample, not the study — which is what makes a GEO series actually decomposable for reuse.

The evidence for the decomposition is the sharpest methodological result in the paper. They ran a head-to-head against one generic structured extraction prompt. For tissue, the generic prompt left fifty-four percent of samples with no annotation at all; the task-specific agents left twenty-one percent. And where both produced an answer, they agreed almost perfectly — ninety-nine percent concordance. So the failure mode of the naive approach isn't wrong labels. It's silence. The generic prompt simply declines to commit. And their argument is that this is unrecoverable: whatever gets missed at extraction cannot be reconstituted downstream, no matter how good your normalization and ontology mapping are. Curation quality is decided in the first stage, and everything after that is bookkeeping.

The bookkeeping does work, for what it's worth. Tissue collapses from two thousand two hundred raw strings to fifty-six Human Protein Atlas categories; disease from three thousand four hundred to about twelve hundred controlled terms; compounds get PubChem identifiers. Checked against two independently hand-curated resources, disease, tissue, and case-versus-control assignments come in around ninety-six to ninety-seven percent concordant.

Then the part I genuinely enjoyed. Having curated the labels, they train models to predict those labels from expression — and then treat the model's disagreements with the metadata as an audit signal. On sex, a hundred and seventy-three of three and a half thousand held-out samples had a prediction that contradicted the recorded label. They went back to raw sequencing reads for ten of the female-labeled ones. Five showed genuine coordinated Y-chromosome expression with XIST absent — the metadata is simply wrong. Four were too shallowly sequenced to call either way. One was really female, and the model was wrong. So model disagreement is a triage signal, not a verdict. That's the right framing, and it's rarer than it should be.

The downstream benchmarking is where it gets uncomfortable for a fashionable line of work. With half a million labeled samples in hand, they benchmarked single-cell foundation models transferred to bulk. Fine-tuned CellPLM came out best overall — and on sex prediction it hit ninety-five percent, exactly matching a two-layer perceptron trained on thirty-five known sex-marker genes. The foundation model bought nothing. On fine-grained disease it sits between fifty and sixty-four percent. And on age it scored sixty-eight percent on one holdout and thirty-six percent on a prospectively annotated cohort from a few months later. Same model, same task, different submission window, half the accuracy. If you want a clean demonstration that these representations are not shift-robust, that's it.

Cost turns out to be the practical unlock, and they benchmarked twenty-two frontier models from eleven providers to pin it down. Curating seven thousand samples with the strongest reasoning model took fifteen hours and fifty-three dollars, about fifty cents per GEO series. The cheap Flash-class models came in twenty to thirty times cheaper with comparable completeness and cross-model agreement — and the cheapest of them also produced the most complete output. Meanwhile a couple of models with excellent coding reputations produced lots of missing annotations, which is a useful reminder that agentic coding skill and scientific data inference are not the same capability.

The honest limits are stated plainly. There is no gold standard for GEO metadata, so cross-model consensus measures stability, not truth. Correlated errors stay invisible. And the schema captures sample attributes but not study design, so paired, longitudinal, and multifactorial experiments still need a human to read the series description before you build signatures from them.

The framing I'd take away is the one they close on. Metadata standardization is not preprocessing. It's an infrastructure layer — and it is now cheap enough to maintain continuously rather than curate once.
