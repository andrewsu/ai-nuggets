# GatorOnco — an eight-billion-parameter agentic LLM that reaches into live guidelines mid-reasoning and plans colorectal cancer treatment on par with oncologists

Paper link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.09142

## Script

Here's a result that cuts against the "just make it bigger" reflex. A group at the University of Florida — the same lab behind the GatorTron clinical models — built an eight-billion-parameter agentic language model called GatorOnco for colorectal cancer treatment planning. In a blind, randomized evaluation, it performed on par with board oncologists, and it did that while beating a general-purpose model roughly nine times its size. The interesting part for us isn't the clinical headline. It's how they got there, and it's a data-integration story wearing a clinical coat.

Start with the problem they're actually solving. Treatment planning in oncology means synthesizing a messy patient history against clinical guidelines that change constantly. The National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines — the standard of care reference — went through two hundred forty-one updates across eighty-eight guideline documents in a single year. A model that memorized last year's standard is not just stale, it's dangerous. And about a quarter of colorectal patients already receive care that diverges from guidelines. So the real challenge is currency: how do you keep a frozen set of weights aligned with a moving target?

Their answer is agentic retrieval, and the clever move is where they put it. Instead of bolting retrieval on at inference time, they baked it into training. During reinforcement learning, the model runs an interleaved retrieve-then-reason loop: while it generates a plan, it issues its own subqueries against the current guidelines, pulls the relevant evidence, and reasons over it — and it's rewarded for doing that well. So the agentic behavior — proactively deciding what to look up and when — is learned, not scripted. At inference the same loop produces explicit reasoning traces that link each recommendation back to the specific guideline passage it retrieved, which means a clinician can audit the chain rather than trust a black box.

Two more design choices matter because they change how you should read the result. First, catastrophic forgetting. When you pre-train a general model on a huge clinical corpus — here, two hundred eighty-two billion tokens, including a hundred sixty-six billion tokens of real University of Florida health-system text — it tends to lose its ability to follow instructions. Rather than fight that with more fine-tuning, they used model merging: they mathematically blend the clinically-soaked model back together with its instruction-tuned sibling, keeping the clinical knowledge and the instruction-following in one set of weights. Second, the reward. They co-designed what they call a safety-oriented hierarchical reward with the oncologists: the model only gets credit for naming a specific regimen — something like FOLFOX — if it first correctly identifies the high-level modality, chemotherapy. That's a structural guardrail against the classic failure mode of confidently proposing a precise wrong answer. It ties the specific to the general.

Now the evaluation, because this is where the paper earns its claims. Five oncologists blind-rated plans from GatorOnco, from a general open-source model, and the real oncologist-written plans, all shuffled together. On the dimensions that matter most — correctness and safety — GatorOnco was statistically indistinguishable from the human experts. On safety it scored identically, four-point-two-two against four-point-two-two, while the un-adapted general model dropped to two-point-seven. On the harder categorical task of picking the actual therapy delivered, it hit an F1 around zero-point-nine-two, beating a seventy-billion-parameter general model outright.

Here's where I'd push back, and it's the honest read. GatorOnco actually edged out the human plans on the composite score, and it beat the oncologists on readability and completeness. Be careful with that. The ground truth here is the treatment plan as documented in the clinical note — and clinical notes are terse, rushed, written for billing and memory, not for a reader. A model that writes clean, well-organized prose will of course look more "complete" and more "readable" than a busy doctor's shorthand. That gap is partly a documentation-style artifact, not evidence the medicine is better. The result that survives scrutiny is the parity on correctness and safety — and that's genuinely the bar that matters. This is also one cancer, one health system, one institution's guidelines and one institution's writing style, so external validity is an open question.

But strip away the caveats and the takeaway is sharp, and it generalizes past oncology. An eight-billion-parameter model, deeply adapted to a domain and taught to retrieve as an agent, matched experts and beat a model nine times larger. The authors put it bluntly: scale is not a silver bullet. For high-stakes, fast-moving domains, data quality and domain alignment beat raw parameter count — and the way you stay current isn't retraining, it's teaching the model to go fetch the current evidence itself, as part of how it thinks. That's an agentic-RAG lesson that applies to a lot more than treatment plans.
