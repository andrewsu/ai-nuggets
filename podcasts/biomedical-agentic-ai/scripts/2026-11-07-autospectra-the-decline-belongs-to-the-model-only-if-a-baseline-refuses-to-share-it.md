# AutoSPECTRA — a coding-agent protocol for auditing where a foundation model stops generalizing

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.11.03.763979v1

## Script

Today's paper comes out of the Broad Institute and it is called AutoSPECTRA. The pitch is simple: when a new scientific foundation model drops — a protein structure predictor, a pathology vision model, a perturbation model — somebody should find out where it stops working, and that somebody should not have to be a postdoc with three free weeks. So point a coding agent at it. What makes the paper worth five minutes is not the agent. It's the part the authors had to build because the agent, left alone, wasn't trustworthy.

They started by finding out what an unsupervised coding agent actually does with this job. Twenty-four independent runs, three different agent-and-model combinations, four target models. Asked to name likely failure modes from their own knowledge and the open web, every single run produced plausible, literature-grounded hypotheses — and not one of them tested a hypothesis against data. Told explicitly to quantify a relationship and check whether it replicated on an independent dataset, all twenty-four went and did it: found public resources, wrote the code, ran the analysis, reproduced a known effect in twenty-two cases and returned two interpretable negatives. So the capability is there. The initiative is not.

Then they asked for something harder: find a limitation nobody has reported for this model. Here the agents came apart, and not in the way you'd guess. They generated plenty of hypotheses. What they couldn't do was hold a consistent standard for what counted as evidence. Agents examining the same model and the same candidate axis disagreed about whether prior work had already found it — four such disagreements spanning eleven of the twenty-four runs. In two cases, agents started from essentially the same initial association and reached opposite conclusions, because they chose different confirmation data, different groupings, different controls. And three runs proposed or revised the property they were testing after looking at the target model's errors. That last one is the cardinal sin. If you pick your axis by inspecting where the model failed, then confirm it on the same errors, you have discovered nothing except the shape of your own noise.

So AutoSPECTRA is not a multi-agent architecture. It's a protocol — literally a markdown file at a URL that you tell your coding agent to go fetch and follow. Its rules are the obvious ones that nobody enforces. Define the input property, the levels, and the analysis plan before you look at a single target-model prediction. Run fixed reference methods on exactly the same examples. Confirm on data that was not used to choose the axis. And you are allowed to finish the audit having found nothing.

That third-party reference method is where the whole thing earns its keep, and two of the four case studies show why. Take CONCH, the pathology vision-language model. An audit found that as you strip fine detail out of a tissue image — downsample it, then blow it back up to the model's input size — top-one accuracy falls from about eighty percent to twenty-eight percent. Alarming, but meaningless on its own, because a blurry image is harder for everything. So run an off-the-shelf ImageNet ResNet on the identical transformed images: it goes from about eighty percent to fifty-six. Relative loss of sixty-six percent versus twenty-nine. That gap belongs to CONCH. Now take AlphaMissense. An audit found that its agreement with deep mutational scanning data declines steadily as alignment occupancy at the mutated position drops — from about zero point five-two down to zero point four-zero, and it holds even at positions with deep alignments. Same shape of result. But the reference methods decline too, including BLOSUM sixty-two, a substitution matrix from the nineties that doesn't know what position it's scoring. That's not an AlphaMissense limitation. That's a hard region of the problem. Two audits, same procedure, opposite verdicts — and the only thing separating them is a baseline you could compute in an afternoon.

The best moment in the paper is a run that kept going. Auditing STATE, the cell perturbation model, an agent hypothesized that accuracy would climb with the number of training examples for a perturbation. That gradient didn't appear. Rather than quietly dropping it, the agent sharpened the question to a binary one — had this perturbation ever been in training at all — confirmed it in three more cell lines, and then went and looked inside the model, where it found that unseen perturbations get essentially zero-valued embedding vectors. The model isn't being told which gene was hit. The audit went from a performance curve to a mechanism, which is what you actually wanted.

Two things to keep honest. When they ran six independent audits per model, convergence was loose: for the protein model, three of them landed on relatedness-to-training-data in three different operationalizations, one found a charge effect, one found nothing, and one audited the wrong version of the model entirely. That's a family of related answers, not reproduction. And the authors are clear that four case studies cannot tell you an audit's false discovery rate.

What I'd take from this for my own work is the artifact, not the agent. Every audit emits a card recording the axes tested, the data, the controls, the limitations, and — unusually — the hypotheses that were rejected and the interpretations that changed, which is exactly the material that evaporates from every model evaluation ever written. The proposal is that a model card tells you what a model is for, and an audit card tells you, in computable terms, where its performance has actually been measured to bend. Given how fast these models are being released as callable components inside other people's pipelines, that second document is the one I'd want before I wired one into anything.
