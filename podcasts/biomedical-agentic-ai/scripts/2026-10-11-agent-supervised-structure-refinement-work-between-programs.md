# Agent-supervised structure refinement — Claude Code runs Phenix, Coot and ModelAngelo across 24 cryo-EM and X-ray cases, and the result that matters is when it changed course

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.10.05.756902v1

## Script

Ask a structural biologist what slows down a structure and they will rarely name a missing program. Phenix refines. Coot rebuilds. ModelAngelo turns a cryo-EM map and a sequence into a protein model. MolProbity tells you what is wrong. The slow part is the work between those programs: reading one report, noticing that the fix for one problem caused another, changing a setting, and deciding whether the new model is actually better than the old one. That judgment usually lives in the head of the one experienced person in the lab. A short preprint from Kuen-Phon Wu's group at Academia Sinica, posted this week, asks whether an agent can take over that between-programs work. Their answer is a careful, qualified yes.

The setup is very plain. Claude Code, running the new Opus 5.5 model, was given the specialist programs on a single workstation with one consumer graphics card. Its job was to prepare inputs, run the established software, read the validation output, compare candidate models, and choose the next step. It never computes a density map or moves an atom itself. The authors describe it as a workflow supervisor rather than a new refinement engine. That is a deliberate choice: the agent does nothing it could hallucinate, because every number it acts on comes from a program the field already trusts.

Across 24 cases, finished in about 36 hours of project time, the headline numbers are good. There were fifteen deposited cryo-EM models with weak geometry. On those, the median MolProbity score went from 2.62 to 1.76, and the median clashscore fell from about 21 to 6. Fit to the map held steady or improved, and the backbone barely moved. That pattern means many small local corrections, not a different fold. In five more cases the agent started from only a map and a sequence. It took ModelAngelo's first draft, refined it, and ended within about a fifth of an angstrom of the published models. In three in-house X-ray cases, built from diffraction data without looking at the published coordinates, the free R values came close to the published ones but did not beat them.

But the authors say outright that the global numbers are not the main result. What they find most informative are the moments when the agent changed course after looking at an intermediate result. During a scan of the map weight, a better map correlation came with more clashes, so it switched to automatic weighting and checked geometry before choosing a model. Side-chain fitting in Coot was dragging the backbone out of place, so it kept the side-chain angles and put the original backbone back. When B-factor refinement made the map fit worse, it ran a second branch that kept the deposited B-factors and compared the two. None of these is a single command. Each one requires knowing that two programs can disagree, and that improving one number can quietly damage another part of the model.

The case I find most telling is one where the agent did not ship an improvement. For one of the lab's own cryo-EM structures, candidate models scored better against the map but failed the geometry acceptance rule, so the deposited model was kept. The authors say this decision matters as much to their argument as any case where the clashscore went down. An agent that can conclude "nothing I tried was a defensible improvement" is far more useful than one that always hands back a newer file.

There are two places where the limits are clear, and the paper is honest about both. First, chemistry. In the map-and-sequence cases, ligands, ions and glycans were out of scope, and the water search shows why that matters. Comparing afterward against the published models, three, eight, and seventeen of the agent's candidate waters in three structures sat on sites the original authors had assigned to ligands or ions. Density can tell you something is there. It cannot tell you what it is. The authors' rule is that sample chemistry is an input, supplied by the researcher just like the sequence, and a site the evidence cannot resolve should stay unresolved. Second, the metrics conflict. The CaBLAM outlier rate, a backbone check, went up in seven of the fifteen re-refined structures, even as the median MolProbity score improved. A summary number that improves can hide a local problem that gets worse.

So what I take from it is about what the agent should hand back. In this view, the output is not a refined coordinate file but a change report: for every altered region, the operation used, the local fit, the geometry change, the candidates that were rejected, and the sites still unexplained. Trust becomes a property you can inspect, rather than a score you accept. The scientist's time moves to the few places where judgment matters, which could be most useful for labs that solve a structure only occasionally and have no full-time crystallographer.

The caveats are real, and the authors list most of them. This was an interactive project, not a one-prompt run, and the investigator asked for extra checks along the way. Neither human time nor token use was measured, so the 36 hours shows throughput, not a speed comparison. There is no comparison against a fixed automated pipeline, an earlier model, or a human doing the same cases, and there is no blinded review of the changed regions. The authors propose exactly that experiment: fix the tasks and the acceptance rules, swap the agent, log every intervention, and have independent structural biologists judge the edits blind. Until someone runs it, this is a dated demonstration, not a benchmark. Still, it gives a clear picture of where an agent fits in an experimental science lab: running the transitions between trusted tools, and then being judged against the data.
