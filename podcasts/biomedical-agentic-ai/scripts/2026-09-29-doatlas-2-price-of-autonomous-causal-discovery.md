# DoAtlas-2 — a self-evolving causal discovery loop, and the first honest price tag on one

Paper link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35107

## Script

Today's pick is a technical report out of the Mohamed bin Zayed University of Artificial Intelligence describing a system called DoAtlas-2. It is the most complete attempt I have seen to build a genuinely closed research loop for causal biology. It is also, almost incidentally, the first paper I know of that publishes an honest price tag for running one.

The architecture is the part that deserves copying, so start there. DoAtlas-2 keeps its entire scientific position in a single object with three components: a ledger of what is currently believed and on what evidence, a frontier of open research questions, and a standing list of the evidence it still needs. A discovery agent reads that graph alongside the literature, finds gaps and outright contradictions, and proposes mechanism questions. Research agents take a selected question and freeze the study design — the population, the causal estimand, the identification assumptions — before any outcome is revealed. Analysis agents execute. Results come back, rewrite the ledger, reshape the frontier, and generate the next round.

What separates this from a co-scientist that generates hypotheses is the bookkeeping discipline. Designs are prespecified and frozen. Every prespecified outcome gets reported, including diagnostics and the cases where nothing was estimable at all. Every revision to the ledger carries its source, its applicable context, and its remaining uncertainty. They have effectively built a preregistration machine, and the thing it preserves that almost no automated discovery system preserves is the denominator. You can see everything it asked, not only everything it found.

The scale is real. The evidence base holds roughly ninety-three thousand canonical concepts, a hundred and forty-nine thousand candidate causal relations, and four point seven million evidence records extracted from the literature. Against that, the system opened a research space of eight thousand seven hundred candidate questions, actually ran two thousand and thirty-one of them across three rounds, and produced nearly ninety thousand individual estimates. The empirical testing happens in the Human Phenotype Project, an Israeli deep-phenotyping cohort with about thirteen thousand participants through a first visit.

Now the number I want you to sit with. Of the two hundred relations that came out of this with a population-derived state, eighty-one were supported, one hundred seventeen were unresolved, and exactly two were challenged. The modal outcome of a research question in a fully autonomous causal discovery loop is "we still don't know." And the system's own flagship finding did not survive its own second round. A serial path running from waist-to-hip ratio through an inflammation marker to systolic blood pressure to arterial stiffness looked solid in round one, and then shrank about sixfold once the follow-up question jointly adjusted for body mass index and smoking. The loop refuted itself. I think that is the single strongest piece of evidence in the paper that the loop actually works, and it is not what the abstract leads with.

Then there is the cost, parked in a table most readers will skim straight past. Those two thousand research questions consumed approximately one point five nine trillion model tokens over a seventy-day run, all on GPT-5.6 Sol at extra-high reasoning. That works out to a mean of seven hundred eighty-one million tokens per research question, and five and a quarter hours of wall clock each. Seven hundred eighty-one million tokens to move one edge in a graph from unresolved to unresolved.

I don't raise that to dunk on the work. That number is a gift. Everyone publishes papers about autonomous scientific discovery and essentially nobody reports what a single unit of discovery cost, so the field has no denominator on price either. This one does, and what it tells you is that the economics of this approach at today's inference rates are, let us say, not obviously favorable.

There are a few things worth holding against the headline framing. The report opens with seven hundred seventy-one research resources, seven hundred twenty thousand participants, forty-eight countries — that is a catalog of the data landscape, not of the analysis. The analysis is one cohort. Causal here means regression-based serial mediation on observational data with covariate adjustment, not intervention, and the standardized path effects are down in the hundredths. The interpretability claim is technically airtight and substantively thin: the final predictive model is nineteen nodes and twenty-nine linear connections, so of course every prediction decomposes exactly into its terms. That is arithmetic, not insight. And when they transfer the frozen network to a US survey cohort to predict five-year mortality, it reaches an area under the curve of zero point seven nine eight, against zero point eight zero three for an off-the-shelf gradient-boosted tree. To their credit, the authors run that comparison themselves and report it.

So where does this land. Not as a discovery engine — the discoveries are small and the price is enormous. But the ledger is the right abstraction, and the ledger is the transferable part. A causal knowledge graph where every edge carries an explicit state of supported, challenged, or unresolved; where unresolved is a first-class citizen rather than a silence; where every state change is attributable to a design that was frozen before the data were seen; and where the questions you asked and abandoned stay in the record. That is a data structure worth stealing outright. And you do not need a trillion tokens to adopt it.
