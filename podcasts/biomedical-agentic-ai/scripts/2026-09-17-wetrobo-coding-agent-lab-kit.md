# WetRobo — the lab robot ships as a kit and a skill file, and a coding agent writes the controller on site

Paper link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.18435

## Script

Today's pick is a robotics paper, and I want to lead with the head-to-head, because it settles in the physical world an argument the field has mostly been having in the abstract.

A group at the University of Tokyo, with collaborators at NYU and RIKEN, built something called WetRobo. It is not a model. It's a kit: one six-degree-of-freedom arm, a few pieces of lab equipment — an incubator, a reagent bottle with a screw cap, a Petri dish — the base code that drives the arm, fifteen teleoperated demonstrations per task, and a plain-text skill file laying out how to behave around culture equipment. A biologist assembles it, types a sentence like "lift the bottle cap," and a coding agent — here, OpenAI's Codex — looks at the local bench through the cameras and writes the control program itself.

Now the comparison. On the cap task, they fine-tuned pi zero point five, a vision-language-action policy, on twenty-five demonstrations collected in one lab — call it Lab X — for a hundred thousand steps. In Lab X it lifted the cap ten times out of ten. Move it to Lab Y, different gripper, different lighting, different camera arrangement: zero out of ten. The coding agent, given the same kit and nothing local to train on, succeeded in both.

The reason isn't that the agent is smarter. It's that the agent can do a category of thing the policy structurally cannot, which is re-derive its own calibration. In Lab Y it made three small test movements, one per axis, captured camera images before and after each, ran optical flow to see how the pixels moved, and solved for a local mapping from millimeters of arm motion to pixels of image displacement. That's hand-eye calibration, invented on the spot, in about a minute of robot time. A fine-tuned policy has that mapping baked into its weights from one lab's camera geometry. When the camera moves, there's no mechanism to notice, let alone fix it.

The agent also wrote its own success detector, which I think is the underrated move. Commanding the gripper to close and actually holding something are different events, and nothing in the kit told it how to tell them apart. So it calibrated the measured jaw opening and the gripper's effort reading into two populations, empty close versus non-empty close, and used that as the test. An empty close read about half a percent of full opening; a real grip on the incubator handle read thirty to forty percent. It then added a five-millimeter proof pull before committing to swing the door. Defining success criteria from local observation is normally the human's job in lab automation, and it's exactly the part that doesn't transfer between benches.

It also reached for outside tools without being told which ones. Segment Anything to find the Petri lid. A physics simulator to check whether a candidate grasp would collide with the bench. And when it worked out that the shadows it had been using to judge the incubator door's angle were unreliable, it fit a plane to the depth data and measured the angle directly instead.

The costs matter, because they decide whether anyone actually does this. Forty-one million tokens in one lab, twenty-three million in the other. About an hour to the first successful cap grasp. The incubator door took two hours and forty minutes the first time — and three and a half minutes on a repeat run in the same setting, because by then the program exists. That's the real economics here: adaptation is a one-time cost, and what you're left with is a script, not a checkpoint.

There's plenty to push back on. Three tasks, and they're basic — lift a lid, unscrew a cap, open a door. None of them is cell culture; they're the preconditions for cell culture. Lab Y failed the door task outright, and the paper says so. One agent, one model. And the success rates are softer than they look: the ten out of ten is repeated execution after the agent had already adapted and succeeded once, not ten independent adaptation runs. The policy baseline is also doing a harder job — zero-shot transfer with no feedback — against an agent that got an hour of instrumented trial and error. That asymmetry is real. But it's also the argument: the agent can use that hour, and the policy architecture has nowhere to put it.

The thing I'd actually take away, and the reason this belongs on a show about open data as much as one about robots, is that the unit of distribution changed. Instead of shipping weights per lab, you ship a git repository, and every adaptation leaves behind an inspectable program as a branch — they published the evolved programs from both labs that way. The skill file is the shared artifact, and the authors have already folded rules learned in one lab back into it. That's a protocol-sharing model for lab automation that looks far more like how software gets shared than how policies get shared. It's diffable, it's reviewable, and reproducing it doesn't require a GPU.
