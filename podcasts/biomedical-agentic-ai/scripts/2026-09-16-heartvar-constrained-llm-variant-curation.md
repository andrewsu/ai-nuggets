# HeartVar — the model gets seven criteria out of twenty-eight, and the code assigns the tier

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.09.10.750569v1

## Script

Today's pick went up on bioRxiv yesterday, from the Victor Chang Cardiac Research Institute in Sydney. It's a tool called HeartVar, and on the surface it's a narrow thing — an open-source web app that does first-pass clinical classification of DNA variants in cardiovascular disease. I'm picking it because of how it divides the labor between the code and the model, which is the most defensible version of that split I've seen actually shipped in a biomedical tool.

The problem it's attacking is this. Classifying a variant under the ACMG framework means evaluating twenty-eight separate evidence criteria, weighing each one's strength, and combining them into one of five categories from benign to pathogenic. The evidence sits scattered across population frequency databases, in-silico predictors, clinical report archives, gene-disease validity registries, protein annotations, expression atlases, mouse phenotype data, and the primary literature — each with a different schema, vocabulary, and query interface. A curator can burn hours on a single variant, and experienced curators routinely disagree about which criteria apply and at what strength.

So the obvious move is to point a language model at it, and the obvious failure mode is that the model invents the evidence. What HeartVar does instead is refuse to let the model near most of the decision. Nineteen of the twenty-eight criteria are computed deterministically in Python before anything reaches the model, because the evidence they rest on is structured and rule-based — allele frequencies, splice predictions, whether a different pathogenic change has already been seen at the same residue. The model is handed exactly seven criteria, the ones that genuinely require reading unstructured evidence and making a call: does a well-established functional study show a deleterious effect, is this patient's phenotype specific to a single-gene disorder, is the variant enriched in cases. And then the model does not emit a classification. It returns one structured object with its seven verdicts, and the server adds up the points and assigns the tier. Architecturally, the model cannot tell you the answer. It can only contribute evidence to an arithmetic it doesn't get to perform.

The retrieval side deserves a minute for anyone building this kind of thing. Twenty public sources, and most are not queried live — they're mirrored locally, as small databases or indexed slices of the precomputed releases, to avoid getting rate-limited under multi-user load. Only PubMed, PubTator, and the PubMed Central open-access set go over the network, behind a shared cache with request de-duplication. And one detail there is the most useful sentence in the methods. Each source returns a structured response that includes a deliberate error message when the record isn't found, so the prompt downstream can distinguish "this variant is absent from this database" from "the lookup failed." Those two things mean opposite things for the evidence, and if your retrieval layer collapses both into an empty field, your model will quietly treat a broken API call as a negative result.

Does the model layer earn its place? They ran it both ways, which more papers should do. Without the interpretive layer, HeartVar reproduces the expert tier on sixty-seven percent of a hundred-and-six-variant benchmark drawn from ClinGen expert panel records. With it, seventy-two. The more informative number is per-criterion recall, which climbs from fifty-seven to seventy-three percent while precision slips slightly. The model isn't making the tool broadly smarter — it's recovering the specific criteria a purely programmatic tool has to leave blank.

But the part worth taking away is the head-to-head against existing tools, because it's a small masterclass in reading a benchmark. HeartVar lands at seventy-two percent exact-tier agreement. GeneBe gets fifty-eight, VarSome fifty-one, InterVar twenty-five. On concordance alone you'd call this a solid incremental win. Then look at how GeneBe earns its fifty-eight. Of its sixty-one exact matches, forty-seven applied one of two criteria that credit, essentially, "a reputable source reports this variant as pathogenic" — and the benchmark variants were drawn from exactly such a source. Every single one of its correct pathogenic calls used that criterion. It isn't reconstructing the classification from primary evidence; it's reading the answer key. Current ClinGen guidance says to retire both criteria precisely because they recycle prior classifications as evidence. HeartVar excludes them from scoring entirely. Which means those two headline numbers were never on the same scale, and you can see it in precision: seventy-two percent against thirty-nine.

The direction of the errors matters more than the rate, too. HeartVar's mistakes are conservative under-calls. Of forty variants the panel called pathogenic or likely pathogenic, it agreed on thirty-two and dropped the rest to uncertain significance, and it never flipped anything from pathogenic to benign or the reverse. VarSome errs the other way, systematically — it returned seventeen of eighteen likely-pathogenic variants as outright pathogenic, and promoted sixteen of twenty-eight expert-uncertain variants up into the pathogenic range. For a first-pass assistant a human is going to review, those are not equivalent failure modes.

The residual gap is instructive about the ceiling here. The under-calls trace to case-level evidence — odds ratios, segregation within families, phenotypic detail — that expert panels have and the open literature simply doesn't carry. One apparent error turned out not to be one: the benign frequency evidence the panel cited sat in an older release of gnomAD and falls below threshold in the current one. The tool was right and the reference was stale.

The caveat the authors put up front is that the interpretive layer runs on a commercial model at fixed temperature, so reproducibility rides on a vendor's versioning. Their mitigation is the architecture itself — rule-based tier assignment, and every model assertion displayed next to the source data it came from. That's the pattern worth stealing, and it travels well past cardiology. Compute what you can compute. Hand the model only the part that actually needs judgment. And never let it do the addition.
