# Evidence-grounded accuracy — AI scientists that ace single-cell benchmarks without needing the data

Paper link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.04915

## Script

Today's pick is a short workshop paper out of Carnegie Mellon with a long-overdue question in its title: are we measuring scientific intelligence? Their answer, for data-driven biology benchmarks, is mostly no. What we're measuring is a lot of recall.

Start with the example that opens the paper, because it's the whole argument in one run. A single-cell benchmark asks which marker gene was used to identify endothelial cells in a human tendon dataset. The options are PDGFRB, TRDN, PECAM1 and PAX7. The authors delete PECAM1 from the data — from the expression matrix, every layer, the raw counts. The agent loads the file, writes in its own trajectory that PECAM1 is not in the dataset's gene list, and then answers PECAM1 anyway, as "the only option left." The benchmark marks it fully correct. The answer was right. It just didn't come from the data.

The method is simple to state, and the care is in the execution. For each benchmark item they build three versions of the data. A null version shuffles cells and genes and renames barcodes, which shouldn't change anything. A withdrawal version removes the evidence for the correct answer, so the data no longer decide the question. And a flip version moves the evidence to a different option — in the tendon case, swapping the PECAM1 and PAX7 values in every cell, so PAX7 now looks endothelial. Every edit is checked against a pre-registered reference statistic, so the right response to each version is known in advance. They even had to scrub file metadata after one agent recovered a swap from a filename stored in a gzip header. Then they run the same agent on every version and ask two things: does the answer follow the evidence, and does the score reward it when it does?

They ran Claude Code with three Claude models, Codex with two GPT models, and Biomni, on eighteen single-cell questions from BAISBench — the subset of a hundred and ninety-three that the supplied data can actually decide — plus four synthetic statistical-genetics problems from GeneBench-Pro.

The headline numbers. The Claude agents are ninety-five percent accurate with the data. Given only the question and no data at all, they're eighty-three percent accurate. The dataset is worth twelve points. When the evidence is withdrawn, the benchmark still hands out seventy-seven percent of its credit. Only about a third of those runs say the data can't decide — and most of the ones that say so pick the old answer anyway. When the evidence is flipped to point at a different option, only sixty-one percent of runs follow it. Notice-without-acting is the pattern, and it's in every scaffold, Biomni included.

So the authors define evidence-grounded accuracy: you get credit for a correct answer only if you also react when the evidence is pulled and follow it when it's reversed. By that measure the Claude agents fall from ninety-five to forty-one percent. And the ranking inverts. The two agents that answered every question correctly, Sonnet and one of the GPT models, have the lowest grounded accuracy. Haiku, the least accurate, has the highest. Part of that is Haiku changing its answer without saying why, so I wouldn't crown it — but the leaderboard and the grounded ordering clearly measure different things.

The experiment that turns this from an observation into a mechanism is gene-name anonymization. They replace every gene symbol with an opaque identifier, consistently, in the data, the question and the options. No-data accuracy collapses from eighty-seven percent to twenty-seven, roughly chance. Accuracy with the data barely moves, so the tasks are still solvable by analysis. And now the agents follow flipped evidence ninety-three percent of the time instead of fifty-eight. The agents can do the analysis. What they know about PECAM1 just outvotes it. On the synthetic genetics problems, where there's no prior to lean on, the failure flips around: answers track the data, and the limit is plain analytical precision.

Here's the part I'd underline for anyone building or using these benchmarks. The evaluators share the blind spot. Compare a run that follows the flipped evidence to one that ignores it: the benchmark score prefers the one that ignores it about nine times in ten. An LLM process judge reading the trajectory, with the original key in hand, does exactly the same, for both Claude and GPT judges. Process scoring, which is supposed to be the fix for outcome-only scoring, grades against the same key and inherits the same error. The authors even prove the general version: no evaluator that sees only a single run on the original data can tell a grounded answer from an ungrounded one. Two agents can produce identical trajectories and answers and differ only in what they'd do if the data changed. Grounding is a counterfactual property, and you have to run the counterfactual.

Repairs exist, but each fixes one failure. An explicit "the data are insufficient" option more than halves the credit on withdrawn evidence without costing accuracy on intact data, but does nothing for flips. A judge prompted to grade grounding rather than agreement pushes the flip comparison well above chance. None of them covers everything, and none can be validated without the paired counterfactuals.

The caveats are real. Eighteen questions, hand-built counterfactuals, three replicates per model, one for Biomni. Multiple choice makes elimination easy in a way open-ended analysis wouldn't. And the authors are careful that responding to evidence is necessary for good science, not sufficient.

But the take is hard to argue with. As models know more biology, accuracy on a data-analysis benchmark increasingly measures what the agent already knew. If your agent's score on your single-cell benchmark barely drops when you hide the data, you haven't measured analysis at all. Ship a withdrawal and a flip with every item, and report grounded accuracy next to the headline number. That's a cheap fix compared to an agentic literature quietly certifying recall as discovery.
