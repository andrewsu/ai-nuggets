# MutexaGPT: an agent that turns enzyme-engineering hunches into molecular dynamics

Paper link: https://www.nature.com/articles/s43588-026-01049-y

## Script

Every protein engineer has an intuition like this one: make the active site bigger and the enzyme will accept a bigger substrate. It sounds obviously true. It is also, according to a paper published this week in Nature Computational Science, essentially untestable by any of the cheap shortcuts the field actually uses — and that turns out to be the most interesting thing in the paper.

The system is called MutexaGPT, from Zhongyue Yang's group at Vanderbilt, the same lab behind the EnzyHTP high-throughput simulation platform. It's a multi-agent large language model system sitting on a public web app, and the design decision I want to highlight is that its agents don't predict anything. They translate. One agent interrogates your request until it's scientifically complete — you asked for a bigger pocket, but which enzyme, which mutations, what protonation states. A second pair of agents maps your stated goal onto physics-based quantities that the simulation backend can actually compute, and maps your description of a mutant library onto the backend's mutation syntax. A third agent reads the numbers that come back and turns them into a ranked list with caveats. The language model is doing interface work between a human's vocabulary and a simulation's parameter file. Everything load-bearing is molecular dynamics.

The first demonstration starts with the user typing, in plain English, "help me find mutations that make the active site larger," against a halide methyltransferase being pushed toward bulkier substrates. The system comes back with a library of six hundred multi-point mutants, runs fifty nanoseconds of molecular dynamics on each one — thirty microseconds of sampling in total — and ranks them by the cavity volume averaged over the whole conformational ensemble rather than measured on a single structure. Of the top ten, four were confirmed active hits in a previously published experimental screen. A forty percent hit rate, about four-fold over the background rate in that library. They then ran a harder version where the user supplies almost no prior knowledge — just "all single point mutations within four angstroms of the substrate" — and let the system rank by the electric field projected onto the breaking carbon-iodine bond, filtered by folding stability. Three of ten confirmed, and that's a floor, because four more on the list were never characterized experimentally.

Now the part that made me sit up. Having generated six hundred trajectories, the authors asked whether the molecular dynamics was actually necessary. Could you get the ensemble-averaged cavity volume from one static structure with optimized side chains? The correlation is an R-squared of about two times ten to the minus five. Not weak — absent. Could you at least estimate how much a pocket grows from how much side-chain volume you deleted? R-squared about two times ten to the minus four. Also absent. Both of the shortcuts that people reach for when acting on cavity intuition carry no information about what the pocket does in solution. The reason is cryptic pockets: volume that only opens through collective loop motion and is simply closed in the crystal structure.

That's a result about enzymology, not about agents, and it's the paper's real contribution. It explains why intuition-guided cavity engineering fails so often, and it makes the expensive path non-optional — which in turn is the honest argument for why you'd want an agent here at all. If thirty microseconds of sampling is the only way to cash out a one-sentence hunch, then the barrier isn't the idea, it's that most protein engineers can't set up thirty microseconds of sampling.

Case two is prospective and wet, which I appreciate. A statistical observation from prior simulation work says bidomain amylases get better at working in the cold when the two domains sit further apart. The system designed linker variants predicted to increase that domain separation, and the group made them and assayed them. The best variant showed a nearly four-fold increase in activity at zero degrees relative to forty-five degrees, compared to wild type. A real closed loop from a sentence of intuition to a characterized protein.

Two things to be skeptical about, and the authors raise both. First, the system can only answer questions that map onto its finite library of computable metrics — electric field, binding free energy, substrate positioning, folding stability, cavity volume. Ask for anything else and the correct behavior is an explicit not-supported notice rather than a guess, which is what they built. That's a knowledge-representation problem wearing a chemistry costume: the whole product hinges on a mapping from the open-ended space of things a scientist wants onto a closed set of observables a solver can produce. They claim adding a metric takes editing two files. I'd like to see someone outside the lab do it.

Second, they're refreshingly clear that this is not a co-scientist. They borrow a three-level taxonomy — assistant, collaborator, scientist — and place themselves at assistant reaching toward collaborator. It refines the hypothesis you brought; it does not propose an alternative one, and it won't tell you your intuition was wrong in an interesting way. Given how much of this field is currently claiming the top rung, landing on the bottom one with a wet-lab result attached is the more useful contribution.
