# ProClaim-eval — the claim benchmark built from what database curators took back

Paper link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40906

## Script

Today's pick is an arXiv preprint from the European Bioinformatics Institute and the Institute for Computational Biomedicine in Heidelberg — the Saez-Rodriguez group, who most of us know better for OmniPath and CollecTRI than for agent work. The paper asks a narrow question about literature agents, and answers it with a benchmark that I think is the bigger contribution.

The narrow question first. Essentially every scientific literature agent now runs the same loop: retrieve something, write down what you found, decide whether what you've written down is enough, and if it isn't, use it to steer the next retrieval. These systems get evaluated end to end, on the final answer, usually without ground truth — which means that when the whole thing works, nobody can say which part did the work. This paper pulls the loop apart along two axes, what the recursion builds and what decides when it stops, and varies one while holding the other fixed.

To do that they needed a task where the right answer is genuinely known. And this is where it gets interesting. They define what they call consensus claim verification. Not "does this passage support this claim", which is the setting every existing dataset actually measures, but "what does the literature as a whole currently hold about this claim". The distinction matters, because a verdict grounded in one retrieved passage is a statement about that passage, not about the field.

The trick for getting gold labels is lovely. They take five actively maintained expert-curated databases and mine their own revision history. A SUPPORT claim is an entry curators added with multiple supporting publications behind it. A REFUTE claim is an entry curators rejected, removed in a later release, or kept but flipped the sign of. SIGNOR contributes a hundred and one re-curated signed protein-protein interactions. ConnectomeDB contributes three hundred and five ligand-receptor pairs — support if added in the twenty twenty-five release, refute if rejected by the curators back in the twenty twenty release. ExTRI, part of CollecTRI, contributes transcription-factor-to-target regulations. And then, to find out whether any of this generalizes past biology, the NASA Exoplanet Archive contributes planets later shown to be false positives, and the NUBASE nuclear structure evaluation contributes isomeric states that were reported and then shown not to exist. Seven hundred and eighteen claims in total.

I want to sit on that for a second, because for anyone who builds or maintains a curated resource, it reframes something. The deletions are the asset. Every time a curator looked at an entry and took it back, they produced a labelled negative that no annotation budget could have bought — a claim the published literature once supported strongly enough to get it into a database, and that the field has since abandoned. That is exactly the hard case, and it is already sitting in the version history of resources that exist today. They also strip out, from the three older subsets, every claim a model gets right with no retrieval at all, so what remains genuinely requires going to the literature rather than recalling it.

Now the findings, which are mostly confirmations with one surprise.

The evidence state helps, but what matters is what you put in it. Holding the retrieved papers completely fixed, they hand the same model the same evidence in four forms: abstracts, full text, the stance-labelled facts their system extracted, and facts plus abstracts. The extracted facts beat full text — seventy-seven percent agreement against seventy. The distillation outperforms the thing it was distilled from. And an agent that keeps those same papers only in its growing context, rather than in a structure persisted outside it, does worse than reading the raw papers.

The surprise is the judge. They replaced the stopping rule with a two-layer neural network — sixty-four and thirty-two hidden units, reading ten pooled features off the evidence state, things like claim-to-paper similarity and per-paper entailment scores. They fit it once on an unrelated public dataset and never showed it this benchmark. In pure transfer, it gives the highest agreement of any stopping rule on three of the five subsets, matches Claude Sonnet as a judge, and beats Claude Haiku. The mechanism they report is that the language model judges are overconfident — they stop after fewer rounds, consistently, across every subset — and the small trained model is conservative. Whether you have looked enough turns out to be a property of the evidence you already hold, and apparently a simple enough property that ten features and a tiny network capture it.

One more result deserves your attention, because it is an argument against trusting any benchmark intuition outside the domain it was built in. Claude Code and Codex, used as general-purpose harnesses, are the strongest systems on the biology subsets — Codex leads on SIGNOR. On the nuclear physics subset, both fall below the random baseline, abstaining on roughly sixty percent of claims. Same tools, same prompts, same retrieval. Where the corpus and the entity names stop looking like anything the model has seen, the whole thing collapses into refusal. That's the same failure shape we talked about with Munin a couple of weeks back — thin evidence read as disconfirmation — and it is apparently what these systems do by default when you move them off their home turf.

The caveats are theirs and they are honest. One frontier backbone. The design-axis studies run on forty claims per subset, so small differences there are indicative rather than settled. And the gold labels inherit whatever coverage gaps and update lag the source databases carry. Their REFUTE label also deliberately merges active contradiction with substantive absence of support, which is defensible as an evidential-warrant framing but does mean those two quite different failures are not separable in the score.

Where I'd push this: their own stated next step is to point the design back at the databases — to use consensus verification to help curate OmniPath. That is the loop closing. A resource's revision history trains and evaluates the system, and the system then proposes the next revision. Worth watching carefully whether the labels stay independent enough for that to still mean anything.
