# EpiBench — a benchmark shows frontier LLMs still can't ground antibody-epitope reasoning in sequence

Paper link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.06022

## Script

Today's paper asks a question that anyone building AI into a drug-discovery pipeline should want answered honestly: can a frontier language model actually reason about where an antibody binds its target, starting from nothing but sequence? The paper is called EpiBench, from a team at Valhalla Technology and Zhejiang University, and the short answer is no — not yet, and the way it fails is more instructive than the headline.

Here's why the question matters. An antibody's whole therapeutic personality is set by its epitope — the specific patch of residues on the antigen it grabs. The epitope decides whether the antibody blocks function, whether two antibodies compete for the same site, and whether a single point mutation in a virus lets the target slip the antibody's grip. If a language model genuinely understood antibody-antigen interfaces from sequence, you could imagine folding it into triage, into binning campaigns, into escape forecasting. So the authors built a closed-book, sequence-only benchmark of about sixteen hundred curated samples, grounded not in the model's memory but in real structural contacts, real functional B-cell assays, and real deep mutational scanning escape measurements. And they were careful — they stripped out PDB identifiers, antibody names, antigen names, any metadata a model could use to just look up the answer it half-remembers from training. Five connected tasks, spanning the actual antibody-development workflow: find the targetable region, identify the epitope given a specific antibody, decide whether two antibodies bin to the same site, judge functional consequence, and predict escape from a mutation.

They ran nine models through it — GPT five-point-five, Gemini 3 Flash, Grok, DeepSeek, Qwen, the current tier, both reasoning and non-reasoning variants. And the results are a clean, unglamorous verdict.

The single most damning number is on the task of telling epitope residues from non-epitope residues across a sequence. Measured by area under the ROC curve, every model sits between fifty and fifty-five — where fifty is a coin flip. That's the discrimination that actually matters, and the models are essentially guessing. Now, they aren't uniformly useless: on a coarser version of the task, just proposing a handful of broadly plausible surface residues, the better models do beat chance. So they've picked up something — a sense of which regions of a protein tend to be antibody-accessible. What they haven't picked up is the fine-grained, residue-level correspondence that separates a real binding footprint from a plausible-looking patch.

The second finding is the one I'd attend to most as a practitioner, because it's about scale. They stratified by antigen length, and the region-recall score falls off a cliff. On antigens under two hundred residues, the best model recovers about eighty-one percent of the targetable region. On antigens over eight hundred residues — which is where a lot of real therapeutic targets live — that collapses to about thirteen percent. As the protein gets longer, the number of decoy surface patches explodes, and the models lose the thread. This is long-context grounding failing exactly where you need it, and it's a good reminder that a big context window is not the same thing as reasoning across that window.

Third: escape assessment, arguably the highest-stakes task — will this specific mutation let the antigen evade this specific antibody — barely clears random. Best model around fifty-five against a baseline of fifty. That's the durability question for any antibody therapeutic against a mutating target, and the models can't answer it from sequence.

A couple of details sharpen the take rather than soften it. There's no single winner and no reasoning bonus. Gemini leads on region discovery, GPT is most consistent and leads binning, and the non-reasoning Grok actually leads on identification and escape. So flipping on chain-of-thought does not buy you epitope understanding — this isn't a reasoning-effort problem, it's a grounding problem. And the specialized, purpose-built tools — an older dedicated epitope predictor like BepiPred — still beat the general models on the tasks where you can compare. The frontier hasn't absorbed the specialist here.

So what's the intuition to walk away with. Language models have clearly learned some coarse statistics of protein surfaces — enough to gesture at where antibodies tend to land. What they have not learned is the thing that makes epitope prediction hard: the latent three-dimensional contact geometry, conditioned on a particular antibody, read off two one-dimensional strings. That's a structural inference problem wearing a sequence costume, and general-purpose text reasoning doesn't crack it.

I like this paper because it's a negative result done properly — leak-controlled, workflow-shaped, with specialist baselines to keep everyone honest — in a domain where the temptation to overclaim is enormous. If you're tempted to drop a frontier model into antibody triage because it sounds fluent about immunology, EpiBench is the cold water: fluent about epitopes is not the same as grounded in them. The benchmark and data are open, which means the more useful use of it is as a target — a diagnostic to train against, not just a scoreboard. That's the honest state of play for sequence-aware biomedical language models today.
