# Intern-S2-Preview — an open-weights scientific agentic foundation model with native multi-omics and molecular reasoning

Paper link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13505

## Script

Today's pick is a big one, and it's really a bet about where agentic AI for biology should live. It's called Intern-S2-Preview, an open-weights scientific agentic foundation model from the InternLM team at the Shanghai AI Lab. The weights are already up on Hugging Face, and that matters for the argument I want to make.

Here's the tension it speaks to. Most biomedical agents you and I have seen — including plenty on this show — take a frozen general-purpose model, a Claude or a GPT, and wrap it in scaffolding: tools, retrieval, a knowledge graph, an orchestration layer. The intelligence is general; the biology lives in the wrapper. Intern-S2 makes the opposite bet. It bakes the science and the agency into the model itself.

Concretely, this is a three-hundred-and-ninety-seven-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts model, and it's multimodal in a way that's unusual for a science model. Alongside text and images, it carries a dedicated time-series encoder for scientific signals — spectra, sensor traces, numerical measurements — so it can both understand and forecast the kind of raw numerical data that real experiments produce, instead of only reading about it in a table.

But the part worth your attention is the training. After the usual pre-training and fine-tuning, they run what they call a black-box and white-box agentic reinforcement-learning stage. The idea is a harness-times-task abstraction: they decouple the agent runtime — the tool-using harness — from the task itself, and they align the semantic action-and-observation trajectory with the token-level rollout. In plain terms, they're teaching the model to use tools and to sustain long, multi-step work as a first-class skill, learned under verifiable rewards, and then distilling that agentic expert together with the reasoning expert back into one unified model. Agency isn't a prompt trick layered on top — it's trained in.

Does it pay off for biology? On the multi-omics side, it leads on Biology-Instructions, a benchmark that tests understanding of genomic, transcriptomic, and proteomic sequences with real reasoning attached, beating strong open and closed models. It tops molecular-reasoning benchmarks like Mol-Instructions, and a broad scientific-reasoning suite that includes medicine. And it goes beyond reading: it's evaluated on de novo protein binder design across nine targets, with candidates scored the way a structural biologist would — interface confidence, binding energy, contact geometry, the kind of check you'd run with AlphaFold 3 or RFdiffusion in the loop. So this is a model that reasons over sequences and molecular graphs, and can act inside a real design pipeline.

Now the piece I think this audience will find most interesting is a side experiment they call the Memory Decoder. Instead of retraining the giant backbone to add domain knowledge, they attach a separately trained parametric memory to the frozen model. On the biology benchmark, that lifted the score from about fifty-seven to about sixty — without touching the backbone at all. That's a modular, open-science story: you keep one shared, expensive, frozen foundation, and specialize it for your subfield by training a small memory module and bolting it on. If that holds up, it's a much cheaper path to a domain-tuned scientific model than the current fine-tune-the-whole-thing default.

So why is this the pick today, beyond the leaderboard? Two reasons. First, it's a clean, open, testable instance of the bake-it-in versus wrap-it-around question. We've spent a lot of episodes on clever scaffolds around frozen general models. Intern-S2 lets you ask, empirically, whether native scientific-multimodal training plus agentic reinforcement learning actually beats the wrapper approach on your assays — because the weights are right there to download and probe. Second, the multimodality is the right kind. A model that natively ingests numerical signal data and molecular structure, not just their textual descriptions, is closer to what an actual lab agent needs than another text-only reasoner.

The honest caveats. It's a preview, self-reported, with the usual benchmark-optimism risk, and "competitive or leading" is doing some work — it's state of the art on some scientific benchmarks and merely competitive on general ones. The protein-binder results are in-silico pass rates, not wet-lab-validated binders. And nearly four hundred billion parameters is not something most labs will run casually, though the open weights at least make distillation and the memory-decoder trick possible.

But strip all that away and the signal is clear: an open-weights model where agentic tool use and scientific-modality understanding are trained in, not prompted in. For anyone building biomedical agents, that's the experiment to watch — and this is one you can actually run yourself.
