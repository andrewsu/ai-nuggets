# MMedAgent-Plus — the medical agent that plans better once it can see what just happened

Paper link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27233

## Script

Today's pick is a system paper, and it's useful precisely because it's not chasing a clever trick — it's doing the unglamorous engineering of getting a planner to call the right specialist tool, out of eighteen of them, across twelve kinds of medical tasks and eight imaging modalities. It comes out of a Duke and Stanford-led group, building on their own earlier MMedAgent system from last year, and the headline number is almost beside the point. The interesting part is where it breaks, and why.

The design philosophy is worth sitting with for a second, because it's the opposite of the generalist push. Instead of training one big vision-language model to do radiology, pathology, and retinal imaging all at once — which is what most "foundation model for medicine" efforts attempt, and which tends to produce something fluent but mediocre at all of them — this system keeps eighteen separately-trained, best-in-class specialist models exactly as they are, one for chest X-ray classification, one for pathology, one for retinal scans, and so on, and trains a single planner whose only job is deciding which specialist to call, in what order, and how to fold the results into one coherent answer. Think of it less as a doctor and more as a very good triage coordinator who knows exactly which colleague to page.

They trained that planner on over two hundred thousand examples pulled from thirty-two public medical benchmarks, and they tested it three different ways, which is where the real finding is. When the planner gets to call one tool, see what comes back, and then decide on the next one — a multi-round conversation — it picks the right specialist generatively well, north of ninety-eight percent of the time. But when it has to commit to the entire sequence of tools up front, in one shot, before any of them has run, accuracy on that full plan drops to under eighty-one percent. Same planner, same tool library, same tasks. The only thing that changed is whether it got to see an intermediate result before finishing the plan.

The reason that matters is tool ambiguity. Several of these specialists overlap in what they can accept and what they produce — a pathology classifier and a general medical visual-question-answering model can both take a pathology slide and both return text, so from the request alone it's genuinely hard to tell which one the task wants. Once the planner has already run one tool and sees its output, that output disambiguates the next step. Asked to commit to the whole plan blind, it guesses wrong on exactly the pairs of tools that look alike on paper. That's a pattern worth generalizing beyond this one system: a planner's reliability isn't just a function of how many steps a workflow needs, it's a function of when the planner is allowed to look.

The other failure mode is almost the mirror image of that one. Some tasks have a step that's necessary but never stated outright — generating a written report is implied by "read this X-ray," not requested in so many words. The planner's error pattern on those is striking: it doesn't call the wrong tool, it calls nothing. Precision on the report-generation step is effectively perfect; it's recall that collapses, because the omission is invisible to a system that only reasons about what it was explicitly asked to do.

And then there's a third result that I think is the most important one for anyone building on this kind of architecture, because it shows up even after routing is fixed. Take pathology classification, restrict to only the cases where the planner called the exactly correct specialist — so routing is no longer the issue — and compare the specialist's own raw accuracy to what the full agent reports back to the user. The specialist alone scores point six nine. Passed through the agent's own synthesis step, reporting what that same specialist just said, the number falls to point five one. Nothing about the underlying prediction changed; what happened is that turning a specialist's structured output into a fluent natural-language answer quietly drops information. Good routing is necessary, but it isn't sufficient — there's a second, separate failure budget sitting in the handoff from tool output to final sentence, and it's one most agent evaluations don't even measure, because they only check whether the right tool got called.

Put together, this is a clean demonstration that orchestration across heterogeneous specialists is its own research problem, distinct from how good any individual specialist is — and that the honest way to evaluate it is to decompose the error into pieces: did it pick the right tool, did it know a step was required at all, and did it faithfully carry that tool's answer through to the words it finally produced. Most medical-agent papers report one end-to-end number and call it a day. This one reports three failure points instead of one score, and that's what makes it something you can actually learn from rather than just admire.
