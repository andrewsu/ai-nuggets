# Genome Skeptic — the same model that picks the right next analysis gets the answer wrong

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.09.23.753765v1

## Script

Almost every agentic system in biology asks one model to do two completely different jobs. It decides what analysis to run next, and then it decides what the accumulated evidence means. We evaluate the whole thing end to end, get a single number, and quietly assume that a model good at the first job is good at the second. A preprint that went up on bioRxiv yesterday, single-authored out of University College Cork, takes those two jobs apart and measures them separately. The answer is not the one most agent architectures are built on.

The system is called Genome Skeptic, and the task is deliberately unglamorous: deciding whether a bacterial genome carries a tetracycline efflux gene, tet-A or tet-B. That's a good choice, because both genes sit in the major facilitator superfamily, a sprawling family of membrane transporters. Detecting similarity to a transporter is easy. Deciding that you've got this specific transporter and not a relative means excluding the competitors, and that's where annotation actually goes wrong.

The architecture separates three layers that are normally fused. Deterministic tools — BLAST, MMseqs2, DIAMOND, HMMER — generate every sequence-level measurement. A controller can request follow-up analyses, but only from a fixed registry; it cannot invent or modify a measurement. And a rule-based adjudicator makes the final call. Twenty genomes, ten positive and ten negative, protocol and truth criteria locked before anything ran, blinded, nothing rerun after unblinding. Truth came from two independent routes that had to agree.

First result. Four different controllers — a fixed policy, a frontier model, an exhaustive strategy that runs everything, and a compact non-language classifier — all produced identical calls. Eighteen of twenty, every one of them. What differed was effort: the frontier model needed thirty-nine follow-up analyses against fifty-seven for the fixed policy and seventy-four for exhaustive. That's a third less work than the fixed rules and nearly half the exhaustive run, for exactly the same answers. And the evidence it gathered genuinely differed from fixed control in twelve of the twenty genomes. So this isn't a tie because nothing happened. The model found a shorter route to the same place.

Here's the part that should sting a little. The compact, non-language decision model matched that exactly — same thirty-nine analyses, same eighteen of twenty — at roughly five times lower latency and a hundred and thirteen times lower cost. If your agent's job is routing over a well-defined action space, you may be paying frontier prices for a routing problem.

Now the experiment the paper is really built around. Take the evidence record the frontier model itself produced, checksum it so you can prove it's identical, and hand it to that same model as the final judge instead of the rules. No new analysis. Same tools, same measurements, same evidence state. Accuracy falls from eighteen of twenty to eleven of twenty. Specificity collapses from ten out of ten to three out of ten. Paired difference in accuracy, minus thirty-five points, McNemar exact P of zero point zero one six.

The failure mode matters more than the number. The model did not start hallucinating genes that weren't there — sensitivity was unchanged. What it did was convert nine confident absent calls into unresolved. And seven of those nine were already correct. Meanwhile it corrected neither of the two errors the rules actually made. So the caution landed almost entirely on the easy cases and missed the hard ones completely.

That's worth being precise about, because abstention is not automatically a virtue. Selective classification says a model may decline the difficult cases in exchange for being more reliable on the ones it accepts. That's a real trade. But it only pays when the uncertainty is concentrated where the difficulty is. Here it wasn't. Here it would have generated seven unnecessary referrals to a human while leaving both genuine mistakes in place. Uncalibrated hedging is not humility; it's noise with good manners.

The mechanistic argument the author makes is the right one. Proving a gene is absent is not the same kind of computation as picking the next tool. Absence requires affirmatively excluding plausible competing explanations — that this hit is a paralogue, that this domain match is family-level and not specific. A next-token objective will narrate that comparison fluently without ever committing to a calibrated decision boundary over it.

I'd flag the limits honestly. One gene family, twenty genomes, one model configuration. Wilson intervals on eleven of twenty run from about zero point three four to zero point seven four, which is wide. The compact comparator is a vendor's product cited to the vendor. None of that undermines the role-swap design, which is internally airtight because the evidence was provably held constant — but it does mean this is a demonstration of a distinction, not a measurement of its size.

What makes it land for me is the timing. A different framework posted to arXiv this same week proposes agents for sequencing pipelines on the explicit premise that you confine language-model reasoning to the judgment layer, because that's where it's reliable. This paper tested that premise and found the judgment layer is precisely where it breaks. Orchestration and adjudication are separable capabilities, they fail differently, and an end-to-end score averages the good one and the bad one into a number that tells you nothing about either. If you're building agents over biomedical data, measure the two roles apart before you decide which one to hand over.
