# Virtuous Machines — an agent stack recruits 288 humans, runs the study, and writes the paper

Paper link: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13573717/

## Script

Today's pick went up yesterday in Advanced Science, from a Brisbane outfit called Explore Science working with the University of Queensland. The reason it's the pick is one sentence in the methods: the system recruited two hundred and eighty-eight human participants, and ran a study on them.

We've covered a lot of autonomous science here — agents designing CRISPR libraries, agents driving a robotic protein lab, co-scientists ranking hypotheses in tournaments. Almost all of it closes the loop on a simulation or a machine. This one closes it on people, and as far as I can tell the first-of-its-kind claim holds up.

Architecturally there's nothing exotic, and the authors say so plainly: the contribution is the architecture, not the models. More than fifty agents arranged hierarchically — a top-level orchestrator that can spin up its own sub-orchestrators, with specialists underneath for coding, troubleshooting, review. Every model is stock, pulled off the shelf from five different providers and deliberately mixed so each stage gets whichever one is best at it. Nothing fine-tuned. The argument is that no single frontier model does this end to end, but a well-organized committee of them does.

The part worth dwelling on is how the loop actually reached reality. The experiment was a set of standard cognitive psychology tasks hosted as a web page. The implementation agent read the pre-registration, derived the recruitment parameters — sample size, age range, vision requirements — wrote code against the recruitment platform's API, and instantiated a complete draft study. It could have pressed publish itself; there's a single API call for that. The authors didn't let it. A human checked the parameters and launched it, under a real human-research ethics approval, with two experimenters monitoring participant communications throughout. So the agent's tool interface was scoped to exactly what the ethics committee had already approved, and the irreversible action stayed with a person. That's the right design, and it's the piece that ports to any domain where an agent's actions touch somebody.

There is a caveat here that the abstract soft-pedals, though. The paper says three studies. There was one data collection. Study one powered itself at two hundred and eighty-eight participants, and that's what ran. Studies two and three then generated their own independent hypotheses, did their own power analyses, landed on a hundred and twenty and five hundred and sixty-six participants respectively — and then all three analyzed the same two hundred and seventy-seven usable datasets from study one. Study three, by its own specification, was underpowered by better than half. One real experiment, three analyses of it. The headline survives, but it's narrower than it sounds.

Here's what I think is the most valuable thing in the paper, and it's buried in the limitations. The obvious worry about an AI scientist is that it confabulates whichever result its priors already expected. How would you test that? They made the system commit to explicit hypotheses in a pre-registration document before it had any access to the data, and then they checked. Its hypotheses were violated, repeatedly — in one study, two of five got any support at all, and even those only partially. That's a genuinely clean test, because whatever the training corpus put in the system's priors, those priors could not determine the specific numbers that two hundred and seventy-seven strangers would produce. And it's cheap. Next to building a better autorater to grade the finished manuscript, forcing the agent to write down what it expects before it looks costs almost nothing, and it's far harder to game.

The failure mode they identify cuts the same direction. The system's biggest vulnerability isn't execution — it's anchoring at the front of the pipeline. A conceptual error introduced during hypothesis generation or study design propagates the whole way through, and the downstream verification cycles almost never reverse it. Once a false premise is in the chain of thought, later stages build on it instead of correcting it. Which inverts where most of us instinctively put the human: not reviewing the finished draft, but standing at the point of highest conceptual leverage, which is the first hour.

Two more numbers are worth carrying out of this. On reproducibility, they reran one study five times — different analytic designs, different interpretive emphases, same broad conclusions, which is about the best available answer to the seed-variance objection. And on cost: roughly seventeen hours of wall clock per study, thirty-two and a half million tokens, a hundred and fourteen dollars. Half that time and half that money is the data analysis stage alone, where the agents sustained eight and a half hours of coherent coding across seventy-odd run-observe-fix cycles and produced about seven and a half thousand lines of debugged statistical code.

Expert reviewers reading the manuscripts liked the writing, the literature integration, and the statistics — including a moment where the system chose to emphasize practical significance over a statistically significant but trivial effect, which is better behavior than plenty of human papers manage. What they flagged was conceptual: theoretical frameworks misapplied to tasks they don't cover, interpretive claims the data didn't support, an incidental significant result simply never discussed, and in one manuscript two different participant counts in two different sections.

Read that list and the shape of the bottleneck is obvious. Execution is solved, and it's cheap. Judgment isn't. The honest framing here isn't that an AI did science — it's that the expensive, slow, mechanical middle of empirical work now costs about a hundred dollars, and everything that made the work worth doing still lives at the two ends.
