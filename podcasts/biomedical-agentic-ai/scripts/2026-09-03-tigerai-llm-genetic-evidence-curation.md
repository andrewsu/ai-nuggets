# TigerAI — an LLM reproduces years of genetic-evidence curation, and the coverage gap lands exactly where you'd worry

## Script

Today's pick is a bioRxiv preprint from Penn and MD Anderson called TigerAI, and it takes on a question that anyone who has ever built or maintained a curated biomedical resource will feel in their gut: can a language model do the curation, and how would you know?

The setup is unusually well chosen. There's a well-known result in drug development that target-indication pairs with human genetic support succeed in the clinic at roughly two and a half times the rate of pairs without it. The most recent and most exhaustive version of that analysis was assembled by hand — years of expert curation integrating multiple genetics databases across the whole Citeline drug development pipeline. So there is a gold-standard human curation effort, and crucially, there is an outcome to score it against. Not "did the model agree with the annotator," which is the usual and rather circular benchmark, but "does the label the model produced actually predict whether the drug launched."

So they take thirteen thousand target-indication pairs that reached at least Phase One since the year 2000, and they hand each one to GPT-5 under a heavily engineered instruction protocol. The protocol is the real contribution and it's worth describing. The model is told to act as a human geneticist, and it is explicitly forbidden from using any drug, therapy, or clinical trial information — the reasoning is supposed to stay upstream, in genetics and functional genomics. It has to gather evidence across four defined channels: human genetic causality, biological coherence, animal and model organism support, and consistency across the evidence types. It has to reason about direction of effect, tissue of action, and paralogs and network context. It has to explicitly reconcile conflicting evidence rather than smooth over it. Every substantive claim has to carry an indexed source. And the whole thing collapses into a single zero-to-six integrated causal evidence score, thresholded at four.

The headline result is that GPT-5 matches human curation and then exceeds it on coverage. Relative success from Phase One to launch is two point seven four for the model versus two point six three for the expert baseline. Among launched programmes, the humans found genetic support for twelve percent of pairs and the model for twenty percent. And when you stratify by who found what, the pairs both approaches agree on are the strongest signal, but the pairs only the model found still carry a relative success of two point two — better than the pairs only the humans found, which land at one point seven.

Two things inside the paper are more interesting to me than the headline. The first is the model comparison. GPT-4o, run under the identical protocol, found more than twice as many supported pairs as GPT-5 in the early phases — and its relative success came out below the human baseline. More coverage, worse signal. The older model just scores everything higher. So what makes GPT-5 useful here is not that it knows more; it's that it's more willing to say the evidence is weak. Calibration is the whole product, and if you're building a curation agent, that's the axis to optimize.

The second is architectural, and it will divide people. TigerAI is not a database. There is no precomputed table of genetic evidence. Each query runs the prompt live, at about twelve cents a call, because precomputing twenty thousand genes across thousands of indications is neither affordable nor durable when the underlying model turns over every few months. That's an honest read of the economics. It also means the resource has no stable, citable state. Ask the same question twice a year apart and you may get a different answer with no changelog. For a field that has spent two decades learning why versioned, archivable knowledge bases matter, that's a real cost, not a footnote.

And now the thing I'd push back on hardest. In the methods, tool use is disabled. No web search, no function calling. Everything the model produces comes out of its weights. So that source-linked evidence ledger, the one where every claim has to be tied to a primary study or a database record — those citations are recalled, not retrieved. The paper never checks whether they resolve to real records. For a system whose entire pitch is traceability, that's the unexamined load-bearing assumption.

It compounds with a pattern in the numbers. Look at where the model's extra coverage actually appears. Phase One, the model is one and a half points ahead of the humans. Phase Two, one and a half points. Phase Three, it's slightly behind. Launched programmes, it's eight points ahead. The advantage is concentrated almost entirely in the programmes that worked. That is precisely the shape you'd expect if the model already knows which drugs got approved — and relative success is computed from exactly that stratification, so the headline number is the one that inflates. The authors did anticipate leakage and scanned every output for drug-development terminology, flagging two percent and manually clearing all of them. But that check catches leaked words. It cannot catch a model that simply knows the answer and reasons backward into the genetics.

There is a clean way to settle this, and I hope somebody runs it: take programmes that reached launch after the model's training cutoff and score those separately. Until then, the honest claim is narrower than the abstract's, and still worth having — a well-specified prompt reproduces years of expert curation at twelve cents a query, which tells you something real about where curation labor should go next.
