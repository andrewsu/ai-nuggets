# Emet — an agent asks a knowledge graph narrow questions, and two of four untested drug pairs hold up in cells

## Script

Today's pick is a bioRxiv preprint from BenchSci, posted yesterday, and it's the rare knowledge-graph paper that ends in a dish. They pointed an agentic system at lung squamous cell carcinoma, asked it for novel therapeutic targets with no target list specified, took the top four things it proposed, and had a contract lab test them in two cell lines. Two of the four produced combination effects that beat either single agent.

The disease choice is doing real work in the argument. Lung squamous is one of the most mutationally complex solid tumors — TP53 inactivated in over ninety percent of cases, CDKN2A gone, amplifications of SOX2, PIK3CA, FGFR1 — and crucially, no single dominant druggable driver. The adenocarcinoma playbook, find the driver and inhibit it, has failed here repeatedly: FGFR inhibitors, PI3K inhibitors as monotherapy, anti-angiogenics closed off entirely in squamous histology after fatal pulmonary hemorrhage. So it's a disease where reasoning one hypothesis at a time performs worst — exactly where you'd want to test a system claiming to reason across everything at once.

The infrastructure underneath is a biomedical knowledge graph of more than one and a half billion triples, with relations derived from over six million papers, canonicalized to a common identity system, and wired to more than a hundred specialized databases spanning cancer genomics, expression atlases, variant data, pharmacology, structure, pathways, trials and patents — plus a validation layer that traces every claim back to the records supporting it.

But the actual contribution isn't the graph size, and I'd encourage you to read past it. It's a claim about how you're supposed to use a link-prediction model, and it's a genuine critique of the last decade of this field. Every knowledge-graph drug-discovery pipeline since Hetionet has the same shape: train one model, run it once over the entire space of gene-disease or drug-drug pairs, get back a single global ranking. The prediction task is fixed before any biological question is posed. So whether a candidate is ever visible to you depends on where it sits in one universal ordering, not on whether it's relevant to the line of reasoning you actually care about.

What they do instead is a graph-of-thoughts search where each node is one hypothesis pursued under one strategy — synthetic-lethal dependency, protein-interaction convergence, expression-based vulnerability, precedent from other indications. Each node samples only the region of the graph its strategy makes relevant, then specifies what to predict over that region: which entities, which relationship type, in which direction. That scoped sub-problem, not the whole graph, goes to the graph neural network. So the same model gives different answers depending on which branch is asking, because both the input scope and the interpretive frame move with the branch. Outcomes commit to a memory that persists across rounds, so later rounds don't re-derive settled conclusions.

Their headline hit illustrates why that matters. Pairing a CDC7 inhibitor with a PKMYT1 inhibitor has no precedent in any combination screen anywhere, so a model ranking drug pairs by expected synergy has no signal to place it highly, and a supervised synergy predictor trained on historical screens is structurally blind to it. But the narrow question — which kinase dependency becomes essential when you suppress replication origin firing in cells whose G1 checkpoint is already dismantled by TP53 loss — hits locally dense evidence, and the pairing becomes reachable. Same class of model. Different question.

The mechanism is nice: block CDC7 and late origins never fire, so replication stays incomplete; block PKMYT1 and you remove the brake holding CDK1 inactive, so those same cells are driven into mitosis carrying under-replicated chromosomes. Two non-overlapping checkpoints in a tumor that has already thrown away its G1 checkpoint. In a seven-by-seven dose matrix the highest-single-agent synergy score was nineteen at day five rising to twenty-four at day seven in one line, and eleven rising to fourteen in the other — above their synergy threshold of ten in all four conditions, concordant under a Bliss reference model, and strengthening with time, as you'd expect of something compounding across cell cycles rather than acute toxicity.

The second hit is the more interesting one editorially, because the standard metric would have thrown it away. USP13 sits on the 3q26 amplicon next to PIK3CA and SOX2, and it's a deubiquitinase that stabilizes MYC and MCL-1 — so one arm removes protein stabilization while the other blocks replenishment through signaling. Combine a USP13 inhibitor with alpelisib and you get synergy scores of eight and of one point six. Additive, not synergistic. And that's what the hypothesis predicted, because the goal was never supra-additive killing; it was reopening a therapeutic window for a drug class that has failed as monotherapy in this histology. Thirty micromolar alpelisib alone gets about fifty-one percent inhibition; fifteen micromolar with the USP13 inhibitor gets roughly the same. A synergy-ranked screen would have discarded that as a negative.

And they went after the mechanism directly, which is what makes this more than a hit list. Immunoblots at twenty-four, forty-eight and seventy-two hours show MCL-1, c-Myc and SOX-2 all falling — and falling with the USP13 arm, not the PI3K arm, which is precisely the assignment the mechanism predicts, since blocking synthesis can't destabilize a protein pool that already exists. The time course matches too: nothing at twenty-four hours, because a stability-driven effect only shows up as the existing pool turns over.

Now the honest accounting. Two cell lines, one bulk ATP viability endpoint, and no cell-cycle profiling, no mitotic scoring, no micronuclei or gamma-H2AX quantification — so the mitotic catastrophe story is a rationale consistent with the data, not a demonstrated mechanism, and they say so. Neither line was genotyped for the strongest known predictors of PKMYT1 sensitivity. The USP13 tool compound also hits USP10. And the other two of the four failed: a POLQ-plus-TERT pair read out at ninety-six hours, probably too early for telomere attrition to accrue, and a GOLPH3 pair where no inhibitor exists at all.

The deeper gap is that there's no comparator. Two of four is a hit rate on four hand-picked candidates out of twenty out of dozens, with no arm where a global knowledge-graph ranking, or a team of humans, got the same four slots. So you can't say the architecture beat anything. What you can say is that its hypotheses arrived with an explicit mechanism attached and then survived contact with cells — a more useful property, for target discovery, than a score. And the authors are clear the loop isn't closed: the Director records its own outcomes, but validation results, including negatives, don't yet flow back as prioritization signal. Until they do, this is a hypothesis generator with a good memory, not a system that learns which of its own mechanistic arguments turn out to be right.
