# UMLS injection — the knowledge graph was never the variable

Source: arXiv 2610.33801v1, posted 24 Oct 2026. University of Bologna.

## Script

Today's pick is a survey, which is not usually how this show opens, but this one runs its own experiments and the experiments are brutal. It's from a group at the University of Bologna, and the title is a question: are we done with injecting the Unified Medical Language System into language models for medical question answering? Their answer is "maybe not" — and the reason is not that we've squeezed the resource dry. It's that nobody has yet run the experiment that would tell us whether UMLS was ever doing anything at all.

Here's the setup. UMLS is the canonical structured resource in biomedicine — the 2025 release carries about three and a half million concepts stitched together from a hundred and ninety source vocabularies, and the whole point of it is the stitching. For five years now there's been a steady stream of papers that take UMLS, turn it into a graph, retrieve a subgraph per question, feed that to a model, and report a gain on a medical exam benchmark. The gains are always small. Nobody has asked whether they're real.

So the Bologna team did a systematic review — twenty-five studies, screened and double-annotated — and then went and probed each pipeline stage in isolation. What they found is a chain of silent failures, and I want to walk the chain because every link is a lesson for anyone building knowledge-graph-grounded agents.

Start with provenance. Eighty-four percent of these papers do not say which UMLS release they used. That matters more than it sounds, because consecutive releases differ by hundreds of thousands of concepts — the library restructured and retired a chunk of the Metathesaurus mid-decade. Two systems reporting different scores may simply have been handed different knowledge, and neither paper records enough for you to tell.

Then coverage. UMLS ships twenty-three tables. Essentially every paper uses two of them: concept names and relations. Definitions appear in eight percent of the work, hierarchical context in none of it. And the most common move is to restrict even the names table to a single source vocabulary — SNOMED, say — which throws away the cross-source integration that is the entire reason UMLS exists rather than just using SNOMED directly.

Then linking, which is where it gets genuinely surprising. These systems reason over paths from a question concept to an answer concept. The Bologna team went into the released preprocessing artifacts of three of the best-known systems and counted how often the linker actually grounds each side. Question stems are long clinical vignettes, so they link fine — only about three percent come up empty. Answer options are short fragments, and they fail to link in fifty-nine percent of cases. For most of the benchmark, there is no question-to-answer path, because the answer side never entered the graph. The architectural premise fails silently on the majority of instances.

And what happens when it fails? This is my favorite detail. The papers never say. You only find it by reading the code. When no question entity links, the systems insert a dummy placeholder concept — serine. When no answer entity links, ethanol. Not a hub, not a principled fallback, just two arbitrary concepts sitting in place of the evidence, and the resulting subgraph bears no relationship whatsoever to the question being answered.

So then they ran the experiment the field skipped. They retrained two of these systems end to end on the main medical exam benchmark, three seeds each, under seven different UMLS constructions — four releases, two source subsets, and one condition where the subgraph is emptied of everything except those two placeholder concepts. Test accuracy across all four releases and both subsets: thirty-seven and a half to thirty-eight point seven percent. The empty graph, the one with nothing in it but serine and ethanol: thirty-seven point four. Inside seed variance of every real configuration. They also swapped the placeholders for a random concept and got thirty-eight, and skipped retrieval altogether and got thirty-seven point nine — all of it indistinguishable.

For scale: the published increments that this entire line of work rests on are zero point eight points for the first system over its own text-only baseline, and zero point five for the second over the first. The variation you get from choosing a different UMLS download is larger than the effect being claimed.

Now, the paper is not a demolition, and I think that's the right call. The constructive half shows that when you give modern instruction-tuned readers linearized UMLS paths, you do recover real accuracy on the cases they'd otherwise get wrong — double-digit gains across ten models from five families. But no single evidence view wins. Whether the reader wants bare relation neighborhoods, or question-to-answer paths, or paths enriched with definitions and semantic types, flips depending on the model family, the benchmark, and even whether you're prompting for chain-of-thought. And deeper paths beat shorter ones, which inverts the graph-neural-network instinct to minimize distance.

The takeaway I'd carry out of this: in a knowledge-graph-augmented system, the word "UMLS" in your methods section is not naming a resource. It's naming an undocumented pipeline — a release, a filter, a linker, a hop budget, a pruning rule, and a fallback policy you probably haven't written down. Each of those is a bigger lever on your final number than the model architecture you're actually publishing. If you're building agents that retrieve over biomedical graphs, report the construction, and report answer-side grounding, not just question-side coverage. Otherwise the benchmark table is a statement about your plumbing, not about the knowledge.
