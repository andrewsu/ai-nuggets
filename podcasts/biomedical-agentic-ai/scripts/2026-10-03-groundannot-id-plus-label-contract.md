# GroundAnnot — a closed-vocabulary contract that makes the LLM narrate enrichment results instead of inventing them

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.09.27.754741v1

## Script

Here is the result from today's paper that I can't stop thinking about. Give a small local language model a list of fifteen familial Alzheimer's genes and ask for the ten most enriched Gene Ontology biological processes. It comes back with synaptic transmission, presynaptic membrane, neurotransmitter secretion, synaptic vesicle exocytosis. That's a coherent, plausible story you could paste straight into a results section. Every one of the ten GO accessions it attached to those labels is real. And every one of them means something else. The IDs all sit in one numeric block of the ontology whose actual labels are visual perception, learning, mating behaviour, courtship behaviour, adult walking behaviour. They're fly-neuroscience terms, wearing synapse labels. The model returned the identical answer, bit for bit, in thirty-six out of thirty-six runs.

The preprint is called GroundAnnot. It's a single-author paper from the South African Medical Research Council's Genomics Platform in Cape Town, and it's squarely on the ontology beat. The tool itself is deliberately boring. It sends a gene list once to three live enrichment backends, PANTHER, Enrichr, and g:Profiler, and writes two files to disk. One file holds every GO term ID any backend returned, together with that backend's own label for it. The other holds only the terms the backends flagged as significant. Then there are two contracts. Contract A says any GO ID an agent cites must appear in the backend payload, and the label it displays must match the backend's label for that ID. Contract B says any claim of enrichment must point to the significant set. In the author's words, the model is a narrator, not an annotation authority.

The interesting design choice is when the check happens. GeneAgent, from Zhiyong Lu's group at NCBI, lets the model generate first and then verifies its claims against databases. GroundAnnot bounds the vocabulary before the model says anything. You could call that a minor engineering difference, but it changes which errors are even possible.

The benchmark tells you why that matters, and the numbers are worse than I expected. Three open-weight models were tested: a seven-billion-parameter Qwen running locally, a twenty-seven-billion Qwen, and GPT-OSS one-twenty-B, on six curated disease gene lists. Unconstrained, they produced terms the backends actually called enriched only fifteen, twenty, and thirty-five percent of the time. Their picks also barely track significance. Overlap with the backends' own top ten by false discovery rate averaged between a third of a term and two-thirds of a term out of ten. The author's back-of-envelope null for drawing ten terms at random from the enriched pool is about three-quarters of a term. So these models do no better than chance at ranking enrichment.

For anyone building validation layers, the most important line in the paper is this one: zero fabricated accessions. Every unsupported ID was a real GO term, just the wrong biology, the wrong branch, or obsolete. So the check most pipelines implement, "does this ID resolve?", would have passed every single error in this study. And the Alzheimer's case shows that even "is this ID in the right set?" isn't enough, because the failure lives in the pairing between ID and label. A downstream agent that reads the label and trusts it never sees the problem. The paper is candid that the label check wasn't in the original design. The benchmark forced it in.

Now the part I'd push back on, because the paper's headline result is its weakest. When the enriched shortlist is placed in the prompt, all three models stay in-list, a hundred and eighty out of a hundred and eighty picks. That's presented as proof that a bounded vocabulary removes these error classes. Well, of course a model can pick ten items from a list of a hundred and fifty it's been handed. That shows compliance, not judgment, and the author says so directly: it doesn't show the ten chosen were the right ten. It's also worth knowing what the bounded condition costs. Filtering the unconstrained runs against the enriched set after the fact leaves one and a half to three and a half surviving terms per run, and for the small model, a third of runs keep nothing at all. Contract A on its own is also weak, since the allowed file pools thirteen to fourteen thousand IDs per list. Contract B and the label match do the real work.

The other limits matter too. No frontier or tool-using model was tested, so we don't know how Claude or GPT-five-class agents with a live GO lookup would score. There was no head-to-head with GeneAgent. The gene lists are small, fifteen to twenty-eight genes. And the ten-out-of-ten label mismatch was audited on the Alzheimer's list only.

There's one more detail I'd flag for anyone who cares about reproducible annotation. Two backend snapshots pulled on consecutive days moved valid-enriched rates by up to about three points, and once, during an active backend update, the swing was about twenty points on the same day. Enrichment results are versioned data, whether we treat them that way or not. GroundAnnot ships dated snapshots, plus all three hundred sixty-five runs, the gene lists, and the code under MIT. The per-gene context, incidentally, comes from MyGene.info.

So here's my take. This isn't a model paper, and it isn't really a tool paper. It's a specification paper, and what it specifies generalizes well beyond enrichment. Any time an agent emits an ontology term, whether for GO, a disease ontology, or a knowledge-graph edge, the claim should be the pair of identifier and label, checked against a dated source. Checking that the identifier resolves isn't enough. Mungall's group warned in twenty twenty-three that LLM gene-set summaries invent plausible terms. This paper shows the more insidious version, where the terms are real and the meaning is invented, and gives you a small, open contract to stop it.
