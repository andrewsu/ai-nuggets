# INF — an inverse-document-frequency fix for knowledge-graph hub bias in target safety, and a recall gain the ranking metric can't see

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.11.19.767791v1

## Script

Every biomedical knowledge graph has the same shape problem. A small number of famous nodes collect most of the edges, and any model that learns from connectivity ends up learning fame. Usually that's just an annoyance. In drug safety it defeats the purpose: you need a prediction most for the obscure target nobody has drugged yet, and that's exactly where a hub-trained model has nothing to say. Today's preprint, from Valerie Gillet's cheminformatics group at Sheffield working with Evotec, tries a very old fix for this, and its results include a mismatch between two metrics that's worth more than the headline.

Here's the setup. Evotec started from the Drug Repurposing Knowledge Graph, which is mostly Hetionet, STRING, DrugBank and literature-mined relations. They stripped out the animal data, refreshed the Gene Ontology annotations, and added a curated set of about sixty-six hundred known links between protein targets and adverse events, drawn from Open Targets, in-house text mining and pharmacovigilance statistics. The skew is dramatic. Only four hundred seventy-three of nearly nineteen thousand genes have any adverse-event link at all, and the top twenty targets account for forty-five percent of all the links.

The prediction method is deliberately old-school and interpretable: metapaths fed to a random forest. A metapath is a template for a route through the graph, say target, then compound, then another target, then adverse event, and the raw feature is how many such routes connect a given target to a given adverse event. With raw counts the hub problem is stark. Recall is ninety-six percent for targets that already have more than a hundred and sixty known adverse events, and twenty percent for targets with five or fewer.

The fix is where the intuition is nice. They borrowed inverse document frequency from text retrieval, the trick that stops the word "the" from dominating a search. For each node, within each relation type, you take the log of the total number of edges of that type divided by how many of them touch that node. A compound that binds hundreds of proteins gets almost no weight as a bridge; a compound that binds three gets a lot. They pool those weights along each hop of a path and multiply them into the count. The key difference from Daniel Himmelstein's degree-weighted path count, the standard correction from the Hetionet work, is that the rarity is computed separately for each relation type rather than from a node's total degree. Being a hub in protein interactions doesn't penalize you as a drug target.

The most revealing result is which settings won for the sparsest targets. The best three all threw away the raw path count entirely and predicted from the rarity term alone. So for an understudied target, the useful signal isn't how many routes reach an adverse event. It's whether any of those routes run through specific, sparsely connected nodes. That's a real biological idea, not just a normalization detail, and the most important single path type supports it: target to shared compound to another target to adverse event, the same logic pharmacovigilance uses when it says that proteins sharing ligands tend to share liabilities.

The headline numbers say that on held-out links for targets with five or fewer known adverse events, recall goes from twenty percent with raw counts, and twenty-six percent with the degree-weighted correction, to forty-eight percent with the rarity-weighted ensemble. On an external set of nearly eight thousand target–adverse-event pairs from a different curation method, overall recall roughly doubles, from twenty-two to forty percent. For the sparsest targets in that external set, it goes from under five percent to twenty-five.

Now the mismatch, which the authors report honestly. On the ranking metric, which asks whether the true adverse event lands in a target's top ten predictions, all three methods are essentially tied on the holdout set, thirty-five or thirty-six percent. On the external set, ranking collapses below seven percent for everyone. So the rarity weighting isn't putting the right answers at the top of the list. What it does is push more true pairs over a fifty-percent score threshold. The authors themselves suggest it may lift scores near the threshold while knocking some confident cases out of the top ten.

That's where I'd push. Recall alone can't tell you whether a method has found signal or just raised everyone's scores. A model that called every pair dangerous would have perfect recall. As far as I can find, the main text never reports a false positive rate, precision or ROC curve for the assumed negatives. The training negatives were also drawn only from the four hundred seventy-three targets that already had a known adverse event, which makes them harder but leaves the truly unannotated proteome untested. For a safety triage tool, a doubled recall bought with an unknown false-alarm rate is a question, not an answer. The authors do recommend reading the full predicted list instead of the top ten, which makes that false-alarm rate the number a user needs.

Still, I like this paper for the Su Lab audience, and not because of the random forest. It's a clean demonstration that hub bias is a property of the graph, and that one of the oldest ideas in information retrieval, discounting what's common, transfers when you apply it per relation type. The code is public and archived in Software Heritage, and the authors point to obvious places to drop the same weights into embeddings or relational graph networks. For anyone building knowledge graphs for prediction, the practical lessons are to report performance by node degree, not as a single average, and to be suspicious when recall and ranking disagree.
