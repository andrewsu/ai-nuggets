# OntologyBench — dense retrieval can ground a concept but can't put three phenotypes together

## Script

Today's pick is a benchmark paper out of the Centre for Molecular Medicine and Therapeutics at BC Children's Hospital and the University of British Columbia, posted to arXiv yesterday. It's called OntologyBench, and it asks a question that anyone building a biomedical agent should already be nervous about: when you replace an ontology with an embedding index, what exactly do you lose?

The setup is disarmingly simple. They take three curated resources — the Monarch Disease Ontology for diseases, the Human Phenotype Ontology for clinical features, and HGNC plus NCBI Gene for genes — about sixty-six hundred diseases, eighty-two hundred phenotypes, twenty-three hundred genes. Then they build retrieval tasks in three tiers of increasing structure. Tier one is concept grounding: given a surface alias, retrieve the canonical definition. Tier two is single relations: disease to phenotype and back, phenotype to gene, disease to gene. Tier three is the interesting one — you get three phenotypes drawn from a single disease's phenotype set, and you have to retrieve the disease. Three, deliberately: one phenotype is compatible with hundreds of diseases, four or more collapses the candidate set to near-determinism, and three lands in a regime where about five diseases are genuinely relevant. So the model has to combine constraints rather than latch onto whichever finding is most distinctive.

Here's the shape of the result. Grounding mostly works. OpenAI's largest embedding model gets a ranking score of point seven five on gene aliases and point eight six on phenotype aliases, and the small biomedical encoders — SapBERT, BioLORD, MedEmbed — are right there with it on phenotypes. Disease names are shakier, around point five two, which is its own quiet problem. But broadly, that's the part everybody's RAG pipeline is already doing, and it more or less works.

Then it falls apart. On single relations, the best score anyone gets on disease to gene is point one one. On the compositional phenotype task, the best off-the-shelf embedding model manages point one seven. Fine-tuning on ontology-derived supervision pulls that up to point three one, which is a large gain — hit rate in the top ten goes from seventeen percent to fifty-seven percent — so the structure is clearly learnable and clearly not there by default.

And the comparison that should sting: the authors run a Phenomizer-style baseline as a reference — information-content semantic similarity over HPO with set-level aggregation, the method Peter Robinson's group published in two thousand nine. It scores point seven four on the same task. Seventeen years old, no neural network anywhere in it, and it more than doubles the best fine-tuned embedding model.

The error analysis explains why, and it's the most useful part of the paper. They classify the failures where the ground-truth disease doesn't come out on top, and fifty-nine percent are what they call aggregation failures: the model returns diseases that match some of the query phenotypes but not the conjunction. Another quarter are driven by whichever phenotype in the triplet is most generic. Which is exactly what you'd predict from the geometry. Cosine similarity in a shared embedding space measures overlap. It has no mechanism for conjunction — no way to insist on this feature and also that one and also the third.

The obvious response is to fix it downstream, and the paper closes that door too. Late-interaction reranking with a ColBERT-style model actually performs worse than the first-stage retriever. LLM-based candidate scoring — they try Qwen three point six at twenty-seven billion, Gemma four at thirty-one billion, and GPT five point four mini — does better than that, and still moves the end-to-end score from point three one three to point three one five. Essentially nothing. Part of the reason is structural: for eighteen percent of the queries, no relevant disease appeared anywhere in the top fifty candidates, so there was nothing to reorder. You cannot rerank your way out of a recall failure, and a reranker that reports beautiful numbers on the queries where a candidate happened to survive is measuring the wrong thing.

Two caveats worth holding. The ontology-aware reference isn't a fair competitor — it reads the curated relations directly, while the embedding models only see text, so some of that gap is an information gap, not a representation failure. And the fine-tuning is transductive: same ontology vocabulary in train and test, so it's multi-task transfer rather than generalization to unseen concepts. The authors are careful about both, more careful than the abstract lets on.

But the practical lesson survives all of it. A lot of biomedical agent architecture right now consists of pointing a retriever at a pile of text and trusting that whatever comes back is grounded. This paper says the failure is at the retrieval stage, before any reasoning happens, and it's silent — you get plausible, phenotypically adjacent diseases rather than an obvious error. If your system needs to satisfy several constraints at once, and biomedical questions almost always do, the structured resource isn't legacy infrastructure you're modernizing away from. It's still the best-performing component you have, and the interesting engineering question is how to put it back in the loop rather than how to embed around it.

Code is on GitHub under MIT, and the dataset is on Hugging Face under CC BY. Nearly six hundred thousand query-document pairs, all built from open ontologies. It's about as easy as a benchmark gets to actually run against your own retriever.
