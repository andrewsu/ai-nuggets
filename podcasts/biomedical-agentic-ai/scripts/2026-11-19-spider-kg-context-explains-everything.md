# SPIDER — a knowledge graph that keeps each finding's study conditions, and a "context explains it" verdict that also absorbs its own extraction errors

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.11.11.765839v1

## Script

Every biomedical knowledge graph eventually runs into the same embarrassing edge: drug X increases apoptosis and drug X decreases apoptosis, both with citations. Most graphs either keep the edge with the most support or keep both and leave you to sort it out. SPIDER, a bioRxiv preprint from a Shanghai Jiao Tong group, posted this week, takes the problem seriously. Its argument is that a triple isn't the unit of knowledge. The unit is a single finding, tied to the sentence that reported it and the conditions it was measured under. I think that's the right design. The paper's headline number, though, deserves more skepticism than the authors give it.

Here's the construction. They start from forty-eight databases and ontologies, the usual suspects like Gene Ontology, MONDO, HPO, ChEBI, DrugBank and Open Targets. That gives about three point seven five million entities. They cut that down to a research-oriented subgraph of about two hundred thousand entities, then run nearly two million PubMed abstracts through a three-agent extraction pipeline. One agent finds and normalizes entities, one proposes relations, and one assigns a standardized relation type. Splitting the job that way matters. With the same model underneath, the multi-agent version raises triple-level F1 from about point four seven in a single pass to point seven nine. The literature pass adds two point three million entity pairs the databases didn't have. It also lifts the share of entity pairs backed by at least one publication from about a third to almost eighty percent.

The design choice I like is that every supporting publication is kept as its own evidence record, with the PMID and the supporting text. Where two relations for the same pair look incompatible, an extraction step fills in a structured condition template: experimental model, perturbation, time point, readout, control. If you've worked with Wikidata, this is a statement with qualifiers and references, applied to the biomedical literature at scale. The graph also keeps explicit negative findings. The most common incompatibility it found pairs a reported "not associated with" against some positive relation, which is exactly the kind of evidence aggregated graphs throw away.

Their worked example is nifedipine and apoptosis. One paper says the calcium channel blocker blocks apoptosis in human endothelial cells exposed to oxidized LDL. Another reports more apoptotic smooth muscle cells in injured rat carotid artery after five to seven weeks of treatment. Those findings are different experiments that happen to share two entity names, and the pipeline labels them a context-dependent difference.

Then comes the headline. Across about ninety thousand pairs of apparently conflicting findings, the classifier calls seventy-nine percent context-dependent and only six percent genuine disagreement. The authors read this as evidence that most apparent conflicts in the literature reflect study conditions, not real scientific disagreement.

I'd be careful with that conclusion, and their own validation is the reason. Eight medical professionals labeled a random sample of 618 comparisons, blind to the model. Overall agreement was a respectable eighty-three percent. But look at the relation-mapping errors, the cases where the extraction simply got the relation or its direction wrong. The pipeline estimates those at under three percent of the whole set. The experts found about twelve and a half percent in their sample, seventy-seven cases. Of those, the classifier caught fifteen. It labeled fifty-three of them "context-dependent." The authors do flag this tendency in their limitations. Its consequence is bigger than a limitation, though. "Context explains it" is the category where extraction mistakes end up, and it's also the label that sounds most reassuring.

There's a structural reason this happens. Any two published studies differ in something, whether model, dose, time point or readout. If you give a language model two filled-in condition templates and ask whether a difference in conditions could account for the divergent results, the honest answer is almost always "it could." The bar is "could account for," not "does account for," so the category is close to unfalsifiable. That doesn't make the condition layer useless. It means the seventy-nine percent measures how often studies differ, not how often biology is context-dependent. A stronger version would make the classifier name which condition field explains the flip, and then check whether that same field predicts flips across many entity pairs.

The benchmark results need a similar caution. On their own conflict benchmark, structured conditions take a model from about fifty to seventy-seven percent accuracy, and SPIDER beats five other graphs, including PrimeKG and RTX-KG2, by at least twenty-eight points. But the questions were built from SPIDER's own comparison units. One hundred ninety-one of the two hundred questions ask you to pick the relation that holds under stated conditions, and only nine test whether you can recognize a genuine conflict. The graphs that don't store conditions were never going to win. The external benchmarks tell a more modest story. It's a solid gain on CIViC, where the task is literally whether a paper supports a claim in a specified context, about fifty-four to sixty-eight percent. On GWAS effector-gene ranking it's roughly a tie with MedKGent. On drug cardiotoxicity labels it's a sliver.

One last practical point for open-science listeners. The paper says the data are public, and there is a Zenodo record of about one point six gigabytes. But that record's own license file describes it as a private preparation draft with no dataset license chosen. It notes that redistribution rights for the upstream sources still have to be worked out, and DrugBank is among them. The GitHub repository has no license file either. Right now you can download it but you can't clearly reuse it. That's the usual integration-graph problem, and it's worth knowing before you build on it.

So here's my take. SPIDER gets the data model right. Keeping each finding separate, with its source sentence, its conditions and its negative results, is what a graph needs if agents are going to reason over it rather than just retrieve from it. But the conflict classifier is a hypothesis generator, not an arbiter. Its favorite answer, "different context," is also where its own mistakes collect.
