# HPO severity agent — grading ten thousand phenotype terms for a carrier screening panel, at ten cents a term

Paper link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.19569
Code: https://github.com/T0hid/hpo-classification-agent

## Script

Today's pick went up on arXiv on Thursday, and it's a good example of an agent being useful precisely because the task it's given has no right answer. The work comes out of the biomedical machine learning lab at the University of New South Wales in Sydney, with clinical genetics collaborators at the University of Pittsburgh, and what they built is an autonomous agent that grades the severity of every phenotype term in the Human Phenotype Ontology.

Start with why anyone would want that. Reproductive carrier screening panels — the tests that tell a couple whether they both carry a recessive variant for the same condition — get built from two criteria. One is carrier frequency, which is a number you can look up in a population database. The other is that the condition has to be severe enough to justify screening for it at all, and that is a judgment call. So the panels diverge wildly. An analysis of sixteen commercial panels found the number of conditions screened ranged from forty-one to seventeen hundred and ninety-two. Three conditions were on every panel. Only about ten percent of genes appeared on even half of them.

And it isn't that the labs are being careless. When twelve genetics professionals were put to work classifying a hundred and seventy-six genes by hand, their initial agreement was sixty-one percent. In Australia's Mackenzie's Mission carrier screening program, clinical geneticists disagreed on gene selection a quarter of the time. Severity is genuinely contested.

The agent is deliberately unambitious about the framework. It doesn't invent a severity scale — it takes the four-tier scale the field already uses, which came originally from a survey of a hundred and ninety-two healthcare professionals, plus a pediatric quality-of-life instrument to put some structure behind the obstetrics college's language about a detrimental effect on quality of life. What the agent adds is execution. For each of ten thousand two hundred and eleven ontology terms it pulls the definition, the synonyms and the parent relationships, maps the term onto a Medical Subject Heading to sharpen the search, generates up to fifteen literature queries from five templates, searches PubMed in a widening cascade, and, if it turns up fewer than five relevant papers, identifies the syndromes that phenotype usually appears in and searches again. Then it classifies, in a reason-and-act loop that averaged just under seven steps — and it is forbidden from using its own internal knowledge. Every factual claim has to cite one of the retrieved abstracts by identifier.

Then a second, separate agent fact-checks the first one. It parses the reasoning trace into individual claims, goes back to each cited source, and scores the match: full credit for a direct statement, most of the credit for a valid medical inference, partial for weak support, nothing for nothing. Anything below a half gets flagged for human review. Eleven and a half percent of the ten thousand classifications got flagged.

The accuracy numbers are good — ninety-three and a half percent against nine hundred and forty-one terms independently annotated by two geneticists each, and within one tier ninety-eight percent of the time. But I'd push back on treating accuracy as the headline here, because in a domain where the human experts disagree a quarter of the time, accurate isn't quite the right frame. What this system actually delivers is one rubric applied identically ten thousand times with a citation trail behind every call, for about ten cents a term. Tiering the whole ontology cost roughly a thousand dollars. The product is consistency and auditability, not correctness, and those were the things that were missing.

Two details make me more interested rather than less. The first is the direction of the errors. On a small set of twenty-nine genes with published severity assignments, twenty matched exactly, one came out too low, and the other eight the agent graded higher than the published source, on the grounds that it had looked at a broader phenotype profile. An automated severity grader with an upward bias is, functionally, a panel-expansion engine. That may well be the right direction — expanded screening is where the field is going, and Mackenzie's Mission found nearly two percent of nine thousand couples carried a newly identified risk. But it's worth saying out loud: the tool is not neutral with respect to the argument it's being used to settle.

The second detail is the one gene it underestimated. It called a branched-chain ketoacid dehydrogenase gene moderate rather than profound, and the authors diagnose exactly why — the ontology annotations for that gene didn't carry the intellectual disability phenotypes the wider clinical literature describes. The agent read the resource faithfully. The resource was incomplete. For anyone who builds or consumes these knowledge bases, that's the finding worth keeping: the ceiling on this system is the annotation, not the model, and what the agent does with a gap in the ontology is convert it into a confident-looking tier number with a reasoning trace attached.

Which is also where the framework runs out. About a third of the ontology's terms couldn't be placed in any tier at all — laboratory findings, acquired conditions, entries like tube feeding that aren't really phenotypes. And the evidence gets thinnest exactly where the disagreements live. In the mildest tier, the one holding adult-onset disease and infertility, direct evidence support fell and unsupported claims rose to about fifteen percent. The easy cases are easy, and ninety-five percent concordance with panels everyone already agrees on doesn't tell you much about the hard ones.

The code, the gene-level severity table and a runnable reproducibility capsule are all public, which matters more than usual here. If the output is going to be used to argue about what belongs on a screening panel, the argument itself has to be inspectable. This one is.
