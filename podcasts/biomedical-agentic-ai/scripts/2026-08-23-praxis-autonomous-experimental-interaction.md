# PRAXIS — three agents run a robotic lab for a month and learn what the data couldn't tell them

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.08.14.744985v1

## Script

Almost every AI system we've covered on this show learns by reading. Sequences, structures, papers, atlases — the model consumes what already exists. A preprint out of Duke and Harvard this week, from Philip Romero's group with Pascal Notin, argues that this is the ceiling, not the frontier, and then does something about it. The system is called PRAXIS, and its claim is that an AI can acquire biological knowledge by acting on a biological system and living with the consequences.

Here's the setup. The agent is a surrogate model of the sequence-to-function map — ProteinNPT, sitting on top of protein language model embeddings and zero-shot evolutionary scores. Each round it retrains on everything measured so far, generates candidate variants conditioned on its best hits, and picks what to test next using an upper-confidence-bound rule, so it's explicitly trading exploitation against its own uncertainty. The environment is a real robotic laboratory: a central arm feeding a liquid handler, thermal cycler, and plate reader, doing automated DNA assembly, cell-free protein expression, and a fluorescence enzyme assay. Design to measurement takes about ten hours. And I want to flag something, because it's easy to miss — there is no language model doing the reasoning here. No planner agent, no chain of thought, no tool-calling loop. The intelligence is a Bayesian-flavored active learner wired to a robot. That's the older and, I'd argue, sharper meaning of the word agent.

The task was deliberately unkind. Family one glycoside hydrolases have spent evolutionary history getting very good at glucose. The agents were told to reprogram that preference toward xylose and mannose — sugars that differ from glucose by almost nothing. The search space was a chimeric library: six natural parent enzymes chopped into eight recombinable fragments, about one point seven million possible combinations. And there were three agents, one per sugar, each with its own acquisition policy, but all writing into a shared experimental memory and all learning from a single common model. Every measurement any agent bought was immediately available to the other two.

Then they turned it on and walked away. Twenty-five rounds, roughly one month, no human intervention. One hundred fifty variants designed, one hundred thirty-six successfully assembled, one hundred thirty-two with clean assay data.

Sit with that last number for a second, because it's the most honest thing in the paper. A full month of continuous autonomous operation buys you one hundred thirty-two measurements. About six designs a round. This is not a criticism of the work — it's the actual state of self-driving labs, stated plainly by people who built one, and it's a useful correction to the ambient sense that closed-loop biology is throughput-unlimited. The bottleneck was never the model.

The headline results are sixty-fold and thousand-fold specificity gains toward xylose and mannose. Treat those with suspicion, and the authors say so themselves — when the reference parent's activity is sitting at the assay detection limit, fold-changes get large and meaningless. The defensible version comes from purified-protein kinetics on a panel of twelve variants: several genuinely exceeded the specificity of the parents that contributed their fragments, and two glucose-directed variants went the other way, to complete specificity with no detectable activity on the other sugars at all. There's also a deflating detail worth keeping: most of the non-native specificity came from breaking glucose activity, not from building new activity. Xylose and mannose gains were correlated because both were riding the same downhill move.

The result I actually find compelling is subtler, and it's the one that earns the word "learning" in the title. They took a thousand random chimeras — a fixed probe set, never assayed — and scored them with the model checkpoint from every successive round. Early on, having seen only the six parents, the model believed most of that space would work. By the end it had a completely different picture: glucose activity broadly accessible, xylose activity less common, mannose activity rare. You can watch the prior get corrected, round by round, on sequences the system never tested. That's what makes the knowledge-acquisition claim auditable rather than rhetorical, and it's a design pattern I'd like to see more of — probe your model's beliefs on a held-out grid, not just its score on the objective.

Two more things fell out sideways. The model attributed substrate preference mostly to regions away from the catalytic residues, distributed across the protein — testable hypotheses the agents didn't set out to generate. And three of the best variants independently converged on the same fragment from the same parent, which turned out to drive much higher protein expression, in one case to the point of cytotoxicity. Expression was never an objective. It emerged from having a body of experience rather than a body of predictions.

For anyone thinking about how this scales, the load-bearing idea is the shared experimental memory. Each agent paid for a third of the experiments and learned from all of them, and some of the best variants for one objective were found by an agent chasing a different one. The paper is honest that it never ran the ablation — shared versus isolated agents — so treat that as a plausible mechanism rather than a measured effect. But it points somewhere real: distributed autonomous laboratories, pursuing different objectives, contributing to one model. Not agents that talk to each other. Agents that share a lab notebook.
