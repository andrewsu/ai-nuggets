# Synthetic Hospital — an open EHR benchmark where the ground truth is built before the chart is written

Paper link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.30027

## Script

Today's pick went up on arXiv on Thursday, out of Carnegie Mellon. It's called Synthetic Hospital, and it's a longitudinal electronic health record benchmark. I want to talk about it not for the leaderboard but for the construction trick, which I think generalizes well beyond clinical AI.

Start with why clinical benchmarks are bad. Most of them are multiple-choice questions or single-note vignettes, and models have been crushing those for three years without any of it showing up in actual hospital workflows. The obvious fix is to benchmark on real charts. That fails twice. The first failure everyone knows about: privacy, review boards, data use agreements, so you can't share it openly. The second failure is the interesting one. A chart is not a record of what was true about the patient. It's a record of what somebody wrote down. Findings go undocumented, notes go missing, coding is inconsistent. So when a model misses something, you genuinely cannot tell whether the model reasoned badly or whether the fact was never in the chart to begin with. Your ground truth has no referent.

Their move is to invert the order of construction. Instead of writing charts and then annotating them, they build the patient's clinical state first, as a typed, ontology-grounded knowledge graph, and only then render that graph into prose that reads like a chart.

Concretely: they ingest publicly available medical education material — board-exam questions, flashcard decks, reference documents. A language model proposes candidate diagnoses and typed findings from each case, but those proposals are only candidates; the grounding to I-C-D ten, SNOMED C-T, and LOINC is deterministic. Then they cluster board questions into longitudinal patients by graph structure alone — encounters can only join if they're demographically compatible and share a diagnosis node — and only after that fixed cluster exists does a model write the narrative. Twelve hundred and sixty-eight patients, fifty-six hundred encounters. Every node keeps a provenance link back to the source question.

The consequence is the whole point. The benchmark labels are deterministic functions of the graph, computed before any prose existed, and therefore independent of the model that wrote the prose. The chart the model reads is incomplete, the way charts are. The answer key behind it is complete. That's the property real data cannot give you at any price.

Two validations, and they're both done carefully. On realism: they had ten licensed physicians do a blinded real-versus-synthetic discrimination. Physicians hit fifty-three percent, statistically chance, with inter-rater agreement slightly negative — no shared cue. The part I respect is the control. They took five real MIMIC patients and mechanically reformatted them into the synthetic schema, same eighteen section types, same lab rendering, expanding abbreviations, without rewriting a word of the clinical prose. Otherwise the physicians would just be detecting formatting, and the study would be worthless.

On the labels: a physician wrote down, in advance, a hundred and nineteen diagnosis relationships that a competent graph ought to encode — diabetes to chronic kidney disease, that kind of thing. The graph recovered a hundred and eleven of them. Ninety-six came from SNOMED relations, shared anatomical sites, or overlapping findings; fifteen needed hand-curated edges. And then there are eight it simply cannot get, and this is the most informative result in the paper. Hyperemesis gravidarum and Wernicke encephalopathy are causally linked in medicine, but they share no finding — intractable vomiting on one side, confusion and eye findings on the other — and no ontology edge connects them. Ankylosing spondylitis and anterior uveitis, same problem. They logged those as accepted gaps rather than lowering the shared-finding threshold to catch them, because lowering it would have flooded the graph with spurious links. If you build relevance out of ontology structure, that is exactly the class of knowledge you will be missing, and it's worth knowing the shape of it.

Now the results, briefly. Ten models, none close to ceiling. Best longitudinal diagnosis is severity-weighted F one of zero point seven three; the seven-physician mean on a matched subset is zero point six six, but the best physician is zero point eight nine. Summarization and imaging indication sit around zero point five. The compression at the top is the striking bit — top two models separated by zero point zero three, top four by zero point zero six, across different providers and wildly different sizes. Whatever has been improving in frontier models over the last year is not showing up here.

And the agentic result, which is why this belongs on this show. They gave models a thirteen-tool Epic-style interface over the FHIR standard with a forty-action budget, and compared three conditions: single-turn with the chart handed over, the same chart inside an agent loop, and an agent that has to go fetch its own evidence from a patient identifier. Outside of diagnosis, the agent loop hurt in every single model-task pair — losses of seven to nineteen points on summarization and retrieval. Letting it retrieve its own evidence recovered almost none of that. Failed sessions burned a hundred and forty-five thousand input tokens against sixty thousand for successful ones. The one exception is longitudinal diagnosis, where self-retrieval gained fourteen to thirty-four points — because there, assembling evidence across encounters is the task, not overhead around it.

That's a cleaner statement of a thing we keep half-saying: agency pays when evidence gathering is the problem, and costs you when it isn't.

Two caveats I'd hold onto. They explicitly do not simulate missingness, contradictions, or documentation errors — which is to say, the benchmark fixes the ground-truth problem by removing the mess that makes real charts hard. And look at the physician summarization number: zero point zero five finding-level F one. Physicians score near zero because they don't enumerate every finding; that metric is measuring exhaustive recall, not clinical summarization. The benchmark is a much better artifact than some of its metrics.

Eight hundred patients ship with full ground truth and deterministic scores, which makes this a usable training environment, not just an eval. Code and data are public.
