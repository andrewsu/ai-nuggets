# EIN-RECAP — a multi-agent summarizer of infectious-disease expert discussions that won every automated metric and lost the blinded clinicians

Paper link: https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.11.05.26367381v1

## Script

Today's paper is a medRxiv preprint from the University of Iowa, built with the Infectious Diseases Society of America's Emerging Infections Network. It's called EIN-RECAP. On the surface it's a modest applied system: summarize and search thirty years of a clinicians' mailing list. Underneath is one of the cleanest demonstrations I've seen of a trap anyone building agent pipelines should worry about. The more elaborate multi-agent system won every automated evaluation the authors ran, and blinded experts liked it least.

Some context first, because the data is unusual. The Emerging Infections Network is a sentinel network of more than three thousand infectious-disease clinicians, set up by the CDC and IDSA in the mid-nineties. Its listserv is where people ask things like "has anyone seen this pattern of resistance" before any of it reaches a journal. The archive runs from 1997 to this spring, about six thousand threads. That's real clinical knowledge, but it's opinion, anecdote and disagreement, not settled evidence. So a useful summary has to keep those apart.

The simpler pipeline, version one, does that well. A single generation model gets three inputs kept separate: the thread itself, PubMed articles from a journal list the clinicians chose, and similar earlier threads from the archive. Every claim in the output has to land in one of three bins: supported by a cited paper, supported by a cited earlier discussion, or uncertain. Citations are then checked deterministically against what was actually retrieved, and anything that doesn't check out gets moved to uncertain. There's also a time fence. A 2012 thread can only be backed by literature and discussions that existed before 2012, so hindsight can't make an old opinion look prescient. For anyone who thinks about provenance in knowledge graphs, that's the part worth stealing: community knowledge that hasn't been published yet, labeled as such and anchored in time.

Version two is the elaborate one. Three generation agents each read the thread from a different angle: one factual, one counterfactual looking for minority views and failure cases, one focused on community practice. Then a reflection agent critiques them, a proximity agent finds related threads, a ranking agent runs pairwise Elo tournaments over the recommendations, an evolution agent refines the winners, and a meta-review agent assembles the result. Everything runs locally on open models.

On more than three thousand threads, version two crushed the automated metrics. Its ROUGE-1 against the source was about thirty-three versus twenty-four for the simple pipeline, and its BERTScore about eighty-eight versus eighty. An LLM judge scoring eleven clinical dimensions also preferred it, on synthesis, on evidence calibration, and even slightly on factual accuracy.

Then two network members rated thirty recent, heavily discussed threads, all from 2025 or later so the models couldn't have seen them in training, with the three systems blinded and shuffled per thread. Version two came last, significantly below the simple DeepSeek pipeline after correction for multiple comparisons. The free-text comments show why. Version two drew thirty-one complaints about redundancy. The two simple pipelines drew one each. It also got more comments flagging incorrect or hallucinated content, eight versus four and five, and it was the only system called out for overconfident, dogmatic phrasing.

Here's my read. The redundancy isn't a side effect. It's what the architecture produces. Three perspective agents, then refinement, then a meta-review that pools the top-ranked recommendations from all of them: the same clinical point comes through several times in slightly different words. ROUGE and BERTScore measure overlap with the source thread, so restating it more often raises the score. An LLM judge reads restatement as thoroughness and synthesis. Both automated signals were rewarding exactly what the multi-agent design generates more of. That's worse than noise, because it pushes you toward the wrong architecture. And note which way the accuracy signals point: the judge gave version two a slightly higher accuracy score, while the human raters flagged it for hallucinations twice as often as the simple pipeline.

A second detail is quietly important. The two experts agreed well on whether the retrieved PubMed papers were relevant (they mostly weren't). But on recommendation quality, their weighted kappa ranged from slightly negative to about point three depending on the system. So the dimension you most want a verdict on is where two specialists barely agree with each other. If your gold standard is that soft, a confident automated score aimed at the same question should make you more suspicious, not less.

The authors then built an ensemble: a larger model reads the original thread plus all three candidate summaries and merges them, keeping distinct points and collapsing duplicates. On the nine threads where the raters disagreed most, three experts scored it above every standalone system on coverage and key clinical points. Two caveats. That's nine threads, picked for disagreement. And the ensemble uses a seventy-billion-parameter model while the others use thirty-two, so model size and ensembling are confounded, which the authors list as future work. Retrieval stayed poor for every system, a mean of about two out of five even for the ensemble.

The search tool is the deployed piece. In a beta at Iowa, thirty-two evaluations compared it with the network's existing search. Twenty-six preferred the new one, six were unsure, and none preferred the old one. Users especially liked that it handled abbreviations and misspellings across three decades of changing terminology.

So the takeaway isn't "multi-agent is bad." It's narrower. When the obvious way to make a pipeline more agentic is to add perspectives and refinement passes, make sure your evaluation doesn't reward restatement, because that's what those passes produce. ROUGE does. An LLM judge does. Thirty blinded threads and two clinicians willing to write "this repeats itself" were enough to catch it.
