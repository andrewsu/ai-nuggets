# Does multi-agent collaboration reduce hallucination? — the panel buys accuracy and sells you nothing on the reasoning

Paper link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27027

## Script

Today's paper asks a question that most of us have been quietly assuming the answer to, and then shows the assumption doesn't hold. It comes out of the University of Massachusetts Lowell, and the title is the question: does multi-agent collaboration reduce hallucination in clinical tasks?

Here's the setup. There's a large and growing literature on medical agent panels — three specialists and a moderator, debate rounds, layered proposer stacks — and almost all of it reports the same kind of result: the multi-agent system beats the single model on accuracy, therefore the multi-agent system is better. The unstated second step is that it's also safer. More heads catch more errors. If several agents read a case and have to reconcile, the mistakes any one of them makes shouldn't survive the group.

But accuracy is scored on the answer, and a clinician doesn't act on the answer. They act on the stated reasoning. A system can pick the right option for reasons the source does not support, and in a clinic that gap is the whole risk. So this group did the obvious thing that hardly anyone does: they scored the hallucinations inside the rationale, in the same generation that produced the answer, and reported both side by side.

Four systems — a single model, a panel with a moderator, a two-round debate, and a two-layer hierarchy. Four models in every seat, three clinical tasks. Each rationale gets broken into atomic claims, and each claim is labelled against the case, the answer options, the gold explanation, and when those are silent, against a corpus of three hundred forty thousand medical passages the systems themselves never see. Four error types: a claim the reference refutes, a claim nothing can check, a claim the supplied note doesn't support, and two claims in one rationale that can't both be true.

And the headline is this. Collaboration does buy task performance — a multi-agent system beats its own single-agent baseline in twenty-three of thirty-six comparisons, and the best configuration on every task is a multi-agent one. Hallucination does not follow. Of those same thirty-six comparisons, only twelve move enough to resolve at all, and of those twelve, four go down and eight go up. The four that go down are all the same model. No topology is reliably better than another — for every model, the wiring that minimises refutable claims is not the wiring that minimises unverifiable ones.

What determines the level, and the direction, is the model. Across models, accuracy and hallucination are strongly anti-correlated — the better models hallucinate less, with correlations around minus point eight to minus point nine seven. Within a single model, where only the topology changes, that relationship evaporates; the correlation over one model's four systems ranges from minus point eight two to plus point nine four, no consistent sign at all. The models sit far apart and their topologies cluster tightly around them.

Two follow-ups make the mechanism concrete, and this is where the paper earns its keep. First: scaling the panels from three agents to seven does essentially nothing — the mean change is five thousandths in one direction, two thousandths in the other, and only one of twelve intervals excludes zero. As the authors put it, no amount of conferring gives a model a fact it does not have. Second, and much more interesting: they swapped the aggregator alone — the agent that reads everyone's proposals and decides what survives — while holding the proposal pool fixed. Put the strong model in that one seat over the weak model's proposals, and refutable claims drop from about point one five to point zero four, landing almost exactly where the strong model sits on its own. And the reverse also stays low: the weak model aggregating over strong proposals holds around point zero three. Hallucination is high only when both positions are weak. The structure isn't doing the work; the system lands at roughly what its best participant could already do.

Then the cleanest experiment. Every multi-agent topology, underneath the role-play, is a model revising text written by other instances of itself. So: take the single-agent cases where the judge flagged a hallucination, hand the model back its own flagged claims with the error type, and ask it to rewrite. The ordering of who improves matches the ordering of who collaboration helps. The one model collaboration improves cuts its refutable claims by fifty-three percent when shown them. The small open model whose hallucination collaboration amplifies most reduces nothing measurably, and introduces new self-contradictions at about twice the rate of the others.

The conclusion I'd take away is the authors' own sentence: collaboration redistributes what its members can already do, it does not supply what they cannot. And the honest caveat they flag — handing a model its errors is easier than finding them, and models find errors far less reliably than they repair them. Which points somewhere useful. If the repair step works and the finding step doesn't, the thing worth building is not another topology, it's the external verifier that does the finding — and that only pays off for a model that acts on what it's shown.

For anyone building biomedical agent systems, the operational lesson is narrow and sharp. An accuracy gain from adding agents is not evidence of reduced hallucination, and you cannot report it as one. They are different measurements, taken from different parts of the same generation, and in this study they move independently. If your justification is going to be read by a person who then acts on it, you have to score the justification.

Two limits worth stating. Four models, three tasks, four topologies — representative, not exhaustive. And none of these systems take agentic actions: no retrieval, no tools, no environment. Which leaves the most interesting version of the question open, because retrieval is exactly the thing that might supply the fact that conferring cannot.
