# InfoFlowEX — ontology-guided alignment unifies 40 biomedical extraction datasets so LLMs generalize across domains

Paper link: https://www.cell.com/cell-reports-medicine/fulltext/S2666-3791(26)00392-7
DOI: 10.1016/j.xcrm.2026.102975

## Script

Today's pick is a paper in Cell Reports Medicine called InfoFlowEX, and it goes after a problem that anyone who has ever tried to build a biomedical knowledge graph knows in their bones: you cannot just pile up existing datasets and train on the union.

Here's why. Say you want a language model to pull entities and relations out of biomedical text — genes, drugs, diseases, interactions. There are dozens of beautifully annotated public datasets for exactly this. But a protein-based drug gets labeled a chemical in one corpus and a gene product in another. An adverse drug reaction is its own category called adverse effect in one dataset and folded into disease in the next. One corpus splits DNA, RNA, and cell lines into three types; another collapses all of them into a single gene. And the authors make a sharp point about this: these aren't labeling mistakes you can clean up. They're conflicting implicit ontologies. Each dataset encodes a different theory of what the categories even are. Pool them naively and you don't get more signal — you get a model fed contradictory supervision, and its generalization actually gets worse.

So InfoFlowEX treats that as the real problem. They build one reference ontology — twenty-eight canonical entity and relation types drawn from an existing biomedical knowledge graph — and then train a term-embedding model that takes each source dataset's label names and projects them into a shared space, mapping them onto those canonical types automatically. No hand-written mapping rules per dataset. On a gold-standard check, the alignment hits a macro F-score of about 0.95. Run it across forty public datasets and you get what they call BIE-Corpus: a hundred and eighty-four thousand samples, twelve million tokens, over six hundred thousand curated entity mentions, all speaking one vocabulary. And the framing here is the part I'd underline — they reframe dataset unification from a grubby, per-dataset engineering slog into a scalable ontology-consolidation problem. That's a genuinely useful way to think about it.

The second half is how they train on it. Instead of just handing the model a label name in the prompt — extract disease entities — each of the twenty-eight types is written as a little Python class carrying the definition, the annotation guideline, and worked examples. For any given task they dynamically pull in just the relevant schemas. It's model-agnostic; they run it over seven open seven-to-eight-billion-parameter models — Llama 3, Mistral, Qwen, and the rest.

Then they show it doing real work in three settings. First, entity-aware retrieval for biomedical question answering — inject the extracted entities into the retrieval step and answers improve. Second, and I think the nicest one, diagnosis from electronic health records: rather than feeding raw clinical notes to a classifier, they extract structured entities first and diagnose on those. Top-three accuracy across eight departments lands around 0.84, and because the prediction is grounded in named concepts, you can interrogate it — for grade-three hypertension, the plain model was leaning on words like tablet and capsule, while the InfoFlowEX version keys on serum albumin and nifedipine, which are the clinically meaningful features. Third, knowledge-graph expansion: they run relation extraction over new literature and fold the results into PrimeKG. It recovers a known interleukin-six to JAK-two link, and then flags a hypothesized interleukin-six to CCL-two connection that they went and found literature support for afterward.

Now, the honest read. If you look at the raw extraction numbers, the gains from InfoFlowEX are modest — often one or two points of F-score over a fine-tuned baseline. This is not a paper that blows the leaderboard open. The value is somewhere else: it's the reusable, ontology-aligned corpus and benchmark, and it's the framing that dataset heterogeneity is an ontology problem, not a noise problem. And they backed the open-science side — the corpus is up on Zenodo, the code is on GitHub. The one closed piece is the clinical health-record dataset, which stays access-controlled for privacy, which is fair.

Why does this matter for anyone building agentic systems over biomedical data? Because the flashy demos — the multi-agent hypothesis machines — all sit on top of a retrieval-and-extraction layer, and if that layer is speaking forty mutually inconsistent dialects, everything above it inherits the incoherence. This is the unglamorous plumbing that decides whether your agent's knowledge is actually coherent knowledge. Worth a look, especially if you have a knowledge graph you're trying to keep current.
