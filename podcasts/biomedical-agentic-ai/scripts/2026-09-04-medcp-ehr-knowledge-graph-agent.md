# MedCP — one protocol puts an agent in front of a hospital's EHR and a biomedical knowledge graph at the same time

## Script

Today's pick went live this morning in Frontiers in Artificial Intelligence, out of Sergio Baranzini's group at U-C-S-F with the San Diego Supercomputer Center. It's called MedCP, and the one-line description sounds almost too boring to cover: it's a Model Context Protocol server. What it actually does is hand a single language model two things at once — the de-identified electronic health record of a large academic medical center, over six million patients standardized to the OMOP common data model, and SPOKE, Baranzini's biomedical knowledge graph, roughly forty-five million nodes and a hundred and thirty-three million edges of genes, drugs, pathways and diseases. You ask a question in English. The model writes parameterized S-Q-L against the clinical records and Cypher against the graph, the queries execute locally inside the health system network under least-privilege roles, and only aggregate results come back. The model never holds the credentials.

The architectural claim is the part worth arguing about, and I think they're right. The dominant way to ground a model in biomedical knowledge has been retrieval-augmented generation — embed your corpus, stand up a vector database, tune the retriever. That approach has two costs people underplay. It's infrastructure you have to own and maintain, and the embeddings go stale the moment the source data moves unless you re-vectorize. MedCP throws the whole retrieval layer out and lets the model write native queries. Nothing is precomputed, so every answer reflects the database as it exists at query time, and you get things similarity search fundamentally cannot express — temporal logic, exact denominators, multi-hop graph traversals. Adding a new data source means writing one conformant server. You don't touch the model, the client, or any pipeline.

And the second-order consequence is the one I'd actually carry away. Every claim the system returns is attached to the human-readable query that produced it. The authors draw a distinction I hadn't seen put this cleanly: they're not offering interpretability of the model, they're offering auditability of the data path. You can't ask why the agent chose that query. You can re-run it, change it, and check that the number is real. For research integrity, that's the property that matters, and it's a much more achievable target than opening up the weights.

Now, the evaluation, which is more honest than the abstract lets on. They built a hundred clinical research questions in ten tiers of rising complexity, graded zero to two by a clinician and a bioinformatician. Turning the knowledge graph on moved GPT five point five from one point zero five to one point two seven, and Claude Opus four point eight from zero point eight four to one point zero two. Real, statistically solid, and modest. More telling is the tier breakdown: the benefit does not grow with difficulty. For Claude, the entire gain sits in tiers one through five and is exactly zero in the hard half, where the absolute score is around zero point five out of two. So the graph helps most where you needed it least. On the hardest questions both models are still mostly failing, and the recurring failure modes are the unglamorous ones any epidemiologist would predict — missing washout periods, wrong denominators, incomplete mapping of graph-derived gene sets back into OMOP concepts.

There's a nice orthogonal result on a gene-disease benchmark. With graph access, multiple-choice accuracy climbed fifteen points for GPT five point five and seven for Claude — but true-false accuracy didn't budge for either. The graph helps you discriminate between candidates. It doesn't help you check whether a proposition is true. That's a genuinely useful thing to know about when to reach for a knowledge graph.

The headline they lead with is that the system compresses a real-world evidence study from weeks to under an hour of active work, and here I want to slow down, because their own worked example is the best argument against reading that headline the easy way. They replicated one of their own U-C-S-F multiple sclerosis safety studies and got a hazard ratio of one point nine four against the original two point two seven. Fine — same institution, same patients, different data model. Then they took an external protocol, G-L-P-one receptor agonists versus S-G-L-T-two inhibitors and dementia risk, originally run on a multi-site database with sixteen thousand matched pairs, and executed it end to end locally. It ran. It produced a hazard ratio of zero point seven one — opposite in direction to the original null — on five hundred and twenty-three matched pairs with twenty-five total dementia events. The confidence interval spans zero point three to one point five six. To their real credit, the authors say plainly that this is compatible with the original, not a confirmation of it.

That's the lesson, and it isn't a knock on the system. Writing the queries was never the rate limiter in observational research. Cohort size was. What an agent like this changes is that a single site can now generate an underpowered study in an hour instead of a month — which is either a disaster or exactly the point, depending on whether the next step is publication or federation. The authors clearly know which one they want: they ran the same stack against MIMIC-Four in OMOP form to show it ports. Standardized data model, standardized protocol, one server per site. If that's where this goes, the interesting number stops being how fast one hospital can answer and starts being how many hospitals can answer the same question at once. That's a plumbing story, and plumbing is usually what actually moves the field.
