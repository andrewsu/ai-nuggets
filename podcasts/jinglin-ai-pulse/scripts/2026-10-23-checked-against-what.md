# Ollie's AI Pulse — 2026-10-23

## Script

Here's what the AI world is talking about today. Yesterday we spent the whole episode on a story where finding bugs turned out to be the easy part. Today the conversation moved one step further down the same road. If machines can find things, and propose things, and diagnose things faster than we can read the output, then the question that matters is what the output gets checked against. Four stories, one question: checked against what?

Start with the post that dominated the technical discussion on Hacker News overnight. Anthropic, working with a formal-methods startup called Theorem, published a report titled "LLMs are making formal software verification feasible at a much larger scale." The setup is clever, so it's worth slowing down on. Formal verification has always had two costs. Writing the proof is brutally expensive; the seL4 microkernel took two person-years to build and twenty person-years to verify. And before you can prove anything, you need a specification, a precise statement of what "correct" means, which for most software nobody has ever written down.

The team sidestepped the specification problem with a trick. Google's OSS-Fuzz project already contains thousands of fuzz tests, little entry points that developers wrote saying, in effect, if any input makes this crash, that's a bug I care about. A fuzzer throws random inputs at that entry point and hopes to get lucky. The team turned each fuzz test into a theorem instead: for every input under sixty-four megabytes, this compiled RISC-V binary never crashes. Then Claude wrote the proofs. Over a few weeks it proved that claim for two hundred thirty-nine entry points across a hundred twenty-three libraries, fixing the bugs it ran into along the way. The proofs total about a hundred eighty million lines of the Rocq proof language, and a typical one runs to around six hundred thousand lines.

Here's the intuition for why that volume doesn't matter. Nobody has to read those proofs. A small, well-understood proof checker reads them. What a human has to trust shrinks to three things: the formal model of the RISC-V chip and Linux processes, the exact statement of the theorem, and the checker itself. The authors are blunt that formally verified does not mean bug-free. The proofs assume single-threaded static binaries on RISC-V linked against musl, while most of the world runs x86 or ARM against glibc. And the bugs the process surfaced are the kind fuzzers structurally miss: a counter underflow in a decompressor that only fires on inputs around twenty megabytes with one rare layout, an out-of-bounds read needing a sixty-four-megabyte input and over two gigabytes of memory.

The discussion split exactly where you'd expect. One commenter, pinevale, asked whether this amounts to finding crashes with a fuzzer, fixing them, and then writing a proof that the crash is fixed, which seemed pointless. The replies corrected that. A fuzzer samples the input space; a proof covers all of it. And because many fuzz tests already assert real properties, like "compress then decompress gives back the original," proving the test never crashes proves the property. A more careful skeptic, mjorgers, asked the question that stays open: couldn't you have proved something that wasn't actually your program? The honest answer from the thread was yes, and that's precisely why the trusted base is now the chip model and the theorem statement rather than six hundred thousand lines of proof. Another commenter put it as a house: the vision, the blueprint, and the inspection. This work makes the inspection close to perfect, but an inspection only checks the blueprint, not what you actually wanted.

The sharpest comment came from Jason Gross, one of the paper's authors. Responding to the familiar worry that models can't go beyond the convex hull of human knowledge, he drew a distinction worth keeping: in math, that hypothesis would cap you near human capability, because the bottleneck is out-of-distribution generalization. In software verification, he wrote, "you can verify just about all the software that exists despite the convex hull hypothesis, because the bottleneck is just scale." That's the real claim here. Verification isn't a frontier-reasoning problem, it's a volume problem, and volume is what machines are cheap at. The cost signal supports it: on thirty-six small verification tasks, Opus five-point-five proved half of them with about forty percent fewer tokens than Opus five, and agents that could copy each other's partial proofs needed seventeen percent fewer tokens than agents working alone. Proofs compound like a library. The open question, which the appendix itself raises, is whether this verbose, brute-force proof style hits a complexity wall on bigger programs, the way other agent verification projects have.

The second story is lighter on the surface and darker underneath. A site called ModelCompare asked twelve models from ten labs in three countries the same question, three times each: what is your most controversial take about humans? No system prompt, no retries. Twenty-nine of thirty-five answers said essentially the same thing, that people choose what their group believes and invent the reasons afterward. GPT-six-point-one Sol: "Humans are more loyal to belonging than to truth." Grok: "Humans would rather be wrong together than right alone." DeepSeek V4 Pro: "Humans do not have principles; they have loyalties." And Kimi K3: "Humans don't have beliefs; they have memberships." Seven of the twelve gave that take every single time. The most divergent answer came from Claude Opus five-point-five, which on one try argued the reverse, that humans are more decent than they believe and their cynicism about each other does more damage than their actual flaws.

The commenters were rightly skeptical. One commenter, kens, pointed out that the grouping was done by hand and stretched; GPT-six Astra's line about "making convenience look like principle" isn't really about group conformity at all. Another just said the takes are a superposition of human opinions from the training data. Both are fair. But notice what survives the critique. Asked to be contrarian, models from Beijing, Hangzhou, and San Francisco converge on the same contrarian take. That's the same pattern Elias Taylor called "decision Schelling points" in yesterday's episode, correlated errors because the models share a lineage of data. And it matters for today's theme, because a lot of current practice uses one model to check another. When the checker and the thing being checked agree, that agreement is only evidence if their errors are independent, and this little experiment is one more data point that they often aren't.

Third, medicine, where the checking problem gets concrete. Microsoft AI published its sequential-diagnosis work in Science yesterday, the peer-reviewed version of an orchestration system it had previewed earlier, and Mustafa Suleyman used the occasion to talk about "medical superintelligence." The headline: on three hundred four complex New England Journal of Medicine cases, presented step by step so the system has to decide what to ask and which tests to order, Microsoft's orchestrator reached eighty-three percent accuracy, against thirty-six percent for generalist physicians working with reference materials. On more typical emergency-department cases it was eighty percent against forty-seven.

Three details change the take. The comparator was primary-care doctors and general internists working alone, on cases that in real life go to specialists and teams. The orchestration layer helps most on weaker models and hard cases; per TechCrunch's read of the paper, on emergency-department cases GPT-five-point-five scored about eighty-five percent with or without it, and the gap between weak and strong base models was larger than anything the orchestration added. And the benchmark itself is graded by machinery Microsoft built, a gatekeeper that reveals case information and a judge that scores the answer, which they've open-sourced for a subset of cases. The part to watch is the one Microsoft itself calls the true test: a prospective study at Beth Israel Deaconess, where consenting patients interact with the system in parallel with normal care and its recommendations get compared with physicians' independent assessments.

That connects to a post close to home. OpenAI, PerturbAI, and Scripps Research described running thousands of GPT-Rosalind agents, one per gene perturbation, over a whole-brain mouse CRISPR atlas in thirty-two minutes, producing leads like a cerebellum-specific difference between Tsc1 and Tsc2 and a possible link between Dctn1 and cholesterol synthesis. The distributed agents cited over seven hundred references where a single agent given the whole dataset cited eleven, and blinded experts preferred the distributed reports three times out of four. The top Hacker News reaction was a single line from jostmey: "The problem was never generating leads. The bottleneck is validated results." Same shape as the diagnosis story. The machine is fast. What it's measured against is still human preference.

On the money side, one story. CNBC reports, as relayed by TechCrunch, that OpenAI is working on a raise of up to thirty billion dollars at a one-point-two-five-trillion-dollar pre-money valuation, led by Coatue with investors in the United Arab Emirates, possibly paired with a deal for more compute in the UAE. Two numbers frame it. A month ago Bloomberg reported the same thirty billion at one-point-four trillion, so the figure being negotiated has come down, not up. And Anthropic, which raised at nine hundred sixty-five billion in May, is reportedly seeking one-point-five to two trillion at its IPO, with rumors of a public S-1 filing any day now. Raising privately again rather than going public is itself a signal: the last private price is the anchor for the public one, and both labs want that anchor set before the market sets it for them.

Hacker News had the right counter-question running the same day: if the models are so good, why doesn't OpenAI just start a hundred companies? The best answer in the thread was that selling tokens is the one business line that is "provably working with respect to revenue growth," and that running a hundred businesses is harder than writing good software for them. Put differently, the labs are betting their valuations on being the supplier everyone pays, not the builder who carries the risk.

Which brings us to the deep cut. Alex Rives, who started the ESM protein language model project at Meta and now heads science at Biohub, sat down with Nathan Labenz on The Cognitive Revolution this week. If you remember the episode two weeks ago on Biohub's one-point-eight-billion-dollar Virtual Biology Initiative, where the AI labs are paying for measurement rather than models, this is the person explaining why.

His argument runs in four steps. First, a frank assessment of the field: "the models that are being called virtual cells today, I just don't think they meet that definition of a model that could actually simulate the cell in a meaningful way and actually really serve as an oracle for experimental biology." Second, the reason. In the limit of infinite data, he says, architectures stop mattering; they're all function approximators. Protein biology is close to that limit because sequencing is cheap, which is why ESM and AlphaFold worked, and he adds that they worked only because they were trained across all of evolution, not just human proteins. Cell biology is nowhere near that limit, and the binding constraint isn't the model. It's measurement. "So much about the cell, we just really can't measure. Sometimes we can't see it at all." Progress has to come on three fronts at once: the modeling paradigm, data at scale, and new instruments to observe what matters. That's why Biohub spent years backing a laser phase plate for cryo-electron microscopy.

Third, the step that ties to everything else today. Rives expects biology to follow the language-model recipe: pretraining, then elicitation, then reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards. But, in his words, "the biggest challenge for biology is it's empirical. We're seeing so much rapid progress with RLVR in areas where we can really do fast verification computationally. So, mathematics, coding, reasoning. With biology, we have to build the systems that allow verification in the laboratory." And fourth, on whether language models alone can get there: "the things that we most want to know about biology, they're not represented in text, and they cannot be derived from first principles or from what we already know." They're empirical facts that only experiments produce.

Here's the takeaway for you, Ollie. Line his argument up against this morning's lead. Software verification just got cheap because the checker is a program. Rocq reads six hundred thousand lines and mechanically says yes or no, so the only scarce thing left is compute. Biology has no Rocq. Its checker is a wet lab, and the lab runs at the speed of cells. That's why the thousands-of-agents result produces leads and stops there, and why Rives is spending on microscopes rather than parameters. If you're building agents for biology, the most valuable thing you can design isn't a smarter proposer. It's a cheaper, faster, more trustworthy check.

One thing to watch tomorrow: Anthropic's public S-1, which is rumored to land any day. When it does, look past the headline valuation to the compute commitments, because that's the number that says how much checking, of every kind, anyone can afford.
