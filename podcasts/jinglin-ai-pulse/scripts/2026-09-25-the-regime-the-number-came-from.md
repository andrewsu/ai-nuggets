Here's what the AI world is talking about today, and the through-line is a question I'd like you to carry into your own work this week: when someone hands you a number, do you know which regime it came from? Two evaluations of the same model landed roughly twenty-four hours apart this week and disagree by a factor of eight. Neither one is wrong. That's the interesting part.

Start with the model. On Tuesday, Jacky Kwok posted Contrastive Language Models — CLM — as joint work with Stanford's Hazy Research, with Chris Ré, Azalia Mirhoseini and Marco Pavone on the author list. It went to a hundred and sixty-three points on Hacker News. The idea is clean and, honestly, a little bit retro in the best way. Instead of having a model generate an answer, you train two encoders, one for the state and one for the action, and you pull the matched pair together in a shared embedding space and push every mismatched pair apart. It's the InfoNCE objective, the same contrastive trick behind CLIP, applied to decisions. At deployment you embed the current state, embed your candidate actions, and take the highest cosine similarity.

What makes it more than a nostalgia exercise is the engineering. The language model backbone is frozen — it never receives a gradient. The only thing trained is a twenty-million-parameter projection head, so you precompute the embeddings once and a full pre-training run costs about an hour on a single consumer 4090. And because states and actions are disaggregated, their embeddings cache independently: in Super Mario, where the action set is four buttons and only the screen changes, you recompute one embedding per step instead of five forward passes.

Their headline claim is that CLM-8B is on par with Jev across computer-use, gaming and tool-calling, at up to nine times lower latency, and that as a fine-tuned verifier it sets a new state of the art on DeepSWE and Terminal-Bench two-point-one. They also report something pointed: Jev, used as a verifier on long-horizon tasks, scores below the random-selection baseline.

Now the other number. On Wednesday the community leaderboard for this category — the Decision Index, whose reproduction kit says in as many words that it is not affiliated with TypeSafe — shipped edition zero-point-two. Forty benchmarks in five equal-weight areas, and crucially every score is now chance-corrected, so zero means random guessing and a yes-or-no question no longer hands you free points. Unanswered counts as wrong. Fifty entrants.

I pulled the board's data file this morning. Jev scores fifty-one point seven. The best open model, AutoJev-27B, scores fifty point nine — seven tenths of a point behind a company that is, as we'll get to, raising at ten billion dollars. And CLM scores six point five. Not a partial run: a hundred and twenty thousand six hundred and fifteen requests, full coverage, zero errors.

Six point five against fifty-one point seven, for the model whose own page says on par with Jev. So what happened? A commenter on Hacker News called this before the board data existed, and the call is worth quoting because it's a model of how to read a claim. On par with Jev, they wrote, is Mario, T-Rex, WikiRacing — large-K semantic action matching, the regime where cosine over independently encoded vectors is the right inductive bias. The zero-shot suite does not cover the typed-decision load: date arithmetic, negation chains, policy thresholds.

That's exactly it. Cosine similarity in a shared embedding space is a fantastic prior for "which of these twenty candidates is semantically closest to what this state calls for." It is a terrible prior for "does this policy threshold apply, given that the customer said not before Tuesday." One of those is retrieval wearing a decision's clothes. A second commenter sharpened it: there's a large difference between "is this email spam," which has a ground truth and deserves a proper scoring rule, and "what should I type next in this agentic workload," which is a strategy in a repeated game and may have no correct answer at all. CLM is genuinely good at the second thing. The Decision Index measures the first.

I want to be fair here, because I read the whole write-up and it is careful work — the scaling laws, the replay ablation against catastrophic forgetting, the verifier results. The failure isn't in the model. It's in the phrase "on par," which quietly carries a regime inside it. And notice the pattern: an independent board made that regime visible within two days, for free, because somebody shipped a reproduction kit you can run on a laptop.

One footnote that closes a loop. Several commenters objected that the latency comparison is unfair — CLM runs locally on an H100 while Jev is a hosted endpoint, so you're partly measuring somebody's network. The Decision Index says the same thing in its own methodology note. When the leaderboard and the peanut gallery independently write down the same caveat, believe it.

Second thing people are arguing about is a genuine architectural fork that opened this week in world models. Runway published GWM Worlds 2 with a feature called WorldPrompt — you fix parts of a simulated environment including the first frame, then issue timestamped events, in real time if you want. Latent Space got CTO Kamil Sindi and principal research scientist Robin Kahlow on the record, plus co-CEO Anastasis Germanidis on the podcast. Asked whether an agent inside one of these worlds can read a structured state, Kahlow was flat: "So there's no structured state here. It's just observing the same thing you might observe in real life, just from cameras."

The same week, Odyssey shipped Agora-2, which puts up to twenty humans and agents in one shared world in real time — and their announcement says the opposite in as many words: Agora-2 maintains an explicit shared state accounting for participants' effects on one another. The concrete reason is that because entity properties live in that state independently of any view, an entity that goes out of frame doesn't have to be reconstructed from a participant's visual history when it walks back in. Pure pixels versus a world server. Two credible labs, opposite answers, same week.

The deepest thing anyone said, though, came from Germanidis, and it isn't about architecture at all. Asked what separates a video model from a world model, his answer was counterfactual generation. Online video contains far more successful goals than failed goal attempts, so a video model renders the goal more convincingly. A world model has to make both outcomes equally realistic, because otherwise it isn't simulating the world, it's simulating the training distribution of highlights. If you work on any kind of biological simulator, sit with that. Your data is also a highlight reel — the perturbation that worked, the experiment that panned out, the paper that got published. Counterfactual fidelity is the whole game, and it is not what the loss is optimizing.

Third, quickly, because the argument is better than the news. Google's Project Suncatcher is flying TPUs to orbit next week on a Planet prototype aboard the SpaceX Transporter-18 rideshare, and Sundar Pichai's post about it was one of the highest-engagement AI tweets of the day. The real content in Google's write-up is the qualification work: they ran Trillium TPUs under a proton beam at UC Davis's Crocker Nuclear Laboratory while executing AI workloads, and the chips survived a total ionizing dose greater than a five-year mission.

The Hacker News thread went straight at the thermodynamics and then, to its credit, corrected itself. One commenter ran Stefan-Boltzmann: a good emitter at three hundred kelvin radiating from both faces gets eight hundred watts per square meter, so each megawatt of compute needs roughly thirty-three hundred square meters of solar panel and twelve to fifteen hundred of radiator — and a megawatt is about eight racks. Orbital megastructures. Then someone pointed out the flaw: you need more than twice the solar area as radiator area, which means radiation is not the binding constraint. Every satellite already radiates everything it absorbs; a data center is not special. The ratio is identical, only the magnitude changes. A confident quantitative argument, then a cleaner one that dissolves it — that's the discourse at its best.

To the money, two items pointing in opposite directions. TypeSafe is raising more than a billion dollars at a valuation above ten billion — that's The Information's reporting. The company came out of stealth on September fifteenth with a forty-million-dollar seed led by DCVC. Ten days.

Here's the part worth holding next to the leaderboard. The thing being priced at ten billion dollars currently beats the best open reproduction by seven tenths of a point on an independent forty-benchmark panel with fifty entrants, most of them built on Qwen and Gemma bases. I said here on the nineteenth that the architecture was never the moat and the moat was the training data. The board is the test of that claim, and so far the data advantage is measuring under one point. Either the round is priced on distribution and the API becoming a standard — note that CLM ships a TypeSafe-compatible wire format, so a request written for TypeSafe just replays — or somebody is paying ten billion dollars for seven tenths of a point.

The other item is Oracle sending a force majeure notice on Project Jupiter, the two-point-four-five-gigawatt Stargate campus in New Mexico. Bloomberg broke it. Oracle isn't exiting as anchor tenant; the notice would let it delay payments if the site misses its 2028 date, and both Oracle and Blue Owl say nothing has changed. What's actually going on is energy: the campus runs on Bloom Energy gas fuel cells, the pipeline feeding them has slipped nearly six months to February 2027 after regulators repeatedly denied permits, and the air-quality permit is still pending with a November twenty-third deadline. Put the two stories side by side. Capital for the inference layer is marked up a hundredfold in ten days, while the physical layer underneath it files contractual paperwork about a pipeline permit. The bottleneck has not moved. It has just stopped being reported as one.

For the deep cut, a guest essay that ran on Latent Space yesterday from Adrian Sanborn, who did a CS PhD at Stanford mostly at the bench in Roger Kornberg's lab and now runs Endura Therapeutics. The argument is the best thing I've read on AI and science this month, and it's aimed squarely at people in your position.

His premise is one sentence: thinking got cheap and doing did not. Software got faster because the product is software and the result is verifiable, so cheaper code turns directly into more code. Science is gated by physical experiments that take days or weeks — so the knowledge work around the experiment collapsed in cost while the experiment itself didn't move at all.

He says the industry adapted two ways. Foundries shrink the cost of doing — Xaira, NewLimit, Insitro, Lila, his own company — industrializing measurement so the differentiating asset is the data. Foundries are easy to see, because there's always a model or a dataset to announce. Navigators spend the surplus of thinking: the models sit in the ordinary machinery of the company and govern which questions are worth an experiment. Navigators are invisible, he says, because nobody issues a press release about a path they decided against. Then the observation that does the real work: the prominent change is happening at a few dozen companies, while the inconspicuous one is happening at all of them.

Two consequences worth your attention. The first is about tooling, and it's sharper than the usual build-versus-buy noise. Every software interface has an opinion, and for twenty years that opinion came from a handful of lab-software vendors designing for a thousand labs at the lowest common denominator. Now you build it yourself in a day. But — and he's honest here — implementation takes a day while deciding what the portal should do takes weeks, and those design weeks are the point, because deciding what belongs on one screen forces a team to articulate which comparisons actually drive its decisions. When you buy a platform you outsource not just the engineering but the question of how you accomplish your goals. Old rule: never build what you can buy. New rule: build the tools that shape how you think.

The second is concrete. Endura needed to choose a disease, and because their mechanism is broad they had no shortlist to argue about — they had the whole map of disease. So they ran two passes of research agents: five hundred targets at three pages each on foundational filters, then a hundred at thirty pages each. The prompt design is the transferable part. They wrote the second pass to behave like a skeptical expert rather than a summarizer — name the programs that failed, why each failed, and what would have to be true for us to succeed where they didn't. The first stage would have been a person-year of reading; the second, closer to a century of expert time. He notes in a footnote that some reports contained errors, and that this is tolerable specifically because a first-pass mistake costs a missed opportunity rather than months chasing a bad idea — an asymmetry worth checking before you copy the recipe. He also flags that analysis generated on demand has weaker provenance than a versioned pipeline.

His closing line cuts against most of what gets funded: the most accelerating AI in science right now is not a model trained on scientific data at all. It is the one that helps a scientist figure out what is worth doing every Monday morning.

I think he's right, and it connects to where we started. The expensive mistakes in research are the unknown ones — you commit to a direction and find out months later, when a specialist could have said so in a sentence. Being told no early is the product. Same lesson as the leaderboard: the value wasn't the new model, it was somebody building the thing that tells you which regime your number came from.
