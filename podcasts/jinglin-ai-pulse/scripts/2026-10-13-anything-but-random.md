Here's what the AI world is talking about today. Anthropic's red team says small open models may be months away from being able to copy themselves across cheap smart-TV boxes, and that each copy gets better by inheriting its parent's notes. A NeurIPS paper shows you can hide a capability inside model weights so that no audit can find it. Rich Hickey says AI isn't a tool at all, and a lot of people pushed back. And on Machine Learning Street Talk, Akarsh Kumar makes the case that evolution, which most of AI treats as dumb random search, is the most powerful algorithm we have, once you add a good enough mutation step. By the end you'll see why those two halves of the episode are the same story.

Twitter Pulse first. The most-argued post of the last day is from Anthropic's Frontier Red Team, published Monday by William Zhou and Daniel Freeman. Their opening claim: "small AI models may be months away from being capable enough to self-replicate on commercial internet-of-things devices, like smart ovens and off-brand Android TV boxes."

The demo is physical. They bought fifteen off-brand Android TV boxes at about fifty-five dollars each, networked them, and ran a twenty-six-billion-parameter Gemma model split across them, two layers per box, at about zero point four tokens a second. Slow, but running. Then the experiments. A nine-billion-parameter Qwen model got an inference server working on eight of the nine hardware types they tried, from a Jetson Nano to a Mac. And in a firewalled cluster of eight emulated Android machines, they dropped in different open models as the worm and asked each to find a new machine, copy itself over, and stand up inference there. Only GLM-5.2 did the whole chain. It spread to all eight nodes in four of five runs.

Two details stood out to me. First, the adaptation result. Each Qwen instance wrote short notes on what it tried and what failed, and the next generation inherited them. They call it Lamarckian evolution. Setup success went from forty-five percent in the first generation to between sixty-one and eighty-four percent in later ones. Second, giving the small model a frontier advisor, Opus 4.8, did not measurably help. In their words, small models "tend to not know the right questions to ask." The timeline comes from a trend line: small open models gain about fourteen points a year on Epoch's capability index, which puts one at GLM-5.2's level by the end of this year.

The caveats are the authors' own. The hacking step was made trivial, with an open, unauthenticated debug port, so this is an upper bound. The worm was asked to stand up a small Llama model, not itself. And at full scale they estimate a botnet the size of the two-million-device Kimwolf network would add up to roughly forty H100s' worth of inference. Their image for it is "a digital slime mold."

Hacker News was harsh. pythonaut_16 called it "Anthropic's next round of fearmarketing," while granting there's "a cool technical demo here." atleastoptimal laid out what they see as the agenda: show dangerous capability, show that open-weight models are where there's no guarantee against it, and the policy conclusion writes itself. flanked-evergl argued anything that weak would be "absolutely atrocious at actually doing anything useful," and easier to stop than ordinary malware. The post itself is fairly modest about defense. It points to the Mirai botnet cleanup, where quarantining infected customers cleared ninety-two percent of infections within fourteen days, and frames the goal like epidemiology: push the reproduction number below one.

Put that next to a paper that hit the Hacker News front page overnight: "AI Models Can Provably Hide Arbitrary Capabilities," by Andis Draguns of Contramont Research and colleagues at Oxford, accepted at NeurIPS. The worry it addresses is the opposite end of the pipeline. Labs decide what to deploy by trying to elicit dangerous capabilities before release. The authors show that's not guaranteed to work against an adversary. They encrypt a small neural network with a cryptographic primitive called a digital locker, compile it into the weights of a two-billion-parameter host model, and it only decrypts and runs when it sees a secret trigger. The hidden skill was a chemical reaction-feasibility classifier, standing in for chemistry-weapons uplift. Fine-tuning, weight noise, gradient-based trigger search, and latent adversarial training all failed to surface it, and under standard cryptographic assumptions, that's what the math predicts.

Again, the honest limits are in the paper. It hides only a small binary classifier, the overhead is large, and they explicitly don't claim the modified model is indistinguishable from a clean one. An auditor could still flag the weights as weird. daemonologist put it simply on Hacker News: "They proved it is possible, by doing it themselves." ben_w added that byte-for-byte reproducible training is "neither necessary nor sufficient," because we don't know how to detect these things in the first place. So the takeaway isn't that audits are useless. It's that elicitation gives you a lower bound on what a model can do, and under adversarial conditions, nothing more. Provenance of the weights starts to matter as much as the eval.

Second topic, quicker. Rich Hickey, the creator of Clojure, posted an essay titled "'AI' is Not a Tool." His definition: "A tool is something you wield." LLMs, he says, "are not wielded. LLMs are automation." His analogy: "A drive-thru is not a tool for cooking, it is automation for avoiding cooking." And on using models to read papers: "It read the papers, not you. What do you miss by abdicating doing the reading? Pretty much everything."

Huahai Yang's rebuttal also hit the front page. His argument is that "Automation can be a feature of a tool." A compiler and a spreadsheet do lots of work unsupervised, and we still wield them. He says the useful line is "between wielding an instrument with skill and delegating work without adequate understanding or oversight." The best Hacker News comment split the difference. esafak wrote that agentic AIs are agents: "you set the goal, they decide the means," which is a different thing from both a tool and a traditional program.

Why does a word fight matter? Because institutions are drawing the line in practice. Last week QGIS, the open-source mapping project, approved a policy that flatly bans AI-assisted contributions, after its maintainers said reviewers couldn't keep up. One contributor's comment in the thread: "By doing this, we've broken a trust contract between the reviewer and the contributor." And Diomidis Spinellis, a software engineering professor, wrote the gloomiest version: "Thinking will become a hobby," the way hunting became one after farming. Notice the link to the first topic. Anthropic's worm and Hickey's drive-thru are both about the same thing: you set the goal, and something else decides the means.

Now the Deep Cut. On Machine Learning Street Talk, Tim Scarfe interviewed Akarsh Kumar, an MIT PhD student working with Phillip Isola and with Sakana AI, and first author of the ASAL paper on automating the search for artificial life. Here's his argument in four steps, quoted from the episode transcript.

Step one: study the space, not the instance. "If we wanna claim that we understand intelligence or we understand life, we can't just talk about this specific form of life." His analogy: "You can't just look at GPT-5 and be like, all intelligences are like this." So in ASAL, rather than hand-build one simulation, "you parameterize a space of simulations," across Game of Life, Lenia, Boids, and Particle Life, and search it.

Step two: let the simulation run and use a foundation model as the critic. "You don't need to know what this physics rule is gonna do. Just simulate it, see what happens, plug it into a foundation model, and see what the foundation model thinks about what happened." Why a foundation model? Because "it's really hard to describe what complexity is, what emergence is," and if you write a formula and optimize it, "you'll find something that you asked for, but not something that you wanted." A model whose representations roughly match human judgment is the cheap stand-in for a human in the loop. When they mapped all two hundred sixty-two thousand Life-like rules this way, they found "a big island of solutions which are not open ended, and there's a small island of solutions where all of the coolest simulations lie." In Particle Life, some worlds grew cell-like objects with a membrane, which he describes as "exploiting the physics in a way to create a persisting object."

Step three: the path matters, not just the destination. This is where he disagrees with mainstream training. Humans and evolution learn in sequence, but "you can train them on calculus, they'll learn calculus. You don't have to train them on arithmetic first." He calls today's models "statistical intelligence," and contrasts it with "regularity-based intelligence," where symmetries get built into the circuits because of the order things were learned. Kenneth Stanley, in a clip, makes the same point about benchmarks: "It matters not just where you get, but how you got there."

Step four is the line the episode is named for. In the Digital Red Queen work, they used an LLM as the mutation operator to evolve programs in Core War, a nineteen-eighties game where assembly programs fight to crash each other. Zero-shot, the LLM is "terrible" at the language. With evolution and a verifier, the first round already beats ninety-six percent of a dataset of human-written warriors, and later rounds get more general. Then the punchline: "evolution is anything but random." Selection means "if you solve even one part of a puzzle and you hang on to that and you search for the other ones, you can turn your exponential search problem into a linear search problem." And "all it needs is your mutations to be good, like, one percent of the time." Random program mutations are almost never useful. LLM mutations are useful much more than one percent of the time, and that's why the combination is "not even close" to LLMs alone.

Here's the takeaway, Ollie. Kumar's step four is exactly why Anthropic's result deserves a closer look than "fearmarketing." Their worm used a weak model, but with inherited notes acting as selection, success climbed from forty-five to as much as eighty-four percent in a few generations. Weak generator, plus memory, plus a verifier, is a strong search process, for Core War warriors, for replicators, and for the molecule and protein design loops you work on. Two habits follow. When you build an evolutionary or agentic search loop, the verifier and the selection step are where the power is, so spend your effort there. And treat any capability eval as a lower bound, because the Oxford paper shows that a model's weights can carry a skill no elicitation will find.

One thing to watch: Meta says a full Muse Wonder safety report is "available soon." See whether it includes any autonomous replication testing, now that Anthropic has published numbers for open models a fraction of Muse Wonder's size.
