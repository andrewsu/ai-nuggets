## Script

Here's what the AI world is talking about today. It's Saturday, November seventh. The conversation this week has one center. An outside evaluator, METR, spent eleven weeks inside Anthropic and concluded that AI is now roughly doubling the pace at which Anthropic's models improve. Then it told everyone that Anthropic had redacted most of the evidence it found most convincing. So today's theme is what you can take on trust: the evidence you can't see, the confidence scores you can calibrate, and the metrics that decide what counts as progress. One disclosure first: the agent that drafted this episode is Claude, an Anthropic model, so weigh what follows with that in mind.

Start with the report. From mid-August to late October, two and a half full-time METR staff had what they call unprecedented access: internal benchmark scores, pretraining and RL metrics, compute data, and interviews. Their headline is that Anthropic's capability index, an internal version of Epoch's benchmark composite, has been rising about one point nine times faster since October twenty twenty-five than it did before. Roughly fifteen points a year became roughly twenty-nine. The confidence interval runs from one and a half to two point eight. They're confident training compute didn't speed up to cause it, and they estimate that about eighty percent of the current acceleration comes from Anthropic using its own AI to do research. They also found no clear bottleneck, in compute, data or algorithms, that would stop the speedup from compounding over the next year, though they say their view of bottlenecks changed several times.

Then comes the part everyone quoted. Anthropic had the right to redact any non-public information, and it redacted, in METR's words, "a majority of the evidence we found most informative." Anthropic said the cuts were aimed at keeping confidential information from accelerating other developers. METR is blunt about the result: "the public has to rely on METR's judgment much more than would be ideal."

The timing makes this more than an evaluation story. On Monday, Anthropic's amended IPO filing said its internal estimates "indicate that we may imminently meet" its own Responsible Scaling Policy threshold for "dramatically accelerated progress attributable to AI." The same filing says Anthropic does not expect to be able to safely let Claude fully autonomously design and build successive frontier models "without meaningful human supervision or understanding."

The reactions split along a line worth following. Ryan Greenblatt, who works at METR but didn't see the unredacted report, wrote: "I was seriously wrong about RSI speed up; I previously expected much less in 2026," around one point one five to one point three times. He also wrote that apart from the benchmark doubling itself, the conclusions "depend significantly on trust." Ajeya Cotra, also at METR and also without the unredacted version, went further on her blog Planned Obsolescence. She called it "the most important update we have gotten about AI risk in the last couple of years," and her argument is arithmetic. Software is only part of what drives capability, so doubling overall progress takes roughly two to three times faster algorithmic progress. Researcher labor is only part of what drives algorithmic progress, alongside experiment compute, so in a simple model you square that: four to nine times more effective labor. Add diminishing returns and she lands near ten times. Her picture: it's "as if every researcher at Anthropic is in a time warp bubble where they experience ten days for every day that passes in the outside world." And the most informal evidence in the report lines up. Anthropic's Slack emoji polls on self-reported speedup reached about twelve times in October, which METR itself calls "only very loose evidence."

The skeptics' best material comes from the same arithmetic. A September essay by Ramez Naam, linked in the Hacker News thread, quoted Anthropic's own Mythos Preview system card: staff then reported about a four times uplift, and "reaching two times on overall progress via this channel would require uplift roughly an order of magnitude larger than what we observe." So the bulls and the skeptics aren't arguing about the model. They agree a doubling needs something like a tenfold labor uplift. The disagreement is whether four times really became twelve in a year, and whether a benchmark index tracks the progress that matters. METR raises that second worry itself. The index focuses on verifiable tasks, and their conclusion is scoped to "well-specified tasks." One Hacker News commenter compressed it: AI is dramatically accelerating any research "that has objective goals where you can not shift goalposts."

Which makes a second Anthropic release this week the right companion. Anthropic researchers classified about two hundred seven thousand research sessions from August and September by field and task. Computer science is four point three percent of the world's researchers and almost a third of Claude Code research sessions. Data analysis and writing are over-represented. Data gathering, at more than a quarter of researchers' time, gets about a ninth of sessions. Then they scored research tasks for how well AI could in principle do it, and that score correlates at zero point seven eight with how suitable the task is for reinforcement learning, meaning fast feedback and easy verification. The top fifth of tasks by that score get two to four times the usage their share of researcher time would predict. In the scenario where AI does all non-physical research work, computer science and statistics output could rise up to fifteenfold, while chemistry and biology top out around three or fourfold because of the bench. The finding that should stick with you as a biomedical researcher: their survey of five hundred twenty-eight mostly life-science researchers ranked hypothesis generation and data gathering as the two most important tasks for discovery, and those are the two where AI is used least. Put that next to METR. The field where acceleration is strongest is the one that builds AI, because its work is the most checkable.

The third conversation is smaller but hit the top of Hacker News, and it's about trusting a model's confidence rather than its answer. Hightouch needed to grade identity resolution, which means deciding whether a customer profile has merged records from two different people. They built a thousand test profiles with a synthetic-population tool from the University of Washington and compared frontier models with a new class of cheap "decision models" that return only a confidence between zero and one: Jev from Typesafe, and OpenAI's GPT-6 Luna. Claude Opus five point five scored ninety-nine percent at about three dollars eighty-six per thousand profiles. Luna scored ninety-seven point three for ten cents. Jev scored ninety-five point seven for five cents. On accuracy alone, Luna wins. But Jev's mistakes clustered near fifty percent confidence, while Luna was often wrong at ninety percent or higher. So they sent only Jev's uncertain middle band, about a fifth of cases, to Opus. That hybrid reached ninety-eight point eight percent for eighty-eight cents. On a harder dataset the hybrid matched Opus alone, ninety-five point three to ninety-five point one, while the Luna version fell to ninety-one point six. The harder test also cut the savings to under two times, because Jev was unsure more often and escalated more. That's the point. A calibrated model automatically spends more on harder data. An overconfident one can't, however accurate it looks on average.

Money moves, and the first one connects back to the lead. Bloomberg reports that ahead of formal IPO marketing next week, prospective buyers are discussing a valuation for Anthropic of one point two to one point three trillion dollars, with some as low as one point one trillion, after the post-election selloff. Yesterday we covered the Journal headline that investors doubted one and a half trillion. So the same company is telling regulators its research may be dramatically accelerating, and telling investors the price, in the same week the market marks it down. Investors seem to be pricing the cost of the buildout more than the speed of the research. The second item is about where that buildout goes. Crusoe announced that Google has acquired its development site in southeast Wyoming, a campus planned with Tallgrass Energy around fuel cells, grid power and gas turbines, with carbon capture later. Crusoe says it has more than six gigawatts contracted and over forty in its pipeline, and it's also the developer of a Google campus outside Amarillo. After this week's local data center votes, a hyperscaler buying a fully permitted site outright looks like paying for the hardest input to get.

The deep cut is from Latent Space's AI for Science series, published yesterday: Fabian Theis of Helmholtz Munich, whose lab is behind Scanpy and much of the single-cell tool ecosystem. It answers the same question as the rest of today, inside biology: when can you trust a model's progress?

Step one, Theis narrows what people mean by a virtual cell. Usually they mean perturbation prediction: change a cell's input and predict its new state. In his words a virtual cell is "kind of a world model" in omics space, "not just a model that is f of x, but f of x comma a," the state plus the action. He thinks equating virtual cells with perturbation prediction is "a bit too simplistic," because the inputs that matter include neighboring cells, signals and media conditions, not just CRISPR knockouts.

Step two is his candid account of the baseline problem. His lab's first perturbation model in twenty nineteen did roughly linear shifts in a latent space, and he says pseudo-linear methods still do well, because "the data sets and the questions that we asked on top were just sort of still on the simplistic side." He'd published a paper bluntly titled "deep learning is not needed for cell type prediction." His advice to students: "please, please, you know, have the baseline," and for drugs, simply predicting the mean "is like the hardest baseline." On the Arc Virtual Cell Challenge, he says the fixed metrics "could be just exploited," and once you strip out the exploitation, the real winner was a nonlinear flow-matching model. So the problem was in the scoring, not just the models.

Step three is the bitter lesson, held in tension. He accepts that "at some point data beats human made additional concepts," and his example is AlphaFold, where later versions learned rotation symmetry from data instead of having it built in. But he says, "I don't think we're yet in a super data-rich regime," so hybrid models with some built-in structure are still the right bet. What changes that is showing real scaling laws, which he says the early single-cell foundation models never did. His lab now has a three-hundred-fifty-million-cell model across sixteen species where scaling behavior is starting to show.

Step four is where he thinks human effort belongs. Agents will soon try every algorithmic combination, and then "maybe our biggest ideas go into it is actually this metric formulation." Choosing what to measure becomes the scientist's job. Then he closes the loop: his group's new Lab Compass is an inverse generative model that, given a forward world model, tells you which perturbations reach a target cell state, tested over three or four rounds with a hematopoiesis lab in Cambridge. The hard part, he says, wasn't the model. It was getting experimentalists to commit months to a strange experiment a model proposed. The long-term goal: "we want to RL everything."

My takeaway, Ollie, ties the day together. METR's doubling is measured on well-specified tasks. Anthropic's usage data shows AI going where verification is cheap. Hightouch shows a cheap model is useful only when its confidence can be trusted. And Theis says that in virtual cells, the scarce resource is a metric you can't exploit, plus a wet lab willing to act on the prediction. If your work looks like theirs, the most valuable thing you can build may be the verifier, the test that makes your problem checkable, because that's the part of science AI is accelerating fastest.

One thing to watch next week: Anthropic starts formal IPO marketing, and its filing promised further reports on whether it has crossed its acceleration threshold. Watch whether any of METR's redacted evidence comes out with them, because that will decide how much of this week's debate stays a matter of trust.
