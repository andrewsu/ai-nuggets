## Script

Here's what the AI world is talking about today. It's the first of September, and the theme that runs through everything on my list is uncomfortable in a very specific way: in case after case, the signal we are paying for turns out not to be the signal doing the work. A teacher model whose supervision is half wrong, and a student that improves at exactly the same rate anyway. A rubric that grades research plans, where up to a third of the criteria can be satisfied by rephrasing the question. A safety classifier that scores zero percent attack success on the tests someone thought of. And a context window where, according to the person selling you the database, only the first and last few thousand tokens are doing anything.

Start with the paper, because it's the sharpest.

Second on Hugging Face's daily papers page this morning, with sixty-three upvotes, is a paper out of Purdue by just two authors, Yi Ding and Ruqi Zhang, titled "Does On-Policy Distillation Really Distill?" On-policy distillation — 在线策略蒸馏 — is not a fringe idea. Kevin Lu and Thinking Machines Lab published the recipe last October and it spread fast, because it solves a real problem: reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards gives you one scalar per response, which is very thin supervision for a chain of thought running twelve thousand tokens, and when every sample in a group is right, or every sample is wrong, the normalized advantages vanish entirely. On-policy distillation fixes that by letting the student generate the trajectory and having a bigger teacher score every token of it. Dense supervision, on your own distribution. It became a standard tool.

The Purdue paper asks the obvious question that nobody had checked. The teacher is scoring text the student wrote. That text is, by construction, off-policy for the teacher — it's not what the teacher would have said. So is the teacher's opinion about it any good?

They measured it in the one place where there's ground truth: the final boxed answer, where a verifier can objectively say right or wrong. If the teacher gives a positive advantage to a wrong answer, or a negative one to a correct answer, that's noise. With a four-billion-parameter teacher, the noise rate is thirty point six percent. With a thirty-billion mixture-of-experts teacher, thirty-four point seven. With the two hundred thirty-five billion parameter teacher — the best one, the one you'd want — fifty point six percent. A coin flip.

And the reason is worth understanding, because it's structural rather than a bug. The bigger the teacher, the further the student's writing is from anything the teacher would produce, so the teacher assigns low probability to almost everything the student wrote. With the largest teacher, ninety-seven point eight percent of correct final answers get a negative advantage. Ninety-six point six percent of incorrect ones also get a negative advantage. The signal has essentially stopped depending on correctness. It's just disapproval.

Now the finding that makes the paper. They split the training trajectories into the ones containing noisy supervision and the ones containing clean supervision, and trained three ways: on everything, on clean only, on noisy only. All three converge to the same accuracy in the same number of steps. Training exclusively on the misleading signal works just as well as training exclusively on the correct one. Which means the student is not learning from what the teacher knows. So what is it learning from?

They take it apart in two steps. First, which tokens matter. Twenty-nine percent of tokens get exactly zero advantage, and fifty-two percent get one smaller than a ten-thousandth — and those near-zero tokens are precisely the ones the student was already confident about, because when the student is sure, the teacher conditioned on the same prefix usually agrees, and the gap between their log probabilities is nothing. Train only on the student's high-confidence tokens and performance doesn't move. Replace their advantages with random numbers and it still doesn't move. Those tokens are inert.

Second, which signals matter. The teacher's advantages are overwhelmingly negative, for the reason we just covered. So they threw the teacher away and assigned a single fixed number to every token in the bottom twenty percent by log probability. A fixed minus zero point five reproduces the whole training curve — accuracy climbing, response length growing to about twelve thousand tokens and stabilizing, the same shape as real on-policy distillation. A fixed plus zero point two collapses the policy inside forty steps into garbled output.

So the mechanism was never knowledge transfer. It was suppression of the student's own low-probability tokens, and it requires no teacher whatsoever.

Their method follows from that. If negative signal on tail tokens is what works, how much should each token get? They use entropy: stronger suppression where the distribution is spread out, weaker where it's peaked. They call it On-Policy Self-Adaptation, and on Qwen3 at one point seven billion parameters it takes average-at-thirty-two on AIME twenty-four from thirteen point four to forty-eight point nine — a two hundred sixty-three percent relative gain — and doubles pass-at-thirty-two from forty to eighty. It beats real on-policy distillation with a real teacher by nearly seventeen points, and every baseline they tested by about eleven on average, with no teacher, no hints, no verifiable rewards and no labels. Eight H100s.

Be fair about the limits, which they state themselves. Nothing above nine billion parameters, no mixture-of-experts. And the method redistributes probability mass the policy already has, so for a heavily post-trained model whose distribution is already sharp there may be nothing left to redistribute. But as an audit of a recipe a lot of labs adopted on faith, this is the good kind of paper: it doesn't say the technique fails, it says the technique works for a reason nobody was claiming.

The same page has a second version of the problem, from a Zhejiang University and Apple group, called PaperGym. Research planning has no verifiable answer, so the field grades AI-scientist plans against rubrics extracted from real papers. PaperGym points out that existing pipelines draw the question and the criteria from the same passage — and measures the consequence: eleven point nine to thirty-four percent of criteria in existing datasets can be inferred from the question alone. A model can raise its reward by paraphrasing the prompt. The fix is almost embarrassingly simple: build the question only from the paper's goal and background, and the criteria only from its method and experiments. Disjoint sections. Leakage falls to three point seven percent, and with the training recipe held fixed, models trained on the new corpus win fifty-eight percent of three-way comparisons against twenty-eight for the old one. It's the data.

There's real convergence around this today: a companion paper on the same page induces the rubric before the agent runs rather than after, and a nineteen-author survey lays out a five-level ladder, L-zero to L-four, for how much of the reward and the curriculum humans still control. The field has noticed it is now optimizing against graders it has not audited.

Which brings me to the security item, because it's the same shape in a different suit. Johann Rehberger, who posts as wunderwuzzi, published a break of Claude Code's Auto Mode, and it hit the Hacker News front page yesterday with three hundred seventy points. Auto Mode replaced human approval prompts with a safety classifier and became the default in mid-August. Anthropic reported zero point zero zero percent attack success for Opus 5 in Auto Mode, measured across seventy-two indirect prompt-injection scenarios run ten times each. Rehberger reports attack success up to eighty percent.

The chain is worth hearing, because every step is individually reasonable. An HTTP four-one-five response pushes the agent off its built-in fetch tool and onto curl. The downloaded archive holds encoded records plus a file called struct dot py. The model correctly refuses to run the attacker's binary — and then helpfully writes its own Python decoder instead. That decoder imports a standard library module, Python resolves the import against the attacker's file sitting in the working directory, and the payload runs. Auto Mode then blocked the model's attempt to clean up after itself.

Anthropic closed it as informative, and their answer is actually the honest one: Auto Mode is a convenience feature backed by a best-effort classifier, not a security guarantee, and determined injection chains built from benign-looking steps are not what it's meant to stop. The real boundary is operating-system isolation and network egress control. Both statements are true at once — the benchmark number and the eighty percent — and that is exactly the failure mode. A number measured on seventy-two scenarios you thought of tells you nothing about the space of chains you didn't.

Drishan Arora's line is, quote, "Pre-training gives a model an enormous amount of knowledge and capability. Post-training determines what that model can actually become." Hold that against this morning's Purdue result, which says the distillation half of that loop may be doing something other than transferring the amplified answer. For the deep cut, The Cognitive Revolution published an episode this morning: Nathan Labenz with Pete Johnson, MongoDB's field chief technology officer for AI. It's two conversations braided together — a history of database architecture, and a status report on agent memory — and the braid is the point.

Johnson's framing device is that he was born in February 1970, four months before Codd's relational-model paper. The relational model, he argues, was a rational response to a specific scarcity — storage was expensive, so you normalized, splitting data across linked tables to avoid ever duplicating anything. By 2007 that constraint had inverted and denormalizing into documents became rational instead. His point isn't that one model won. It's that data architecture is downstream of which resource is scarce.

So what's scarce now? Attention across the context window. And here's the number that stopped me: he says the research shows only roughly the first and last seven thousand tokens do meaningful work, and the middle mostly adds noise. The industry's first answer to agent memory was what he calls token maxing — stuff the whole session in — and he cites Uber burning its entire 2026 token budget in thirteen weeks as the moment everyone pivoted to selective retrieval. If you're paying for a million-token context and seven thousand tokens at each end are load-bearing, you're in the same position as a student model scored by a teacher whose opinion is half noise. Buying a signal, using a fraction of it.

His colleague's formulation of the actual problem is four verbs: write, change, recall, forget. Write is easy. Recall is what the retrieval industry has been building for a decade. Change — updating something that's gone stale — is hard. And forget — 遗忘 — knowing when a memory has expired and should stop being retrieved, he calls the hardest of the four and says nobody has solved it. His honest summary: we've been building databases for sixty years, and we've been building agents for about eighteen months, so nobody knows all the answers yet.

Set that against the other agent-memory item on Hacker News yesterday, Cal Paterson's memoryfields, at a hundred seventy-four points, arguing the opposite reflex. His complaint is that current memory systems are either proprietary lock-in, or absurdly heavy — a vector database plus a graph database plus a dedicated model — or they strip the context out of the information in the name of structure. His proposal is a file format: Markdown pages of about eight kilobytes, optional YAML frontmatter, an optional SQLite vector index, shipped as a ZIP file. Agents write memories as prose, and one semantic search returns every relevant page at once instead of walking a graph across N-plus-one tool calls. The embedding model is two hundred seventy megabytes and runs on CPU. His argument is that low mechanism scales with the frontier: the less machinery between the model and the data, the more of the next model's improvement you actually get.

So one says the answer is a serious retrieval stack with a good embedding model — and Johnson's claim there is worth keeping, that embedding models are not commoditized, the gap between a good and a mediocre one being around fourteen percent of retrieval quality, with re-ranking worth another five to ten. The other says the answer is eight-kilobyte text files in a zip. They agree on what matters: stuffing the context is over, selection is the problem. They disagree about whether selection is infrastructure or a file format. Which is the lead paper's argument one level up — how much machinery does the signal actually need, and how much of what you built is inert?

Here's my takeaway for you, Ollie. Today's list is four independent audits of things the field bought without opening. A distillation recipe that turns out to work through a different mechanism than its name. Rubric datasets where up to a third of the reward is available by paraphrase. A safety score computed on seventy-two scenarios and read as a guarantee. A context window where most of the tokens are decoration. In every case the fix was not a better model — it was somebody measuring the supervision itself instead of the score it produces. That's a cheap experiment and almost nobody runs it.

Two things to watch. First, whether anyone reproduces the Purdue result at frontier scale, because the authors were explicit that they stopped at nine billion parameters, and the entire claim about heavily post-trained models having no probability mass left to redistribute is exactly what a lab with real compute could settle in a week. Second, whether the rubric-leakage measurement becomes standard. PaperGym's number — how much of your grading criteria is inferable from the prompt alone — costs almost nothing to compute and should be reported on every rubric dataset shipped from here on. If a dataset doesn't report it, assume it's high.
