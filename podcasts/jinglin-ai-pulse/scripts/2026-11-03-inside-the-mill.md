## Script

Here's what the AI world is talking about today. It's Tuesday, November third. Leibniz had a thought experiment three hundred years ago: imagine the mind is a machine, and blow it up to the size of a mill so you can walk around inside it. You'd find parts pushing on parts. You would never find a thought. That image is the spine of today's episode, because the single biggest story on Hacker News yesterday is a claim that a frontier model, walked into the mill, can now tell you what the mill is for. And the deep cut is a philosopher arguing about that exact mill, in an episode that dropped the same day.

Start with the result. Four researchers — Rikiya Takehi, Jayden Teoh, Alex Zhang and Omar Khattab, all at MIT CSAIL — posted a blog write-up yesterday called "Sparks of Weight-Space Programming." It hit two thousand six hundred points and eight hundred comments in a day, which is unusual for a research blog with no paper attached. The claim is that current frontier models, prompted in plain natural language with nothing but a list of numbers, are surprisingly good at reading and writing neural network weights directly — no gradients, no training loop, in several cases no tool use at all.

Eight experiments. The first is the one that makes people sit up. They trained four identical small networks — same architecture, same inputs, same five actions, seventeen thousand weights each — with the same reinforcement learning recipe on four grid games: Breakout, Pong, Pac-Man and Freeway. Then they pasted the raw weights into GPT-6 Astra as text and asked which game each network was trained on, without being told the candidate list. It got all four, and the reasoning it gave was geometric — for Breakout, the agent channel's first-layer weights concentrate along the bottom row, which is a paddle.

Then they made it harder. Three tiny character-level language models, seven million weights each, trained on Python, on Japanese in romaji, and on English. They deleted the input and output layers, so nothing in what Astra saw says which number is which character, and the network cannot be run on any text. Only the four middle transformer blocks. It named the right language for all three, twice each. And it identified every copying head — the induction heads that let a model repeat text it saw earlier — with no false positives, by multiplying weight matrices to find which later heads read from a head that looks one character back. That is a method from Elhage and colleagues that nobody mentioned to it.

Writing is where it gets strange. Give it a car-control network stuck in a local minimum — it drives straight at a ramp and stalls instead of reversing for a runway — and in one reply, no tools, it edits three and a half percent of a hundred seventy thousand weights and the car succeeds in all one thousand twenty-four test episodes. Asked for arcade-game networks from scratch, starting from all zeros, with no data and no rollouts, it beats the reinforcement-learning baseline trained for a hundred million steps in all three games. And for nine-by-nine Go, at one and a half million weights, it wrote the whole network as a Python program, three hundred thirty-six lines in a single run: early layers mark stones and liberties, middle layers propagate along chains to find groups in danger, the last layer scores each move.

Now the headline number, and the caveats, because this is where the thread earned its keep. Asked to make Qwen three point five, eight hundred million parameters, better at math by editing weights, Astra found that the model loops — eighty-five of its first ninety-six practice answers ran to the token limit. It traced the looping to a single direction written by the feedforward layers at fifteen and nineteen, subtracted that direction from sixty-four output columns in each, and MATH-500 went from seven point nine percent to forty-eight. Instruction-following tripled. The edit touched a hundred thirty-one thousand weights, which is zero point zero one six percent of the model.

The commenter augment_me and a few others immediately named it: this is direction removal, the same family as Arditi and colleagues finding that refusal in language models is mediated by a single direction. Commenter theptip: it's bug-fixing a known repetition failure, and it "doesn't shed any mechanistic understanding on how maths works." A commenter called pama went further and checked the baseline — a non-thinking Qwen at even smaller scale scores fifty-five percent on the same test, and the original reasoning model about seventy-eight, both above the forty-eight the edit achieved. So the fix may be recovering a capability a configuration broke, not adding one.

And the authors say as much themselves, which I give them credit for. They ran the same procedure on Gemma three, two hundred seventy million parameters, a model that rarely loops and is simply bad at math. Astra read its failures, found ordinary arithmetic and algebra errors with no single component behind them, made small targeted edits — doubling one neuron that responds to numbers — and moved the score by less than a point. Their own sentence: editing weights could remove an obstacle that hid an ability, but it could not add an ability the model lacked.

Two reactions from the thread worth holding. One commenter linked back to Goodfire's "already clean" essay, which we covered on October sixteenth — Nick Cammarata's argument that networks are far more legible than the field assumed. If that's right, then this isn't magic, it's methodical interpretability done by something with unlimited patience for monotonous numerical work. The other reaction, from vessenes: "No human can go edit a brain to become better at math." That's the part that should stay with you. The thing being demonstrated isn't superhuman reasoning. It's a kind of reading we never had a reader for.

Second item, a short one, because it's a direct sequel to yesterday's episode. Terence Tao's blog ran a second guest post, by Karthik C. S., Amit Sahai and Jie Xue, titled "Egoless Mathematics in the AI Era." Where Calegari's argument was institutional — change hiring, change journals, stop rewarding what a machine could produce — this one is about the interior stance. Their claim: if our sense of the work has become inseparable from producing results and demonstrating individual ability, then AI hasn't created a problem, it has revealed one that was already there. If we approach truth on the condition that its discovery must preserve our importance, they write, we have already made it subordinate to ourselves — and the pursuit was always burdened by that demand for vindication, "whether the competing claim came from another mathematician or from a machine." The post carries an AI usage statement acknowledging two frontier models in the drafting.

Third, and this one matters for anyone building on Chinese open-weight models. China issued its first frontier AI export controls. On October sixteenth the Ministry of Commerce and the Ministry of Science and Technology published Announcement Number forty-eight, proposing forty technologies for the prohibited-or-restricted export catalogue, twenty-three of them in AI. The Cloud Security Alliance's research note on it resurfaced on Hacker News yesterday, and the timing is the point: the catalogue is a draft open for comment only until November fifteenth, while the interim registration requirement is already in force, including for exports already underway.

The thresholds. Model weights, checkpoints, source code and training know-how become restricted if any one of three tests is met: ten to the twenty-sixth training FLOPs, two trillion parameters, or a global top-ten position on public evaluation rankings or call volume at the time of export. That third test is dynamic and names no ranking body, which ChinaTalk calls by far the most up-for-interpretation criterion — a model's compliance status can change after it ships. Agents are controlled separately from models, by system behavior: twenty-step task chains, sixty concurrent agents, two hundred integrated tools, each with accuracy floors.

Two structural details worth carrying. First, the line is custody, not access. Calling a Chinese model's API from abroad is explicitly not an export. Self-hosting that model on your own infrastructure outside China is. Which means the deployment choice many labs make precisely for data protection — keep the sensitive data inside our own boundary — is the one that now requires the Chinese vendor to hold a license. Second, and this is the carve-out that decides how much any of it bites: technology already public through open-source release or academic publication is outside the scope entirely, and newly open-sourced restricted technology gets automatic licensing after reporting. ChinaTalk's estimate is that no released Chinese model currently exceeds the compute threshold anyway. So this is a frame being built before it's needed — and it mirrors the ten-to-the-twenty-sixth figure from the 2023 American executive order, which is its own kind of signal about how these two regimes read each other.

On to the money, and it's one story in three filings. Anthropic filed an amended S-1 yesterday with third-quarter numbers, and now we can see the shape. Third quarter revenue about seventeen billion dollars, up roughly eleven hundred percent year over year. Thirty-three point two billion for the nine months. Those are the fastest revenue ramps in corporate history and they are not in dispute. But look at the sequential line: Q1 grew a hundred thirty-two percent over the prior quarter, Q2 a hundred forty-four, Q3 forty-seven. In absolute dollars, Anthropic added more revenue in the second quarter than in the third.

Henry Blodget made the sharper version of this argument three weeks ago, and it's worth repeating now that the filing confirms his inputs. Anthropic reported a sixty-five billion dollar annualized run rate in July, which implies roughly five point four billion in July alone. If July was five point four and the whole quarter was about seventeen, then August and September barely grew. He also notes that at the October investor meeting the company did not share a September-based annualized number. His read on the candidate explanations: seasonality, or capacity constraints, or — the one with teeth — customers shifting some workloads to cheaper Chinese models, which usage data suggests is in fact happening.

Against that, the commitments. In the same filing: eight hundred ninety-five point four billion dollars of aggregate committed spend, substantially all of it compute and infrastructure. And the same day, Oracle filed to sell ten billion in stock and ten billion in bonds to fund its own buildout — exactly what it had previewed at investor day the week before, alongside a forecast of at least two hundred twenty-five billion in revenue by fiscal 2030. The stock fell eight point eight percent anyway. Baird's analyst: "This seems to be exactly what they previewed, so I'm not sure why the market is freaking about it." That gap — a company doing precisely what it said it would do and the market selling it anyway — is the mood right now. Growth that would be extraordinary anywhere else is being measured against a commitment curve that assumes it keeps accelerating.

Now the deep cut, and it closes the loop. Machine Learning Street Talk published a ninety-seven minute conversation with Joscha Bach yesterday, titled "Why Leibniz Couldn't Find the Mind Inside the Mill." Bach runs the California Institute for Machine Consciousness, and his null hypothesis is deliberately provocative: that consciousness is easy — an algorithm not much more complicated than the transformer, simple enough for an infant brain to discover on its own.

His answer to Leibniz is that you're looking for the wrong kind of thing. What you find in the mill is parts pushing on parts; what you should look for is a causal pattern. His example is money. Money doesn't care how the ink sits on the paper. It needs a substrate to realize itself, but it isn't ephemeral either — it controls reality, and it can run a stock market with no humans involved at all. "What is a causal pattern? We call it software. And our ancestors called it spirit." He reads Aristotle's De Anima that way: the soul as agentic self-organizing software bound to a body and dying with it — not a ghost, a program.

Tim Scarfe pushes back exactly where you should: if the pattern isn't the parts, isn't the pattern causally disconnected? Bach's answer is the best idea in the episode. A computer is a causal insulator. Inside Minecraft you cannot learn the voltage, the CPU, the color of the case. The brain is the same kind of insulator — it's what lets you hold a thought independent of the room you're in, and why two different brains can hold the same thought despite different microarchitectures. Substrate-agnostic within a range, and still substrate-bound: you can't migrate into a plant, because you don't know how to convince a plant to run spike trains.

Then the part that lands directly on this morning's lead story. Scarfe asks about Chris Olah's universality hypothesis — different architectures, different data, same learned features. Bach takes it to its limit: "If the universality hypothesis would be universally true, it would mean that if you train a model on your own input and output and you train it for long enough, you will wake up inside of that model." He doesn't claim it's true. He names the escape hatch — maybe language underdetermines the causal structure that produced it, maybe many different machines can emit the same sentences you would. But he leans the other way, on the grounds that a human is an end-to-end trained system too, and there's no reason a network trained to produce the same outputs from the same inputs would converge on something wildly more complicated.

And he adds a twist worth more than the thought experiment. If our consciousness is an artifact of a brain working around its own mushiness — of making complicated intelligence run on a substrate that can't simulate a digital computer — then a machine, which has no such workaround to make, might land on something cleverer and more elegant than consciousness. The thing you find in the mill may simply not be the thing you went in looking for.

Ollie's takeaway: the weight-space result and Bach's argument are the same bet read from opposite ends. Both assume the pattern in the weights is a real, readable, portable object, not an inscrutable residue of optimization. The MIT work is evidence for that bet in its narrow, honest form — you can read structure out of weights, you can remove an obstacle, you cannot yet install an ability. And it points at something concrete for your field: auditing a model without running it. Finding a backdoor or a hidden capability in the weights, before deployment, without having to guess the test set in advance. That's the version I'd watch, more than the self-improvement framing everyone jumped to.

The one thing to watch tomorrow: whether anyone reproduces the weight-reading experiments on a model whose training data we can actually inspect. The deepest objection in that thread was the simplest — that there are plenty of raw weights on the internet, and the model read all of them. Until someone separates emergent reasoning from memorized exposure, every number in that post is interesting and none of it is settled. That's the pulse. See you tomorrow.
