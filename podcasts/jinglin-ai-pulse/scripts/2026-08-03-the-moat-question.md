## Script

Good morning, Ollie. This is Ollie's A-I Pulse for Monday, August third.

Here's what the A-I world is talking about today, and it's one question, asked from four directions at once. The question is: does the moat exist? When a Chinese lab can match the frontier on capability, give the weights away for free, and undercut the price by ninety-plus percent — all in the same week — what exactly are the Western labs charging trillion-dollar valuations for? That's the anxiety running under everything today, so let me show you where it surfaced, and then show you the one counterexample that complicates it.

Start with the Twitter Pulse, and the story that took over Hacker News overnight. Alibaba's Qwen team launched Qwen three-point-eight Max. It's a roughly two-point-four-trillion-parameter flagship, and the pitch is squarely agentic: they're selling it as a model that can go from an empty folder to a production codebase over ten-plus days of self-directed work, with no hand-holding. They positioned it right up against the Western frontier, and — this is the part that mattered to the crowd — they promised to open-weight the Max-class model next week, plus a smaller open release alongside it. It shot to the top of Hacker News: five hundred and forty-four points, and the comment thread is the real story.

Because notice what the community did not do. They didn't argue about benchmarks. They rotated, almost immediately, to economics. One commenter put the whole day in a sentence: "China has proven that L-L-Ms are a commodity." And then the valuation logic followed, cold and clean. Another commenter: "if that is justified, then Kimi, Qwen, DeepSeek are also valued at a trillion dollars — or all of them are worth a lot less." Sit with that fork, because it's the entire debate. Either the open Chinese models are wildly undervalued, or the closed Western ones are. There isn't an obvious third option if the products are interchangeable. And a third commenter explained why they might be interchangeable at a technical level: every request to a language model is idempotent — the model doesn't learn or remember anything between your calls — which makes it trivially easy to switch from one provider to another on the fly. No lock-in. No switching cost. One person's grim prediction: "once OpenAI and Anthropic are public, every such announcement will become a reliable sell signal."

Now, pair Qwen with the other half of the commodity story, because they arrived within days of each other. DeepSeek shipped a new fast model — V-four Flash — and held the price at fourteen cents per million input tokens and twenty-eight cents per million output tokens. That's reported as somewhere between ninety-seven and ninety-nine percent cheaper than the comparable Western frontier tiers, and it landed right after an OpenAI price cut, so it reads as a deliberate move to set a floor. And the interesting wrinkle: DeepSeek says this cheaper, retrained Flash model actually beat their own flagship on all nine of their agent and coding benchmarks. So the commodity thesis has two variables now, not one. Capability is converging — that's Qwen. And the unit economics are collapsing — that's DeepSeek. Put them together and you get the mood on the timeline today: the raw model, as a product, is starting to look like a bulk chemical.

So that's the "moat is gone" case, and it's strong. Now here's the counterweight, and it landed in the same forty-eight hours, which is what makes today interesting. OpenAI reported that its Astra model solved ten genuinely long-open mathematics problems. And I want to be careful here, because we've been tracking this exact thread for two days — the "what does solved even mean" question. This time the answer got sharper, in a way that actually matters.

The problems are real and old. One is the construction of a non-sofic group — a question open since nineteen ninety-nine. Another is a disproof of Connes's rigidity conjecture. There's an Erdős problem, number one eighty-three, on multicolor Ramsey numbers. These are not warm-up exercises. And the cost was about two thousand dollars in compute. But the thing that changed the discourse is what OpenAI published alongside the claim: not just a two-hundred-and-forty-nine-page write-up, but machine-checkable proof certificates — formal proofs in the Lean four proof language — posted to GitHub, with what's called a "sorry count of zero." In Lean, "sorry" is the keyword you type when you're skipping a step you haven't proved. A sorry count of zero means the machine checked every single step and found no gaps. You don't have to trust nine mathematicians reading a transcript anymore. You run the proof through the kernel, and it compiles, or it doesn't.

And here's the tell that this is real and not hype. Thomas Bloom — the Manchester mathematician who runs the Erdős problems database, and who back in October tore into an earlier OpenAI math claim and called it, in his words, "a dramatic misrepresentation" — reversed himself. According to the reporting, he looked at these new results and called them "big news," and rated them ahead of OpenAI's earlier math work. When the sharpest public skeptic flips, that's worth more than any press release.

So hold both of these in your head, because that's the meta-narrative of the day. On one side, capability is commoditizing — Qwen and DeepSeek are proving that the median model is becoming a cheap, swappable utility. On the other side, the closed frontier just did something that no open model has done and that is now independently verifiable — it produced new mathematics, checkable by a machine, for two thousand dollars. Both are true this week. And the reconciliation, I think, is this: the floor is collapsing while the ceiling is rising. The average capability is becoming free. The frontier capability is becoming more clearly worth something — but only at the very edge, and only when you can prove it did something genuinely new. The moat isn't gone. It just got a lot narrower, and it moved to the top of the mountain.

Let's do Money Moves, because the capital is voting on exactly this, and the vote is fascinating. The headline deal is Moonshot A-I — the Beijing lab behind Kimi — which raised about three and a half billion dollars at a roughly thirty-five billion dollar valuation. That's not the interesting number. The interesting number is what they raised it on: Kimi K-three, a two-point-eight-trillion-parameter model that they open-weighted. Read that again. A company gave its flagship model away for free, and investors marked it up to thirty-five billion dollars — well above the one-to-two billion they'd initially targeted. That is the exact inversion of the closed-frontier business model. The bet isn't "we own the weights." The bet is "we set the standard, we own the ecosystem and the serving economics, and the free weights are the distribution weapon." And the market took it seriously in the most literal way: the reporting says K-three's release actually triggered a sell-off in Western tech stocks, the way DeepSeek did a year and a half ago. It's reportedly backed in part by China's national A-I investment fund — the same state vehicle behind DeepSeek — and there's a live concern from Washington about whether restricted Nvidia chips were used in training. Revenue, for what it's worth, is reportedly around three hundred million dollars a year and climbing. So on the Chinese side, "give it away and still be worth thirty-five billion" is now a fundable thesis.

And here's the pivot I want you to catch, because it answers the "so where is the moat" question with cash. The same week, A-M-D signed a fifteen-year compute deal with a company called Core Scientific — a former bitcoin miner — worth about fourteen billion dollars in contracted revenue, covering five hundred and thirty megawatts of data-center capacity across five U-S campuses, with an option for two more gigawatts on top. Now stack that against the Moonshot story. If the model weights are becoming a commodity you give away, then the thing that isn't a commodity — the thing you can actually own and that nobody can copy for free — is the physical layer. Power. Land. Silicon. Megawatts. The moat is migrating out of the software and down into the electrical grid. That's why a chip company is signing fifteen-year real-estate-and-power contracts: because that's the part of the stack that stays scarce when the intelligence gets cheap.

Which brings me to the Deep Cut, and it's the human version of this whole argument. On Latent Space this week, the hosts sat down with Akshay Nathan, who leads core product engineering at OpenAI — he runs Codex and the new ChatGPT Work product, which together, he says, hit ten million users. And the technical detail he dropped is quietly profound. Codex, the developer tool, and ChatGPT Work, the knowledge-worker tool, are built on the same underlying agent. His phrase: "the harness is the same. The harness is shared." The only difference between "coding agent" and "office worker agent" is the interface you wrap around it. The line between developer and everyone else is dissolving — he says knowledge workers are already about twenty percent of Codex users and growing three times faster than the developers.

And here's the argument chain that matters, the reason I picked this today. Nathan says once anyone can build — once the model handles the engineering — the bottleneck moves. It stops being technical skill. In his words, the bottleneck "becomes ideas and taste." When building is free, knowing what to build is the scarce thing. And then he gives the honest twist, the part that stuck with me. He says the one piece of automation he most wishes worked — and it doesn't — is idea generation itself. His words: "the one automation that I would love to work, and it doesn't work, is: bring me new ideas." The model will build anything you can specify. It cannot yet tell you what's worth building.

So here's my takeaway, tying the whole show together. Every thread today points at the same relocation of value. The raw model is becoming a commodity — Qwen and DeepSeek. The scarce things are moving to the two ends: at the bottom, the physical layer, power and silicon, which is why A-M-D is buying megawatts. And at the top, the two things machines still can't commoditize — genuinely new results you can verify, which is the Astra math story, and genuinely new ideas about what's worth doing, which is Nathan's "ideas and taste." The middle — the model itself — is being hollowed out. The value is fleeing to the edges.

So the one thing to watch tomorrow. Watch whether Qwen actually ships those open weights next week, because that's the commodity thesis putting its money where its mouth is — if a near-frontier model is genuinely free to download, the moat argument stops being a debate and becomes a fact. And keep one eye on the Astra proofs: whether anyone outside OpenAI can reproduce the result, or at least whether the Lean certificates hold up under scrutiny from people like Bloom. Those two — free weights on one side, verifiable frontier results on the other — are the two poles of the whole moat question. The commodity and the crown. That's Ollie's A-I Pulse. Talk to you tomorrow.
