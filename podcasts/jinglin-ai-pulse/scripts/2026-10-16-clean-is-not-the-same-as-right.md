Here's what the AI world is talking about today, and the theme comes out of an interpretability essay and a drug discovery podcast that have nothing to do with each other: being able to read what a model learned is one problem, and whether it learned the right thing is a completely different one. The field got good news on the first problem yesterday. The second one is still there.

The biggest intellectual story on AI Twitter in the last day is an essay from Nick Cammarata at Goodfire titled "Neural Networks Are Already Clean." Cammarata's launch post was the second most engaged AI tweet of the day, behind only Mark Zuckerberg's, and his biography is what gives it weight. He co-authored Zoom In, the Distill paper that introduced the circuit the field read as superposition, and he started OpenAI's first large-scale automated interpretability project. In his words: "years later, I started openai's first large-scale automated interpretability project, finding about 2% of neurons legible with the primitive tools we had. with modern agentic tools and a much closer look, the picture looks totally different."

The argument chain matters more than the headline, so here it is. For years the field believed neurons are polysemantic, one neuron firing on cat faces and the fronts of cars, because networks run out of neurons and start folding unrelated concepts together where they don't interfere. That's superposition, and if it's true, you can't study the network directly; you first train a second model, a sparse autoencoder, to unfold it, and study that instead. Cammarata's claim is that the canonical evidence was misread. Go back to InceptionV1, the vision model the early field cut its teeth on, where almost nothing in the last layer looked interpretable. He argues that was an ordinary learning failure from the architecture, compounded by weak tools. With modern architectural fixes, and no change in model size or data, he says ninety-nine to a hundred percent of that last layer becomes easily interpretable. He trained versions across a three-hundred-fold range in size, and smaller models didn't fold unrelated things together; their neurons just got coarser, one car neuron for many kinds of car, which splits into specific cars as the model grows. And the original Zoom In circuit, the car feature that seemed to hide inside dog neurons? He now says nothing downstream reads that combination. It was ordinary composition.

For language models, the preliminary audit of Qwen3-32B estimates about ninety percent of neuron poles have one main job, another seven percent have two related jobs, and two to four percent are not yet understood, with similar results in models up to trillions of parameters, like Kimi K3. Most of the leftover apparent mess he calls recursive generalization: a neuron grows offshoots like a tree branch. One neuron that detected the letters "iy" grew to detect "diy," and then "do it yourself," which doesn't contain "iy" at all. So there's one learning principle, generalization, and it's fractal.

The part I'd hold onto is the method, because it's an AI-for-science result as much as an interpretability one. Agents ran it. They mapped neurons, reviewed a hundred thousand attribution graphs, and ran a test he calls blind maximum: one agent investigates a neuron and writes a short explanation, and a second agent, seeing only that explanation, writes a sentence meant to fire it as hard as possible. That sentence often beats the strongest example found in ten billion tokens of real text. In another test on two hundred random neurons, an agent wrote five examples that should fire each neuron and five near misses that shouldn't, before anything was measured; the predicted examples fired strongly seventy-six percent of the time and the near misses eleven percent. His framing is borrowed from Rich Sutton: "it may be a bitter lesson for interpretability: the simple thing, at absurd scale, works."

The reaction split cleanly, and the split is the interesting part. On the enthusiastic side, roon wrote that it "may be remembered as a major moment in scientific history," and joked that the news is so good even Eliezer will have to lower his p-doom. Then the interpretability researchers weighed in, and they were much more measured. Neel Nanda's thread is the one to read. He opened with: "This is interesting work, but I don't think it's a significant update to the big picture of interpretability." He's on board with using neurons for pragmatic questions, like why a model did something sketchy, and he praised the agent angle as neglected with "a very high ceiling." But his crux is about facts. Language models seem to hold far more memorized facts than they have neurons, and in his words, "Facts/memorized content is largely an unstructured database of key-value pairs IMO and I don't see how this is compatible with a monosemantic view of neurons." He expects the weak activations, not the peaks, to carry that superposed information, and he's skeptical that vision results transfer, since small image models memorize far less.

Jake Mendel made the same point from another angle: "in almost all forward passes, almost all of the neurons that activate are activating less than their max-ever activations," and the essay, he says, doesn't really address that heavy tail. Leo Gao's objection is about evaluation: "imo if you can't simulate a neuron you haven't really explained it," and he guesses that simulating neurons is far harder than finding plausible explanations. And David Pfau delivered the line that sets the posture: "It's a blog post advertising a paper that hasn't been written yet." Hacker News was split differently, mostly into people who loved the Distill-era feel and people whose phones crashed on the interactive three-D header. One top comment complained that sixteen thousand words in, the section titled "What I mean by clean" still didn't say.

Here's my read. Notice what both sides actually agree on. Nanda concedes the strongest activations may well be monosemantic, and that "the most important concepts" may correspond to a neuron or a handful, which might suffice for practical purposes. Cammarata concedes his numbers move with where you set the threshold for weak activations. So the live disagreement is narrow and testable: does the tail matter? That's what the forthcoming paper has to show. And notice his own caveat about asymmetric mistakes. If you assume a network is clean and you're wrong, a messy neuron is a lead you go investigate. If you assume it's packed and you're wrong, you stop looking. That's a research-strategy argument, and it holds even if the ninety percent figure shrinks.

The second story is Meta releasing open weights for Muse Spark Open: eight hundred sixteen billion total parameters, forty-two billion active, a one-million-token context, natively multimodal, built for coding and agents, and pitched as running on two Apple M5 Ultra Mac Studios. Nathan Lambert called it "my superbowl," writing that before yesterday US open models were still behind Chinese models "from as early as Feb. 2026," and that this "cut the gap by at least half." He also called the license, in his words, "trash"; it requires attribution and a separate agreement for model-as-a-service businesses with more than twenty million dollars in revenue. The independent numbers are what make it interesting. Artificial Analysis scores it thirty-nine on its Intelligence Index, first among US open-weight models but behind Chinese leaders GLM-5.3 and Kimi K3. Muse Spark Open uses the fewest output tokens per task of the top nine open models, but it ranks last of those nine on the four agentic evaluations, and GLM-5.3's entire six-point lead comes from that agentic segment. So a model sold as "built for coding and agentic work" is strongest on everything except agentic work. The best Hacker News comment reframed the strategy: once a model clears a certain capability level, price decides, and Meta is betting that standardizing everyone on its weights makes the whole ecosystem optimize its infrastructure for free.

For the deep cut, the Latent Space AI for Science podcast, hosted by Brandon Anderson, with Ian Quigley of Leash Bio, released October fourteenth. Leash runs DNA-encoded library screens, billions of protein and small-molecule measurements, and the episode is the perfect companion to the Goodfire debate, because it's about models learning something perfectly clear that happens to be wrong.

The argument starts with a story. Years ago, Quigley and his co-founder were at Recursion looking at a molecule on a screen, and a medicinal chemist walked by and said, "hey, looks like a Stuart Schreiber molecule." Quigley's reaction: "you can tell just by walking by?" and then, "That's horrifying." So Leash tested it. They pulled every chemist in ChEMBL with more than about thirty papers and six hundred molecules, which came to eighteen hundred fifteen chemists, and trained a classifier to guess the chemist from the structure alone. Top-five accuracy was sixty percent. "It's a very, very easy problem."

Then came the step that makes it alarming. They threw away the chemical structure entirely and represented each molecule only as a vector of probabilities over who made it. Trained on that, a model predicted activity from ChEMBL as well as one trained on chemical formulas, and combining the two wasn't meaningfully better. His conclusion: "we really think that it's learning a lot of sociology. It's not learning ground truth." The mechanism is human. Chemists keep elaborating molecules that are working, so the structure gets more baroque; a building block that worked in a clinical program becomes a lucky charm; a molecule that couldn't be made because the building blocks weren't in stock leaves a fingerprint on what does get made. In his words, "when you train models on the exhaust, you might be in trouble." He named it after Clever Hans, the horse that seemed to do arithmetic but was reading its owner's posture, and failed the moment the questioner didn't know the answer.

The same failure showed up in their own model, Hermes. Attention maps showed it wasn't looking at binding pockets or conserved domains, but at little non-conserved stretches of the protein. "Hermes was probably coming up with a nickname for the protein." Instead of learning the physics of binding, it memorized ten or twelve distinctive amino acids that identify the protein. Their fix was experimental, not architectural: engineer pocket mutants at scale, so proteins that look nearly identical behave differently, until the nickname strategy stops paying off and attention spreads across the pocket. This links straight back to the lead story. Here, interpretability did its job: the model was legible enough to catch the shortcut. Legibility told them what it learned. Only new data told them whether that was right.

The forward-looking part is an agent sandbox. Leash taught open models like Qwen and GLM to describe molecules and the differences between them in plain English, then gave them ten binders and ten close non-binders, a catalog of millions of molecules, and a budget of a hundred picks to find the best binder, with results returned instantly from data already collected, "like it's a coding sandbox." Because those small activity-cliff changes are measured, the trajectories become causally grounded reasoning traces for reinforcement learning. Asked the Sri Kosuri question, if you can collect the data, why do you need the model, Quigley's answer is that the model earns its keep by transferring: get good at chemical reasoning, and you can work in agriculture, veterinary science, or materials without re-screening every protein.

Ollie's takeaway: the Clever Hans test is the one to steal. Before trusting any activity model trained on pooled public data, check whether a trivial non-chemical feature like author, lab, or molecular weight predicts as well. If it does, the model is learning who did the work, not what the molecule does.

The one thing to watch tomorrow: whether Goodfire's companion paper lands, and whether it answers Nanda and Mendel on the weak activations. If the tail turns out to be clean too, this week will be remembered. If it doesn't, the knots are still there, just smaller. That's the pulse.
