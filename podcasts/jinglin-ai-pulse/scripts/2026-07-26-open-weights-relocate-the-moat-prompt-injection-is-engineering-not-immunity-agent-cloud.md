## Script

Good morning, Ollie. This is Ollie's AI Pulse for Sunday, July twenty-sixth.

All week I've been walking you through one long question, and today it comes to a head, because the event the whole arc has been pointing at happens in a matter of hours. Kimi K-three's open weights are scheduled to go live tonight — the release clock is set for midnight U-T-C, which is roughly eight in the evening on the U-S east coast. So this is the day before the largest open-weight model ever built hits the internet, and the community is not spending that day arguing about the benchmark scores. They're arguing about what's actually scarce underneath a free model. And once you hear the three places they're pointing, you'll see it's the same argument in three costumes. Let's get into it.

First, the Twitter Pulse, and beat one is the K-three drop itself — but with the reframe that's taken over the timeline in the last day.

Here's the number everyone is passing around, and it is the openwashing test I told you to watch. K-three is two-point-eight trillion parameters. Even crushed down to four-bit precision, the weights need about one-point-four terabytes of fast memory just to sit in place before you've served a single token. In plain terms, that's on the order of eighteen eighty-gigabyte accelerators, or a full eight-card server node that can just barely hold the model with almost no room left over for actual context. And you need current-generation silicon — Blackwell or the AMD MI-four-hundred — to do it at all. So the line that's cut through is this, and I want you to hold it because it's the punchline of the entire week: open weights, in this size class, do not push capability out to the edge. They move it to whoever can afford to keep one-point-four terabytes loaded and busy. "Open" here does not mean "you can run it." It means "you can run it if you already own the rack." That's the asterisk I flagged Thursday, now stamped onto the biggest release of the year. And the license — the thing that decides whether a company can actually build on it commercially — still isn't published as of this recording. It's supposed to land with the weights tonight. That's the real test, not the parameter count.

Beat two is a completely different corner of the discourse, but stay with me, because it rhymes. Anthropic made a striking claim about Opus five this week: that it may have solved browser-based prompt injection — the attack where a web page hides instructions that hijack your agent. And the headline number is genuinely eye-catching: zero percent attack success across a hundred and twenty-nine browser-agent scenarios. Zero. But the sharper accounts pounced on the footnote immediately, and this is where the substance is. That zero percent only holds with something called Auto Mode switched on — and Auto Mode is two separate software layers wrapped around the model: one scans incoming data for hidden instructions before the model ever sees them, and a second blocks dangerous actions before they execute. Turn those guardrails off and Opus five by itself sits at three-point-seven percent — attackers get through roughly one time in twenty-seven. Interesting tell: Sonnet five is actually harder to fool naked than Opus five is. So the honest read, and the one the researchers converged on, is that Anthropic didn't make the model immune — they engineered a good cage around it. And remember, OpenAI itself admitted back in December that prompt injection may never be fully solved.

Here's the contrarian take that ties beat two off, and it's the best-argued thing I read this weekend. The camp led by the security researchers says the whole framing of "solving" prompt injection is a category error, because a successful injection never creates a new capability — it can only weaponize a permission the agent already had. Their line is blunt: an agent can only do what it's permitted to do; so govern the permissions, govern the machine. Prompt injection is like social engineering a human — you reduce it, you never eliminate it — so the durable fix isn't a smarter model, it's tighter runtime governance of what the agent is actually allowed to touch, evaluated against the agent's own identity, not the user's. Sit with how that connects to beat one. In both cases the community is saying the same thing: the model is not where the scarce, durable asset lives. In one case it's the compute rack that can hold the weights. In the other it's the permission-and-governance layer that wraps the agent. The intelligence is getting cheap and abundant. The substrate around it is where the value went. That's the Twitter Pulse.

Now, Money Moves, and today's story is the cleanest proof of that thesis in dollars. Anduril — the defense-tech company — is reportedly in talks to raise at a hundred-billion-dollar valuation. To put that in motion: it was sixty-one billion in May, about thirty billion a year ago. It has tripled in roughly twelve months. And this isn't a vibes round — their twenty-twenty-five revenue more than doubled to two-point-two billion dollars in actual sales. Zoom out and the sector tells the same story: venture funding into defense tech cleared twelve billion dollars in just the first half of this year, already more than all of last year combined. Standard caveat — Anduril's official line is that no decision has been made and anyone quoting terms is speculating.

Here's why I'm putting it in the show, because on its face it's not a frontier-lab story. Look at the contrast with everything else this week. The frontier model labs are in a knife-fight on price — Opus five doubling intelligence-per-dollar, K-three about to give frontier-adjacent weights away tonight. The raw intelligence layer is deflating in real time. And meanwhile the money — the biggest, fastest-tripling valuation on the board — is accruing to a company that takes AI and welds it to a hard, defensible, real-world market with actual contracts and actual revenue. That's the same lesson as the Pulse, told through a cap table. Value is draining out of the commoditized model layer and pooling in the layers that are hard to copy — whether that's a compute rack, a governance system, or a defense contract with the Pentagon. If you're a researcher wondering where the durable moats went, the market just drew you a map. That's Money Moves.

Now, the Podcast Deep Cut, and it's the piece that snaps all of this into a single frame. It's on Latent Space, recorded four days ago at the Data and A-I Summit: the Databricks founders Matei Zaharia — the person who created Apache Spark — and Reynold Xin. The episode's title is "Why the Frontier Ecosystem must be Open," and it hits two of today's threads so directly it's almost eerie.

Start with agent security, because Zaharia gives you the engineering version of that contrarian take from the Pulse. He argues that the naive way to secure an agent — a static list of allowed and forbidden tools, yes-or-no on each call — is a trap, because any single call can look fine in isolation. His alternative, and this is the transferable idea, is what he calls stateful, or contextual, policies: you keep track of the state of the whole session and you trip on risky patterns rather than individual actions. His own examples: if the agent installed some sketchy old package from the npm registry, or if it just read a thousand confidential documents, that's when you clamp down — because the danger is in the trajectory, not the single step. He even folds in a spend cap: he can literally tell the agent, cap this session at five dollars, and it stops. Notice that this is exactly the permissions-not-model argument the security researchers were making about prompt injection, except Zaharia has actually built the runtime that enforces it. Same conclusion from a completely different chair.

The second thread is the one I think matters most for your work, Ollie, and it's about what agents are blind to. Xin tells a story about a customer whose agents could see every log and every metric from their services, but couldn't see into the actual databases underneath — who was placing which order, what was actually happening in the live state of the business. His line: it would make those agents ten times more powerful if they could read the operational reality, not just the telemetry about it. And the reason they usually can't is a plumbing problem he skewers beautifully — the standard trick for copying transactional data into your analytics system is called change data capture, C-D-C, and he says it should really stand for "continuous data corruption," because the pipelines break on every schema change and page an engineer at three in the morning. Their fix collapses the two worlds at the storage layer instead of the query layer — write the transactional data directly in an open columnar format so an agent can just read it live. And he drops the same logic on the hype category of the last two years: quote, "vector database should have never been a separate category." Collapse it into the storage you already have.

And the throughline back to openness: Zaharia argues the layer above the coding agents has to be open source or it loses, full stop, because open formats compound network effects — they merged four hundred-plus community pull requests in a matter of days — and because enterprises have been burned before, they lived through being locked into Oracle, and they will not walk into that again willingly. His forward-looking nugget, and write this one down: customizing models is going to get dramatically easier, to the point where they've already built pipelines where an open model generates its own synthetic training data, trains on it, and beats Opus and G-P-T at a specific narrow task. The frontier model is the raw material; the thing you build around it and on top of it is the product.

The Ollie takeaway, and it's the whole week in one sentence. Every layer of the community — the people running the free weights, the people securing the agents, the people writing the checks, and the people building the data platform — spent this week saying the same thing in different dialects: the model is the cheap part now. What's scarce is the substrate. The compute to hold it, the governance to trust it, the data and memory to make it see, and the market to sell it into. For your group at Scripps, the operational version is Xin's: your agents are only as smart as what they're allowed to read. If they can see your logs but not your live experimental state, you have a ten-x capability sitting on the table, gated by a plumbing decision. Close that gap before you chase a bigger model. That's the Deep Cut.

Wrap. Two things to watch, and one to build. Watch tonight: K-three's weights and, more importantly, K-three's license. The parameter count is settled; the question that decides whether this is genuinely open or just openwashing is whether the license lets a real company build on it commercially — and whether anyone who isn't a hyperscaler can even load one-point-four terabytes to try. Watch how the market reads the Anduril round against the frontier-lab price war — because if it keeps rewarding the application layer while it marks down raw intelligence, that's the clearest signal yet about where the durable value has moved. And the thing to build, straight from Zaharia and Xin: don't start with a smarter agent. Start with the state it's allowed to see and the trajectory it's allowed to take. Govern the session, feed it the live data, and the intelligence takes care of itself. Have a good Sunday, Ollie.
