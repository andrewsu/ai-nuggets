So I went and found the testimony. Every quoted line comes from the Senate Judiciary subcommittee hearing on July twenty-fifth, twenty twenty-three, and the official transcript matches the viral quote card word for word. Amodei said, quote, "if the path continues, I think we could get to a very dangerous place." He said that when you deploy a model yourself, "you can revoke a user's access," and that an uncontrolled release is "entirely out of your hands." And he said "we should confront the advocates of this with that problem and challenge them to solve it."

What the clip leaves out comes just before those lines. In the same answer he said, quote, "in most scientific fields, open source is a good thing. It accelerates progress." He said there's room for small and medium open models, that "the risks are relatively limited" for open models released so far, and that "construed very narrowly, I'm not sure I have an objection." The worry he described was about the trajectory, in a twenty twenty-three forecast of biorisk two to three years out. And his current written position, from July of this year, says, quote, "Anthropic has never advocated for a ban on open-weights models," and calls open-weight models without dangerous capabilities "a public good." What he does argue for is no chip sales to China, a crackdown on industrial-scale distillation, and mandatory safety testing for all sufficiently capable models, open and closed alike.

That doesn't settle the argument, and the best Hacker News comments knew it. One commenter pointed out that a firm profiting from a warning doesn't make the warning false, and used the New Orleans levees as the example. Another asked open-model advocates for what Amodei asked for in twenty twenty-three, a concrete counterproposal: if an open model can someday find and exploit vulnerabilities, how do we protect critical infrastructure? So the real disagreement isn't ban versus no ban, because nobody in this exchange is proposing a ban. It's who tests a model before release, against what bar, and whether that test can be run on weights that are already out.

Which brings us to the second story, and it lands right on that question. On Monday, Z.ai, the lab behind the GLM models, released GLM-5.4, its first loop transformer, and it's worth understanding the architecture. A normal transformer runs each layer once per token. A looped model runs a block of shared-weight layers several times, so it gets more depth without adding parameters. The idea dates back to the twenty eighteen Universal Transformer, and the known catch is that looped models trained from scratch lag behind ordinary ones at the same pretraining budget. Z.ai's move was not to pretrain at all. They took the existing GLM-5 base, around seven hundred and fifty billion parameters, and through continued training they run twenty-eight of its layers twice.

Two engineering details carry the result. First, if you simply overwrite the hidden state on each pass, activation norms blow up and downstream layers get inputs they've never seen. So each pass writes through a per-token gate, and after training the gates split the work. The first pass writes cautiously and builds context. The second pass opens up and refines, and on hard tokens the first pass writes more aggressively too. Second, memory. A naive loop doubles the key-value cache, so the second pass reuses the first pass's cache and keeps only a short sliding window of its own. At a million tokens of context, an extra loop costs one small window, not a second full cache.

The evidence so far is thin, though. The only controlled number is a thirty-billion-parameter ablation in which, per Z.ai's chart, AIME twenty-five went from about nineteen to about thirty with roughly ten percent more training compute. There are no benchmarks at all for the flagship. teortaxes called the release compute-hungry and argued that three months of better reinforcement learning could have done more. On Hacker News, users complained about what Z.ai's own blog admits is the main limitation: "marathon thinking" loops. And the launch post ended with two sentences that would have been unusual from a Chinese open lab a year ago: "API access will open after safety evaluation. Model weights will be released responsibly." Hold on to that line for the Deep Cut.

For the Podcast Deep Cut, I went to the 80,000 Hours podcast. Host Tom Reed interviewed Julian Gewirtz, a historian and Biden's former senior director for China and Taiwan at the National Security Council. It was released November nineteenth but recorded October second, so keep that in mind as I connect it to this week. The question running through the episode is what would actually change Beijing's mind about AI risk.

Here's Gewirtz's chain. Step one: start with what the Communist Party cares about, which is "regime security, regime stability." So when Chinese discourse warns about "extreme loss-of-control risks," it means losing political control as well as losing control of the models. Step two: Beijing doesn't distinguish between American labs the way Silicon Valley does. He said that "to a stunning degree, Beijing has been on the record going after Anthropic." State media commentaries ran the headline "Anthropic has contracted the American disease," and called the company "a monster bred by the American path." So no CEO's messaging will move Beijing. What could, in his ranking, is first "their own domestic sense of the risks," and second, seeing the US regulate at home, a "costly signal." And he separates regulation from pacing. China regulates deepfakes and chatbots for minors, he notes, and nobody in Washington thinks that has condemned China to second place.

Step three is the part that matters this week. He says China partly stumbled into open source, because open weights were "great to undercut American companies," great for spreading influence, and great "on a very limited compute budget," and he names Z.ai as his example. The party, he says, was "happy to go along for the ride," and then found it could spin openness geopolitically. But it isn't a principle. Quote, "They just need to be more so-called 'open' than the United States is." And as of October, he saw the discourse turning. Beijing's AI Safety Governance Framework three point oh warns that open-source releases lower the barrier to sophisticated cyberattacks, and Chinese commentators had started saying, quote, "We can't have unrestricted openness. Constructive forces can use it; destructive forces can also use it." His final step: watch materials, not speeches. If Beijing came to believe transformative AI was close, you'd see it centralize compute and suddenly buy far more of the Nvidia chips it has been rationing.

Now put that next to Monday. The lab Gewirtz named as the example of cheap Chinese openness shipped its flagship with a safety evaluation before API access and weights "released responsibly." The LeCun thread framed openness against control as East against West, and that framing is out of date on both sides. Amodei's written position is mandatory testing for open and closed models alike, and in Gewirtz's account a Beijing technical body is writing down the cyber risk of open releases. The two governments may end up converging on the same gate, which is testing before release, for opposite reasons.

Ollie's takeaway. If your research stack depends on open-weight models, and for most of biomedical AI it does, plan for open weights becoming staged or delayed releases rather than disappearing. Pin and archive the exact checkpoints you build on, where the license allows, and record version hashes in your methods, because "released responsibly" may mean the model you validated isn't the one you can download later. And the testing bar most likely to decide what gets released is biology, the domain where Amodei worries about a strong attacker-defender asymmetry. If your group builds or evaluates bio-capable models, those evaluations will be the evidence regulators on both sides use.
