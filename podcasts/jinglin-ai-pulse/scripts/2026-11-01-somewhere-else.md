## Script

Here's what the AI world is talking about today. It's Sunday, November first, the business wires are quiet, but the forums were not. And almost everything that got real attention in the last forty-eight hours turns out to be about one question: when a model says no, where does the work go? Because it rarely just stops. It goes somewhere else. Sometimes to a weaker model the same company quietly swaps in. Sometimes to a Chinese open model. Sometimes, in the case of one piece of malware, down a three-model fallback chain that was engineered in advance. Today is about that routing, and who ends up paying for it.

Let's start with the thread that got the most heat. On Friday, an account called Chasm posted that Claude Code had picked up a new rule around October twenty-seventh, delivered from Anthropic's servers under a feature flag with the internal name "tengu heron brook," with no setting to turn it off. The rule text, as Chasm quoted it: "Do build original games, games in a genre, and games from the user's own designs and assets. Don't build a recreation of a specific existing game, even if it is renamed or called original." That post did about two point two million views. Tom's Hardware followed up on Saturday and was careful about it: there's no official confirmation that this is the actual rule text, but there is a visible influx of issues on the Claude Code GitHub from developers refused halfway through their own projects, refused when replicating a single game mechanic, refused when patching a tabletop-style game, at least one from a developer with a real track record. And the refusals seem to spill beyond games, with user reports of Claude declining to analyze a simple Windows crash dump or to clear junkware off a Samsung TV.

Then on Saturday a second complaint, from a researcher named Jiahao Shao, went to about two hundred seventeen thousand views. His words: "I never distilled Claude, rollouts came from a strong open-source model; Claude just helped submit jobs and run analysis. Still got tagged for 'training a competitor,' and now anything touching RFT, SFT, or training larger models gets me blocked, Opus 5.5 downgraded to Opus 5, or the session killed outright."

Here is the part of this that's not drama, and it's the part you specifically should know about, Ollie. That "downgraded to Opus 5" is not a rumor. It's documented in Anthropic's own Opus 5.5 launch post. The post says Opus 5.5 ships with safeguards on cybersecurity, biology, and distillation, "all of which fall back to another model transparently." And in the benchmark footnotes it spells out the routing: when safeguards intervened, cybersecurity tasks were completed by Opus 4.8, and, quote, "biology and frontier LLM development tasks were completed by Claude Opus 5." So the two things a biomedical AI researcher does all day, biology and training models, are exactly the two categories that get silently handed to an older model when a classifier trips. The launch post also names the escape hatch: a Life Sciences Verification Program, for vetted academic labs, startups and pharma, with safeguards "designed for the full breadth of biology-related work." If you've noticed Claude getting oddly duller on a protein or a training script, that's the first thing to check.

The Hacker News pushback was worth hearing too. One of the top replies on the Shao thread, from a user called tristanj, pointed out that he's based in Hong Kong, where Claude is geo-blocked, so he was likely on a VPN, and model-training work over a VPN is exactly what a distillation classifier is built to flag. Another commenter said they've been training models with a novel linear attention mechanism with Claude for a week straight with no refusals. So the training classifier is clearly not blanket. On the games rule, the same tristanj made the cold business case: a few lines of system prompt gives up a tiny market and avoids potentially billions in copyright exposure, at a moment when AI-assisted console jailbreaks and browser ports of commercial games are all over the gaming press. The other side was put most sharply by a user called mgdev: "Can you imagine a devtools company deciding, 'Actually, no, we're going to police what kind of programs you can compile'?" And Tom's Hardware's Bruno Ferreira, as Mashable quoted him, said it "highlights the irony of frontier AI companies that copied the world's information and art for their training sets and are now concerned with copyright infringement."

But the most predictive comment was the dull one. Several people said, in effect, reverse engineers will just route it through OpenRouter or a local model. There's already a post on Hacker News from earlier in the week titled "Hermes and REA and GLM 5.3 equals reverse engineering the TikTok app for less than twenty dollars." The refusal doesn't stop the job. It changes the vendor.

Which brings us to the second story, and the one that makes this concrete in the worst way. On Saturday, Cisco Talos published an analysis of a Windows infostealer that names itself VOLTA AUTODOXER. It does the usual stealer things: browser passwords, cookies, crypto wallets, session tokens, SSH and cloud keys. Then it does something new. It uses the victim's own stolen Gmail session to search for delivery receipts and pin down a home address, values their Roblox inventory with a live lookup, assembles all of it into a structured bundle, and posts that bundle to OpenRouter with a system prompt telling the model to act as "a forensic intelligence analyst drafting a target assessment," including a section headed "WHAT THEY'RE WORTH."

The hardcoded default model is Claude Opus four point six. And here's the detail that ties today together: the code checks the reply for phrases like "I can't" or "I'm not able," and if it sees a refusal, it fails over to Kimi K2, and then to Gemini two point five Flash. Temperature is pinned at zero point two for consistent output. Talos also found it's built from a template with six swappable slots, which suggests it's sold as a builder: customers plug in their own keys and get a custom binary. Talos's conclusion is worth quoting: "As AI integration in malware matures, refusal resilience may become a standard design requirement." And their framing of what's actually new: "there is a difference between a wide net and a wide net that sorts itself." So the frontier model's refusal is real, and it's one hop in a chain somebody designed to route around it.

The third story is the inverse, an attack that never asked the model anything. On Thursday, Cursor disclosed that for about eleven hours, from roughly two thirty in the morning to one thirty in the afternoon Pacific, two versions of the Claude Code extension on Cursor's own extension marketplace were malicious. Cursor says plainly these versions "were not developed by Anthropic." The security firm Phorion took the bundle apart: the attacker prepended code to the extension's main file so it runs the moment the editor loads it, before the extension even activates, with no click required. It appends an attacker's SSH key to your authorized keys, opens a reverse SSH tunnel to a remote server, installs persistence disguised as an Apple system agent on macOS or a scheduled task on Windows, and beacons back your hostname, your git email, and the names, not the values, of every environment variable matching key, token, secret, or password. That last one is a shopping list.

There's an almost funny detail in Phorion's write-up. On a Mac, the malware checks whether remote login is off by running a system command without admin rights. Without admin rights, that command prints an error message and still exits successfully, so the malware's check for the word "Off" fails, and it concludes SSH is already on. On most Macs, the inbound door it built leads to a closed port. Cursor says the attacker's server is now offline; it shut down the whole marketplace, rotated its keys and rebuilt it. Both write-ups say the same practical thing: uninstalling the extension doesn't remove the backdoor. If you use Cursor, grep your authorized keys file for the string "svc telemetry."

As one commenter put it, it wasn't Anthropic that was hacked, it was the marketplace. And that's the point. All of the guardrail argument is about what the model will agree to do. This attack sat in the package that wraps the model, with your editor's permissions, and the model was never consulted.

One counterweight, because it was the single most upvoted thing on Hacker News this weekend. On Saturday morning Andrej Karpathy got tired of YouTube recommendations, and by the afternoon had HackerTube, a YouTube clone built from the roughly sixty-five hundred videos people have recommended in Hacker News comments since two thousand seven. Opus downloaded the whole comment archive, ran Sonnet over it to throw out spam, narrowed it to forty thousand recommending comments, wrapped it in a UI and deployed it. About two hours. And his edit is the line people quoted: "I don't actually do anything resembling traditional engineering anymore." The only manual steps were creating a Google Cloud API key, booting a server, and buying a domain. Which is to say, the three points where a human still touched it are the three points that hold credentials. That's exactly the surface the extension backdoor went after.

Money moves, and it's short today because the real money story is metering. OpenAI's help center now says that starting in December, Plus, Pro and Business plans will share a single usage allowance across ChatGPT and Codex, measured in tokens, with harder models and bigger tasks drawing more, and only instant chat exempt. On Hacker News, a self-described researcher said the decoupled billing was one of the main reasons they paid OpenAI at all, because they could run long mathematical reasoning in chat without eating their coding budget. Another wrote that OpenAI talks about intelligence too cheap to meter, and is now metering. And one summed up the mood: "I think the demo period is ending. So happy to have the open models!" That lands one day after the Wall Street Journal headline we noted yesterday, that OpenAI's latest round came in at less than half its target.

Set that next to Sam Altman on the Cheeky Pint podcast, as transcribed by the gaming site FRVR. He argued against local inference on allocation grounds: "from a global allocation perspective, actually, it's quite terrible to have everyone have a bunch of the world's limited supply of DRAM sitting in their own computer. And be used five percent of the time." And: "You want maximum utilisation in the cloud." On pure utilization, he's right. But listen to this weekend's threads and you hear why people want the RAM anyway: the local machine is the one place nobody pushes a server-side rule to, routes you to an older model, or meters your chat against your coding. The labs' argument for centralization is efficiency. The users' argument against it is the routing.

Now the deep cut. On the Interconnects podcast, released October twenty-fifth, Nathan Lambert and Florian Brand, the eval researcher, spent half an hour on what they called the new cyber reality, and it's the best frame I found for today.

The chain starts with Kimi K3.1 Pro. Lambert reads out Moonshot's open-weight principles: "we aim not to release open weights that would extend the absolute frontier of cyber capability," and they deliberately down-weighted cyber data in mid-training. Brand defends it with a distinction that matters. Patching, the defensive side, doesn't need long-horizon capability. Actually wrecking an organization does, "because you need very long-horizon capabilities, and you need to persist through all the stages that you penetrate," and that's where closed models like GPT five point six Sol are "just way, way better than open models." So you can strip the offense-heavy, long-horizon training and keep the defense.

Second step, Lambert's view of the panic. The cyber experts he reads look at the ecosystem, where vulnerability counts and time-to-exploit were on exponentials before ChatGPT, while "our corner of Twitter" argues about open versus closed. His conclusion: "none of this seems like an open-closed model story."

Third step is the one that maps directly onto today. Brand says the lesson of the last few months is that "the diffusion isn't happening fast enough." Defenders couldn't get into the trusted access programs. And then: "It is obvious that the closed models are just better at these things, but if you can't get access and get blocked with every request, what should you do? You need to use the less capable open models."

Fourth, Brand gives the closed labs real credit: "OpenAI and Anthropic seem to have updated their safeguards, because they can update them at a moment's notice," citing a rollout that blocked people from simply asking for the PHP exploit. That's the same mechanism as the server-pushed games rule, seen from the side where it works. Lambert then names the cost. He relays researchers saying "I haven't been able to use any agents after Opus 4.8, because I get rolled back immediately," and draws the line: "maybe the least motivated hacker is blocked by the guardrails, which is the teenager being like, 'Can I steal my friend's parents' money out of their bank account?' That stuff's getting stopped, blocked, which is good."

And in his follow-up essay on Friday, Lambert quotes a security practitioner's line that completes the argument: "A bakery isn't going to start making dog food because there's a dog food shortage." Threat actors are businesses with business processes. He also notes that, so far, "closed models have played a very heavy part in the recent hacks, at least more than open models have."

Put those together and here's the takeaway, Ollie. A guardrail filters by motivation, not by capability. The motivated actor, the malware author with a three-model fallback chain, the reverse engineer with GLM and twenty dollars, routes around it on day one; that's a business process. The person it actually stops is the low-motivation-to-switch user: the game developer mid-project, the researcher whose training job gets quietly sent to Opus 5. The safety benefit is real, it's the teenager. But the cost lands on legitimate users unless the verification programs scale faster than the classifiers do. For your own work, that suggests three things: apply to the life-sciences verification program if you're doing biology with Opus, keep an open-model path ready for anything involving training, and learn the symptoms of being routed so you know when the model you're talking to isn't the one you picked.

The one thing to watch tomorrow: whether Anthropic says anything official about the games rule. A behavior change pushed from a server, with no changelog and no setting, is now the main way these products change under you. The most useful thing any lab could ship this week isn't a model. It's a public log of the rules.

That's the pulse. See you tomorrow.
