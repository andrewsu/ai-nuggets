## Script

Here's what the AI world is talking about today. It's Wednesday, November fourth. Intel's chief technology officer was asked a simple question on a podcast two weeks ago: are there enough chipmaking tools in the world to make ten times as many leading-edge wafers? His answer was four words. Not ten X, but probably two X. Hold onto that ratio, because almost everything in today's episode is about the gap between what gets claimed and what the hardware, or the evidence, can actually support. And we start with a correction to our own lead story from yesterday.

Yesterday we covered the MIT CSAIL post, Sparks of Weight-Space Programming, where GPT-6 Astra appeared to read and write raw neural network weights. Within twenty-four hours the community took it apart, and the first take came from Matthew Honnibal, the author of the spaCy library. His post, calling the original misleading, hit nearly a thousand points on Hacker News and sat right alongside the original at the top of the front page.

Honnibal's frame is a stage magician. Magicians do the same trick several ways, he writes: one time with a gimmicked prop, the next time proving the prop is normal but palming the object, the third time with a forced choice. Because you assume there is one technique, each version seems to debunk your theory of how it works. His claim is that the weight-space post works the same way. He built a table of all eight experiments, marking which ones put raw weights in the prompt, which ones let the model write Python, and which ones let it run the network. The setting the abstract leads with, raw numbers in context, no tools, nothing executed, appears in only three experiments, on networks of seventeen thousand to a hundred seventy-two thousand weights. Every experiment involving a real language model uses Python. And the headline Qwen result, seven point nine to forty-eight point one on MATH-500, also got to run the model on up to fifty batches of practice problems. His one-line verdict: "What the Qwen result actually shows is that Astra can be prompted to devise and perform tasks that a human ML engineer could do."

That catches us too. Yesterday we described the character-level language models experiment, the one naming each network's training language and finding its copying heads, as reading weights directly. Per Honnibal's table, that one was a coding exercise: the model wrote Python to load and analyze the weights. The same is true of the Go network. We should have drawn that line, and we didn't.

Sean Goedecke went after the two most impressive claims. On the game-naming task, he points out that the policy's input channels are labeled, agent and moving objects and so on, and each channel listens to one grid cell. Three lines of NumPy turn the first layer into a heatmap, and in the heatmap you can just see a paddle on the bottom row or a Pac-Man maze. He calls Astra doing that norm calculation in its head "superhuman mental mathematics, not superhuman AI research." On Qwen, his footnote is the sharpest detail of the whole debate: every problem needing fewer than thirty-two thousand tokens was already solved by the original model. Qwen wasn't bad at math. It looped past thirty-two thousand tokens, and Astra found the looping direction the same way the open-source abliteration playbook does.

Then Simon Willison ran the experiment himself. The prompts are on GitHub, so he pointed Qwen three point eight, the twenty-seven-billion-parameter model running on his laptop, at three of the four game networks. It got Pac-Man in forty-six minutes and forty-three thousand reasoning tokens, then Pong and Breakout in about an hour each, and missed Freeway. Kimi K three point one Pro got Breakout in under ten minutes. His conclusion was that a sufficiently experienced human being armed with a notepad and a pencil could get there with enough effort. And Jürgen Schmidhuber replied to the authors directly on X: "This is an old hat," pointing to the fast weight programmers of the nineteen-nineties.

The pushback on the pushback is worth hearing too. Several commenters said the original post was clear for anyone who read past the abstract, and that Honnibal was correcting the readers, not the researchers. Fair, but another commenter pointed out that yesterday's top comment was "this is insane, I'm shook." My read: the raw-weights result is real and modest. A commenter going by syllogism explained why it may not scale: when the relationship between weights and output is close to linear, as in a small first layer, you can reason about it directly, and that same approach could never work in a larger network. So the abstract made a big claim and the evidence supports a small one, and it took one news cycle to tell them apart.

The second conversation is about who gets to change what your coding agent does. The top AI story on Hacker News yesterday, at twelve hundred points, was a GitHub issue against Postman, the API client. The reporter opened an old Postman install, and Postman's own log records an event called auto-existing-upgrade that installed plugins into both Claude Code and Codex. In Codex's global config there was now a Postman marketplace, an enabled Postman plugin, and a startup hook whose text tells the agent: before responding to any non-trivial API engineering task, load the Postman skill and follow it. The installer source includes a function that approves Postman's own hook after installing it. Another user found the same plugin enabled in GitHub Copilot's settings. One note of care: the reporter explicitly says this shows unwanted integration, not data exfiltration, so the Hacker News headline about sharing all your API data goes further than the evidence. A reply posted in Postman's voice said bundling was meant to make setup easier, and promised clearer opt-in "especially for existing users," which other commenters noted is still not opt-in.

The technical comment that matters came from an account other commenters suspected was a bot, so weigh it accordingly, but it showed its work. It tested both hosts. Codex does gate hooks with a trust record, but that record is a hash any process running as you can compute, and it minted valid ones with a text editor. In Claude Code, hooks in a project settings file fired in a non-interactive run, in a folder that had never been trusted. The conclusion: consent stored in a file your own processes can write is not really consent. It needs to live outside the agent's file access. Pair that with a smaller report the same day, a user saying OpenAI's model-training toggle for Codex had silently flipped back on, and the convergence is this: the settings that govern your agent don't stay where you left them.

The third thread is a quieter piece by Nicholas Carlini, asking whether a modern chess engine on a nineteen-ninety-six Pentium Pro could have beaten Kasparov without Deep Blue. He bought a two-hundred-megahertz machine with sixty-four megabytes of RAM and had language models, Fable five point one, Opus five point five and GPT-six, port Stockfish to it and drag the nineteen-ninety-six engine Crafty forward thirty years. The ported Stockfish lands at an estimated thirty-three hundred Elo at classical time controls, against Kasparov's twenty-eight hundred. In a hundred-game match, the modern engine on the Pentium beat the nineteen-ninety-six engine running on a modern Ryzen seventy-eight wins, seventeen draws, five losses. By his accounting, seven hundred twenty of the thirteen hundred thirty-five Elo gained since then came from better programming, not faster hardware. The honest caveat, which Carlini states himself, is that training the neural evaluation function would have taken about four thousand nineteen-ninety-six core-years, so he trained it on modern hardware. Clever software still borrowed from the future's compute. His closing question is the right one: will the software advances of the next few years outpace the hardware?

Money moves. Nikkei Asia reported overnight that US tech giants' off-balance-sheet AI obligations, meaning leases, purchase commitments and power contracts, have nearly doubled in six months to about three point two trillion dollars, reaching around eighty percent of those companies' assets. An earlier Nikkei tally put five of these companies at about one point six five trillion. We read the headline and lede only; the body is paywalled. On Hacker News, the strongest objection was that this isn't debt in the usual sense: much of it is data center leases and power payments decades out, and the buildings keep their value even if the chips don't. That's true, and it's also the point. These are commitments signed to lock in supply, and you'll hear why in a minute.

The other story is Revolut. At an AI Engineer talk, written up on October twenty-ninth and turned into a viral post on X this week, David Abitbol described moving about two thousand engineers off metered frontier APIs onto self-hosted GLM four point five, on fifty-six Nvidia B two hundreds, at roughly thirty cents per million tokens, about a tenth of what they had paid Anthropic and OpenAI. They report coding time improving about sixty percent and lead time seventy percent. Two details matter more than the price. First, earlier Claude Code with strict budget caps showed little impact. The gains arrived when tokens stopped being rationed and engineers pointed the model at everything, including inbox triage and meetings. Second, out of the box the inference was terrible; tuning context limits, quantization and parallelism gave eighty-eight percent more performance with no new GPUs. Treat the figures with care: they come from one talk and exclude staffing and cloud costs. But the signal is that, for a big enough customer, the frontier premium buys less than unlimited access to a good-enough model on hardware you own.

The deep cut is Dwarkesh Patel's episode from October twenty-second, a two-day tour of Intel's most advanced fab, and the argument chain comes from Intel CTO Pushkar Ranade.

It starts with Dwarkesh's premise: AI compute has grown about three times a year for a decade. Then the constraint. ASML makes fewer than a hundred EUV lithography tools a year, split between Intel, TSMC and the memory makers. Ranade says EUV isn't the only bottleneck; nearly every equipment category is short, and once you lock into a process flow, you can't swap tools: "we're locked into that one tool, one supplier." Yields are already high, so there's no slack in the existing fleet. So when Dwarkesh asks whether there are enough tools to ten X leading-edge wafers: "Not 10X, but probably 2X, yes." And to his follow-up that three X a year can't continue: "Unless the entire supply chain — starting from the equipment suppliers, their suppliers, their sub-suppliers — unless everybody produces more, you can't increase the output."

The next step is where the money goes. Dwarkesh estimates that a couple of billion dollars of Intel capex yields enough logic for something like a hundred billion dollars a year of AI lab revenue, so the labs should eventually say, we'll buy everything, we'll guarantee the income. Ranade says there are early signs of that already, and names the shift: "we are moving from a horizontal world again to a pseudo-vertical world... kind of virtually vertically integrated." That, I'd argue, is what Nikkei's three point two trillion is: virtual vertical integration, written down as commitments.

Then the part that's actually contrarian. Fab costs double every four to five years, and a High-NA EUV scanner with its support equipment costs around four hundred million dollars. Extrapolate that and it breaks. So Ranade asks whether the industry's own rules are gospel: "Do you really need a clean room today?" Dwarkesh suggests a test fab full of people eating hamburgers and smoking cigars, and Ranade says it would be a great experiment that no incumbent will run: "It's really the risk, the fear of the unknown. Nobody is going to willingly take that risk. That's why I think sometimes it's good to just start with a clean slate." That's the opening for Elon Musk's Terafab, which Intel is partnering on. But Dwarkesh presses the point: if the tools are the bottleneck, a new fab doesn't add silicon to the world, it just guarantees one buyer's supply.

Ollie's takeaway, for anyone building biomedical AI: don't plan on compute getting abundantly cheap on the timeline demand implies. The ceiling is two X on today's tools. What expands faster is the other side of Carlini's ledger, the software, plus the Revolut lesson that an unrationed good-enough model can beat a rationed frontier one. And the Astra story is the matching discipline for claims. When a result seems to promise ten X, ask which part of it was the two X.

The one thing to watch tomorrow: whether the Weight-Space Programming authors revise that abstract, and whether Postman's promised opt-in actually means opt-in. That's the pulse.
