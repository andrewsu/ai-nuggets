## Script

Here's what the AI world is talking about today. Three separate stories hit the front page in the last forty-eight hours, and once you line them up they turn out to be one story: almost nobody wants to pay for the best model anymore. The interesting part is that price is only the third-best explanation.

Start with the number everyone is arguing about. The Financial Times reported yesterday, working from Ramp's card and bill data across about seventy thousand companies, that spending on Fable five has never gotten past roughly eleven percent of what those companies spend on Anthropic's tools overall. That went to Hacker News and sat near five hundred points and four hundred fifty comments through the night.

Some of that eleven percent is a supply story — Fable five launched in June, got pulled at the White House's request on national security grounds, and was later allowed back. But the people quoted don't lead with that. Miles Clements at Accel, a firm that has put close to a billion dollars into the company, framed it as structural: most people don't need to operate at the frontier, and the era when customers only chose frontier models was, in his words, not a durable era. The price gap is the obvious mechanism — ten to fifty dollars per million tokens at the top tier against under a dollar for DeepSeek-class models — and the behavior it produces is model routing. Send the easy work somewhere cheap, reserve the expensive model for the calls that actually need it. There's an irony worth noting: Ramp, whose spending data is the entire basis of the story, shipped its own model router three days before the story ran, offering OpenAI, Anthropic, DeepSeek, Moonshot and Z-dot-A-I through one interface as though they were interchangeable parts. The company measuring the flight from premium models also sells the plumbing for it. And the timing matters, because a public offering is expected within weeks at a valuation of at least two trillion dollars.

Now the part the FT didn't write, which is what the practitioners in that thread actually said. Almost none of it was about capability. The top comment argued that the company treated monetization the way it treats model training — as something to run experiments on — and that this is exactly wrong for a utility. The line quoted downthread all night was that most people want to not care, that we want our AI like electricity, just there regardless of how hard the supply is. Under it, dozens of variations on quota resets, expiring promotions, policies that flip and quietly flip back. And then the strategic observation, which I think is the real one: switching providers now costs one command in a terminal. If your product depends on people not opening another tab, your 护城河 — hùchénghé, your moat — is effectively zero.

Two counterweights, because the thread supplied them itself. Cheap consumer tiers were never a business; they're a developer-acquisition funnel, and the discount providers everyone is celebrating will ratchet their own pricing the moment they have the attention. And the boring explanation is probably closest to true: not having enough GPUs during a period of absurd growth reads, from the outside, as contempt.

Second story, and this is the one that moved my priors. Six hundred fifty points for a writeup of someone rooting a five-year-old Amazon Fire tablet. Five months with Claude on conventional approaches, all of which died on protected packages holding reboot permissions. Then Kimi K-three found an unpatched Mali GPU use-after-free specific to that exact firmware and built the whole toolkit over about thirty hours and six hundred twenty-one messages, for a hundred sixty-four dollars — then dead-ended on kernel memory coherency after five hundred-plus failed attempts. GLM-five-point-two, twenty-two dollars, diagnosed the design flaw but concluded the remaining barrier was a hardware limit, and ChatGPT agreed and declined to suggest a way around it. GLM-five-point-three found the actual cause — kernel relocation offsets differing between the over-the-air image and the build actually on the device — and had root in eight hours and five minutes. Total model spend, two hundred sixty-six dollars, on a hundred-fourteen-dollar tablet.

And the same day, three hundred forty points: Qwen three-point-eight twenty-seven-B, eight-bit, on a single Grace Blackwell desktop with a hundred twenty-eight gigabytes of unified memory, about fifty tokens a second. Pure static analysis of thousands of lines of ARM sixty-four. It recovered a deliberately obfuscated RSA verification key, and when its first attempt came back wrong it flagged its own mismatch and kept going until the bytes matched. Thirty minutes, one machine, nobody's permission required.

Both authors are more honest than their headlines. The tablet author calls himself a prompt kiddie and says what mattered was his own judgment about when to abandon an approach and which model to hand it to. Two anecdotes, not a benchmark. But hold them next to the eleven percent and you get the axis nobody is pricing. The most upvoted read on that thread was blunt: the Chinese models did the work, and the American ones fell back to their safeguards. Someone corroborated it independently downthread — they got Linux booting on an Apple M-four Mac, and the remaining hardware support needs tracing macOS under a hypervisor, which Fable refuses to do. That is not a capability gap, it's a policy gap, and it is now a competitive variable on legitimate technical work. Remember that asymmetry; it comes back at the end of the show as a security vulnerability.

Quick third item, at desk scale. Fabien Sanglard's agent-dot-em-dee post, three hundred six points, is a plain account of concluding that model tier was never his bottleneck. He froze the feedback he kept repeating in review into a persistent instruction file, and says it moved him from arguing about style to arguing about architecture. Same story as the routing data: the returns are in the scaffold, not the tier.

To the money, where the same idea has been packaged and funded. Inherent came out of stealth on Friday in London with a fifty-million-dollar seed round, founded by DeepMind alumni — Edward Hughes as chief scientist, with Louis Kirsch, Kaloyan Aleksiev and Tantum Collins. They built a task space called Replica: three hundred ten tasks drawn from a hundred machine learning and AI-for-science papers, where the agent has to reproduce a figure under a time and compute budget without ever seeing the original plot. Then they post-trained Faraday, a twenty-seven-billion-parameter agent, with long-horizon reinforcement learning on it. They report it beating Claude Opus four-point-eight in Claude Code and GPT-five-point-five in Codex, both at extra-high thinking effort, on held-out domains — widest margins in meta-learning, structural biology and materials science.

The architecture is the whole point. Faraday uses GPT-five-point-five Codex as a tool — a twenty-seven-billion-parameter model directing a model orders of magnitude larger, and it generalizes upward: trained against a smaller coding agent, it adapts to directing the bigger one at test time. Their framing is the sentence I'd underline. As frontier coding agents keep advancing, the value of scientific judgment goes up, not down.

For a working scientist the reward design is more interesting than the leaderboard. Reproducing a plot is not the same as replicating a paper — you also need defensible experimental design, faithfulness to the original claims, and sane use of the compute you were given. So the judge is a language model with auto-generated per-task rubrics, validated against human raters by rank correlation. And their argument for why replication is a good curriculum is genuinely nice: papers report what worked, not the negative results that got there, so the agent has to reconstruct the ninety-nine percent perspiration that never appears on the page.

The caveat is structural: their benchmark, their judge, their choice of baseline harnesses, no independent verification. There is a certain recursion in a company selling research replication publishing an unreplicated result. Treat the numbers as a claim and the architecture as the finding.

One more money note. The Department of Justice is reportedly nearly a year into an investigation of Andreessen Horowitz over partners holding board seats at competing AI companies — Ben Horowitz at Databricks, Martin Casado at Fivetran. Interlocking directorates are old antitrust law; applying them where a few firms sit on everyone's board would change how AI cap tables get built.

For the deep cut, Machine Learning Street Talk published a conversation on Friday between Tim Scarfe and two researchers: Ilia Shumailov, formerly of Google DeepMind, who did his Cambridge doctorate under Ross Anderson, and Alexander Panfilov of the ELLIS Institute Tübingen. The subject is their paper on stealing reasoning traces from proprietary APIs, and the argument runs in four steps.

Step one is the design decision. Providers stopped handing back plaintext chain of thought, to protect intellectual property and limit leakage. But to avoid paying for server-side storage, they return the reasoning to the client as an encrypted block, and the client passes it back with every request. Stateless, and on its own terms elegant.

Step two is the bug. Those blocks turn out to be interchangeable — across sessions, across users, and across different models inside the same provider's family. An independent researcher disclosed the portability back in May; by that researcher's account, the providers did not acknowledge any security implications from side channels or replay.

Step three is what makes this the right story for today. The vulnerability is an asymmetry inside a model family: the frontier model is heavily safeguarded, and the small cheap sibling everybody is routing traffic to is not. Hand the strong model's encrypted reasoning to the weak sibling and ask it to transcribe what's attached, and the strong model's hidden reasoning comes back in plaintext — without ever attacking the strong model, and without tripping its anti-distillation behavior. Demonstrated across Anthropic, OpenAI and Google. The cheap sibling again, in a completely different frame.

Step four is the consequences, and there are four. Anti-distillation is circumvented, and the economics are trivial: decoding ten thousand traces at cheap-tier pricing runs about seven hundred dollars. Second, and this is the one that should worry anyone who ships agent transcripts, they scraped about six thousand seven hundred public agent trajectories off GitHub and Hugging Face and decoded roughly three hundred fifteen thousand reasoning blocks. Just under five percent of those sessions leaked at least one real sensitive item — sixty-two distinct API keys, thirty-three passwords, twenty-four access tokens, seven private keys. The recurring trigger is both funny and awful: the leak fires when the user asks the agent to clean up or anonymize the session, because the model re-reads the whole history in its hidden reasoning and restates every secret it's about to remove. Sixty-four of the recovered artifacts appear nowhere in the visible conversation at all, and users cannot sanitize what they cannot decrypt. Third, harmful content the visible answer correctly refused can still sit in the reasoning. Fourth, invisible prompt injection: plant a poisoned reasoning block in a shared trace, let a victim resume the run, and the model treats the payload as its own prior thinking. All of it was disclosed before publication, and the authors say the attacks stopped working afterward.

Two things from this I'd hand to anyone doing research with these systems. First, an incidental finding: reading decoded traces alongside the summaries the API returns, they kept catching summaries that didn't match. On one competition math problem the returned summary shows a clean law-of-cosines derivation, while the actual trace opens by stating the answer from memory and then works backward to justify it. If you cannot inspect the reasoning, the summary is your entire oversight interface. That's what it's worth.

The second is why this is the deep cut rather than just a security item. They found that prefilling four words of Opus reasoning into Kimi K-three shifts the style of its subsequent reasoning and of its visible answer toward Claude's — on one physics problem, almost the same response. That is a headline waiting to be written about 蒸馏, zhēngliú, distillation. They declined to write it. Instead they built controls: a self prefill, a sibling prefill, and no prefill; a style classifier on character n-grams, cross-validated with problems grouped so none appears on both sides; and they measured their classifier's empirical chance level rather than assuming one half. The result is careful and much less exciting. The prefill does change each model's register against its own control, strongly in several cases. But movement toward the source model's actual reasoning is tiny — for the Opus prefills, separability falls from about zero point nine nine to zero point nine seven, in three cells out of dozens. They also say explicitly that their perplexity comparison should not be read as confirming similarity between models. The episode closes on that distinction: separate the jailbreak they demonstrated from the ordinary benign distillation they did not, and prefer a controlled experiment to a sweeping claim.

Which lands where yesterday's episode landed, from a completely unrelated direction. Two independent groups this week arrived at the same discipline: measure your own noise floor before you believe your effect.

The one thing to watch tomorrow. This particular attack is patched. The structural fact underneath it isn't: your safety guarantee is only as strong as the least-guarded model in your family, and the least-guarded model is precisely where the enterprise routing data says everyone's traffic is going. Watch for anyone publishing per-model safeguard parity across a family — nobody does yet — and watch Ramp's next cut of that spending data, because eleven percent is either a floor or a trend, and one month tells you which.
