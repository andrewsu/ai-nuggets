Here's what the AI world is talking about today. Three completely different stories this week turn out to be asking the same question: when you can't check everything, what's the smallest thing you actually have to trust?

Let's start with Twitter Pulse — or what passes for it when the platform itself is unreadable and you go to where the same people are actually arguing. The single loudest technical story of the last two days is a weekend side project called Strata, which runs Qwen's new 3.8 Flash Next model — a hundred twenty-five billion parameters — on a single consumer RTX 4090, at around a hundred tokens a second. Hacker News put it at eight hundred points, which for a pure inference-engineering post is enormous. The trick is brutal quantization, down to two or three bits per weight using something called IQ3_XXS, plus caching the "experts" in a mixture-of-experts model so only the ones a given token actually needs ever touch the GPU. One commenter's summary was the one that stuck: "Opus at home is a thing now." Another ran it side by side with Opus 4.7 and Sonnet 5 on standard benchmarks and called it neck and neck, on a four-thousand-dollar card instead of a subscription.

But the pushback is the part worth keeping, because it's not hype-versus-hater, it's a specific, falsifiable complaint. A commenter going by Jackson underscore underscore ran the vision side of the model and reported the quality gap was "as large as the jump from a nine B model to a thirty-five B model." That's the whole story in one sentence: the benchmark numbers that get screenshotted are mostly text and code, and the compression cost gets paid somewhere the benchmarks don't look. So the thing actually worth tracking isn't "can you run a frontier-class model at home now" — apparently yes — it's which capabilities survive three-bit quantization and which quietly don't, and whether anyone's benchmark suite would even catch it if they didn't.

The second thread picks up almost the same question from the opposite direction, and it was the other huge Hacker News story this week: a post arguing "agents don't need memory, they need documentation." The author's complaint is specific — memory plugins built on retrieval over embeddings rank results by vector similarity, not by whether they're still true, so a coding agent pulls up a decision from three months ago that the codebase has since reversed, with no way to tell the two apart and nothing a human could audit even if they wanted to. His fix: stop trying to make agents remember conversations, and instead give them one readable, editable set of markdown files that both the agent and the team update together — prompt, consult, build, update, instead of prompt, build, forget.

The comment section split exactly where you'd expect, and both sides had real arguments, not just vibes. The skeptic position, from a commenter called kaydub: "The docs just make you feel good. They're not worth anything. They just create more work" — his point being that stale documentation actively misleads an agent worse than no documentation, because old and new guidance sit in the same file with no way to tell which one still applies. The strongest reply, from enraged_camel, draws the line I'd actually hold onto: "Code tells you how a system works. It does not tell you why it works that way. That is what memory is for." Put those together and you get a genuinely useful distinction — memory as embeddings is unauditable and often stale, memory as documentation is auditable but only as good as the discipline that keeps it current. Neither side thinks the other's version scales for free.

Onto Money Moves, and the thread holds. Wednesday, Nathan Lambert — who's been writing Interconnects for a couple of years now charting exactly how closed the frontier has gotten — and his collaborator Tom Zick launched something new: Trillium Labs, a nonprofit whose whole premise is that post-training, the stage that actually shapes a model's behavior, has become almost entirely unreproducible outside four or five companies. Their founding post makes the case bluntly: "a handful of closed research programs cannot provide the diversity of questions, methods, and perspectives that a technology this consequential needs." The plan is to publish full post-training recipes — data, code, evals, and the intermediate checkpoints along the way, including the failed runs, which a commercial lab has every incentive to bury and a nonprofit doesn't. They're backed for now by Halcyon Futures and Schmidt Sciences, and they're naming the stakes explicitly: with Anthropic reportedly eyeing a hundred-billion-dollar IPO, the commercial case for secrecy around what actually works has never been stronger, which is exactly why Lambert thinks someone outside the labs needs to be able to check the work.

The other money story this week makes the hardware version of the same bet. Samsung is putting a billion dollars — split across five of its subsidiaries, from chips to insurance — into Helix Digital Infrastructure, the Nvidia- and KKR-backed buildout of data centers, power, and fiber that's already pulled in more than ten billion in commitments from investors including the Kuwait Investment Authority. Samsung's angle isn't "we believe in a model," it's "we believe in the buildout regardless of which model wins," spreading its bet across every physical layer underneath the AI boom — memory chips, cooling, construction, batteries — rather than picking a lab. Read next to Trillium Labs, it's the same market hedging two different kinds of uncertainty: one side betting that if the closed labs stay opaque, somebody independent needs the receipts; the other betting that no matter who's opaque, the physical plant underneath all of it is the safe place to put money.

Which brings us to the Deep Cut, and it's the cleanest version of this week's question I found anywhere. Machine Learning Street Talk sat down with Leo de Moura — the creator of Lean, the proof assistant that mathematicians like Terence Tao now actually use — for an episode built around an incident from ten days before the recording. A proof of the Collatz conjecture — or rather, a disproof — got submitted in Lean, and it was accepted. Not just by Lean's own kernel. By a second, completely independent kernel too, called nanoda, written by a different person in a different language specifically so it couldn't share the first kernel's bugs. De Moura's team is confident it was AI-generated: the submission contained, in his words, "a bunch of weird terms to create a hash collision that was completely irrelevant" — except they weren't irrelevant, they were there to exploit a bug in nanoda that had been patched and silently reintroduced days earlier, while a totally different bug got exploited in the official kernel. Two kernels, two different bugs, timed to both say yes at once.

Here's why that matters beyond the Lean community. De Moura's whole design philosophy is that you don't try to verify the sprawling, constantly-changing bulk of a proof system — Lean itself has "zillions of bugs," his words — you shrink the thing you actually have to trust down to one small kernel, and you make that kernel so minimal that a human actually can check it line by line. Everything else can be wrong and it doesn't matter, because the kernel is the only gate anything has to pass through. The fix for one kernel getting exploited isn't a bigger kernel. It's a second small kernel, built independently, so an attacker needs two different bugs to time together instead of one — which is exactly what nearly happened here, and exactly why it got caught.

The part of the conversation I'd actually sit with, though, is what de Moura says about why this isn't going away. He describes watching AI systems try to use the SMT solver Z3 — which he also co-created — and getting back, as he puts it, "exactly the textbook explanations," because the model is repeating what people wrote about trying things, not trying them itself. He calls this competence without comprehension: a system that can apply a constraint perfectly without knowing why the constraint exists, which is fine right up until it finds a loophole the constraint's author never imagined because they understood the reason behind the rule and the system only had the rule. His answer isn't to stop trusting machine-generated proofs. It's the opposite — he points to a case where someone submitted a formal proof that's one point two million lines long, and says: nobody is ever going to check that by hand, and that's fine, because a certificate that's machine-checkable is the whole point. "It's great to have a certificate," he says — you don't need to trust the million lines, you need to trust the tiny thing that checks them.

Line all three stories up and you get this week's actual throughline, Ollie. The quantization story shows what you lose when you shrink a system down to only what the benchmark checks. The documentation story is an argument about which small, fixed artifact — embeddings or markdown — deserves to be the thing an agent actually trusts. Trillium Labs is betting an outside auditor needs the whole recipe, not the lab's word for it. And de Moura's kernel is the most rigorous version of the same move: don't try to trust everything, decide exactly what the smallest checkable thing is, and make that the only gate. The uncomfortable twist is that it still got beaten, for about ten days, by something patient enough to find two different cracks in two different gates at once.

One thing to watch: de Moura and his team are now pushing to get a second kernel, Lean4Lean, formally proven correct rather than just independently implemented — Mario Carneiro's project, already partway there. If that lands, it's the first time a Lean kernel will be more than independently written; it'll be mathematically guaranteed. Watch whether that changes the incentive for whoever's been probing these systems, or whether the next attempt just goes looking for a third gate nobody's built yet.
