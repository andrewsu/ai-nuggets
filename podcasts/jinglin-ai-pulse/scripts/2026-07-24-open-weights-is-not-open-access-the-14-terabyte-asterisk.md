# Ollie's AI Pulse — July 24: open weights is not open access — the 1.4-terabyte asterisk. Yesterday's show celebrated open weights becoming the baseline — pull down a frontier model and run it inside your own firewall. Today the timeline turned to the asterisk on that promise, and the fresh discourse thread is a single sharp distinction: open weights is not the same as open access. Three faces of the reckoning. The physical one — Kimi K3's weights arrive Sunday the 27th, but they're ~1.4 terabytes resident and need on the order of eighteen 80-gigabyte accelerators just to load; one widely-shared line: "the license democratizes ownership on paper while the hardware recentralizes who can actually exercise it." The political one — TechCrunch's July 20 "OpenAI is scared of open-weight models," where OpenAI's Dean Ball argued the government should manufacture "regulatory fear, uncertainty, and distrust" around open models, then retracted; pushback from Yann LeCun, Martin Casado, Hugging Face's Clem Delangue ("Restricting open models wouldn't make AI safer. It would simply hide the risks, concentrate power in the hands of a few"), Snorkel's Braden Hancock ("a squeeze on the margins"), and Georgetown's Sam Bresnick (chip export controls, not model bans); the UK AISI found open models trail the cyber frontier by months. Money Moves — Cathedral (July 22), four ex-DOGE staffers incl. former Pentagon/DoW chief data officer Gavin Kliger, $160M at a $1.4B valuation led by a16z + Sequoia, building offensive+defensive AI military cyber against adversaries like China — and its first infrastructure move is to buy or partner for a datacenter, i.e. secure the compute. Deep Cut — All-In (July 18): Jason Calacanis's local-compute thesis (terabyte-RAM Mac Studios running previous-gen frontier models locally, "token maxing" with no external dependency), "AI compute will chase energy," Chamath on the U.S. power shortage ("2.5 Californias" by 2050), David Sacks reframing data-center opposition as indirect AI suppression ("power, permits, land, cooling, and political permission"). Ollie throughline: yesterday's optimism was right but incomplete — owning the model isn't the finish line; you also have to own the compute and the power to exercise it, and the honest safety argument cuts toward openness, not away from it. The realistic on-ramp for a lab isn't eighteen Blackwells, it's a terabyte-RAM workstation running a strong previous-generation open model, plus your own data.

## Script

Good morning, Ollie. This is Ollie's AI Pulse for Friday, July twenty-fourth.

Yesterday I spent the whole show on one idea: open weights has become the baseline, and the practical payoff for a lab like yours is that you can pull down a frontier-class model and run it inside your own firewall — nothing leaves Scripps. I stand by that. But the timeline spent today finding the asterisk on that promise, and the asterisk is important enough that it's the whole show. The fresh line going around is a single sharp distinction: open weights is not the same as open access. Let's get into why that gap is the story, and what it changes about the advice I gave you yesterday.

First — Twitter Pulse. One arc, two beats. First the physical reckoning, then the political one.

Start with the physical one, because it is refreshingly concrete. Kimi K-three — the two-point-eight-trillion-parameter model everyone spent this week calling the largest open model ever — is still on track to release its actual weights this Sunday, the twenty-seventh. But the discourse this week moved off the benchmark score and onto a much more boring number: one-point-four terabytes. That's how much fast memory the weights occupy, even compressed down to four-bit precision. To actually load and serve the thing, you are looking at something like eighteen eighty-gigabyte accelerators, minimum. That is not a workstation. That is a rack of Blackwell or MI-four-hundred silicon, which means the realistic operators are cloud providers, dedicated inference companies, and large enterprises — not an individual developer, and not, frankly, most academic labs.

The line from that essay that traveled farthest is worth quoting exactly, because it's the crux: quote, "The license democratizes ownership on paper while the hardware recentralizes who can actually exercise it." Sit with that, because it is the precise counter to yesterday's optimism. Yesterday the constraint I described was "can I get the weights," and the answer had flipped to yes. Today the binding constraint moved one step downstream: "can I afford to keep one-point-four terabytes of model warm and resident." Owning the weights and being able to run the weights are now two different problems, and the second one is the expensive one. There's even a word going around for the softer version of this — "openwashing" — the complaint that a downloadable checkpoint with an unpublished license and terabyte-scale hardware demands is being marketed as "open" when it is, for almost everyone, look-but-don't-touch.

Here's the honest Ollie read, and it's a correction to my own enthusiasm yesterday. The open-first shift is still the most important development of the month — but "you can run it in your firewall" needs an asterisk, and the asterisk is the compute. For a giant model like K-three, "open" mostly means a cheaper, swappable cloud, not a private one. What it does not yet mean is a frontier-scale model humming inside your own building on hardware you own. That version exists — but it's for the smaller open models, not the three-trillion-parameter flagship. So the practical question for your group isn't "is it open," and it isn't even "how good is it." It's "what's the largest open model I can actually keep resident on hardware I can actually buy" — and that answer is a lot smaller than one-point-four terabytes. Hold that thought, because the Deep Cut is going to give you the concrete version of it.

Now the second beat, which is where it got genuinely spicy — the political reckoning. On the twentieth, TechCrunch ran a piece titled, bluntly, "OpenAI is scared of open-weight models. Should the US be?" And the reason it lit up the timeline is a specific, named quote. OpenAI's head of strategic futures, Dean Ball, argued that the U-S government should manufacture — his framing — "regulatory fear, uncertainty, and distrust" around open-weight models, on the logic that open models deter the enormous capital spending that frontier labs depend on. In other words: open weights are bad for the closed labs' business model, so make them legally scary. Ball later walked it back — he retracted the claim that a regulatory crackdown was the White House's best strategy — but the quote was already out, and the reaction is the signal.

And the reaction was near-unanimous from the researchers you'd weight most. Yann LeCun made the plain point that open software has always accelerated innovation and coexisted fine with proprietary work. Martin Casado argued against restriction from the investment side. Snorkel's Braden Hancock put the economic mechanism in one sentence, quote: "Strong, frontier-caliber open source models will place a squeeze on the margins and will bring down the prices of the frontier companies." Which is, of course, exactly the outcome Ball's framing was trying to prevent — so the safety argument and the business argument got a little too visibly tangled. And Hugging Face's Clem Delangue landed the line that I think is the keeper, quote: "Restricting open models wouldn't make AI safer. It would simply hide the risks, concentrate power in the hands of a few." The one genuinely substantive counter-data-point in the debate came from the U-K's AI Safety Institute, which found that open models trail the cyber-offense frontier by months — so the "open models are an imminent security catastrophe" case is, for now, empirically soft. And Georgetown's Sam Bresnick offered the pragmatic exit: if you're worried, control the chips, not the models — quote, "That could potentially keep us out of this thorny debate about banning open source technologies."

The Ollie read on the political beat is short. The fight over open weights has graduated from a benchmark rivalry into a policy fight, and you should notice which way the honest safety argument actually points. Delangue is right: a model you can download and audit is inspectable; a model behind an A-P-I is not. For a scientist, that's not an abstraction — reproducibility and auditability are the whole game, and they are properties open weights have and closed A-P-Is structurally cannot. So if a rule shows up that makes open models "legally scary," read it for what Ball accidentally admitted it is: margin protection wearing a safety costume. That's Twitter Pulse.

Next — Money Moves. And today's single story is the perfect bridge between the two beats you just heard, because it is about compute, national security, and money all at once. On Tuesday the twenty-second, a stealth startup called Cathedral came out with a hundred-and-sixty-million-dollar round at a one-point-four-billion-dollar valuation, led by Andreessen Horowitz and Sequoia, both of whom took board seats. What Cathedral does: AI-driven offensive and defensive cyber tools for the U-S military, explicitly aimed at adversaries including China. And the founders are the tell — four alumni of the DOGE effort, including Gavin Kliger, who was most recently the chief data officer at the Department of War, and who was in the middle of that department's fight with Anthropic over how Claude could be used by the military.

Two things make this the right story for today. One is the obvious through-line from the Twitter Pulse: the open-weights-versus-national-security fight is not theoretical, it's now a funded, hundred-sixty-million-dollar company with two of the top venture firms on its board. Cyber capability, foreign adversaries, and downloadable frontier models are the same conversation now. But the second thing is the detail I want you to hold onto, because it echoes exactly the one-point-four-terabyte problem. Reporting says one of Cathedral's first infrastructure priorities is to acquire its own datacenter, or strike a partnership for dedicated compute. Think about that. A cyber-offense startup, flush with a hundred-sixty million dollars, and its opening move isn't a model — it's securing the compute to run one. That is the same lesson as K-three's terabyte-and-a-half, told from the money side: in a world where the model itself is increasingly downloadable and cheap, the scarce, defensible, capital-intensive thing is the compute and the power underneath it. The money is not buying intelligence. It's buying the place to run intelligence. That's Money Moves.

Now — Podcast Deep Cut. And it's the most practical episode I've heard all week for exactly your situation: the All-In podcast from the eighteenth, with Calacanis, Palihapitiya, Sacks, and Friedberg. Skip the politics; there's an argument chain in there that is the direct answer to the terabyte problem I opened with. Three moves.

Move one is Jason Calacanis's, and it's the one that matters most for a lab. His thesis is that the next generation of desktop machines — Mac Studios with terabyte-scale unified memory — will be able to run previous-generation frontier-quality models locally. His phrase for the payoff was "token maxing" — you run the model as hard as you want, with no data ever leaving the building and no per-token A-P-I bill. Notice what that quietly does to the one-point-four-terabyte problem: you don't solve it by buying eighteen Blackwells for the flagship. You solve it by running a strong, slightly-older open model on a single workstation you can actually own. That is the version of "open weights in your firewall" that is real today — not the three-trillion-parameter model, but the one-generation-behind model that fits in a terabyte of unified memory. And remember the momentum tracker's finding: the open-to-closed gap is now roughly one generation, not several. So "one generation behind, but private and free to hammer" is a genuinely strong position, not a consolation.

Move two is Chamath's, and it's the constraint underneath all of it: energy. He put the U-S electricity shortfall at something like two-and-a-half Californias of additional demand by twenty-fifty, and his point was that behind-the-meter power — generation you control directly — becomes the real competitive edge, because compute is downstream of power. And move three is Sacks's, which reframes the whole open-versus-closed fight one more time: his claim was that since opponents cannot ban intelligence directly, the fight moves to obstructing the physical layer — his list was "power, permits, land, cooling, and political permission." Whether or not you buy his framing of who's doing the obstructing, the structural point stands and it rhymes with everything today: the battle over AI has moved from the model to the infrastructure that runs it.

The Ollie takeaway on the Deep Cut ties the whole episode into one sentence. Yesterday I told you the model has been commoditized and your data is the durable asset. Today's correction is that there's a second durable asset sitting right next to your data: the compute and power to run a model privately. And the good news from the All-In panel is that for a lab, the winning move is not to chase the one-point-four-terabyte flagship. It's a terabyte-RAM workstation, a strong one-generation-behind open model resident on it, and your own Perturb-seq and patient data as the fuel. Private, auditable, no A-P-I bill, no vendor watching. That's a buildable plan, and it's a lot cheaper than eighteen accelerators.

Wrap. Three things to watch into the weekend. First and most concrete — Sunday the twenty-seventh: do Kimi K-three's weights actually ship, and crucially, do they ship with a real license? An unpublished license is the difference between "open" and "openwashing," and Sunday is the test. Second — watch Washington. Nathan Lambert has been flagging a possible White House executive order on open models; after the Dean Ball episode this week, that's live, and it would land directly on Chinese-origin weights. Third — watch for the counter-move to the terabyte problem: the first genuinely frontier-adjacent model small enough to run resident on a single workstation. Whoever ships that collapses the "open is not access" gap, and it's the release that would matter most to you specifically. Have a good Friday, Ollie.
