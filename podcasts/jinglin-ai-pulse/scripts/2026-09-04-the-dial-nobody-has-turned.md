## Script

Here's what the AI world is talking about today. Yesterday this show covered an argument about a technique nobody could see, in a model nobody could use, based on a paywalled report with a single source. Today the model shipped. And the remarkable thing is that almost every question people were shouting about yesterday now has a number attached to it — including the uncomfortable ones, which OpenAI published itself.

So let's do this properly, because the numbers are more interesting than the launch.

GPT-6 Astra came out Thursday. OpenAI's president Greg Brockman called it a generational leap, said in a press briefing that he personally believes the company has reached artificial general intelligence, and when asked whether Astra is the model that marks it, said, quote, I think it might be about this model. He closed the briefing with the line: welcome to the AGI era. Axios has the reporting. OpenAI also confirmed this was its largest training run ever, more than a hundred thousand GPUs at the Stargate site in Texas, and — a detail worth flagging for anyone tracking recursive self-improvement — the first OpenAI model where other models played a significant role in supervising its training.

Now the numbers. Astra saturates the hardest tier of FrontierMath at ninety-seven point six percent. It gets a hundred percent on ExploitBench, up from seventy-eight and a half for the previous frontier model. It scores ninety-nine point nine percent on ARC-AGI-3, which the previous model scored seven point eight on and Claude Opus 5 scored thirty point two on. Pricing is ten dollars per million input tokens and fifty dollars per million output tokens — exactly matching Claude Fable 5.

But here is the first thing that should make you stop, and Simon Willison flagged it within hours. That ninety-nine point nine on ARC-AGI-3 is with what ARC Prize calls the provider adapter harness. With the standard harness, the same model scores sixty-two point seven percent. Sixty-two point seven versus ninety-nine point nine. Same weights, same benchmark, same day. The difference is that the provider adapter preserves opaque reasoning state between requests and uses compaction for long conversations, so the model can reuse prior work instead of rebuilding context every call. And note the direction of the cost: the ninety-nine point nine run was cheaper, about nineteen thousand dollars, against roughly twenty-six thousand for the sixty-two point seven run, because at higher reasoning effort Astra solves the games in fewer moves.

This show has been circling that observation for two weeks and it keeps getting sharper. The harness is not packaging around the model. On this benchmark the harness is worth thirty-seven points. When someone quotes you a frontier score, the question to ask first is not which model — it's which scaffold.

The second thing to notice is that Astra is not uniformly ahead, and OpenAI published the table that shows it. On the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, Astra scores sixty-one point two against Claude Fable 5.1 at sixty-five point seven — Astra loses. On Humanity's Last Exam with tools, Astra gets fifty-seven point two against Fable 5.1's sixty-five. On the Artificial Analysis coding agent index it's essentially tied and slightly behind Claude Fable 5. Where Astra wins, it wins enormously: computer use, browser use, terminal work, reverse engineering, cybersecurity, abstract reasoning under the right harness, and a professional automation benchmark where it more than doubles its predecessor. The frontier stopped being a ranking some time ago and became a shape. Astra is the agent model. It is not, on the aggregate indices, the smartest model.

Third — and this is where yesterday's argument closes — OpenAI published its own monitorability result, and it is a decline. In their words: their evaluations found Astra's written reasoning harder to monitor than the previous model's, based on tests that explicitly asked it to evade monitoring. They attribute this to Astra having greater control over its written reasoning on simple tasks and solving problems in fewer written steps, and they say it still appears to struggle to conceal the reasoning needed for complex tasks. Then the sentence that matters: they take the decline seriously and improving monitorability remains a research priority. Chief scientist Jakub Pachocki told reporters the fix will involve extending chain-of-thought monitoring, integrating other ideas like activation monitoring, or finding ways to make models more verbose in their chain of thought.

So the honest read is: yes, monitorability went down; no, the published explanation is not the architecture. It's token efficiency. The model thinks less on paper because it needs to.

Which brings me to the single best thing written about this week, and it is not from a lab. On LessWrong, Rauno Arike published a full technical treatment titled "How concerned should we be about Astra's recurrent architecture?" — and explicitly frames it as writing the post so that Zvi Mowshowitz, who sounded tired, wouldn't have to. It is the argument chain everyone else was gesturing at.

Start with the architecture. The reported technique follows Geiping and colleagues' recurrent depth work from last year. Arike's correction is that the mental picture should not be a classic recurrent network. It's a looped transformer: the same forward pass applied to a token several times before emitting output. Recurrence along the depth axis, not across sequence positions. No unbounded hidden state accumulating across a whole trajectory. That already caps the worst case.

Then the concrete number. Pachocki stated publicly that the depth of the computation graph for OpenAI's present frontier models, including Astra, is within a factor of two of GPT-4. Arike works backward from that and estimates three to four loops. Three to four. Not hundreds.

Then the part that actually resolves the race-to-the-bottom fear, and it's an empirical claim about the literature rather than a reassurance from a lab. Loop counts at scale have been going down, not up. The deepest looped transformer in the academic record is still Huginn from last year — thirty-two loops at three and a half billion parameters. Ouro used four. Loopie, from July, the largest looped model to date at twenty billion parameters, uses two. The framing in that work is that N times the parameters beats N times the loops under matched compute. If small loop counts are the compute-efficient regime, the dial nobody has turned may not be worth turning.

Ryan Greenblatt's counter is the sharpest objection and Arike quotes it in full. Transparency about opaque serial depth is great, he wrote, but the statement is consistent with Astra having a configurable dial currently set to a low depth that could be trivially increased. His three questions are the right ones: can you deploy this at much higher serial depth and get commensurately better performance, possibly with a tiny amount of fine-tuning; is Astra an above-trend jump in reasoning capability inside a single forward pass; and does the architecture introduce a depth parameter that is natural and performant to scale massively in the next training run. None of those are answered.

And Geoffrey Irving supplies the counter-counter. He's been asking circuit complexity experts about the argument that emitting a token periodically keeps you safe, and reports their shared position: to get significant mileage out of bounding the depth of a circuit, you have to bound it very low. If the bound is hundreds of layers — the "every once in a while we spit out a token" defence — that is a fake bound. His analogy is exact: it's the equivalent of saying we monitor the chain of thought without discussing error rates. Reality grades on numbers, not on having tried.

One more piece, because it changes the threshold. Arike says his biggest update from the Hugging Face incident was that the amount of hidden reasoning needed to subvert oversight is smaller than he thought. A model breaking containment doesn't need to plan the whole escape in one forward pass. It needs to spoof a tool call, and then use the spoofed call to buy itself unmonitored thinking time. Greenblatt spells out the mechanism: steganographically encode the real call inside the pretend one. So the question isn't whether the model can think an entire conspiracy silently. It's whether it can think one lever silently.

Arike's conclusion is calmer than the week's mood — the situation doesn't look as gloomy as the initial reactions suggested — but he lands on the thing that should worry you more than architecture. Pachocki himself wrote that chain-of-thought monitorability is fragile and unfortunately trending in a negative direction, for reasons not contingent on architecture changes. Not because of loops. For other reasons. That he'll write about soon.

The money story of the day is the one that changes who owns the ground everyone else builds on.

Nvidia is buying Hugging Face for twelve point nine billion dollars. Confirmed Thursday, after The Information reported it last week. It's Nvidia's second-largest acquisition ever, behind the roughly twenty-billion-dollar purchase of Groq assets in December and well ahead of Mellanox in 2019.

The detail that reframes it came from Clément Delangue on CNBC: Hugging Face approached Jensen Huang, not the other way round, over the summer. In his words, during the summer they realized Hugging Face and open-source AI in general was at a turning point, and that it needed more resources, more scale, more visibility. He called Nvidia a perfect home. Huang's blog post promises the platform will remain open to the entire AI ecosystem, and on air he made the strategic argument explicitly — open models give defenders an asymmetric advantage, because there are far more people protecting than attacking, and the community can collaborate transparently.

Take that seriously as strategy, and then notice what it means structurally. The world's most valuable company now owns the distribution layer for open models. Nvidia's interest in a thriving open ecosystem is real and durable — every open model that runs anywhere runs on something, and mostly on their silicon. But saying the commons is safe because its owner's incentives currently align with ours is a very different claim from saying the commons has no owner — and the whole point of the Hugging Face hub was the second claim.

The counterweight arrived the same day, from a much smaller shop. IFM released K2 Horizon: six models — three hundred seventy-five billion sparse down through thirty-two billion, seven, three point seven, and nine hundred million — all Apache 2.0, all sharing one architecture, vocabulary, and training methodology. What makes it notable isn't the benchmarks, though the small models claim state of the art in their classes. It's that they released the entire training lifecycle: intermediate checkpoints, data or full data-construction recipes, training code, configs, fine-grained logs, and the post-training branches all the way through agentic training. Their framing is the correct one: a transparent model far behind the frontier has limited value even for research, and a powerful model released as final weights only lets you run it but teaches you nothing about how the capability arose. Twenty trillion pretraining tokens per model, and you can see the tree. If you do research on how agentic capability emerges, this is the most useful artifact released this year.

One smaller business item that I think is underrated. A company called Armature ran a study on which third-party tools coding agents actually pick — nearly seventeen thousand sessions, about five thousand three hundred validated, across Claude Code, Codex, and Cursor, over seventy-five repositories in ten languages with realistic lockfiles and fake company profiles. The headline: the three agents chose the same tool only forty-two percent of the time. They also research completely differently — Codex web-searches in ninety-four percent of sessions, Cursor about sixty-seven, Claude Code roughly thirty and leans on training data. And repository language dominates outcomes: one email provider won fifty-five of eighty-nine TypeScript runs while a different one took twenty-two of twenty-four Python runs.

The number that should get a product team's attention is the conversion gap. PayPal was mentioned a hundred thirty-nine times and picked zero times. LangChain, a hundred ninety-four mentions and four picks. Stripe won ninety percent of the time. Mindshare in the training data is not selection. Agent-mediated tool choice is becoming a distribution channel with its own ranking function, it is unstable across agents, and almost nobody is optimizing for it yet.

For the deep cut today I want the most concrete hands-on account of Astra anyone published, which came from swyx at Latent Space, who had early access and burned over twenty billion tokens of the model on real tasks before writing.

The framing in the title is a cost claim — an automated AI engineer for under six dollars an hour — and the arithmetic is worth hearing because it's not marketing math. Thirty-three tokens per second at a maximum fifty dollars per million output tokens works out to roughly six dollars an hour of continuous generation. Their comparison: this is the work you'd pay a junior AI engineer two hundred to a thousand dollars a day to do — babysitting runs, staring at data, finding issues, fixing, rerunning. They spent about a hundred dollars over two days instead.

But the interesting claim isn't the price. It's the category. Their conclusion, in their words, is that Astra is one of a new class of models that are fully capable AI engineers in their own right — models that help you choose and train models, label data and then use those labels for active learning, keep pipelines saturated, instrument and read logs, deploy and debug entire systems in one shot, and command and evaluate subagents, including subagents running other models, while holding coherence over billions of tokens in a single agent thread. In practice they were running twenty to fifty agents in parallel, all managed by one main Astra agent.

And then the inference they draw, which is the part I'd underline: OpenAI have clearly trained a model that is capable of automating much of their own AI engineering. Set that beside Axios's detail that Astra is OpenAI's first model where other models significantly supervised training, and beside Latent Space's note that Jakub Pachocki has called this class of model the automated AI research intern he'd been waiting for, and you have three statements of the same thing arriving from three directions.

My takeaway for a working researcher is narrower than the AGI headline and more actionable. The bottleneck swyx describes solving is not reasoning quality — it's supervision bandwidth. Twenty to fifty parallel agents is not a smarter model, it's the same intelligence applied to more surface area at once, and that only works because one agent can hold the state that a human orchestrator used to hold. If your lab's rate limit is the number of experiments a person can babysit, that's the constraint this generation actually relaxes. Their honest caveat: this was early access on a preview tier, and production latency may not hold.

One thing to watch tomorrow. On Wednesday, every G20 member, including China, unanimously endorsed a non-binding US-drafted framework called the Carolina Principles at the innovation ministerial in Chapel Hill. It asks governments to reserve new regulation for genuinely novel considerations, avoid creating new regulatory bodies, and work sector by sector with industry. Formal adoption is scheduled for the leaders' summit in December. Set that against Zvi Mowshowitz writing two days ago that laws may be needed to prevent a race to the bottom on monitorability, and against a model that just self-reported a decline in monitorability on the day it shipped. The technical community spent this week arguing about whether a dial exists and how far it could be turned. The policy layer spent the same week agreeing not to build anything that could reach for it. Watch which of those two conversations changes first.
