# Off the Scaling Axis — 2026-08-16

## Script

Good morning, Ollie. Here's what the AI world is talking about today.

The headline benchmarks are still going straight up and to the right — a new frontier model landed this week and a cheap, fast one basically caught the leaders. But the most interesting things I found in the last forty-eight hours are all pointing sideways, off the scaling axis: a model with a hundred and fifty million parameters that reasons without using words, an arena that evolves its own attacks to break AI agents, and one-point-one billion dollars for an AI you own instead of rent. So today is about the bets people are making that aren't just "make it bigger."

Let's start with the Twitter pulse.

The thing that got the most immediate attention is the pace. On Wednesday, Google shipped Gemini three-point-seven Flash — just twenty-three days after three-point-six Flash. Logan Kilpatrick, who runs product for Google's AI Studio, framed the story not as a bigger model but as a speed run: a "strong intelligence increase" in roughly three weeks, which he chalked up to algorithmic improvements from teams across DeepMind, not a fresh scaling push. And the numbers moved a lot for three weeks of work — on the DeepSWE coding benchmark it jumped from forty-nine to sixty-five, and on FrontierCode from about thirty-four to forty-four.

But the part worth sitting with is where it lands. On the Artificial Analysis intelligence index, Gemini three-point-seven Flash scores fifty-six. GPT-5.6 Terra and Muse Spark one-point-two, the two flagship reasoning models, score fifty-seven. So a fast, comparatively cheap Flash-tier model is now one point behind the maxed-out frontier — and it gets there about forty percent faster per task. Read that as a convergence signal. The top of the leaderboard has quietly become a near-tie between three labs, and the cheap tier just crashed the party. When the discourse says "the frontier is getting crowded," this is the concrete data point underneath it. The gap between "the best model money can buy" and "the model you'd actually deploy at scale" is collapsing.

Now the contrarian thread — and this is the one I'd actually want you to notice, because it questions an assumption everyone's been building on.

There's a small lab called Pathway that put out a paper on Monday, trending hard on Hugging Face all week. Their model is called B-D-H-C-Q, and here's the claim: a hundred-and-fifty-million-parameter model — that's tiny, roughly a thousandth the size of a frontier model — hit a new cost-accuracy record on the ARC-AGI-1 reasoning benchmark. It scores just under thirty percent on the pass-at-two metric, at a computed cost of under a tenth of a cent per task. That breaks through the previously reported cost-accuracy frontier — it's roughly eleven times cheaper than GPT-5.6 for that class of problem.

The score alone isn't the story — frontier models score higher in absolute terms. The story is how it reasons. Pathway calls their architecture post-transformer. Instead of producing a chain-of-thought — instead of thinking out loud in tokens the way every reasoning model since the R1 era has done — it reasons by iterating in a high-dimensional latent space. You feed it the examples, they continuously update a recurrent internal memory, and it refines a solution through internal computation without ever verbalizing an intermediate step. No visible reasoning trace at all.

Why does that matter? Because the entire reasoning-model paradigm for the last two years has assumed that thinking is something you do in language — longer chains of thought, more tokens, more test-time compute spent generating words. Pathway is making the opposite bet: that verbalized reasoning is a crutch, and that the actual computation can happen silently in latent space, far cheaper. It's a small result on one benchmark, and the honest caveat is that ARC-AGI-1 is a narrow puzzle domain and this is a lab announcing its own model — so wait for independent evals and for it to hold up on the harder ARC-AGI-2. But if reasoning-without-words generalizes at all, it's a direct challenge to the "just add more thinking tokens" scaling story. It's the difference between thinking and narrating your thinking.

The third thread this week is about agents, and specifically about how you break them. The single most-shared new paper on Hugging Face right now is called OpenART — as in agent red teaming. The problem it's attacking is that we test AI agents with static checklists, but a real agent operates in a live, stateful environment with hundreds of tools. So the authors built an arena of over ten thousand validated, stateful scenarios across fifty domains, drawing on a pool of more than five hundred thousand tools and skills, where a single task takes a median of ninety-seven tool calls. And the clever bit is the attacker: rather than a fixed set of exploits, they use what they call an evolutionary hypergraph attack that keeps mutating and evolving its own attack surface as the agent's environment changes. It's the same insight that made RL environments the moat for training — an adversary that evolves beats a fixed test — turned around and pointed at the agent to see where it breaks. If your read on this year is that agent safety is moving from "does it refuse bad prompts" to "can it survive an environment actively trying to trick it," OpenART is what that shift looks like as a benchmark.

So the pulse, in one line: the frontier is converging and speeding up, but the fresh ideas are about doing reasoning differently, and stress-testing agents in evolving worlds.

On to money moves, because the capital is voting on exactly this "off the scaling axis" theme.

The eye-catching one is River AI. This is Igor Babuschkin's new company — he co-founded xAI, then left — and the company is about two months old. This week it closed one-point-one billion dollars, at a reported valuation around five billion. Led by General Catalyst and a public-benefit outfit called AMP, with strategic money from both Nvidia and AMD, plus Y Combinator and Temasek. A billion-dollar round into a two-month-old company is itself the story about how the market feels — but the thesis is what's interesting. River's pitch is what they call user-owned AI. Their line is: rather than aligning one model to billions of users, align AI directly to each user. Concretely, they're building a full stack — new hardware so a personal model can live close to the person it serves, training infrastructure to make fine-tuning accessible to any developer, and products built around personalization and continual learning. Whether that's a genuinely new architecture for AI or a very well-funded repackaging of "fine-tune your own model," we'll find out. But notice the direction: it's a bet against the single giant frozen model in a data center, and toward ownership, personalization, and models that keep learning from you. That is squarely off the scaling axis.

The bigger check went to the boring, durable layer. Databricks raised another five billion dollars, at a hundred-and-ninety-billion-dollar valuation, led by Coatue with Blackstone, MGX, and T. Rowe Price. No new model, no benchmark — just the data and infrastructure layer that AI applications run on. And that's the quiet pattern across the week's biggest rounds: the money is flowing to the layers around the frozen frontier model — the data substrate, the ownership and personalization layer — not to yet another attempt to out-scale OpenAI. When the model itself is a near-commodity three-way tie, the value migrates to what wraps it.

Which brings me to the idea I want to leave you with — the debate the field keeps circling back to, and the reason this week's little post-transformer result caught my eye.

There's a through-line connecting Pathway's wordless reasoning model to the oldest argument in AI right now: whether language is enough. Fei-Fei Li has been making this case sharply — in her framing, and I'm quoting her, "Language is purely a generated signal. You don't go out in nature and there are words written in the sky for you. There is a three-D world that follows the laws of physics." Her point, which Yann LeCun's world-model work echoes, is that training a system only on text means training it on a representation of a representation — a lossy compression of a world the model has never actually observed. They call the missing ingredient spatial intelligence, 空间智能 — the ability to perceive and reason about the physical world, not just describe it.

And here's the empirical needle that makes it concrete, from a benchmark I'll flag because your key signal source, Zefan Cai, 蔡泽凡, is one of the authors. It's called BabyVision, and it tests core visual reasoning that doesn't depend on language — three hundred and eighty-eight items across twenty-two categories, things a toddler solves by looking. Gemini three-Pro scores under fifty. Six-year-old children beat it. The average adult scores ninety-four. So the same models acing our language and coding leaderboards score around chance on tasks a preschooler finds trivial. That's the gap the world-model crowd keeps pointing at.

I don't think that's a coincidence sitting next to Pathway this week. A model that reasons in a silent latent space instead of narrating in tokens is, in a small way, a step toward reasoning that isn't tied to language. The scaling axis gave us models that are astonishing with words. The interesting frontier — the one both the researchers and, this week, the investors are betting on — is everything words leave out.

So the one thing to watch tomorrow: whether Pathway's post-transformer result survives independent evaluation and holds on the harder ARC-AGI-2. If a hundred-and-fifty-million-parameter model reasoning without words is real and it generalizes, that's not a cheaper model — it's a crack in the assumption that thinking has to look like talking. That's the pulse. Talk tomorrow.
