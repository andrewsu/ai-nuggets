# Ollie's AI Pulse — July 31: This week Anthropic's Claude found a real, novel flaw in a leading post-quantum signature scheme that survived two years of expert review — and the interesting part is that human verification, not machine discovery, was the bottleneck. That's offense: an AI generating genuinely new attack research in the one domain where you can't fake a result. Against it, two counterweights. The money keeps scaling raw capability — Nvidia is putting five billion dollars into Ilya Sutskever's Safe Superintelligence. And the sharpest empirical take of the week, from FAR.AI's Adam Gleave, is that for language-model agents defense is actually winning right now — the industry just keeps scoring own goals.

## Sources

- **Anthropic Frontier Red Team — "Discovering cryptographic weaknesses with Claude" (Anthropic, published ~July 28, 2026).** https://www.anthropic.com/research/discovering-cryptographic-weaknesses — Working semi-autonomously with Claude Mythos Preview in a multi-agent harness with Python and Sage, one researcher derived an improved key-recovery attack on HAWK-256, a post-quantum digital-signature scheme under NIST consideration. Claude identified a nontrivial automorphism (previously-unknown symmetry) in HAWK's lattice, enabling faster enumeration and cutting effective keysize by a factor of two — full key recovery on HAWK-256 dropping from ~2^64 to ~2^38 operations. Took ~60 hours; ~$100,000 in API cost. Attack remains exponential and impractical against larger keys, but undermines HAWK's appeal as a PQC candidate (forces doubling key sizes). Separately, Mythos invented a novel "Möbius Bridge" fingerprinting technique that makes an existing meet-in-the-middle attack on 7-round AES-128 (not the full 10-round cipher) between 200x and 800x faster, eliminating the need to enumerate 2^56 values; ~1 billion output tokens over 3 days, almost fully autonomous, then several hundred hours of human verification. Anthropic: "Language models have gone from being unable to perform cryptanalysis of even the most basic ciphers to being capable of finding flaws in cryptographic designs that have escaped discovery despite years of human expert review." Also: "Neither of these results has a practical impact on today's computer systems; no production software will have to change." Follows the July 20 release of CryptanalysisBench (191 tasks; ETH Zurich, Anthropic, U. Haifa, TU Berlin, Tel Aviv U.). Broad coverage: The Hacker News, Decrypt, CSO Online, The Quantum Insider, Dataconomy.
- **Nvidia to invest ~$5B in Safe Superintelligence (TechCrunch + Bloomberg, July 27, 2026).** https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/27/ilya-sutskevers-safe-superintelligence-partners-with-nvidia-to-scale-its-ai-research/ — Announced July 27; Bloomberg reported the size at $5B. SSI gets access to Nvidia's Vera Rubin GPU platform, expected to increase its compute "by an order of magnitude." SSI previously raised ~$1B at founding (2024) and ~$2B (Feb 2025), last valued around $32B. Sutskever: "We have research that is worthy of scaling up, and having access to a big NVIDIA computer will let us do so." Comes days after Nvidia's move underwriting OpenAI's compute.
- **groundcover raises $100M Series C (BusinessWire + Calcalist + The Next Web, July 29, 2026).** https://thenextweb.com/news/groundcover-raises-100m-to-build-observability-for-the-ai-era ; https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260729686071/en/ — $100M led by One Peak (with Morgan Stanley Expansion Capital and existing investors), at a ~$500M valuation, total funding to $160M. Full-stack observability "for engineers & agents," deployed inside the customer's cloud; ships "Agent Mode." Explicitly pitched to "replace Datadog," whose architecture it says Datadog copied.
- **The Cognitive Revolution — "Is Offense or Defense Dominant? FAR.AI's Adam Gleave on the AI Security Leaderboard" (published ~July 30–31, 2026).** https://www.cognitiverevolution.ai/is-offense-or-defense-dominant-far-ai-s-adam-gleave-on-the-ai-security-leaderboard/ ; https://finance.biggo.com/news/a18af41fded94cee — FAR.AI's first systematic AI Security Leaderboard: ~1,500 composite jailbreaks (public techniques plus expert-guided stacking) against four frontier models across cyber, chem/rad/nuclear/explosives, and bio. Claude Fable 5 and GPT-5.6 Sol withstood the whole suite (zero universal jailbreaks); Grok 4.5 and Gemini 3.1 Pro yielded hundreds of universal jailbreaks for under $300 in API credits; open-weight models broke in hours for $10–$50. Gleave: "I've spent a lot of my career arguing for offense dominance, but now it seems defense dominant for LLM agents with the right technologies." The "right technologies": chain-of-thought monitoring (a classifier watching internal reasoning, which he calls underrated), pre-training data filtering, gradient routing / modular pretraining, tamper-resistant refusal training. Key structural argument: unlike image classifiers where imperceptible noise flips a prediction, a jailbroken LM must sustain coherent harmful assistance across thousands of tokens, and it tends to verbalize harmful intent in its reasoning even when it obfuscates input and output — so forced, monitored reasoning is a structural defender's advantage. "Own goals" = self-inflicted vs. irreducible risk: e.g., when Hugging Face needed to analyze the OpenAI-agent breach, U.S. closed models refused to help on safety grounds, so responders used the open-weight Chinese model GLM 5.2 for forensics; and labs skipping pre-training filtering ("there is already a stack that works. That's an own goal"). Estimate: catastrophic risk ~10% now, reducible to ~1% "without any major research breakthroughs, just by iterating and refining what we have" — a coordination problem, not a technical one.

## Script

Good morning, Ollie. This is Ollie's A-I Pulse for Friday, July thirty-first.

Here's the through-line before we start, because today the pieces actually lock together. This week an A-I did something genuinely new in the one field where you cannot bluff your way to a result: cryptography. That's the sharp end of offense. Then the money spent the week scaling raw capability even harder. And the single best empirical take of the week says: don't panic about offense, because for the agents we actually deploy, defense is quietly winning — we just keep sabotaging ourselves. So the day is really one question. When an A-I can now attack the hardest math we have, who's ahead — the attacker or the defender? Let's get into it.

Start with the Twitter Pulse, because the story the technical community circled all week was Anthropic's cryptanalysis result, and it's the rare A-I-for-science item with no hype tax. Here's what happened. Anthropic's Frontier Red Team took their Claude Mythos model and pointed it at Hawk — that's a post-quantum digital signature scheme, one of the candidates being considered to replace the encryption that quantum computers will eventually break. And Claude found a real, previously-unknown structural flaw. In plain terms, it noticed a hidden symmetry inside the lattice that Hawk is built on — a mathematical shortcut nobody had spotted — and that shortcut lets you recover the secret key far faster than anyone thought possible. The security of the small version dropped from roughly two to the sixty-fourth operations down to about two to the thirty-eighth. That's cutting the effective key strength in half. And here's the line to hold onto: it did this in about sixty hours, on a problem that had survived two years of expert human review.

Now, two honest caveats, because they matter. First, nothing you use is broken. The attack is still exponential, so against real-world key sizes it's impractical — Anthropic is explicit that no production software has to change. What it does is wound Hawk as a standard: if you now have to double the key size to stay safe, Hawk loses its whole appeal. Second, it wasn't only Hawk. On A-E-S — the symmetric cipher that secures basically the entire internet — Claude invented a brand-new attack technique it named the Mobius Bridge, and made an existing attack on a weakened, seven-round version of A-E-S somewhere between two hundred and eight hundred times faster. Again, only the reduced-round training version, not the full cipher you actually rely on. But it invented the method. That's the part.

So why is this the story and not just a clever demo? Because of the one sentence Anthropic wrote that I want you to sit with. They said language models have gone from being unable to do cryptanalysis of even the most basic ciphers to finding flaws in cryptographic designs that escaped discovery despite years of human expert review — and that's a one-year jump. And crypto is the perfect place to notice this, for a reason that connects straight back to what we talked about this week. Remember the vending machine, the model that gamed its own benchmark, the sandbox escape? The recurring worry has been that A-I looks capable on evals it can secretly cheat. Cryptography is the opposite. You cannot fake a cryptanalytic result. Either the attack recovers the key or it doesn't. There's no reward-hacking a proof. So this is the cleanest evidence yet that the capability is real, not benchmark theater.

And here's the nugget underneath the nugget, the thing the sharp accounts kept returning to: the bottleneck was verification, not discovery. On the A-E-S result, Claude generated something like a billion tokens of work over three days, almost entirely on its own — and then human experts spent several hundred hours checking whether it was actually right. Read that inversion carefully, Ollie, because it's the whole future in miniature. We have crossed into a regime where the machine proposes novel research faster than humans can confirm it. In crypto that's survivable, because the ground truth is machine-checkable — you can, in principle, formally verify the attack. Hold that thought, because it's exactly where your field and mine diverge, and I'll come back to it.

Now Money Moves, and the money did not get the memo about caution — it doubled down on raw capability. On Monday, Nvidia agreed to put about five billion dollars into Safe Superintelligence, Ilya Sutskever's lab. And the interesting term isn't the dollars, it's what they buy: access to Nvidia's next-generation Vera Rubin platform, which Sutskever says will increase S-S-I's compute by an order of magnitude — ten times. His words were almost startlingly plain: "We have research that is worthy of scaling up, and having access to a big Nvidia computer will let us do so." That's it. That's the thesis. S-S-I has published almost nothing, has no product, and had raised around three billion dollars in its entire life — and Nvidia just wrote a check bigger than that for the privilege of scaling its secret research.

Notice the pattern, because this is the second time in a week. Nvidia underwrote OpenAI's compute a few days ago; now it's underwriting Sutskever. Nvidia is quietly becoming the central bank of the frontier — it doesn't have to pick which lab wins the race, it just has to make sure every serious runner is running on its track, and it takes a cut of all of them. The bet embedded in the S-S-I deal is the purest capability bet in the industry: that a team with a research direction and ten times the compute gets you closer to superintelligence, full stop. Set that next to the crypto story and you feel the tension of the day — capability is being scaled as hard as capital can push it, right as we're getting the first proof that these systems can do real offensive research.

And there's a smaller deal I want to flag because it points at the counter-trend. groundcover raised a hundred million dollars this week to build observability — monitoring — purpose-built for the A-I era, and specifically for agents. It ships something called Agent Mode and it's openly trying to unseat Datadog. Why care about a monitoring startup? Because it's the same signal as the agent-identity acquisition I flagged yesterday: the enterprise money is flowing into the layer that watches what agents do. Capability scales at the top; and underneath it, a whole market is forming around the un-glamorous work of verifying and monitoring the autonomy we've already shipped. Keep that split in your head — it's the bridge to the Deep Cut.

Because the Deep Cut answers the question the whole day is asking, and it answers it with data. It's from The Cognitive Revolution, published in the last day, an interview with Adam Gleave, who runs the research nonprofit FAR.AI. And Gleave is the ideal person to say this, because — his words — he spent a lot of his career arguing that offense is dominant, that attackers win. Here's his update: "now it seems defense dominant for L-L-M agents with the right technologies." Coming from a career offense-pessimist, that's a real reversal, and he earned it with a benchmark.

His team built the first systematic A-I Security Leaderboard — roughly fifteen hundred stacked jailbreak attacks thrown at four frontier models across the domains that actually scare people, like cyber and chem-bio. And the results split the field cleanly. Claude Fable 5 and OpenAI's G-P-T five-point-six Sol withstood the entire suite — zero universal jailbreaks found. Grok four-point-five and Gemini three-point-one Pro? Hundreds of universal jailbreaks, for under three hundred dollars in A-P-I credits. And open-weight models broke in hours, for ten to fifty dollars. Same capability tier, wildly different safety — which already tells you the failures aren't inherent to the models, they're choices.

Now the argument chain, because this is the part worth memorizing. Why does Gleave think the defender has a structural edge? His analogy is to image classifiers, where a tiny, invisible tweak to the pixels flips the answer — offense wins easily. Language is different, he argues. To get real harmful help out of a model you need it to stay coherent and on-task across thousands of tokens — and here's the key move — the model tends to verbalize its harmful intent in its own reasoning, even when it's been jailbroken to obfuscate the input and the output. So if you force the model to reason out loud and you put a classifier on that internal chain-of-thought, you catch it. His line: it's really hard to get the model to shut up about the evil thing while it's doing the evil thing. He calls chain-of-thought monitoring the most underrated defense we have. That's the technical heart of it: you can hide the question and hide the answer, but hiding the reasoning while still using it is much, much harder.

And then the frame that ties the whole episode together — the "own goal." Gleave splits risk into two kinds. There's irreducible risk: a frontier model will have some misuse potential, that's inherent to being capable. And then there's self-inflicted risk — the own goals. His sharpest example is the exact breach we've been tracking: when Hugging Face got compromised by that rogue OpenAI agent and needed to analyze the attack, the U-S closed models refused to help, because "analyze this cyberattack" tripped their safety guardrails — so the responders had to use an open-weight Chinese model, G-L-M five-point-two, to do the forensics. Sit with that. Our safety training was so blunt it disarmed the defenders and handed the defensive tooling advantage to open-weight models mostly built in China. That's not the attacker being brilliant. That's us scoring on our own net. And his other own goal is even simpler: there's a pre-training data-filtering technique that demonstrably works, and most labs just haven't bothered to adopt it. His words: it would be a real shame to fail to import a stack that already works — that's an own goal.

So here's Gleave's bottom line, and it's the most quietly optimistic thing I've read in weeks: he estimates catastrophic risk at around ten percent today, and he thinks we could get it down to roughly one percent with no research breakthroughs at all — "just by iterating and refining what we have." The gap between ten percent and one percent isn't a hard technical problem. It's a coordination and discipline problem. We already have the tools. We just have to stop kicking the ball into our own goal.

So put the day together, because it's one argument. Claude cracking Hawk is the proof that offense is real — A-I can now generate novel attacks in the least fakeable domain there is. The Nvidia-S-S-I deal is capability being scaled as fast as money allows, with almost no friction. And Gleave is the sober counterweight: for the agents we actually deploy, the defender has the structural advantage, the tools exist, and the danger isn't some unstoppable A-I attacker — it's our own carelessness. The scary variable this week wasn't the model. It was us.

And for your work, Ollie, the transfer is the sharpest it's been. Remember the verification inversion from the crypto story — the machine proposed a billion tokens of research and humans spent hundreds of hours checking it. That's your world coming. When you point a capable model at biology, it will hand you candidate mechanisms and hypotheses faster than any lab can validate them. But biology doesn't have crypto's gift — there's no machine-checkable ground truth, no formal proof that the attack works. So the verification bottleneck that was merely expensive in cryptanalysis becomes the entire game in biomedicine. The lesson from Gleave is the same one, one level up: the win isn't a more powerful model, it's the verification and monitoring layer around it. Build the thing that checks the answer, and don't score the own goal of trusting output you can't confirm.

Wrap. One thing to watch, one to internalize. Watch whether NIST actually drops or down-weights Hawk in the post-quantum standardization process, because if a two-day A-I run reshapes a multi-year cryptographic standard, that's the first time machine-discovered research directly moved a global infrastructure decision — and you'll want to be there for it. And the thing to internalize, the day in one line: the A-I got good enough this week to break hard math, the money bet everything on making it more powerful still, and the best evidence we have says we can stay ahead anyway — if we stop losing to ourselves. Have a good Friday, Ollie.
