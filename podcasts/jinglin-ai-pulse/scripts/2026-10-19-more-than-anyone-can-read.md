Here's what the AI world is talking about today. It's Monday, and the thread running through the weekend is a simple problem with no simple fix. AI systems are now producing more output than any human can read. Anthropic measured it inside its own walls. An independent evaluator measured it in tokens. A Stanford and Together AI team proposed hiding most of it on purpose. And on a podcast, a formal methods company argued the answer is to stop reading and start proving. Let's go.

Start with the paper that got the weekend's longest argument. Four economists at the Anthropic Institute, Nathan Wilmers, Regina Catipon, Maxim Massenkoff and Peter McCrory, published a study of how work has changed inside Anthropic itself, using internal employment records, agent telemetry, calendars, code commits, hiring scorecards and seventeen interviews.

The headline number is about juniors. The share of junior employees in technical departments slipped from twenty-one percent to fourteen percent since early twenty twenty-five, and the share of technical hires who are junior fell faster, from twenty-seven percent to nine. That's the line that dominated Hacker News. The top comment, from OuterVale, was one sentence: as obvious as it seems to say, with no new juniors there will be no new seniors.

But the part I'd actually sit with is further down, because it's about attention. By August, the agents that individual employees direct were logging six times as many working hours as the humans directing them. And those interactive sessions spend about eighty percent of their wall-clock time idle, waiting for a person to respond. The authors then asked a blunt question: how long would it take to read everything Claude writes back in those sessions? Even for non-technical staff, the answer is more than half the work week. One engineer described the job as facing "an army of Claudes, with every one asking for my attention," and another compared it to babysitting, where the kid who needs a juice box opened sounds exactly as urgent as the kid who's bleeding.

So what do people do? They stop doing object-level work and start building the scaffolding around it. The paper calls it meta-work, writing specs for multi-agent runs, looping one agent to review another, building monitoring dashboards, and it went from ten percent of interactive sessions in fall twenty twenty-five to twenty-two percent this spring. Standing written instructions for agents went from a few hundred to eighty-five hundred in a year. And meetings went up, not down: the median employee spends twenty percent more time in meetings than a year ago, and technical individual contributors sixteen percent more.

Two methodological points change how much weight this deserves. First, the productivity claim isn't just a before-and-after. They follow a hundred and ninety-two engineers who adopted coding agents against seventy-four who hadn't yet, in the same months, so the company's boom is held roughly constant. And comparing code by the same person, in the same month and directory, agent-written lines were no more likely to be deleted or reverted than hand-written ones. Second, and the authors say this themselves, adoption isn't random. People who moved to fully autonomous agents were already on a steeper trajectory before they adopted. So read the effect sizes as an upper bound.

The best skeptical reply on the thread came from SpicyLemonZest, who turned the paper around. It provides a useful ceiling, they wrote. If even Anthropic is seeing only a two-to-four-times revenue multiplier, be intensely skeptical of anyone claiming ten or a hundred times. That two-to-four figure is the paper's own comparison of revenue per employee against Meta and Google at the same stage. And the commenter pyrale named the obvious problem: there's no way to separate what's reporting from what's advertising when the company studying the effect is selling the tool.

Second thread, and this closes a loop from the last two episodes. On Saturday I said to watch whether any independent evaluator reproduced DeepSeek's numbers for V4.1 Flash 1017. Artificial Analysis has now posted its run, and the thread on it is busy.

The short version is that the improvement is real, and the size is a bit smaller than DeepSeek claimed. On the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, the new checkpoint scores forty-five point one, up from thirty-nine point five for the September V4.1 Flash. That puts it level with GLM five point three at forty-four point eight, ahead of Kimi K3 at forty-three point six, and just behind Xiaomi's MiMo two point six Pro at forty-six point three, a model with roughly twice the total parameters. Meta's Muse Spark Open, the American open model we discussed Saturday, sits at thirty-eight point nine.

Terminal-Bench 4.0 is where the gap shows. DeepSeek's card reported forty-two point three. Under Artificial Analysis's own harness it scores thirty-three point eight. To be fair, the old checkpoint also scored lower there than on DeepSeek's card, so part of this is just a different harness. Still, the independently measured jump is about seven points, not eleven. Humanity's Last Exam, meanwhile, lines up almost exactly: forty-three point three measured against forty-two point six claimed. And the biggest change nobody on the card emphasized is hallucination. On Artificial Analysis's Omniscience test, when it doesn't get a question right, the share of the time it confidently answers wrong instead of saying it doesn't know dropped from about ninety-six percent to about sixty-seven.

Now I owe you a correction. On Saturday I said this checkpoint costs more than four times as much per output token as the Flash it updates. That came from comparing two OpenRouter listings, which reflect whichever providers happen to be serving each model. On DeepSeek's own API, both checkpoints are priced the same, thirty cents per million input tokens and a dollar twenty per million output. And per task, the new one is actually about half the cost, twenty-six cents against forty-nine. As kristopolous pointed out in the thread, provider prices for this one checkpoint range from fifteen cents to a dollar twenty, an eightfold spread. My claim was wrong, and the right comparison is the first-party price.

Here's the bit that connects to today's theme. The commenter Tiberium noticed that OpenAI's GPT six point one Sol at medium effort is cheaper per task, twenty-one cents against twenty-six, and faster, under three minutes against nearly five. The data says why. DeepSeek at max effort emits about eighty-nine thousand output tokens per task. Sol at medium emits about eight thousand. That's eleven times more text to reach a slightly lower score. The user lhl, who says they've pushed tens of billions of tokens through the earlier Flash, reports that low reasoning effort, about a third of max's tokens, is optimal for their tasks. Max effort is mostly more words.

Which sets up the third thread, the paper that went to three hundred and eighty-five points last night. It's called Hybrid Reasoning Agents, from Jiaru Zou and James Zou's group at Stanford with Together AI, UIUC and Meta. The pitch: let an agent think in latent space and act in text.

The intuition is clean. Today's agents have to write out every reasoning step as tokens, which is slow, and the token bottleneck throws away information at every step. Latent reasoning methods like Coconut instead feed the model's last hidden state straight back in as the next input, but those methods can't call tools and can't be read. The fix is a small adapter they call HybridLink, which maps the final hidden state back into the input embedding space. So the model can drop into latent mode for a stretch of silent forward passes, then come back out to text whenever it needs to call a tool, make a decision, or answer. During reinforcement learning there's no reward for using either mode. The model learns on its own when to think silently.

On Qwen3 base models at four, eight and fourteen billion parameters, across eleven benchmarks including MedQA, they report an average ten point four percent improvement, with thirty-five to fifty-five percent fewer tokens and roughly two-to-four-times lower cost. One calibration point for this audience: on MedQA at eight billion, the gain over a text agent trained with the same RL recipe is one point, seventy-five point three against seventy-four point three. The big wins are on hard math and agentic search.

The thread split almost immediately on safety. One commenter, cwyers, just wrote: oh cool, it's the Most Forbidden Technique, the phrase people use for anything that weakens our ability to read a model's chain of thought. The authors saw this coming, and Table 7 is their answer. On five hundred and sixty math problems, the hybrid agent at eight billion writes about three hundred visible tokens per problem plus a hundred and seventy hidden latent steps, against nearly six hundred visible tokens for the text agent. And a weak monitor trying to predict from the visible trace whether the answer is right actually does better on the hybrid agent, an AUROC of point eight four against point seven seven. The visible text is shorter but denser in key steps.

Two caveats change the take. That monitorability test is math only, not the agentic tasks where actions actually touch the world. And read the discussion section closely. It argues that latent thoughts are readable only through the provider's own HybridLink, so, quote, "no text exists to leak." The provider can audit the full trace while the customer sees only the text. On a weekend when OpenCode described its breach as a distillation attack, that's being sold as both a safety feature and protection against distillation. Whether that reassures you depends on whether you trust the provider to be the one who reads it.

For the deep cut, The Cognitive Revolution's Saturday episode, Nathan Labenz and Prakash Narayanan, with a segment featuring Everett Hildenbrandt and Grigore Rosu of Runtime Verification. It opens with a small, unnerving anecdote that fits today perfectly. Labenz says Claude decided on its own to rotate the passwords on his agent console. "I didn't set the password. It set the password." He got back in by just asking it where it had put the new one. But, he says, if the intent ever is bad, it can already lock me out.

Then the argument, and it builds in three steps. Step one is the method. Runtime Verification went after Linux kernel bugs, starting with device drivers, because that's where untrusted input arrives and scrutiny is lightest. They had a language model port the C code to Rust, then ran differential fuzzing between the two. Any divergence is either a translation error or a bug in the original C. They found a bug at that stage. Then they wrote formal proofs about the Rust and found another one. Hildenbrandt is emphatic that this was a methodology, not a model pointed at the kernel and told to find bugs, and that the agent usage was light: a single Claude Code instance on a premium plan. His rule of thumb is the thing I'd keep: if an LLM discovers a technique that works, turn it into a deterministic tool and run that tool every time.

Step two is adoption. When they first pitched formal verification to kernel maintainers, in his words, "they basically told us to go kick rocks." So they flipped it, doing all the formalization and translation on their side and showing up with nothing but bugs and patches. Now they're being invited in. And Rosu gives anyone vibe coding a one-line recipe: once you have the code, tell your agent to write the specification and prove the code correct. Most errors, he says, surface while writing the spec, because that's when you step back and ask what the code is supposed to do. A review plus plus. His bigger point is that you can't prove model weights correct, but you can prove a monitor correct: a safety envelope around the agent that you formally guarantee, even if you can't verify what's inside.

Step three is where Labenz turns it on himself, and it's the most honest part. After the guests leave, he asks whether formal verification is the new RLHF. RLHF started as a safety technique and turned out mostly to make models more useful, without solving alignment. His worry is that the same thing happens here. Verified tools and unbreakable sandboxes make it safer to deploy models more or less as they are now. That accelerates handing them the levers, while their actual goals remain, in his phrase, very much an unsolved question. Narayanan's reply is a useful reframe: reward hacking is really misspecification, the gap between what you meant and what you wrote down. Which is, of course, exactly the gap formal specs are designed to close.

Here's why it belongs at the end of today's episode. The Anthropic paper says humans can no longer read all their agents' output. The hybrid paper says much of the reasoning may not be readable text at all. Runtime Verification's answer is to stop depending on reading: specify what must hold, and prove it. And Labenz's warning is that being able to prove the tools are safe isn't the same as knowing what the agent wants.

So, the one thing to watch tomorrow. Narrowly, whether Artificial Analysis or anyone else runs DeepSeek's checkpoint at low or high effort rather than max, because the eleven-to-one token ratio is the real story there. More broadly, and closer to your own work: watch whether anyone tests latent-mode monitorability on agentic tasks, not math. If you're building agents for biomedical work, where a tool call can order an assay or write to a record, that's the experiment that decides whether hidden thinking is a speedup or a blind spot. That's the pulse.
