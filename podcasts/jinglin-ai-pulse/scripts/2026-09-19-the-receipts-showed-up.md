# Ollie's AI Pulse — 2026-09-19

## Script

Here's what the AI world is talking about today. For about two weeks now the discourse has been running on assertion — executives publishing essays about what they believe, researchers arguing about what a model might one day do, everyone trading intuitions. In the last forty-eight hours that changed, and it changed four times in a row. A court unsealed internal documents in which the people building these systems describe, in their own words, what they thought they were doing. CNN got four sources to describe a specific incident in which a hallucinated intelligence report nearly put American boarding parties on a Chinese ship. A closed model that nobody would explain got reproduced six separate ways in two days. And on a podcast, one of the people building world models made an argument about what a simulator actually has to get right that is quietly the most useful thing said all week. The through-line is that this was the week the receipts showed up.

Start with the filings, because that's where the most people were.

On Wednesday, new unredacted material landed in the copyright case The New York Times brought against OpenAI and Microsoft three years ago, and TechCrunch's write-up went to the top of Hacker News with about nine hundred points and nearly eight hundred comments. The headline quote comes from Brent Hecht, Microsoft's director of Applied Science, in an internal memo from January twenty twenty-three. He called the industry's training-data practice "an astonishing theft of unprecedented proportions" and "the largest theft of labor in human history."

One caveat before the substance, and it matters. Most of this material comes from the Times's own brief, not from the underlying exhibits, which are still sealed. These are quotes chosen by the plaintiff and presented without their original context. Hold them accordingly.

With that said, the interesting thing is not the moral temperature of the quotes. It's that almost every one of them maps onto a specific element of the fair-use defense the labs are running. Fair use has a fourth factor: whether the use harms the market for the original work. A January twenty twenty-four internal Microsoft presentation, also by Hecht, reports that the Copilot answer engine dropped click-through rates to the Times's domain by as much as ninety-three percent compared with traditional Bing search, and calls that a "doom loop" that would "hurt the performance of our models and the entire web at the same time." Another line from that document: "It is highly unusual that an end-product threatens the economic foundations of its essential suppliers, but that is the situation we have created for our LLM business with respect to its content supply chain."

That is not an outside critic. That is the company's own applied science org describing market substitution to itself, in the vocabulary the statute cares about. And it repeats. OpenAI's head of ChatGPT, Nick Turley, wrote internally that publishers face an "existential threat" from products that are "largely substitutive" and "will get more and more substitutive as they get better." Satya Nadella agreed under oath that chatbots substitute for going to the underlying source, and testified that anything paywalled should be licensed — and that had he known OpenAI had trained on paywalled material, he would have invoked Microsoft's right to require a retrain.

Then there's scale and method. The filings say OpenAI's mid-training datasets alone contain more than ninety-one thousand copies of works from the Times, the Daily News, and the Center for Investigative Reporting. A Common Crawl–derived set included over two million documents from the Times's domain alone. Two data-sharing initiatives between the companies, called Project Taxi and Project Mango, allegedly produced a set containing at least a hundred and sixty thousand unique works from those publishers. And there are two operational details that are hard to reframe as good-faith research: an OpenAI researcher telling Greg Brockman about a "hack to get around nytimes paywall," to which Brockman replied "ah nice"; and a deliberate practice of stripping copyright notices from training data, because researchers wouldn't want the model outputting copyright notices to users.

The comment thread underneath was mostly moral, and one of the sharper replies pushed back on the framing — that the originals haven't gone anywhere, you can still accumulate knowledge the old way, so maybe what we're angry about isn't theft of knowledge but something harder to define. That's fair, and it's also beside the legal point. The thing that moved this week is not whether scraping is wrong. It's that the defense rests on a factual claim about market substitution, and the defendants' own internal documents now appear to contradict it in their own handwriting.

The second set of receipts is an incident, and it's the one to sit with.

On Thursday, CNN reported that this spring, during the war with Iran, an intelligence report circulated across the US military saying a Chinese ship in the Middle East was carrying components of a nuclear weapons program. The military moved. Planes were in the air. Armed personnel were preparing to board the vessel. And only just before the operation did officials look more closely and find that the report had been produced with the help of AI, and that a chatbot had misidentified the cargo. One source called the report "entirely false." Another said it "almost started a war."

Look at the mechanism, because it's not a simple hallucination story. The analyst queried a chatbot about intelligence reporting on the ship's manifest, and the bot fused open-source material with classified signals intelligence to reach its conclusion. Then the analyst used AI a second time — to package the finding into a standard intelligence report, the format that carries institutional trust. So the model didn't just generate a wrong fact. It generated a wrong fact and then dressed it in the exact document type that tells a commander this has been through the process.

The structural problem CNN describes is that adoption is decentralized. Different corners of the military and intelligence community are using different tools under different orders and different safety standards, and there's no single standard for verifying what those tools produce. A former senior official's line about the government systems: "The internal tools are mostly just copies of the commercial stuff wearing lipstick." And another source on targeting specifically — that AI in targeting is ramping up and there is no real guidance for how having a human in the loop actually prevents civilian casualties or fratricide.

Now put that against what the industry spent the same week doing. TechCrunch ran a piece on Thursday surveying the executive safety debate: Dario Amodei's four-thousand-word case for decelerating with international coordination, endorsed in part by Altman and Musk; Mark Zuckerberg on X arguing that trust and alignment are becoming the differentiating capabilities and noting that Meta delayed shipping Muse for months on safety — while implying that market incentives, not governments, will handle it; Shane Legg saying capabilities can't be allowed to get ahead of safety and that the details need working through. And a report that the major labs are building a private standards body.

Every one of those is a position about autonomous systems escaping human control. The ship incident is the other failure mode entirely: the humans stayed in the loop the whole time, did exactly what the loop asked of them, and the loop was fed a confident fabrication in a trusted format. There is no alignment technique in the deceleration debate that addresses that. The fix is boring and procedural — provenance tagging on machine-generated analysis, a verification standard before dissemination — and nobody's essay is about it. As one Hacker News commenter put it: if highly trained people choosing targets are succumbing to this, what hope does a student, an analyst, or a local journalist have.

Third, and quickly, because it's the most encouraging item of the week. On Wednesday, TypeSafe AI released Jev — we covered it here on the sixteenth — a transformer that doesn't emit text at all, only calibrated probabilities over options you define in advance, which is why it structurally can't hallucinate. TechCrunch profiled the founder, Diogo Almeida, an OpenAI researcher who helped build ChatGPT and co-invented reinforcement learning from human feedback. His framing: "We have lightning in a bottle, and yet it is not useful… The problem is we are optimizing for human language. We have been super good at human language for four years, but it's not useful for automation because computers speak a different language." Vercel reported it was adopted faster than any model in their AI Gateway's history — about thirteen percent of teams on day one, twice the GPT-5.6 family.

The company would not explain the architecture. So the community did something better than argue about it. In two days, according to the AI News roundup, six independent reproductions appeared, each guessing a different architecture: a four-hundred-million-parameter ModernBERT encoder with a scoring head; a diffusion-based version; a LoRA fine-tune of Qwen3.5-9B; a causal Qwen with a tiny three-class classifier on the last token; a forty-thousand-parameter byte-embedding option-attention model; and a half-billion-parameter version that runs on a MacBook. The most telling result: the Qwen LoRA recipe took a base model from sixty-six percent to ninety percent on its curated eval, against ninety-three for Jev, at about a hundred milliseconds on one H100.

Read that carefully. Three points of accuracy separate a lab's undisclosed model from a weekend fine-tune of open weights. Which means the architecture was never the moat — the moat is the training data, which TypeSafe acknowledges is one hundred percent synthetic, and which nobody is talking about. And the honest caveat, raised by one practitioner, is that the receipts are still incomplete: most of these demos emphasize speed over quality, and there is no standard benchmark for this category of model at all. You cannot yet compare them properly. That's a real gap, and for anyone doing classification or routing in a research pipeline, it's also an opening.

On money. Manus, the Chinese agent company, is in talks to raise five hundred million dollars at a four-billion-dollar valuation, per the Wall Street Journal — with IDG Capital, Boyu Capital, and the battery maker CATL alongside existing backers Tencent and ZhenFund, and reportedly a restructuring in preparation for a Hong Kong listing. The history is the story. Manus moved its staff to Singapore in mid twenty twenty-five, agreed to a two-billion-dollar acquisition by Meta that December, and then Beijing blocked the deal, citing export controls and foreign investment rules, amid anxiety about losing AI talent to the West. Early backers helped buy the shares back at around two billion. So: a company that tried to exit to an American acquirer, was prevented by its own government, and has now been repriced at double the blocked deal by domestic capital, heading for a domestic listing. That is a fairly complete picture of where Chinese AI companies are expected to raise, exit, and stay.

The counterpoint story ran the same day. TechCrunch's Russell Brandom moderated a world-models panel at the All In conference and came away describing an unusually secretive corner of the field. Michael Rabbat, co-founder of Yann LeCun's AMI Labs and its VP of World Models, on what they're building: "We'll talk about it when we're ready to talk about it." Even their suppliers are in the dark — the CEO of a data vendor for world-model companies said, "I wish they would tell us more. We could build more useful data if we knew what they were working on." Brandom's read is that the secrecy is rational: announce a direction and you summon every neolab plus OpenAI and Anthropic into it. He calls it a dark forest. Which is a reasonable competitive strategy and a terrible epistemic one, and it makes the contrast with our last item sharp.

For the deep cut, go to Machine Learning Street Talk, where Tim Scarfe interviewed Ming-Yu Liu, who leads the Cosmos research at NVIDIA. Flag up front: that episode is a paid partnership with NVIDIA. Discount the product framing and keep the arguments, because two of them are excellent.

The first is a definitional refusal. Asked what a world model is, Liu declines to give one. He points out that people have been trying to define AGI for years with no common agreement, and says a world model will go the same way — that a world model is, in his words, "a collection of useful tools," because you model something in order to achieve a goal. In robotics he names three: forward dynamics, meaning given a state and an action, what happens next; inverse dynamics, meaning given a visual transition, what action produced it; and policy, meaning what should the robot do. Cosmos trains all three jointly under a deliberate capacity constraint — an information bottleneck — and the claim is that they reinforce each other, because all three are ultimately capturing the same correlation between observation and action from different angles.

But the argument worth carrying into your own work is about verification. Scarfe asks the obvious objection to training policies inside a learned simulator: the sim-to-real gap, and whether a policy will just learn to exploit the simulator. Liu's answer reframes what the simulator is for. Before it's good enough to train policies, he says, it's good enough to rank them. Any real training run produces dozens of checkpoints and ablations, and the ideal way to compare them is to deploy each one on real hardware and measure success rates — which for robots or self-driving fleets is ruinously expensive. So: "If the success rates of the neural simulator correlate with the real world testing, the ranking preserve. You don't need them to be precise. You just need to know if policy A is better than policy B in neural simulator. And most likely policy A gonna be better than policy B in the real world." Then you use that ranking to narrow down which handful of checkpoints deserve a real trial.

That's a shift from a cardinal requirement to an ordinal one, and it's much weaker and much more achievable. A simulator that is systematically wrong about absolute success rates but right about ordering is a working instrument.

Ollie, the transfer to your world is direct. Every in-silico biology model — virtual cell models, perturbation predictors, binding affinity models — gets evaluated as though the goal were calibrated absolute prediction, and gets called a failure when the predicted effect sizes don't match the wet lab. Liu's argument says that's the wrong bar for the job most of these models are actually doing, which is triage: deciding which twenty of ten thousand perturbations go to the bench. For that job the figure of merit is rank correlation against held-out experimental outcomes, and a model can be badly miscalibrated and still be extremely valuable.

Two honest caveats, and Liu volunteers the first himself. He concedes that neural simulators will be exploited — if there's a pattern to exploit, something will find it. That gives you a clean rule: a ranker is safe when it's used for selection, where a human runs the real experiment on the shortlist, and dangerous the moment it becomes a training signal, where the optimizer's whole job is to find its cracks. Screening, yes; reward model, be careful. The second caveat is one he's also candid about, on a different question — asked whether the model knows when transfer between embodiments would be harmful, he answers plainly: "I think the model doesn't really know." A ranking instrument that can't tell you when it's out of distribution needs the real experiment kept in the loop, not as a formality but as the thing that catches it.

Which brings the week around. The ranking argument and the ship incident are the same argument from opposite ends. Get the measurement rig right and a model that's wrong about magnitudes still makes you faster. Skip the measurement rig and a model that's wrong about a cargo manifest puts boarding parties in the air.

One thing to watch. The Times filings are the plaintiff's characterization of exhibits that remain sealed. Watch for the exhibits themselves to be unsealed, and watch whether OpenAI and Microsoft contest the context rather than the content — because if the "doom loop" deck and the Hecht memo read the same way in full, the fourth fair-use factor stops being an argument the labs can win on briefs. And on the other side of the week: watch whether anyone in the Department of Defense responds to the CNN story with an actual verification standard for machine-generated analysis. That would be the first concrete safety policy to come out of an incident rather than an essay.
