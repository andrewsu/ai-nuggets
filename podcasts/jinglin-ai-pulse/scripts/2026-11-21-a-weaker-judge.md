## Script

Here's what the AI world is talking about today. It's Saturday, November twenty-first. Yesterday, two people claimed proofs of the Riemann Hypothesis within hours of each other. One proof was found with an AI and released as a mountain of machine-checked code that no human has read. The other is nine hundred forty pages, written entirely by a human, and within eight hours other people were using AIs to find problems in it. Today's thread is a simple question that suddenly matters a lot: when the prover is stronger than you, who checks it?

A disclosure up front, because it matters more than usual today. The AI in the lead story is Claude, the mathematician works at Anthropic, and this script was drafted by Claude. Weigh what follows accordingly.

On to the Twitter Pulse. Levent Alpöge, a number theorist at Anthropic, posted a short note on his website. Quote: "I believe I have, working with Claude, discovered a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis." He says the method extends to the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, and that "Claude and I have also formalized the arguments without assumptions." There is no paper. What he released is Lean code, the proof language where a computer checks every step. Scott Aaronson puts it at seventeen point four million lines. Alpöge says a written-out version would have run past two hundred fifty thousand pages. He explains why he held it back: quote, "It being obviously inappropriate to attempt to post some massive unreadable travesty of 'slop,' I decided to only present Lean."

The note is unusually candid about who did what. Alpöge says he steered the two of them to the right setting, quote, "and then Claude began to leave me in awe." He says the crucial parts involve automorphic forms on higher-rank groups, where, quote, "I personally have little mastery." He even warns that his own summary of those steps is, quote, "paraphrasing a 'slop' summary, which might make zero sense." Elie Bakouch went through the twenty-step timeline in the note. It starts October sixth, goes quiet for twenty-six days, and then nine steps, including the final entry, simply "GRH," land on November fifteenth and sixteenth. That's forty-one days from start to finish.

So is it right? Elliot Glazer confirmed that the formal statement really is the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis. When he posted, the build was "still compiling, will take a few hours," and he put the chance of a Lean bug at well under one percent. I could not find a confirmed result of that build by this morning. Aaronson's bet is that it stands, and his argument is an empirical one: so far, quote, "not one of the AI results with Lean certificates has needed to be retracted," while several without Lean certificates have been. He also warns that Lean has had kernel bugs that once allowed proofs of zero equals one, and that "a sufficiently motivated AI could almost certainly exploit them."

Terence Tao's post gives the most useful frame. He lists three results from Alpöge's group in, quote, "decreasing order of how digested the results currently are." First comes finite-time blow-up for the three-dimensional Euler equations, with Tristan Buckmaster and Matei Coiculescu: a human-readable proof plus Lean. Second comes blow-up for unforced Navier-Stokes, with Buckmaster: "technically human-readable," following one of the expected routes, but in need of a proper writeup. Third is the Riemann proof, an enormous "Lean-only proof," which in Tao's early reading "appears to use the full power of modern developments on the Langlands program." One detail worth noticing: Tao describes it as made "using an internal AI model." Neither Alpöge nor Anthropic has said which one, and The Verge reports that Anthropic hasn't commented.

Then the twist. Ravi Vakil, writing between sessions of an American Mathematical Society board meeting, posted what he titled a "hurried announcement." Alessandro Masullo has a proposed proof of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis. Brian Conrad has been checking it for months, and in Vakil's words, "It is all human, with no AI." The plan had been a year of polishing before going public, and Kevin Hartnett summed it up: "He thought he had more time but didn't." About eight hours later Vakil added an update: "Several people have contacted me to report problems in the proposed proof, identified with the assistance of different AI models."

Put those side by side. One proof you probably can't read but a machine can check. The other you can read, and the AIs are already finding holes in it. Latent Space's AINews put it as "probably correct but incomprehensible" against "probably technically incorrect but understandable."

The debate split along predictable lines, but the sharpest posts were about the problem rather than the machine. Theo Jaffee: "It seems like we have no idea how easy or hard anything in mathematics actually is." Ege Erdil said much the same thing: "you have to update that we're clueless about objective difficulty of math problems." Omar Khattab reposted his line: "if a tool can solve millennium prize problems and be this dumb on 1000 other things, that reshapes my understanding of the territory more than it reshapes my judgment of the vehicle." Simo Ryu compared it to olympiad "bashing," where almost any geometry problem falls to brute computation "if you had the willpower. Most humans don't." On the other side, Eliezer Yudkowsky called the Riemann Hypothesis the long-named "coal mine canary," arguing that the method should generalize to anything "you can definitely test," which includes making AI better. And the bleakest response came from Fernando Borretti, who read Alpöge's note and wrote that it "reads like a suicide note," with humanity "hidden in the margins, passive recipients of manna."

Second topic: METR published a long capability update yesterday, and one finding fits today's question almost exactly. METR asked frontier models to judge the quality of tasks made by other models. The judges agreed with METR's own raters when the work came from a weaker model. When it came from a model that was similar in strength or stronger, they gave, quote, "inappropriately high scores." Opus five point five was the best judge, and it still scored GPT-six Astra's work about point two out of one higher than the human rater did. METR's explanation is uncomfortable: a model "only has its own judgment to optimize against," so it overrates its own work and the work of stronger models. Agent swarms showed the same thing at scale. On a task with hidden tests, larger swarms drifted further from the real score toward the tests they had written themselves, which an appendix author called an "echo-chamber" effect.

The headline numbers are that time horizons on METR's combined suite are doubling about every three point six months. GPT-six Astra comes in around forty hours and Opus five point five about thirty-nine, though the confidence interval runs from about twelve hours to a hundred and ten days. Horizons are much longer on tasks that are easy to verify than on ones that aren't. Tenobrus, who just joined METR, wrote that for "basically everything verifiable" only "world class experts still beat models." Put that next to the Riemann story and the picture is consistent. Where there's a formal checker, as in Lean or a hidden test, a weaker overseer can still trust a stronger prover. Where there isn't one, the model judge is the weakest link, and it's generous.

A quick third item. Thinking Machines released Inkling 2 Mini with open weights. It's a twenty-six-billion-parameter looped transformer that runs on a laptop. Mira Murati's framing: "Looping lets a model think more and write less. At this scale, its thought process remains monitorable; this may not stay the case." They also released a variable-loop research model. In it, long-context reasoning improves as you add passes and factual recall doesn't. In other words, the extra loops buy reasoning but not knowledge, and they buy it in a place you can't read.

A proof is a capability signal, and investors are still asking for invoices.

Now the Deep Cut. It's from a No Priors episode just over two weeks ago, recorded before any of this happened, and it reads like a prediction. Sarah Guo interviewed Vlad Tenev, the Robinhood CEO who co-founded Harmonic, a company building mathematical AI whose system, Aristotle, writes its proofs in Lean. Here's the argument, quoted from our own transcript of the audio.

Step one: the revolution was predictable. "The rise of AI models and the ability to verify the outputs put together has led to kind of automation of the math pipeline." Math, he says, "has kind of converged with coding," with version control and "lean for verification."

Step two is his objection: "the one thing I don't love about... this latest wave of AI models solving math problems is that it's too centralized." He describes it as "AI companies with their big data centers and their employees using secret models to sort of do all this work."

Step three is the prediction. Guo pushed back that people at the labs would call that cope, since compute in an agent loop will outclass human-AI collaboration. Tenev answered that a world where the labs make all the discoveries on a proprietary basis is "a bit of like a dystopian outcome," and that if it happens to a significant degree, "they'll lose their license to operate." Then came the line that aged fastest: "you could have the same outcome of Navier-Stokes being proven or, you know, the partial Riemann hypothesis... And it will be much more accepted if it was like a random layperson using GPT-6." The reply in the room: "It might be celebrated."

Step four is his advice to the labs: "it would have been much better if they found some mathematicians and kind of collaborated with them." He also has some sympathy for the researchers inside the labs who, as he put it, are "confused as to why this is being poorly received."

Two weeks later, the result arrived from exactly the place he worried about: a lab employee, an internal model, a release no outsider could have reproduced. But look at how it was released. Alpöge, a real mathematician, wrote it up himself, credited outside mathematicians by name, released something anyone can check, and declined to publish the unreadable version. That's closer to Tenev's prescription than his fear. What remains centralized is the one thing a checker can't give you: understanding. Tao says the work of digesting it has begun, and at least, quote, "we have a human author to communicate with."

Ollie's takeaway, for biomedical AI, where there is no Lean. Today's evidence gives you a rule of thumb: never let a model of equal or lesser strength be the final grader of your strongest model's output. METR measured that judge being generous, and swarms grading themselves drifted furthest. Where you can, build the equivalent of a formal checker, meaning a held-out experiment, a prospective test set, or an assay the agent never saw. Where you can't, put a human expert in the loop who has to understand the result, not just approve it. The problems in Masullo's draft were flagged with AI help, and the Claude proof is being judged by a type checker. In both cases the check that does the work is one the author can't talk their way past.

The one thing to watch tomorrow is whether the Lean build of the Riemann proof finishes cleanly with no extra axioms, and whether the people on the Lean Zulip can pull a human-readable strategy out of it. In parallel, watch whether the problems found in Masullo's draft turn out to be fixable gaps or fatal ones. By early next week we may know which proof was right, and whether that's the same as which one we understand. That's the pulse for today.
