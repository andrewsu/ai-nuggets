## Script

Here's what the AI world is talking about today. For the last week this show has tracked labs building gates — vetted access programs, tiered safeguards, the same weights behind two different doors. Today the argument moved somewhere much more uncomfortable. It stopped being about who is allowed to use the model and started being about whether anyone can see what the model is doing while it works. And the people raising the alarm are not outside critics. They are the researchers whose whole job is reading model reasoning — plus, in one case, OpenAI's own chief scientist, replying in public.

Let's start there, because it is the story with real convergence behind it.

On Tuesday night The Information's Amir Efrati reported that Astra — the OpenAI model designated at a critical cybersecurity capability threshold two days ago — uses a technique called recurrent depth, which some people are calling opaque recurrence. The plain version of the mechanism is this: instead of doing all its reasoning by writing tokens out in a sequence, the model passes the same internal state through a loop several times before producing anything. The computation happens, performance improves, and the intermediate steps leave fewer legible traces. Efrati's phrasing is worth keeping exactly: an innovative technique that improved the model's performance also means the model, and others like it, will reveal less of their thinking, making them harder to monitor for signs of bad behavior.

Hold the word "reportedly" firmly. That piece is paywalled and sourced to a single person with knowledge, OpenAI has published no architecture, and recurrent depth is an existing published research direction rather than a secret. So what happened next isn't a technical result. It's a fight about a norm.

Buck Shlegeris, who runs Redwood Research, wrote that he is extremely concerned by the reporting — and his argument is specifically not about Astra. He said he doesn't know whether Astra is much less monitorable than previous models, but that if OpenAI pushes the technique further, they'll have the option to massively increase the recurrence and totally destroy chain-of-thought monitorability. Redwood's chief scientist Ryan Greenblatt named the same slope more precisely: his biggest concern, in his words, is a natural progression to scaling up the opaque reasoning until the model reasons entirely or almost entirely in latent space. He hopes, he wrote, that it isn't too late to avoid the most concerning architectures, and that OpenAI will stop here.

Zvi Mowshowitz put the norm argument most cleanly. The technique, he wrote, is playing with fire, risking a taboo that OpenAI and Anthropic have fought to establish — that we work hard to maintain chain-of-thought faithfulness and monitorability for as long as we can. And then the line that makes this a policy story rather than a discourse story: laws may be needed to prevent a race to the bottom.

OpenAI answered directly. Chief scientist Jakub Pachocki posted that the lab has worked to preserve and utilize chain-of-thought monitoring since its very first reasoning models, and that it's a core goal of the current research program. The company pushed back on any suggestion it is moving to neuralese, and the reporting itself says Astra's use of the technique appears limited, with its chain of thought still expected to be legible.

Here is why this landed hard rather than becoming a footnote. In OpenAI's retrospective on the agent swarm incident, chain-of-thought records were the forensic instrument — the way investigators reconstructed why the agents did what they did, a point Ajeya Cotra made on Dwarkesh in yesterday's episode. So the industry spent a week building a safety story that rests on legible reasoning, and then the reported source of the next capability jump is a technique that erodes exactly that. It isn't a safety tax on capability. It's the reverse — a monitorability cost charged by the very thing that makes the model better. That is a far worse incentive structure, and everyone quoted above is reacting to the structure, not to Astra.

One counterweight worth knowing about: the week's most-upvoted paper on Hacker News, from Tom McCoy, Paul Soulos, Tal Linzen and Paul Smolensky, argues that network representations implicitly realize symbolic structure, and shows it by swapping the whole representation-generating process for a closed-form symbolic equation without changing behavior. If the traces go dark, reading representations directly is the fallback, and that's evidence the fallback isn't hopeless.

The second thing people are talking about is the cyber gate, which just closed for a third time in thirty-six hours — except that Google inverted it.

Yesterday Google shipped Gemini three point eight Flash, and alongside it Gemini three point eight Flash Cyber, available only through a new limited-access program called Fairwind. That's the third Flash release in six weeks, at the same price as the one from three weeks ago. But the interesting sentence sits in the cyber section, and it is a direct rebuttal of OpenAI's framing. Google says it invested in vulnerability fixing from the start and prioritized it over offensive capabilities like exploitation. Google's security vice president Four Flynn turns that into a slogan in the companion post: spotting weaknesses creates awareness and fear; autonomously finding and fixing vulnerabilities delivers security.

So the numbers Google chose to publish are patching numbers, not exploit numbers. On an external patching benchmark, the cyber model essentially ties a leading frontier model at forty-seven percent pass at one, at much lower cost. Google's Chrome security team reports two point six times more correct patches than the best commercial models, which are far larger. And its cloud vulnerability research team says it found a critical foundational flaw in under two hours, work that normally takes months. Fairwind is already more than six hundred and fifty partners — governments, critical infrastructure operators, core platform maintainers — with contractual limits on which employees may touch it.

And then, the same day, the field result that undercuts the whole genre. A small company called AISLE published a head-to-head with an unusually clean structure, which is why it deserves attention despite obviously being marketing. On August twenty-fourth, curl's founder Daniel Stenberg posted publicly that only three security advisories were pending for the next release, and that after running frontier AI security systems over the codebase, Anthropic's Mythos said it couldn't find any more and OpenAI's Codex Security showed an empty list. That is a public, timestamped zero, recorded before AISLE ran anything. AISLE then ran its own system, and the next day Stenberg posted a two-line scoreboard: Mythos zero, AISLE twenty-nine. Six of those twenty-nine were reviewed by curl's own security team within days and judged serious enough to merit public vulnerability designations, all six now fixed in curl eight point twenty-two point zero.

Say the caveats out loud. All six are rated low severity, which is what you'd expect from one of the most audited codebases on earth; it is one product against one codebase in one run; and AISLE is selling something. But curl's maintainers, not AISLE, decided what counted, this was production code rather than a benchmark whose answers might sit in training data, and Greg Kroah-Hartman, who maintains the Linux stable trees, replied that he is seeing the same thing in Linux — in his words, no idea what Aisle is doing differently, but wow.

The take here is not that the frontier models are weak. It's that the benchmark numbers and the field results are measuring different things, and the gap between them lives in the scaffolding rather than the weights. AISLE calls its position "system over model" — and notice Google effectively agrees, because Fairwind doesn't hand defenders a raw model, it ships the model inside a harness called CodeMender. What's being gated is increasingly the system, and the system is where the performance is.

The third item is a quick one, and it's the day's quietest important story: your data now has a published list price. Meta's Muse Spark one point three launched with two entries on the pricing page for the same model. One is labeled contributor, at ten cents per million input tokens, and the page says plainly: used to improve our products. The other is a dollar twenty-five, and says: not used to improve our products. Twelve and a half times on input, and roughly twenty-one times on output — an explicit exchange rate between your tokens and your privacy. The same day, a Mistral help-center article noting that consumer users are not opted out of training by default while enterprise customers are, and that the chat toggle and the API toggle are separate, pulled four hundred and forty points on Hacker News. And the administration filed a brief in the New York Times case siding with OpenAI, arguing that constraining language model development under a misunderstanding of fair use would thwart creative and scientific progress. Three unrelated documents, one direction: data rights are becoming a priced, negotiated line item rather than a background assumption.

On to the money, where the theme turns out to be the same one. HiddenLayer raised a hundred-million-dollar Series B, with Microsoft's venture arm and Booz Allen participating, on revenue it says grew more than tenfold in a year. The market context is the actual news: Gartner estimates companies will spend two point eight billion dollars this year on securing AI, eighty-three percent more than last year, heading toward roughly four point eight billion next. And one product detail neatly inverts the day's cyber story — they scan about fifty different AI file formats looking for, in the chief executive's phrase, hidden models inside of models. The attack surface is now the artifact you download from a model hub.

Alongside it, Palo Alto Networks paid five hundred million in cash and stock for Console, a two-year-old startup last valued at a hundred and fifty-seven million, and its seventh acquisition this year. Put the two deals side by side and you get the pattern the HiddenLayer piece names outright: incumbents here consistently buy rather than build, which makes a hundred-million round right now as much a price tag as a war chest.

Now for the deep cut, which comes from Latent Space on Monday — a reported piece by Richard MacManus under a headline that reads, in capital letters, pull requests not welcome. It's about open source, and it's the sharpest thing I read this week, because it takes an ideological commitment and shows economics dissolving it.

The setup is Vercel's AI SDK, a project with over twenty million weekly downloads that by late June had more than a thousand open issues and almost eight hundred open pull requests. Instead of recruiting more human reviewers, they built what they call a software factory: a team of agents that triages a report, reproduces the bug, implements a fix, reviews it, and hands a finished thing to a human to merge. Four weeks later, Vercel claims the factory authors twenty-five to thirty-five percent of the pull requests they merge, and closes seventy to eighty percent of issues.

The reasoning is what makes it interesting. Vercel engineer Lars Grammel explained the choice in terms of trust, and his phrasing is precise: if we have a very specific agent with a very specific prompt that we optimized, and we know that over history it was very successful in fixing a certain category of bugs, then we develop trust in that particular agent configuration. His advice to other maintainers follows directly — consider having your own agents and your own setup, and not necessarily trusting the community. That's the whole inversion. A configured agent with a track record is now a more trustworthy contributor than an anonymous human with none.

Fred Schott, who created Astro, described what that did to a five-year-old problem. For five years, he told Latent Space, issues came in faster than they could handle them. With agents doing triage and reproduction, and getting the reporter to verify the suggested fix before a maintainer even looks, that reversed into something he says he had never seen in a decade-plus of open source: treating issues as a thing you prioritize every week rather than a backlog you constantly trim. His new agent framework, Flue, goes further and automatically closes every external pull request, converting bug fixes into issues and feature requests into discussions, explicitly to stop what its guide calls drive-by AI slop pull requests. The drawing tool T-L-draw does the same, and its creator Steve Ruiz put the logic bluntly: it makes less sense to have people contributing code if the issue is decently well-specified and the code can be written by agents.

The best part is that Schott names the cost himself. Pull request review was never only about the code — it was the apprenticeship pipeline, how maintainers taught contributors and evaluated who might become one. Close the pipe and you've optimized the present at the expense of succession. His own formulation: if you just keep narrowing the project, at a certain point, you and I go on vacation — what happens? Ruiz offers the partial answer, which is to limit community contribution to the places it still matters — reporting, discussion, perspective, and care.

And here is where today's stories collide, which is why I saved it for last. Curl is the project most famous for drowning in AI-generated garbage security reports — its maintainer publishes the slop statistics. And the single most valuable inbound contribution curl received in the last two weeks came from an AI system run by an outside company. Both are true, so the variable separating them isn't human versus machine. It's whether the sender bears a cost for being wrong. AISLE attached its company name and its central technical claim to twenty-nine reports and got six accepted. A drive-by pull request risks nothing. Grammel's trust argument is that same argument in engineering clothes: a track record is just accumulated exposure to being wrong in public.

That generalizes straight into Ollie's world. As agent-generated manuscripts and agent-generated reviews arrive in biomedical science, the sorting mechanism that will actually work is not detection. It is making submitters carry reputational weight — which is exactly what a published vulnerability credit does and what an anonymous pull request does not.

Here's the one thing to watch tomorrow. The Information reported Wednesday morning that Anthropic and Google DeepMind are already discussing recurrent depth internally. Watch whether either says anything on the record. Every gate the industry built this week — the vetted cyber tiers, the two-door safeguard levels, the incident forensics that justified all of it — assumes somebody can still read the trace. If that assumption is being renegotiated at three labs at once, it's the most consequential architecture decision of the year, and so far the only people discussing it in public don't work at the labs making it.
