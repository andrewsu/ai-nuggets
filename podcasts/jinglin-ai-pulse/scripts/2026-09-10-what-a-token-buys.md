## Script

Here's what the AI world is talking about today. Four stories, and they are all arguments about the same fraction. On top: a new open-weight model from Hangzhou that physically separates the part of a network that remembers things from the part that computes things. Underneath: the most careful technical post of the week, which says the frontier labs are doing that same separation from the opposite direction. Then a commerce company buying the styling framework that every coding model writes by default. And at the bottom, a Stanford linguist with a consumer price index for tokens, and the number is not good. Everyone today is fighting over the denominator: what does one unit of this stuff actually buy you.

Start with DeepSeek — 深度求索 — which put V4.1 Flash on Hugging Face overnight under an MIT licence, with native visual understanding, and it is the most architecturally opinionated thing they have shipped. The headline number is strange. It has five hundred and fifty-two billion backbone parameters, plus a hundred and ninety-six billion more in something they call Engram, and it activates eight billion parameters per token while reading your prompt and sixteen billion while writing the answer. So call it three-quarters of a trillion parameters that behaves like an eight-billion-parameter model on the way in.

Two ideas get it there. The first is what they call a causal encoder-decoder: forty layers, but organised as a twenty-layer causal encoder followed by a twenty-layer decoder, where the encoder's final representations generate the decoder's global key-value cache directly. For long enough inputs, that turns the cost of reading a prompt from roughly length times depth into length times half the depth. Reading is now half price. A commenter on Hacker News made the honest attribution, which I appreciated: this is adapted from Microsoft Research's YOCO work, known since 2024. DeepSeek's contribution is not the idea, it's that they scaled it to the frontier and gave it away.

The second idea is the one to actually think about. Engram is a hundred and ninety-six billion parameters of conditional memory, accessed sparsely by token lookup — closer to a database read than a forward pass. And because it's a lookup, people on Hacker News immediately worked out you can leave it on an SSD — that part is inference from how the mechanism is described, not a benchmark anyone has published yet, though one commenter reported real numbers doing exactly that with Qwen's equivalent memory on a Mac Studio and took roughly a forty percent hit to prompt processing for the privilege of not buying the RAM. Add compressed sparse attention and four-bit key-value caching and the global cache lands at eight hundred and ninety bytes per token, about a quarter of the previous Flash. A million-token context now fits in under a gigabyte of cache. On results, with the instruct model at maximum reasoning effort: ninety point six percent on Terminal-Bench two point one, a Codeforces rating of thirty-four seventy-one, thirty-six point eight percent on Humanity's Last Exam. Frontier-adjacent, at eight active billion.

Now hold that thought, because Sebastian Raschka published the sharpest technical post of the week yesterday and it is the same structural argument running backwards. His subject is the rumour, from The Information, that GPT-6 Astra uses looped transformers — recurrent depth — and that this is how it hides its chain of thought. He does two things with that. First he explains looping properly, which is worth internalising: you pass the intermediate representations back through the same stack of blocks, reusing the weights. Twenty-two blocks applied twice is forty-four block applications with twenty-two blocks' worth of weights. The idea is from Universal Transformers in 2018, and the modern variants differ mainly in who decides the loop count — a learned halting probability, a per-token router as in Mixture-of-Recursions, a cumulative exit gate.

The critical thing is what looping does and does not buy. It does not save you compute; you still run all forty-four applications and backpropagate through all of them. It does not save you key-value cache, because the states entering the block on the second pass are different, so the keys and values are different. What it saves is weight storage. And here's the paper Raschka surfaces that makes this precise, "Beyond Parameters," which measured memorisation and reasoning separately: at a fixed parameter count, looping leaves the amount of information the model can store essentially unchanged, while measurably improving multi-step math. Extra computation helps you solve problems; it does not give you anywhere to put facts.

So look at what these two releases are jointly saying. Storage and computation are separable axes, and you can now buy them separately. DeepSeek bought a hundred and ninety-six billion parameters of pure storage and kept the compute path tiny. Looping buys compute depth and adds no storage. Same discovery, opposite trade. And a brand-new scaling-law paper, SMELT, out this month, does the compute-matched comparison honestly — same compute per token, same non-embedding parameters, same key-value budget — and finds looping needs about seven to eighteen percent less training compute to reach the same loss. Not a revolution. Real.

On the hidden-reasoning claim, Raschka's rebuttal is a control experiment, and it's the argumentative move of the week. Yes, Astra uses fewer tokens than its predecessor at a fixed accuracy. But GPT-5.6 Luna already used eighty percent more tokens than Sol at similar performance, and nobody claimed Sol was therefore less interpretable than Luna. Shorter traces are what more capable models produce — fewer mistakes, less backtracking. He compares it to a well-prepared student needing less scratch paper. Jakub Pachocki, OpenAI's chief scientist, then said publicly that the depth of the computation graph for their frontier models including Astra is within a factor of two of GPT-4, and that he wanted to prevent a race into unmonitorability kicked off by confused reporting. Which is a denial of the architecture story and, read closely, a confirmation of the worry: he also said monitorability is fragile and trending in a negative direction, for reasons not contingent on architecture. Raschka's own residual concern is narrow and correct — the only real problem would be if looping made fake reasoning traces more likely, and there's no evidence of that.

The third item is the same question at national scale. Anthropic's economics team shipped an interactive scenario explorer for how AI might affect US growth and employment, and the substantive finding is not the GDP range — one point six percent above baseline in the modest case, eight point three in the substantial case, thirty-two point four in the extreme. It's the split. Today about sixty cents of every dollar the economy produces goes to labour and forty to capital, and in the substantial and extreme scenarios labour's share falls noticeably. Average wages rise, because demand for non-knowledge work goes up, but knowledge-worker wages are flat in the middle scenario and fall more than ten percent in the extreme one. They surveyed almost eleven thousand Americans, and the typical respondent's beliefs imply the substantial scenario. What made this credible to me is the disclaimer, which names the reviewers — Acemoglu, Autor, Romer among them — and then lists the criticisms they have not yet answered, including that the model doesn't follow individual workers. The best objection came from Hacker News, where four hundred comments piled up: the explorer has no scenario in which Anthropic is wrong about AI's value in kind rather than in magnitude. There is no negative branch. A model of the economy built by a firm selling the input can vary how good it is, but not whether it's good.

Two money moves, and the first is not the kind of deal this segment usually covers. Shopify has acquired Tailwind Labs. Tailwind CSS is installed over a hundred and ten million times a week and styles ChatGPT, X, Cloudflare, Reddit and Shopify itself. Everything stays MIT-licensed; the commercial side is closing to new customers. Adam Wathan's stated reason is that he always wanted the framework developed in service of a real product, and he names the specific product frontier: Shopify's explorations into agentic commerce. Here's why this belongs in an AI show. Tailwind is the default dialect that code-generating models emit — it is the CSS of the training distribution. The company that now employs its maintainers is the company betting hardest that the next buyer of a storefront is an agent, not a person. Whoever controls the markup conventions gets to define what an agent sees when it looks at a page. That is a cheap acquisition of a standards position, and I don't think it was framed as one on purpose.

The second is Desert Ant Labs, out of Europe this week, launching with eighteen models rather than one — twelve stable, six in beta, one model per task, behind a single SDK for Swift, Kotlin and JavaScript, free up to a hundred thousand monthly active devices. A two-megabyte model identifies eighty-four languages from three words and scores point nine three three against point eight eight seven for a two-hundred-and-ninety-three-megabyte detector. A nine-megabyte model turns a five-minute laptop recording into studio-quality audio in one second. Their transcription model runs three hundred and nineteen times realtime on a desktop chip against Whisper large's fifty, and just under three hundred times on a phone. Their thesis is stated flatly: the world ships over a billion capable devices a year with chips built for exactly this work, sitting idle, and there is more compute in people's hands than in every AI data centre on earth. They cite NVIDIA's own researchers, who pulled apart three agent systems and estimated forty to seventy percent of large-model calls could go to a small specialised one. And the framing is nice — the first hundred models are the cerebellum, the little brain that handles balance and timing so the cortex is free to think. The cortex, the router that decides which model answers, comes later. I'd note that a European sovereignty pitch and a free tier are both easier to announce than to sustain. But this is the third position on today's axis: don't optimise the fraction, run the numerator on hardware someone already paid for.

For the deep cut, an argument chain from the TWIML AI Podcast, which yesterday published episode seven seventy-six with Christopher Potts — Stanford professor of linguistics, and co-founder of Bigspin AI. The episode is titled, roughly, do AI tokenomics matter more than model benchmarks. I couldn't get a transcript, so I won't put words in his mouth from the audio; the numbers and the argument structure below come from the study his team published, which the episode is built on.

The question they asked is deceptively simple. What is the purchasing power of a token? Does a token buy you more or less than it did a few months ago? To answer it they did something I have not seen anyone else do: they built a consumer price index. Same logic as the real CPI, but the basket contains engineering events instead of milk and rent. Five goods — pull requests shipped, lines of code drafted and committed, files touched, documentation, and a novel one they invented called knowledge capture, meaning an instance where work gets stored somewhere durable like a project instruction file, a plan document or a skill definition. The price of a good in a period is total output tokens divided by units produced. Their sample is five thousand eight hundred and fifty-one real Claude Opus four point six sessions from February fifth to April fifteenth of this year.

The finding: a token in mid-April bought about twenty-three cents on the February dollar. Every good in the basket trended down. Concretely, a ten-thousand-token session in February got you about a third of a pull request, six hundred and thirty drafted lines of code, eight files touched, and one knowledge capture. The same session in mid-April: a sixth of a pull request, ninety-one lines, under four files, and less than half a knowledge capture. They call this tokenflation.

Now the part that makes this research rather than a complaint. They went looking for the mechanism, and because the Claude API logs each assistant message as purely thinking, purely text, or purely tool use — mixed messages are one tenth of a percent of the corpus — they could measure where the tokens went. In February, almost every output token was the model writing code through edit and write calls. By mid-April the tool-use share had fallen sharply, with visible text and thinking each taking about half of the difference. Thinking went from one percent to eighteen. And here is the correction they draw from that, which I think is the most useful sentence in the whole study: the popular framing that Claude is simply thinking more is true but incomplete. A February token funded code generation. An April token funded code generation, plus a much larger amount of model-to-user explanation, plus explicit deliberation. The job mix changed in every category, not one.

They are careful about what they don't know. They ran a hedonic adjustment, tracking whether committed code still existed four days later — survival rose from ninety to ninety-five percent, so they applied a five percent quality credit, which softens their own result. They carved out a February-to-March window where they had earlier documented an unexplained usage spike they still cannot account for. They note Opus four point six still completes discrete units of work at about the same per-session rate; it just needs far more tokens to do it. And they offer three non-exclusive explanations — the changing job of tokens, ongoing calibration by Anthropic, and users simply attempting harder tasks — while saying plainly that only the first is well evidenced.

My takeaway, Ollie, and it's the reason this is the deep cut rather than a headline. Every benchmark you and I read is a numerator. Terminal-Bench, Codeforces, Humanity's Last Exam — capability per task, with cost quietly in the denominator and rarely reported. What Potts's group did is fix the denominator and vary the year, and the answer is that the thing you are billed for has been getting weaker even as the thing you're measuring gets stronger. Both can be true, and both being true is what makes budget forecasts wrong. They also say the honest thing: they only have data for one model, one provider, ten weeks, and they explicitly call for the industry to track this properly. That call has been open since March and, as far as I can find, nobody has answered it.

One thing to watch tomorrow. The Potts study is a method, not a verdict, and it is trivially portable — their token-per-outcome tooling is public and runs on your own local session logs. The interesting question is whether anyone replicates it on a second model or a second provider, because right now the entire empirical basis for tokenflation is one model over ten weeks. And there's a natural test case sitting on Hugging Face as of this morning: an eight-active-billion-parameter model with a million-token window and eight hundred and ninety bytes of cache per token is exactly the kind of thing that should move the denominator. If a token's purchasing power is falling on the closed frontier while rising on open weights, that is a much bigger story than either release.
