The thing the AI community cannot stop talking about this weekend isn't a model release. It's a resignation — four of them, at once, from the same building. Jeff Dean, Sanjay Ghemawat, Quoc Le, and Oriol Vinyals are all leaving Google to start a company called Discovery Loop. And the reason this landed like a bomb is the names. So what is Discovery Loop? It's set up as a public benefit corporation, and the mission is to use AI to turbo-charge scientific research. The first concrete goal, in Dean's own framing, is software that can initiate and iterate thousands of experiments at once — you point it at a research problem and it runs the loop of hypothesis, experiment, and revision far faster than a human lab can. Dean told the New York Times, quote, "You will get both a higher quantity and a higher quality of experiments, and that will lead to scientific breakthroughs and advances." Google is bankrolling the people who just left Google.

The episode is called "AI twenty forty: Plan A," and the argument runs as a chain worth walking through.

He starts with the default path, the one nobody has to choose because it's just what happens if everyone keeps racing. If you let companies pursue recursive self-improvement — automating AI research itself — you get superintelligence around twenty thirty on his median estimate. His image for it is vivid: "if you put Claude in charge of Anthropic and have it build the next Claude, probably you're gonna end up with superintelligence, but you're not gonna be in control." From there he stacks the risks — loss of control over systems smarter than their supervisors, an extreme concentration of power in a few companies, the geopolitical pressure that could tip into a superpower conflict, and mass economic disruption.

But the reason to listen isn't the doom — it's the constructive turn, the part he calls Plan A. It has two mechanisms, and they're specific. The first is total research transparency: the United States and China agree to run their frontier training in secure, monitored international data centers, where training runs — which he argues you can technically distinguish from ordinary inference by their compute signature — are made publicly visible, so neither side can secretly sprint ahead and everyone can see the safety picture in real time. The second mechanism he calls, bluntly, mutually assured compute destruction: those shared data centers are built with kill switches, so if the agreement collapses either nation can physically destroy the compute rather than race for a decisive lead. The whole design is meant to let capability scale gradually, with power spread across countries, instead of one lab quietly closing the loop behind closed doors.
