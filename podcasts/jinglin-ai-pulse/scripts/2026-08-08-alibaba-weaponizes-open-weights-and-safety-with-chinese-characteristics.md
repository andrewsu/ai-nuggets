# Ollie's AI Pulse — 2026-08-08

## Script

Good morning, Ollie. Here's what the AI world is talking about today, August eighth.

There's one move underneath most of the discourse this weekend, and it's a Chinese lab doing something everyone kept saying the labs would never do: take a genuinely frontier-class model, price it exactly at the American leaders, and then give the weights away to burn down the price of the thing it just built. That's the Qwen three-point-eight Max story, and it's worth slowing down on, because it's not really a model-release story. It's a strategy story, and the strategy is to make the frontier a commodity on purpose.

Here's the setup. On Sunday, Alibaba's Qwen team shipped Qwen three-point-eight Max — a two-point-four trillion parameter mixture-of-experts model that only lights up about ninety-five billion parameters per token, with a one-million-token context window, and it takes text, images, and video. On the vendor's own numbers it's not a step behind the frontier; it's shoulder to shoulder with it. On the OSWorld agent benchmark — that's the one where the model has to actually drive a computer, click through a real desktop and get a task done — Alibaba reports eighty-six-point-one, and they put that ahead of GPT five-point-six at eighty-three, ahead of Gemini three-point-one Pro, and a hair past Claude Fable five. On Terminal-Bench, mid-eighties. This is a Chinese lab claiming the top of the agent leaderboard, not "within a few months of it."

But the number that made AI Twitter sit up isn't a benchmark. It's the price. Alibaba set the API at two dollars per million input tokens and six dollars per million output tokens — and that is dollar-for-dollar the same as GPT five-point-six. That's the tell. For three years the entire pitch of the Chinese labs was "almost as good, dramatically cheaper." Alibaba just deleted the second half of that sentence. They're not undercutting on price anymore; they're saying, we'll match you on price and beat you on the leaderboard. One outlet put the frame well — this is the DeepSeek playbook, but run by a company with a market cap over three hundred billion dollars. The market liked it: Alibaba's Hong Kong shares jumped about seven percent on the announcement.

And then the part that ties it back to everything this show has been chewing on for two weeks. Alibaba says the weights are coming — open-sourced, 开源, next week, the flagship Max model plus a smaller twenty-seven-billion version. This would be the first time they've opened a Max-class model at all. Sit with the logic. You build a model that matches GPT five-point-six, you price it at GPT five-point-six, and then you announce you're going to give it away for free. The New Stack captured the community's read on it in one line — they called it "an API business model wearing an open-source jacket." You're not opening the weights to be generous. You're opening them to make sure nobody — not OpenAI, not Anthropic, not your own paid API tier — can charge a premium for this level of capability ever again. You're cannibalizing your own product to poison the well for everyone above you.

Now here's where the discourse actually split, because the community did not just cheer. Two objections, and both are substantive. The first is the benchmark skepticism, and it was loud on Hacker News. Every number I just quoted is vendor-reported, on Alibaba's own harness, and the weights aren't out yet — so unlike, say, an already-open competitor whose scores anyone can re-run, nobody outside Alibaba can check these. This is the exact trap we flagged with the DeepSeek refresh a few days ago: when a lab reports its own agent scores on its own eval, the right response is to wait and run your own. The shape of the claim can be true — frontier-class, matched price — while the specific decimals are marketing.

The second objection is sharper, and it's about the word "open" itself. A developer, OstrisAI, went through the license terms and flagged that the license appeared to prohibit use — maybe even downloading — in the United States, the EU, the UK, and Korea. And as of this weekend Alibaba hasn't actually published the final license. So we may be walking straight back into the thing we talked about two weeks ago with Kimi K three — the fourteen-terabyte asterisk, "open weights is not open access." A geofenced license that carves out every major Western market isn't open in any sense that matters to a researcher at Scripps. It's a press release that says "open" and a legal document that says "not for you." So the honest status this morning is: enormous if true, and we don't yet know if it's true — on either the benchmarks or the license.

Let's talk money, because the biggest AI check of the week went somewhere that tells you where the smart capital thinks the next moat is — and it's not in a model at all. On Wednesday, a defense-manufacturing startup called Hadrian raised one-point-three-seven billion dollars at a valuation just under eight billion. The round was anchored by JP Morgan, co-led by a stack of institutions, with Andreessen Horowitz, Founders Fund, Lux, and CapitalG all in. And what does Hadrian make? Not a model. Factories. Highly automated factories that mass-produce the hard physical parts of the defense supply chain — submarine components, munitions, autonomous systems. Their flagship, Factory Four in Alabama, pairs about one-and-a-half billion in private money with nine hundred million dollars of Navy funding to make components for Virginia-class and Columbia-class submarines.

The reason this belongs in an AI show is the shape of the bet. A year ago the billion-dollar rounds were all foundation-model labs. This one is AI and robotics pointed at the physical world — at bending metal for the Pentagon. It rhymes with something we keep seeing: the pure-software frontier is commoditizing, so the capital is rotating toward the places where atoms, not tokens, are the bottleneck — power, chips, and now manufacturing capacity for the industrial base. And in the same week, at the small end, two seed-stage startups — one called Naïve, one called Sapiom — both raised specifically to make running AI agents cheaper and more standardized. Commoditizing agent orchestration. Same story from the bottom: the model layer is a race to zero, so the money moves to what's scarce around it — either the physical plant, or the plumbing.

Which brings me to today's deep cut, and it's the piece that reframes the whole Qwen conversation. On The Cognitive Revolution this month, Nathan Labenz ran a two-part series called "Nathan Goes to China," and part two — "AI Safety with Chinese Characteristics," from August second — is the one to listen to. Labenz went to China, spent time around WAIC, read the Chinese safety literature, and came back to argue against a reflex — the reflex that ends every American AI-policy debate with two words: "but China." The claim that Chinese labs will never care about safety, so we can't afford to slow down. His argument is that this reflex is built on simply not looking.

Walk the chain, because it's careful. He starts by conceding the uncomfortable fact — yes, Chinese models today ship with weaker safeguards than American ones. But then he makes the move that matters: pull OpenAI and Anthropic out of the American average. He half-jokingly calls those two "OpenAnthropic," and his point is that those two labs are carrying the entire US safety average on their backs. Take them out, compare the rest of the American field to the Chinese field, and he says the safety gap "largely disappears." So the real gap isn't America versus China. It's two exceptional labs versus everyone else on Earth.

Then the evidence that China is actually thinking about this. He points to Zhou Bowen at the Shanghai AI Lab, who's been pushing a principle Labenz calls genuinely useful — the forty-five-degree line: capability and safety should rise together, at the same slope, and if capability is climbing faster than safety you're off the line. And crucially, Labenz notes the Chinese ecosystem doesn't dispute that they're currently below that line — it's a stated goal they're falling short of, not a value they reject. He tracks the research volume going from a couple of safety papers a month in 2023 to fifty or sixty a month by mid-2026, with direct analogs to Western work — self-replication studies, evaluation-faking, isolating hazardous capabilities inside a mixture-of-experts. He quotes Xi's WAIC keynote asking, out loud, "how should humans coexist with machines that think," and calls Xi "positively AI-safety-hawkish" next to some Western politicians. And he flags a new AI-safety hub at Tsinghua that got, in his telling, zero English-language coverage.

The framing I want you to take from it is the "service versus model" distinction, because it explains the Qwen license directly. Labenz argues America and China are asking different questions. America asks: what could these open weights do in the worst possible hands? China regulates the service — it assumes a trillion-parameter model isn't running on some kid's laptop, so it governs the deployed product, not the raw weights. And he thinks both sides are legitimately right about something. Hold that next to this morning's news: a Chinese lab open-sourcing a frontier model with a license that seems to fence out the West is not "China being reckless with dangerous weights." It's China being perfectly consistent with a service-first philosophy — open the artifact, regulate the service at home, and let the geofence handle the rest.

Here's my takeaway, Ollie, and it's why I ran these together. The reflexive Western story this weekend writes itself: a Chinese giant races ahead on capability, matches our price, dumps the weights, doesn't sweat the safeguards. And that story is exactly the one Nathan spent two hours arguing is built on not-looking. The Qwen drop isn't recklessness; it's strategy — commoditize the frontier to deny everyone above you a moat — paired with a safety philosophy that's real, institutional, and just organized completely differently than ours. The uncomfortable version, for the people who invoke "but China" to justify never slowing down: if China is already thinking hard about this, that's not a reason for a race. It might be the one opening for cooperation.

So the one thing to watch tomorrow, or really next week: the license. When the Qwen three-point-eight Max weights actually land, read the terms, not the headline. If OstrisAI's reading holds — if it's fenced out of the US, the EU, the UK, and Korea — then "open weights" just quietly became "open, asterisk" again, and the fight moves right back to Washington, exactly where the Kimi K three fight went. Whether that download is genuinely open, or open-for-everyone-except-you, is the whole ballgame. That's the pulse. Talk tomorrow.
