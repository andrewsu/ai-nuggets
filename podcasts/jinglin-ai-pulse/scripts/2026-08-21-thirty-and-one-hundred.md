## Script

Here's what the AI world is talking about today. Claude Opus five scored about thirty on a benchmark. Claude Opus five also scored one hundred on the same benchmark. Same model, same public task set, two different wrappers around it — and the wrapper that got the perfect score was the one that refused to look at the pictures. That result landed this morning from NVIDIA. Also this morning, DeepSeek shipped a model whose entire headline is that looking at the pictures is what made its agent better. Two labs, one day, opposite conclusions about where the capability lives. Let's take that seriously.

Start with NVIDIA, because the number is the loudest thing on the internet right now and it is being repeated without its caveats. The benchmark is ARC-AGI-three, the interactive one — an agent is dropped into an unfamiliar game-like environment with no instructions, no stated rules, and no stated goal. It gets a list of available actions and nothing describing what they do. It has to poke the world, infer the dynamics, and work out what winning even means. The metric is called relative human action efficiency, and the important word is efficiency: you don't score by finishing, you score by finishing in roughly the number of moves a first-time human player needed.

NVIDIA's system is called AVO, agentic variation operators, and here is the part that makes it interesting rather than just impressive. AVO was not built for this. It was built to optimize GPU kernels. In their attention-kernel study, AVO ran continuously for seven days, explored more than five hundred optimization directions, and committed forty kernel versions. The resulting multi-head attention kernels beat cuDNN by up to three and a half percent and beat FlashAttention four by up to ten and a half percent on DGX B-two-hundred systems. Then the agent adapted its evolved kernel to grouped-query attention in about thirty more minutes of unsupervised work. That is a real engineering result, produced by a loop nobody was steering.

Then they pointed the same agent at ARC-AGI-three. Same agent backend. Only the task interface and the evaluation changed. It scored one hundred point zero across all twenty-five environments in the public set, clearing all one hundred eighty-three levels in six thousand six hundred twenty-four environment actions.

Now the comparisons, in order of how much you should trust them. The cleanest is against VISTA, another recent ARC-AGI-three harness also running Claude Opus five: VISTA needed seven thousand five hundred forty-two actions for the same levels. AVO used about twelve percent fewer. Same model, same levels, different scaffolding — and NVIDIA is careful to say this is not a controlled ablation, since the two systems differ in backend, observation format, memory, and context management all at once.

The louder comparison is the shakier one. ARC Prize itself measured Claude Opus five on the public set at about thirty percent, at high reasoning effort. NVIDIA's run used the same model family at a different reasoning setting inside a completely different agent system, so thirty to one hundred is not a measurement of what AVO adds. NVIDIA says this in the post, in plain language. And the whole result is on the twenty-five-environment public set — not the semi-private set, not the private competition set. Anyone quoting "NVIDIA solved ARC-AGI-three" today is quoting the headline and skipping the paragraph underneath it.

But strip the overclaim out and something genuinely strange is left, and it's about representation, not scale. VISTA's primary configuration hands the model a rendered five-twelve by five-twelve P-N-G of the game state. AVO sent no images at all. The model operated text-only, receiving each observation as an exact sixty-four by sixty-four text grid — zero image tokens. And that is the configuration that hit human action efficiency.

Which brings us to DeepSeek, publishing the same day and arguing the reverse. V-four-Flash-Vision-Exp is now live on their API — experimental, and exactly what the name says: their cheap agent model with eyes bolted on. Eighty-three point nine on Terminal-Bench two-point-one, fifty-nine point three on DeepSWE, sixty-four point three on Chartography. Their claim has two halves. On pure text it's on par with the text-only V-four-Flash — no multimodal tax, which is the thing that usually happens when you graft vision onto a language model. And on agent benchmarks that need visual understanding, they say it's a significant leap, bringing multimodal agent capability close to Opus four-point-eight. Bloomberg wrote it up as a test model aimed squarely at Opus, which is the right read of the intent.

Here is the number in that table nobody is pointing at. On Agents' Last Exam, the vision model scores twenty-seven point three. DeepSeek's own flagship, V-four-Pro, which went generally available eight days ago and beats this model everywhere else — eighty-seven point nine on Terminal-Bench, sixty-two point seven on DeepSWE — scores twenty-five point seven on Agents' Last Exam. The cheap model beats the flagship. And the footnote tells you exactly why: on Agents' Last Exam and ApexBench, the text-only models simply ignore the multimodal elements inside the tasks. They're not failing those items, they're not seeing them. So the small model wins by being the only one in the room with eyes.

Put NVIDIA and DeepSeek side by side and the apparent contradiction resolves into something useful. NVIDIA won by throwing away pixels; DeepSeek won by adding them. Both are right, because the question was never "is vision good." ARC-AGI-three's state is already discrete and tiny — a sixty-four by sixty-four grid of cells. Rendering that as a half-megapixel image and asking a vision encoder to recover the grid is a lossy round trip through a representation that costs more and tells you less. Agents' Last Exam and Chartography are the opposite regime: the information genuinely lives in pixels, and a text model is blind by construction. The engineering question for anyone building agents is not which modality is better. It's whether your environment's state has a faithful symbolic form. If it does, send that. If it doesn't, you need the encoder.

Third thing, and it's the one the community can't stop poking at. On Wednesday a model called Ox Alpha appeared on OpenRouter under the provider name "Stealth" — no company, no announcement, nobody claiming it. The spec sheet is not modest: a one-million-token context window, a hundred thirty-one thousand token maximum output, text, image and video input, tool calling. It's billed as a reasoning model for coding, sustained agentic work, and production workloads. The price is zero. The provider says it retains prompts but does not train on them — an unusual promise for an anonymous release — and OpenCode says it's free for a week at effectively unlimited rates.

The detective work is the fun part. A developer going by dax ran tokenizer fingerprinting across twenty-five prompts and found Ox Alpha's raw token counts lined up almost exactly with G-L-M five point three, off by a constant seventy-five-token wrapper every time. That points at Z-dot-A-I, formerly Zhipu. The competing theory is Xiaomi's MiMo team, who have run stealth tests on OpenRouter before under names like Hunter and Healer Alpha, and whose spec trajectory fits. Nobody has confirmed anything. For Ollie specifically, note the shape of this: a frontier-tier agentic coding model with a million-token window, given away for free by a lab that won't put its name on it, one day before DeepSeek's vision release. Whatever the model tier is worth, it is not being priced like a moat.

To the money, and two numbers worth carrying. The first is Etched, which raised seven hundred million dollars at a twenty-one billion dollar valuation, reported Tuesday. The trajectory is the story: about five billion in December, ten point three billion in July, twenty-one billion a month later. Jane Street led the round and is also the company's first paying customer — your lead investor is your revenue. Etched's product is Sohu, an A-S-I-C on four-nanometer T-S-M-C that runs transformer inference and nothing else. No flexibility, no other architectures. That's a bet that the transformer is now permanent enough to etch into silicon, repriced two-x in four weeks on the strength of one delivered rack.

The second is smaller and, I think, more informative. TechCrunch reported Thursday that micro1, a four-year-old A-I data company, took its gross annual run rate from a hundred million to five hundred million in eight months. It keeps about sixty to seventy percent, so net is somewhere between a hundred fifty and two hundred million, and it is still the third-place player — Mercor crossed two billion in gross annualized revenue this summer, Handshake crossed one billion earlier this year. So the market for training data is not just alive, it's compounding. But read the margin structure, because that's where the actual claim is. Micro1's best economics come from data it generates synthetically with no human in the loop — things like automated descriptions of video content — which it can then sell to multiple customers at eighty to ninety percent gross margin. The data business is growing by removing the humans from the data business. Hold onto that, because it is about to become the crux of a debate.

For the deep cut, the Dwarkesh Podcast on the eleventh, with Ryan Greenblatt of Redwood Research, on what happens once AI can automate AI research. The argument chain is worth reconstructing because it's a debate, not an interview.

Greenblatt's mechanism is containerized reinforcement learning environments that train the A-I R and D task directly. His concrete example: take a near-future model, then build containerized versions of the job of getting that model to develop GPT-two-sized models — small training runs, hyperparameter search, algorithmic exploration — and imagine a hundred more environments like it. Put the model through that and the thing that comes out is an excellent M-L researcher with real intuition. His medians: full automation of A-I R and D around twenty thirty-one, and beats-all-humans-at-any-job around twenty thirty-three — and if he actually saw A-I R and D fully automated, he'd expect the second milestone within about a year.

The load-bearing and most contestable claim is about data. Dwarkesh presses him on the deca-billion-dollar industry that has been codifying expert human judgment into R-L environments and supervised traces. Greenblatt's answer: "scaling up the amount of effort spent on getting expert human data has not been hugely important for A-I R and D in general." He'd bet that removing the last two doublings of expert-human data generation would not make a huge difference. What actually improved, he says, is people finding better ways to leverage humans and A-Is to construct the environments themselves. The bottleneck is environment construction, not expert labeling.

Which is where micro1's margin table comes in, because it arbitrates this better than either of them could in the studio. Mercor at two billion says Dwarkesh is right that the expert-data industry is enormous and still growing. But micro1's eighty-to-ninety-percent line is on the synthetic, human-free, resell-to-everyone product — the humans are the low-margin part. The revenue says expert data is valuable; the margins say the frontier of the data business has already moved to constructing data without experts. That is Greenblatt's claim, showing up in someone's P-and-L rather than in a forecast.

Dwarkesh's counter is a transfer argument, and he names the crux honestly as an empirical question. Models are getting rapidly better at getting up to speed and sustaining progress in verifiable domains. How much of that transfers to "go talk to the president and convince him to do X," or "you're now in charge of Google, make it much more profitable"? As horizons lengthen, the tasks you can containerize stay short. The genuinely long ones cannot be containerized given the nature of the task — running a business is not a production environment.

And then Greenblatt volunteers his own downside, which is the best thing in the episode. He doesn't describe failure as the A-Is being too weak. He calls it a "sloppocalypse, or a slopularity" — the most verifiable parts of A-I R and D, the A-Is are, in his words, just destroying. The medium-verifiable parts they do well but weirdly, doing "a bit of weird shit," and people find hacks and work around it. That's enough to make A-I R and D go quite fast. But the parts of building aligned and safe systems that are subtle, hard to check, and depend on in-the-weeds detail — those never get the feedback loop, and he suspects current staff at current A-I companies don't fully have a grip on them either. Capability and safety don't fail together; capability races ahead precisely because it's the part you can grade.

Ollie's takeaway, and this is why I put the two stories in one episode. AVO is the good half of the sloppocalypse, made concrete and dated. Seven days unsupervised, five hundred optimization directions, a kernel that beats FlashAttention four — that is A-I R and D automation in the single most verifiable slice that exists, and it worked, today, on hardware. Greenblatt's timeline for that band looks less like a forecast and more like a description. But look at what NVIDIA had to build to get there: a supervisor module whose entire job is monitoring the trajectory for stagnation and repeated unproductive cycles, and redirecting the main agent when the search plateaus. That component exists because the agent cannot reliably tell when it is spinning. That is Greenblatt's medium-verifiable band, and NVIDIA's answer was to hard-code an outside observer. Which works fine when the failure is "no progress on a measurable objective." It is not obvious what you write there when the failure is "quiet, plausible, wrong."

One thing to watch tomorrow. Two, actually, and they're the same question. Whether anyone reproduces AVO's one hundred on the semi-private set, because public-set-only is doing a lot of load-bearing work in that headline. And whether Ox Alpha gets a name — if dax's tokenizer fingerprint holds and it is G-L-M five point three under the hood, then a Chinese lab just put a million-token frontier agentic coder on the open internet for free, anonymously, and the interesting question stops being who built it and becomes why they'd rather you not know.
