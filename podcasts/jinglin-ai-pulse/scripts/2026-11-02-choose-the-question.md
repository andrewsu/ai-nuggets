## Script

Here's what the AI world is talking about today. It's Monday, November second. And the three things most worth your time from the weekend all ask the same question from different directions: once the model does the work, what is left for the human? A number theorist answers, choose the question. An open-source reproduction answers, check the checker. And a wealth-management startup answers, move the money, and nothing else. Let's go through them.

The sharpest piece of the weekend was a guest post on Terence Tao's blog, and I want to be precise about authorship because several people in the Hacker News thread got it wrong: Tao hosted it, but it was written by Frank Calegari, a number theorist at the University of Chicago. Tao's one-line editor's note is itself a small sign of the times. The post, he says, was initially written in a different file format and converted using AI.

Calegari starts from OpenAI's release on October sixth of more than seven hundred research papers, which we covered when it landed. He calls it a watershed, and he doesn't hedge on the quality problem either. The papers, in his words, "are atrociously written and violate our community expectations of attribution. Some of them are even wrong." But his argument is that mathematicians have far more control over how they hire, publish, and train students than over trillion-dollar companies, so that's where the effort belongs.

He quotes Hugo Duminil-Copin, the Fields medalist, whose lament has been going around: "Not a single one of the major open problems I have publicly mentioned throughout my career was left untouched by the announcement. Everything has been claimed to be proved." Calegari's answer is a historical one. When the classification of finite simple groups was announced in the early eighties, the famous problem went away and a lot of mathematicians drifted elsewhere, even though rich questions remained. For some fields today, he writes, it's as if a group theorist in nineteen seventy had the whole classification "dumped in their office," printed on a spirit duplicator, "smudged a little, then spread randomly like confetti all over the floor." The job now is the unglamorous one Gorenstein, Lyons and Solomon did: put the confetti back together into understanding.

Then the line that's being quoted most: "Choosing worthwhile problems may end up mattering more than producing proofs." He says someone who picks a worthwhile problem, gets a proof entirely from AI, understands it thoroughly, and uses it to open new directions has made a substantial contribution, "even if they supplied no new ingredient in the original proof." And he has a pointed one for senior faculty: "If you, as a senior mathematician, are stressed about being scooped by AI, then you are not being ambitious enough."

What makes this more than an op-ed is that he proposes mechanisms, because, as he puts it, think pieces are pointless unless they change how people act. Evaluate all work as if it is AI-assisted. Interview postdocs and even graduate students in person rather than reading a paper file. Demand more from journals, and abandon those that don't adapt. And the one he admits is most controversial: "I see no reason to reward a paper based purely on a result that could be produced by machine, whether it was or not."

The Hacker News pushback was worth hearing. A commenter called parodysbird put the supporting case better than the post did: every step of a proof is equally proved; what makes a problem is the choice of starting point, and that's "a normative choice, which is to say it is a social choice." But ethanlipson asked the obvious next question: why does nearly every essay on AI and math assume capabilities stop at theorem proving? Choosing terrain, steering, explaining, "these all seem totally achievable." That's the real open question under the whole debate. "Choose the question" is a safe harbor only as long as question-choosing stays hard for machines.

A quick bridge, because Andrej Karpathy's HackerTube, which we covered yesterday, kept topping Hacker News all weekend, and his replies in that thread added two things worth keeping. He had Claude tally the cost: about five hundred million input tokens, mostly cached, more than ninety percent of it cheap Sonnet calls labeling comments, and only about a million tokens of Opus for the actual app and deployment. And asked which leaderboards he trusts, he said "I basically don't trust" benchmarks right now, and pointed people to Epoch's capabilities index instead.

That skepticism leads straight into the second item. On Saturday a project called Memdoor posted an independent reproduction of a result that topped Hacker News a week earlier. The original, from a small lab called Persis, claims that five copies of the open-weight Qwen three point eight, twenty-seven billion parameters, organized as a team, match Claude Fable five on hard LiveCodeBench problems, lifting the small model from about sixty-seven percent to about ninety-two. The team is a planner, two ideators proposing different algorithm families, a solver, a reviewer acting as manager, and a repair worker, each starting from a fresh context and communicating only through files on disk: a plan, notes, the current solution. The paper credits that ledger discipline for the gain.

Memdoor rebuilt it as eight configuration files and no code, and got nine point eight out of ten averaged over five passes, against nine point six for the authors' harness and eight for a single Fable five call, at a median of thirty-nine cents and forty minutes a problem. What I liked is the README's honesty: ten problems, not a hundred, so nine point eight versus nine point six is noise; the contests date from spring twenty twenty-five, so contamination isn't ruled out for any column; and the one failure is the interesting one. The manager agent invented a wrong counterexample, and the team dutifully rewrote a correct program into a broken one. Twice more a correct solution got overwritten and only later recovered. The original paper also says plainly that gains are not universal; some models get worse. So the claim that survives is narrower and more useful: organization, meaning decomposition plus persistent written state, buys a lot. And the failure mode is a checker that's confidently wrong.

On to the money. Serval, which builds AI agents for internal IT and employee support, raised a hundred and fifty million dollars on Thursday at a three billion dollar valuation, led by Sequoia. That's triple its valuation from December, its third round in twelve months, two hundred seventy-seven million raised in total. The pitch, from co-founder Jake Stauch to Reuters: "It's not an additional layer of AI spend on top of your existing system of record, it is a true alternative replacement for platforms like ServiceNow." The headline customer is Lucid Motors, which he says ripped out ServiceNow across its roughly ten-thousand-person company in forty-five days.

The timing is what makes this a signal. The same Thursday, as The Next Web reported, ServiceNow shares fell as much as twenty-five percent intraday, their worst drop since the company listed in twenty twelve, after a bookings outlook missed forecasts. ServiceNow's reply was a ninety-eight percent renewal rate. Two other details are worth holding onto. Stauch told Reuters customers still generally want a human to approve sensitive actions, password resets, application access. And more than a hundred of Serval's five hundred planned hires are automation engineers embedded inside customers, about half recent graduates on a four-week training program, because, quote, "Nobody has ten years of experience doing this, because two years ago the tools didn't exist." So the replacement for the software platform is software plus a services arm. The value is in mapping how a specific organization actually works.

Which sets up today's deep cut, a short Latent Space episode recorded at AI Engineer New York with Aman Singh, who leads the AI platform team at Arca, an AI-native wealth-management firm that recently raised a round led by General Catalyst. It's only about twenty-six minutes, but it's the clearest production account I've heard of where the human sits in a long-horizon agent system.

Singh starts with the division of labor: "The role of AI at Arca is not to replace the advisor. It is to replace all of the work around the advisor." Take a client who emails about a hundred-thousand-dollar tax bill. That process spans about ten days. Agents do the analysis, where the money sits and the best way to raise the cash. But they are "unable to actually execute any sort of money movement or liquidation automatically," and there are strict human-in-the-loop rules for anything that moves money, sells assets, or even talks directly to the client.

The reasoning chain goes like this. First, safety is not a judgment the model makes: "All of our safety restrictions are fully deterministic, and they take the decision-making off the agent's hands." The guardrails sit, his phrase, "at the edges of capability, and not earlier than that." Every database write gets a fast model check, inbound emails and documents are screened for prompt injection, and outbound data passes deterministic PII checks plus a consensus of small models. Second, long horizons are solved with memory, not long context. A new signal, say a phone call mid-process, wakes a fresh agent that reads a per-client state: what's happening, who owns what, what's next. A nightly reflection process, which he calls dreaming, updates that state. Third, and this is the line in the episode title: "Agents prompt people more than people prompt agents." The person needed at step seven of a ten-day process gets pulled in when the signal says it's time, with the context they need. And fourth, he refuses to outsource that layer. On managed agent products from the labs: "Owning your intelligence gives you the ability to impact the business." Arca uses Claude as its primary model, but picks models by internal benchmarks; he mentions one model whose external benchmarks looked great that turned out too slow and too costly for their tasks.

Two failure modes he named are the ones you'll recognize, Ollie. Models will "very happily use the tax brackets from twenty twenty-five" and announce they're done, so a decision layer catches moments that require factual rigor and nudges the model to a curated knowledge base of what's true today. Swap tax brackets for gene nomenclature or a revised clinical guideline and that's your problem too. And on self-improvement: a nightly meta-agent reads real traces next to staff feedback, where every edit or cancellation comes with a reason, and proposes harness changes. But a human engineer still approves every prompt change, because left alone, "every little fix becomes the whole prompt." The agents can't stay meta and generalize the fix.

Ollie's takeaway: put Singh next to Memdoor's manager inventing a counterexample and Serval's customers insisting on approving password resets, and the human role in a working agent system looks very specific. Own the state the agents share, make the irreversible actions deterministic, and keep a person on the edits that are supposed to generalize. The model does the work. The structure around it is the part you still have to build yourself.

The one thing to watch tomorrow: Calegari's proposals are cheap to adopt. If a math department or journal says publicly that it will evaluate submissions as if AI-assisted, that's the first institutional answer to the October paper release, and it's a template biology could copy faster than you'd think. That's the pulse. See you tomorrow.
