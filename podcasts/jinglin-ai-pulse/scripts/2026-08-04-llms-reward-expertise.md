## Script

Good morning, Ollie. This is Ollie's A-I Pulse for Tuesday, August fourth.

Here's what the A-I world is talking about today, and it's a single, uncomfortable question, asked from four directions at once. Does A-I level the playing field — or does it reward the people who already know what they're doing? For two years the marketing promise has been the great equalizer: everyone gets a genius in their pocket, the gap between novice and expert collapses. And today, almost by accident, the community's collective attention landed on the opposite conclusion. From the top of Hacker News, to a clinical trial, to a security report, to a foundation-model founder — the answer coming back is the same. Expertise wins. Let me show you the convergence, because it's unusually clean.

Start with the Twitter Pulse, and the post that took the number-two slot on Hacker News overnight — nine hundred and thirty points. It's an essay by the engineer Sean Goedecke, and the title is the whole thesis: "L-L-Ms reward expertise." His argument, in one sentence: the most important skill in prompting is expertise in the domain you're prompting for. And the reason is subtle. As the models get better, the model stops being the bottleneck. The bottleneck becomes you — specifically, your ability to say exactly what kind of answer you want, and to recognize when the answer is subtly wrong.

His centerpiece example is Terence Tao — the mathematician — working through the Jacobian conjecture with ChatGPT. And Goedecke's point isn't that Tao gets better answers because he's smart. It's that Tao's expertise changes what mode the model operates in. By signaling that he's a mathematician — terse, precise, pushing back in the right places — Tao flips the model out of "explaining-to-amateurs" mode and into what Goedecke calls "talking-to-mathematicians mode." The output gets denser, more useful, less hand-holding. And here's the kicker: Tao repeatedly ignores the directions the model suggests, and steers using his own judgment. You cannot copy that technique. It's not a prompt you can paste. It requires actually knowing the mathematics — knowing which branch is a dead end before the model does. Same model, wildly different value, and the difference is entirely the human.

Now, there was pushback — and I want to give it to you, because the contrarian take matters. On Lobsters, the sister forum, several commenters said this is a strawman: of course expertise makes for good work; every senior engineer already knows the model is a power tool that rewards the person who knows what they're building. Fair. But I think the reason this hit a nerve today is the flip side — because if L-L-Ms reward expertise, they must also punish its absence. And two other stories today are exactly that shadow.

The first is from your world, Ollie — medicine. There's a randomized trial published in N-E-J-M A-I that has been circulating in precisely this conversation, and the result is brutal. They took forty-four physicians, put them through a twenty-hour A-I literacy course — so these are doctors specifically trained to use these tools well — and then had them diagnose clinical cases. The group that was shown a confidently wrong L-L-M suggestion dropped to seventy-three point three percent diagnostic accuracy. The control group, with no A-I, was at eighty-four point nine. That's a fourteen-point collapse. Top-choice accuracy fell from ninety point five percent down to seventy-six. And the punchline is that the literacy training did not protect them. When the model sounds confident and is wrong, even trained clinicians follow it off the cliff. That's the trap: where you have the expertise to catch the model, it amplifies you; where you don't, it drags you down below where you'd have been alone.

And then there's the third variation, which I'd call the expertise tax — and it was number five on Hacker News, seven hundred and eleven points. The security firm JFrog put out a post with a title that is itself the whole discourse: "SQLite Critical C-V-Es, or L-L-M slop?" Here's what happened. A single GitHub account published fifty-five security advisories against SQLite — one of the most-deployed pieces of software on Earth. JFrog audited them. Fifty-four were completely fabricated. Made up. The tells were pure language-model hallucination: they referenced functions that don't exist in the versions cited, line numbers past the actual end of the file, proof-of-concept exploits that don't run, patches that never happened. And here's the part that should worry you — the national vulnerability database and CISA assigned these fake vulnerabilities Critical severity scores before any human caught them. Red Hat had to manually investigate and downgrade one of them, C-V-E twenty-twenty-six dash five-one-three-oh-two, from a ten-point-oh Critical down to a seven-point-six. JFrog's warning is the sharp edge: an autonomous agent that hits a fabricated C-V-E may go try to locate the vulnerable function, generate a patch, and recommend changes — all based on code that does not exist. The slop generates for free; the debunking costs an expert their afternoon. That's a tax, and it's paid entirely by the people who actually know the codebase. Pair it with another thread that trended today — "prevent cognitive debt by manually retyping the code the model gives you" — and you can feel the whole timeline circling the same anxiety: the tool that makes you faster can also quietly make you worse, unless you already know enough to stay in charge.

So that's the convergence, and it's genuinely striking for a single day. Same tool, three faces. An expertise amplifier — that's Tao. An expertise trap — that's the physicians. An expertise tax — that's the C-V-E slop. And the one variable running through all three is expertise. The mood on the timeline today is not "A-I makes everyone equal." It's the opposite: A-I is the biggest multiplier we've ever built for what you already know, and the biggest liability for what you don't.

Let's do Money Moves, because the capital side had its own version of "the constraint is real," and it's about power — literally, electricity. On August third, the Governor of Texas, Greg Abbott, did something that would have been unthinkable eighteen months ago. He froze new data-center connections to the state's power grid. All of them, pending an audit — regulators now have to check every proposed data center's power draw, water use, tax breaks, and ownership before it's allowed to plug in. And the number that forced his hand is staggering: the queue of projects waiting to connect to the Texas grid has reached four hundred and seventy-four gigawatts. That is more than five times the entire state's record peak electricity demand, and roughly ninety percent of it is data centers. The grid, in other words, is being asked to more than quintuple, almost entirely to feed A-I — and the state just said, not so fast.

Now watch what the market does in response, because it's the perfect pairing. The same forty-eight hours, a nuclear startup called Valar Atomics raised one billion dollars at a six-billion-dollar valuation — that's triple its valuation from just months ago — led by Sequoia, with the partner Shaun Maguire taking a board seat, plus a two-hundred-million-dollar credit line on top. And what did they raise it on? A live demo: Valar's Ward two-fifty microreactor powered an Nvidia Blackwell chip on stage in Utah — the first time a next-generation reactor has done that in the United States — and they announced a thirty-megawatt, water-free A-I factory built with Nvidia. But here's the real pitch, and it's the tell for where this is all going. Valar isn't selling one big custom power plant. They're selling manufacturing economics for nuclear — the plan is to mass-produce small reactors on a production line, like cars. Put the two stories side by side and you get the whole logic of the moment: the public grid is maxed out and the regulators are slamming the brakes, so the frontier response is to stop asking the grid and start manufacturing your own power, one modular reactor at a time. Yesterday the moat was buying megawatts. Today it's building the factory that stamps them out.

Which is the perfect bridge to the Deep Cut, because "build the factory" turns out to be the exact argument of the best podcast I heard this cycle. On Latent Space, the hosts sat down with Eiso Kant, the co-founder of Poolside, and the episode is titled "Inside the Model Factory." And Kant's core claim is a direct challenge to how everyone talks about A-I. He says the moat was never the model. It's the factory that produces the models. His numbers: fewer than seventy researchers and about thirty-five engineers, running between ten and twenty thousand training experiments a month, streaming their data live so that changing the training recipe is — his phrase — "just a config." They ship a new model in five to eight weeks, start of training to launch. And his analogy is SpaceX: the first rocket is really hard to build, but the much harder, more valuable thing was building the factory that builds the rockets. In his words, the model "should be an artifact of someone's process. It shouldn't really be a thing in itself."

But here's the line that made me pick this episode today, because it closes the loop on the entire show. Kant's head of applied research, Peng Ming, was explaining why their latest model punches so far above its weight — a one-hundred-eighteen-billion-parameter model, with only eight billion active, beating models two and three times its size. And the reason, he says, is not what you'd guess. Quote: the gains come "not from more intelligence, but more from different behavior — more verification, less taking things for granted, not declaring victory early, and being way more persistent." Read that again and hold it next to the Twitter Pulse. What makes their model good is not raw I-Q. It's that the model checks its work, doesn't assume it's right, backtracks, and keeps going. Verification over intelligence.

So here's my takeaway, tying the whole day together. Every thread today is telling you the same thing: value is fleeing raw intelligence and pooling in verification and judgment — in the model and in the human. The frontier model wins by verifying instead of assuming. The human expert wins by knowing when the confident answer is wrong — which is exactly the skill the physicians lacked, exactly the labor the C-V-E slop is trying to exhaust, and exactly what Tao is doing when he overrules the model. Intelligence is getting cheap and abundant. The scarce thing — for the machine and for you — is the discipline to check it.

So the one thing to watch tomorrow. Watch whether "verification" and "steerability" start replacing benchmark scores in how the labs actually pitch their models — because if Kant and Peng Ming are right, the next competitive axis isn't who's smartest, it's who's most careful. And keep one eye on Texas, because if more states follow Abbott and gate grid access, the build-your-own-reactor play — Valar's play — stops being a moonshot and becomes the default. Cheap intelligence, scarce verification, and a power grid that finally said no. That's Ollie's A-I Pulse. Talk to you tomorrow.
