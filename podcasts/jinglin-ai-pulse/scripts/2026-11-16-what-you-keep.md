## Script

Here's what the AI world is talking about today. It's Monday, November sixteenth. Over the weekend, the arguments were about who gets to keep what. Alibaba kept its biggest new model closed. A Hacker News post with more than eleven hundred points argued that AI-written code shouldn't belong to anyone. A major open-source project stopped paying outside contributors because it could no longer tell who, or what, had done the work. And in the Deep Cut, Isomorphic Labs' president explains why his company won't sell its model at all. The usual disclosure: Anthropic and Claude come up today, and the agent drafting this is Claude.

Start with Qwen4-Max, which Alibaba released overnight. The specs are big: a two point four trillion parameter mixture-of-experts model with seventy-one billion parameters active per token, a million-token context window, and native vision. The architecture is the most interesting part. Three Gated DeltaNet layers, a kind of linear attention that squeezes history into a fixed-size state, sit in front of each sparse attention layer. A lightweight indexer picks which blocks of context are worth attending to. There are four parallel residual streams with learned gates, and an extra three hundred twenty-eight billion parameters of n-gram embeddings that look up local token patterns cheaply. The team reports no loss spikes across the whole pretraining run, and about a sixty percent drop in cost per task on its own work benchmark compared with the previous Max.

The reaction split along a familiar line. Tim Kellogg posted on Bluesky that "Qwen 4 Max is looking an awful lot like a frontier model," pointing to the price, a dollar fifty per million input tokens and four fifty for output, and to how sophisticated the attention design has become. The independent numbers are cooler. Artificial Analysis gives it a forty-three on its Intelligence Index. That puts it ninth among the leading models, behind Moonshot's Kimi K3.1 Pro at forty-six, and well behind the top American models. On Hacker News, the bluntest comment was "Disappointing performance for a 2.4T model in the end of 2026." Qwen's own table backs that up on agentic coding. On Terminal-Bench, Qwen4-Max scores forty-eight and a half while Kimi scores sixty-seven. Another commenter asked whether the open-weights gap is widening, and that's the real story. Max is API-only, as every Max model since Qwen two point five has been. Alibaba says the smaller Qwen4 models will come out with open weights, and the Hacker News thread was mostly people asking when the twenty-seven-billion version ships.

One part of the launch is aimed squarely at you, Ollie. Qwen shows two science demos, and they deserve different levels of trust. In one, the model ran for forty-eight hours on a partner's integrated dry-lab and wet-lab platform. It searched two point seven million candidate molecules for a data-center coolant, directed an automated lab through four design, synthesize and test cycles, and ended with a physical, measured sample. In the other, it worked on the NLRP3 inflammasome as an Alzheimer's target. It made four hundred fifty-four tool calls, designed twenty-four molecules, and reported a relative binding free energy suggesting about three point six times the reference compound's affinity. But that one never left the computer, and the partner's experts say the molecule now needs wet-lab validation. Remember that distinction for the Deep Cut.

The second debate is about who owns code once machines write it. Jakub Jankiewicz's post, "All AI-generated code should be Open Source," was the top non-space story on Hacker News this weekend. His argument has two steps. First, the models learned from decades of open-source code and Stack Overflow answers that legally require attribution, so closing their output is, in his words, "license laundering." Second, purely AI-generated work isn't written by a human and probably can't be copyrighted, so release it as open source or public domain and make that explicit. The top reply took apart the second step in one line: "If AI generated code is not copyrightable, releasing it under an open source license is meaningless." Another commenter pointed to the court cases, saying the GitHub Copilot lawsuit went against the idea that model output inherits open-source licenses, and that Thaler v. Perlmutter found fully AI-generated output can't be copyrighted at all. So the law is heading toward public domain, not copyleft. And one person on Bluesky, Asa Dotzler, summed up the sympathetic side: "It's still theft, but at least it's theft that joins the commons."

What made this more than a philosophy argument is a post from ESLint, the JavaScript linter. Since twenty twenty-two it has paid outside contributors from a monthly pool, fifty-one thousand dollars in total, and the payouts were growing. They shut it down this month, and not because of the cost. AI-generated pull requests came first, which they decided not to pay for. Then disclosure requirements that some people ignored. Then what the post calls "AI monitors," people combing the monthly payout report and messaging the maintainer that certain accounts were really AI. Their conclusion: "We were all tired of spending time trying to determine if a pull request was AI-generated or not." The two stories collide here. Jankiewicz wants the line between human and AI code to decide ownership. ESLint just showed that a well-run project couldn't find that line reliably enough to decide who gets paid.

Geoff Stearns posted the most optimistic version of where this goes. His example is a seventy-dollar pocket synthesizer, the M-Vave FM-1. Owners wrote custom firmware for it, mostly with AI coding tools, including a groovebox and a port of Doom. Within weeks, the company open-sourced its own firmware under GPL version three, and Behringer, Akai and Erica Synths followed with some of their gear. His claim: "Your agent will pick the hackable one." When you ask an agent which router or synth to buy, it will favor the device it can fix later. His test for whether a lock is about security or about lock-in is a good one: ask who controls the signing keys.

Money moves, and it's NVIDIA again, with earnings on Tuesday. On Sunday, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said the board should "go outside the company" for a new chief executive after Jensen Huang's abrupt resignation last week. That resignation followed revelations of extramarital affairs, one of which, Reuters reports, involved a suspected Chinese spy. Bessent also gave NVIDIA's board and outside counsel ten days to answer questions about the company's culture of compliance with export restrictions. At the same time, the Financial Times reports that Huang has talked with confidants about coming back, and that the board approached Arm's chief executive, Rene Haas. And the Wall Street Journal reports the administration plans to name more than two dozen American AI "national champions," including NVIDIA, OpenAI and Anthropic, to help them compete abroad against Chinese rivals. Put those together and Washington wants NVIDIA both shaken up and protected. For research, Nathan Lambert's point from the weekend still holds. Whoever runs NVIDIA next decides whether it keeps funding open models, and Qwen just showed how fast the best open work can move behind an API.

Now the Deep Cut. On last Thursday's No Priors, Elad Gil interviewed Max Jaderberg, president of Isomorphic Labs, the Alphabet-backed drug discovery company that grew out of the AlphaFold work. Jaderberg came up through DeepMind's game-playing projects, AlphaGo and AlphaStar, and says AlphaFold was the moment he saw, quote, "the first proof of concept that you could get a deep learning model that generalizes in a meaningful space in biology." What's worth your time is the argument underneath. Here it is in four steps.

Step one is a diagnosis. Gil laid out the familiar numbers: drug approvals flat for decades while costs have climbed to about a billion and a half per drug. Jaderberg's explanation is that drug discovery is still trial and error, and each program is "a bespoke frontier science project" that teaches you little about the next one. An AI platform underneath every program is how you get, in his words, "compounding improvements the more medicines you go after."

Step two is the target, and it's a sharp evaluation idea. "Internally, we have a dotted line on all of our leaderboards that is experimental accuracy." Every model is measured against how well the real experiment would have done. Once a model crosses that line, you don't need to run the experiment. His stated goal is blunt: "fundamentally, I want to get out of the real world." He isn't against lab automation, and says Isomorphic has used automated labs for a long time. But you can't speed up how long a reaction takes or how long a cell takes to grow. So the goal is to "go into the real world to validate, not necessarily to learn and iterate."

Step three is about data, and this is where it touches your work most directly. He argues that the big public and pharma datasets are "the byproduct of many years of passive data collection" from making medicines, so they're biased toward what chemists already tried. "If you really care about generalization out of these models, there's maybe a different distribution of data you need." So Isomorphic generates its own, and he says they've seen "some really, really amazing data scaling laws as a result." He also goes after the oldest proxy in the field. A rat, he says, is "about 30% predictive of human clinical tox," and if one of his engineers brought him a model that was thirty percent predictive, "you're like, okay, maybe do a bit better than that." His alternative is to model human clinical toxicity directly, combining lab assays, organoids, organs on a chip and AI models.

Step four is the payoff, and I hadn't heard it framed this way before. Gil compared cancer to HIV, where combination therapy finally worked, and asked why we don't use more drug cocktails. Jaderberg's answer: "it's actually because we're not great at engineering exquisite molecules." Each drug carries some liver toxicity or off-target effect, so combining them causes harmful drug-drug interactions. If you can design molecules clean enough, you can combine several to hit "a whole vector of targets" across a pathway. In that view, precise design isn't only about better single drugs. It makes combinations possible.

Two caveats. Isomorphic doesn't sell its model, license it or sell tokens; it uses the model only on its own programs and partnerships, so none of this can be checked from outside. And when Gil asked when the first clinical trial will start, Jaderberg wouldn't give a date, saying only that they're building a clinical development team.

Here's the takeaway, Ollie. Compare Jaderberg's dotted line with Qwen's NLRP3 demo. Twenty-four molecules and a free-energy estimate with no experimental accuracy line drawn: that's a computational result, and the right response is the one the partner gave, test it in the lab. For your own models, ask where your experimental-accuracy line is, and whether your training data is a passive byproduct or chosen for generalization. And today's ownership fights point the same way. When code and molecules get cheap to produce, the scarce assets are proprietary data, validation, and someone who clearly paid attention.

The one thing to watch: NVIDIA's earnings on Tuesday, the first under an interim chief executive, and whether analysts ask about the export-compliance deadline. That's the pulse for today.
