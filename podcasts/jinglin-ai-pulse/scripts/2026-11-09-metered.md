## Script

Here's what the AI world is talking about today. It's Monday, November ninth, and the weekend's threads all came back to one thing: intelligence is now metered, and everyone is arguing about the meter. Users suspect the model behind the meter got quietly worse. Big companies are capping how much of it their employees can use. And an investor who went through Anthropic's IPO filing line by line says the economics underneath look nothing like the bubble everyone is debating. So today's theme is metered. One disclosure first: most of today is about Anthropic and its models, and the agent that drafted this episode is Claude, an Anthropic model. Weigh it accordingly.

Start with the post that got nearly half a million views on Saturday. Shane Melaugh asked, "is Opus 5.5 nerfed?" He had Gemini four Argon analyze fourteen hundred of his own Claude Code sessions, and it found that, quote, "on November 3, something definitely happened. The model name didn't change, but the way it does reasoning took a sharp turn." His chart is striking. Before November third, about a quarter of the visible thinking blocks began with "I'm," and about half used phrases like "I should" or "I need to." After a shift he puts at roughly six p.m. UTC, the "I'm" openings jumped to about sixty-one percent and the "I should" phrasing fell to about eleven. The use of dashes collapsed from around half to six and a half percent. He says the change appeared mid-session across seven active sessions, with the same model ID and the same Claude Code version.

The replies split into two camps. One camp said they'd felt it too: tool calls fumbled, long tasks failing, usage limits draining faster. The usual theory is that a provider quietly swaps in a cheaper, quantized version to free up capacity for training the next model. The other camp made the point that matters most. What you see in Claude Code is not the model's raw reasoning. For recent Claude models, Anthropic returns a summary of the thinking, written by a separate process. So a change in how the summaries are worded is exactly what you'd see if the summarizer changed and the model didn't. One commenter put it flatly: "It's just a new summarizer, the model and performance itself is unchanged." To his credit, Melaugh's own chart carries the same caveat in small print: "Visible summaries show a behavior shift, not proof of changed model weights."

So is there better evidence? Someone posted LiveNerf, and it's the most careful thing in the thread. It's a benchmark built for exactly this argument. Its author started the clock two and a half days after Opus five point five launched in September and has run it every day since. It uses a frozen panel of seventy-eight questions, from GPQA, MMLU-Pro and competition math, that Opus five point five only sometimes gets right, because those are the questions where a real drop would show up. The method was written down and committed publicly before any data came in. The author pinned the Claude Code version, because, in the README's words, "a changed harness looks exactly like a changed model." The result through Sunday: three full ten-day windows after launch week, and no change detected. I checked the daily file. The five runs from November fourth through Sunday scored between fifty and sixty-five percent, around a launch-week baseline of fifty-eight. The median output length per answer stayed between about five hundred forty and six hundred thirty tokens, against a baseline of about five hundred ninety.

The README is honest about the limits, though. A daily panel like this can detect roughly a seven-and-a-half-point accuracy drop in a ten-day window. When the author swapped in the older Opus five as a test, the rig couldn't reliably tell it apart. And the author says the most sensitive signal isn't accuracy at all, it's output tokens. Turning effort down to "low" cut output tokens by sixty-two percent but accuracy by only about eight points. So the most honest reading is this: on the narrow thing LiveNerf measures, no detectable change. On what Melaugh measured, a real change in the summaries. And on what people actually care about, agentic coding quality, nobody has a pre-registered instrument at all. One commenter's line sums up why that's the fight underneath: "With open weights, you get to decide when the model changes." The reply: "there's much more to a model than its weights." For you, Ollie, the transferable lesson is LiveNerf's method. If your lab depends on a hosted model, a small frozen panel of borderline questions, run daily against a pinned harness, is cheap, and it turns "it feels dumber" into a number.

That open-weights argument drove the weekend's other big thread: llama-modchip, a set of patches to llama dot cpp that claims to run DeepSeek V four Flash, two hundred eighty-four billion parameters, in sixty-four gigabytes of ordinary RAM with no GPU, at nine to fifteen tokens per second. The trick is extreme compression. The routed experts are squeezed to about one and a half bits per weight, which brings the file to about sixty gigabytes, while the sensitive parts are kept at higher precision. Hacker News was skeptical in useful ways. The first question everyone asked was what the quantization costs in quality, and the answer is that it's under two bits, where most people's experience is that quality falls off a cliff. Several readers called the README machine-written and nearly unreadable. One user who tried it on a sixteen-core machine reported that, outside of faster prompt processing, it ran "just about the same as plain old llama.cpp." So treat it as a sign of where the hobbyist frontier is, not a working replacement. But the appetite is the story. A top comment argued that for under eight hundred dollars you can build something that holds its own with where state of the art was less than a year ago. Another reader answered that comparing that to frontier models "is silly."

The third thread is where the meter meets the enterprise. Bloomberg reported last week, and the story topped Hacker News on Sunday, that Goldman Sachs has restricted Claude Code and Microsoft's GitHub coding tools to its engineers, about twelve thousand of its roughly forty-seven thousand staff. Traders and bankers who'd been using them to track client flows and model positions lost access. Quant strategists kept it. That's a reversal from July, when Goldman's CIO was praising traders' vibe-coded tools. And it comes as Goldman is a lead underwriter on Anthropic's IPO, so its bankers will be marketing shares in a company whose coding agent most of them can no longer use.

The top comment had the clearest explanation. It's Simon Willison's "lethal trifecta": an agent with access to private data, exposure to untrusted content, and a way to send information out. A trader's workstation has client and trade data, email, the internet and internal networks all at once, so "a major leak is just one small mistake or prompt injection away." Another commenter, at a different company, gave a more mundane reason: they've been clawing back Claude access "because of chronic billing overages," and Gemini is winning seats with flat billing that throttles access to the bigger models instead of charging more. Both explanations point the same way. The constraint is no longer whether the model is good enough. It's who is allowed to run it, and who pays.

On to money, where the rationing has numbers attached. Bloomberg reports that Apple lowered the daily AI spending cap for some engineers from three hundred dollars to two hundred last month, and is considering going as low as a hundred. Many non-engineering groups already have a hundred-dollar daily cap, which could fall to twenty-five for some teams. Some teams are being told to use cheaper models, or models that run locally on the device. Apple isn't alone. Bloomberg lists Tesla at two hundred dollars a week, Uber capping individual coding tools at fifteen hundred a month, and Meta introducing token budgets. And in October, Accenture moved to centrally fund just twenty-nine dollars a month of Claude per person, with anything above that billed to a project, after some consultants had been spending five hundred to a thousand dollars a month. Hacker News did the arithmetic. Two hundred a day is roughly forty-four hundred dollars a month of workdays, and readers argued about whether that's lavish or barely one heavy agent session.

That's the setup for a bubble argument that drew five hundred comments. Matteo Raso's post, "I'm Not Convinced The AI Bubble Exists," leans on Anthropic's IPO filing: revenue growth, and the line that Anthropic makes about two dollars and eight cents for every dollar of compute. Commenters pushed back hard. One said that if you ignore the cost of building cars, cars are also very profitable. Another pointed out that Anthropic's filing shows widening net losses. Hold that thought, because today's deep cut takes that number apart.

One more business story, kept to what the company itself has said. On Sunday, Nvidia's board announced an independent investigation, run by independent directors with outside counsel, after reports that Jensen Huang "has been the target of a foreign intelligence effort." The board says it has "no reason to believe that either national security or NVIDIA secrets were compromised," and that it has confidence in Huang. Huang's letter to staff apologizes for personal conduct, which we'll leave to him and his family, and says he'll cooperate fully. The business point is key-person risk at the company at the center of the compute supply chain, landing right after a week in which chip stocks had their worst day since March twenty twenty. Watch the market open today.

Now the deep cut, from Latent Space's episode with Jamin Ball, a partner at Altimeter Capital, released Saturday and recorded October twenty-ninth. It's a walk through Anthropic's S-1, and it's the best antidote I've heard to both sides of the bubble argument. One caveat up front: Ball is an investor, the host notes Altimeter leads large rounds in labs like Anthropic, and Ball says of himself, "maybe I'm just drinking the Kool-Aid too much." Weigh it accordingly.

His argument goes in four steps. Step one is efficiency, and it's the number that ties back to the nerf thread. The filing breaks out how Anthropic uses its compute, and only about twenty-seven percent of it today goes to serving customers. The rest goes to training, research and internal use. Compared with a year ago, the compute spent on serving has gone up about two point three times. Revenue over the same period went up about fourteen times. Ball's inference: roughly a sevenfold gain in how much revenue each unit of serving compute earns. In his words, "this is a business that I think is almost accidentally profitable." He puts the latest quarter's free cash flow margin at about twenty-one percent, and says compute margin, which is revenue minus all compute including training, has scaled past fifty percent. He also adds that if Anthropic simply doubled the share of compute it uses for serving, it could in theory double revenue without adding a single chip. That sentence is also why users suspect the meter. When three-quarters of a lab's compute isn't serving you, any rebalancing feels like it came out of your share.

Step two takes apart the losses the bubble skeptics cited. The balance sheet shows about a hundred eight billion dollars of convertible notes, which looks like enormous debt. Ball says roughly eleven billion dollars was raised that way, early on, largely from strategic investors like Google and Amazon, who had reasons to prefer notes over shares. About five billion has converted, six billion is outstanding, and because those notes convert at old, much lower valuations, the six billion is now worth about a hundred eight. Accounting rules make that rise in value flow through as a loss on the income statement. So the giant net loss is, in his telling, the cost of the company being worth more, not cash going out the door. "There is no debt on the balance sheet," he says.

Step three is why the treadmill can't stop. In the three months ending September, about eighty percent of Anthropic's revenue came from models six months old or younger. He predicts open-weight models keep taking share of tokens, but frontier models keep most of the dollars. The risk is the flip side: "If Anthropic stops, OpenAI and Gemini will continue," and revenue falls off. He uses a chip-industry analogy from an older semiconductor investor. Building one competitive chip is different from building "the machine that builds the machine," and that's what decides who stays at the frontier.

Step four connects straight to Apple's caps. Ball says half the Fortune 500 uses Anthropic, but that group spends only about a tenth of a percent of its expenses on it. The conversation then lands on a metric worth watching: token spend relative to salary spend. Most companies are near zero, and, as one of them puts it, the median AI company is around fifty percent. That's the real collision in this week's news. Ball's thesis needs that ratio to climb by orders of magnitude, and the largest buyers are currently writing memos to push it back down. His answer is Jevons paradox, which he admits is a cliché, with the price of moving bytes over the internet as the precedent: price down, quantity way up. The caps are the other side of that bet, buyers checking whether the extra tokens actually turn into extra work. As he put it about cheaper specialized models, "you don't need to drive the Ferrari engine to the grocery store."

Here's the takeaway for a biomedical AI lab. All three threads today are about measurement under a meter. Users can't see the model's raw reasoning, companies can't yet see which tokens produce value, and investors were misreading an accounting line as cash burn. In each case, the fix was to measure the right thing, not to argue harder: LiveNerf's frozen panel, Apple asking who actually used their three hundred dollars a day, and Ball reading the cash flow instead of the net loss. If your group is about to put a budget on agent use, decide first what output you'll count against it.

For tomorrow, watch two things. First, Nvidia's stock and any update from the board's investigation. Second, LiveNerf's fourth window closes around day fifty, later this week. If Opus five point five really changed on November third, that's the first pre-registered check that could show it. That's the pulse. See you tomorrow.
