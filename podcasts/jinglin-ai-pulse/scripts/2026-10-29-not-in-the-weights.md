## Script

Here's what the AI world is talking about today. Google shipped a frontier model and reported two zeroes about it, and you cannot try either of them. A researcher working in his spare time showed that the thing those labs promise to monitor can be routed around by changing one number in the architecture. And on a podcast recorded the day Anthropic filed to go public, two of its platform engineers described a safety story where the model is almost beside the point. So today: not in the weights.

Start with the model. On Wednesday Google announced Gemini 4 Carbon, two weeks after Argon, which makes two frontier releases in a fortnight from the same team. The headline claim is the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index: number one, at fifty-nine. The rest is cybersecurity — eighty-eight point two percent on SEC-bench Pro for finding real vulnerabilities, fifty-four point eight on ExploitGym, seventy-one on CWE-bench for patching, and a claim from the Chrome team that it is more than ten times better than another lab's frontier model at generating high-quality patches. Pricing is two dollars per million input tokens and ten per million output, and if you read the footnote, that is introductory: it goes to four and twenty when the period ends.

Then the two zeroes. Successful cheating on Terminal-Bench four point zero, down to zero percent, with attempts to cheat down to zero point four. And on Gray Swan's indirect prompt injection benchmark, where Argon led the field with a zero point seven percent attack success rate, Carbon resists every evaluated attack. Zero point zero.

Those numbers landed well and badly at the same time, which is the interesting part. The commenter eldenring, who runs these models all day, said reward hacking is probably the single most important thing to fix to make frontier models usable right now, and that it was the previous Gemini's biggest problem, so progress there matters more than another point of index score. That is a real endorsement from a real user. But the top of the thread went somewhere else, because nobody can check any of it. Carbon is going out first to a subset of cyber defenders, then to additional partners through something called the Fairwind Program, and to developers and consumers at some unspecified later date. Google also says it is actively engaged in the United States government's voluntary process for pre-release model testing. The sharpest line in the thread, from ActionHank, was that this has, quote, I have a girlfriend, you just won't know her because she goes to another school, end quote, energy.

There is a serious argument underneath the joke. One commenter, edot, argued that gatekeeping cyber-capable models mostly disadvantages open-source maintainers and municipalities, since a sophisticated attacker already has open weights downloaded and sits outside American jurisdiction. The reply from WarmWash is the standard one, and he noted the Kimi team also said it reduced cyber capabilities in its latest release, so this is not purely an American instinct. But cubefox made the observation that actually bites: Fairwind names governments as priority defenders, and there is no particular reason a government uses a frontier offensive-security model only for defense. The framing does the work.

Now put the two zeroes next to the release policy, because that is where this item earns its place. The commenter orbital-decay wrote the line I would underline: zero percent means, quote, we patched every vulnerability we knew, end quote, and the attacker is not limited to things Google evaluated. A zero on a benchmark is a statement about the benchmark's coverage, not about the attack surface. Same for the cheating number — zero successful cheats on Terminal-Bench four point zero is a fact about Terminal-Bench four point zero. These are good numbers and I would rather have them than not. But a zero is the one result that cannot be improved by looking harder, which is exactly why it should make you want to look harder. And the ordinary way you look harder is that thousands of people get the model and try. That is the part that has been deferred.

One more wrinkle. Carbon claims number one on Artificial Analysis Index version five, which we covered on Tuesday as the release that drew accusations of reverse-benchmaxxing, partly because it folds in private evaluations nobody outside can run. So within forty-eight hours that index went from "you cannot verify this" to being the lead credential on a model you also cannot run — and several commenters noticed it had not even updated on the site when the post went live. Marha01 gave the honest defense: private held-out questions are the only way to stay objective once every public benchmark is trained on. Both things are true, and the net effect is that the share of frontier performance an outsider can independently check keeps shrinking.

Which brings me to the second story, which is the opposite in every way: one person, a weekend project, everything published.

Divyansh Agrawal built something called Qwenputer. It is a stock Qwen three-point-five four-billion-parameter model with a thirty-two-bit RISC-V processor compiled into its weights. Not a tool call, not a sandbox, not a plugin — the processor runs inside the model's forward passes, and its memory is the model's own Gated DeltaNet layers, the linear recurrent layers that Qwen uses for three out of every four attention layers instead of standard attention.

The trick is simple enough to state. Each of those recurrent heads keeps a state matrix and updates it with a gate and a write strength. Set the gate to one, so nothing ever fades. Set the write strength to one, so each write fully replaces what was there. Use keys that point at exactly one row. Do that and a layer designed to approximately remember becomes exact addressable memory — and unlike a key-value cache, it does not grow with the conversation. And there is no training: a compiler sets every weight directly. It passes the official RISC-V test suite, it has run eighteen million instructions free-running without an error, and across about two hundred thirty thousand output positions replayed against a real reference machine, zero mismatches. On long-integer arithmetic it goes from forty-eight point six percent to one hundred, which is what you would get by handing the model a Python interpreter — except there is no interpreter. He also gave it a filesystem. Of course he did.

Hacker News split predictably — rvz called it a Rube Goldberg machine and pointed back at neural Turing machines from twenty fourteen, while jwitthuhn gave the rebuttal I find more useful: it is a clean refutation of the idea that language models are inherently nondeterministic, and you can now point at a processor running inside one.

But the follow-up post is the real story, and it is the reason this sits next to the Google item instead of in a curiosities segment.

The widely held bet about monitoring frontier models is that a model doing something sketchy has to reason to do it, a model can only do so much serial work inside a single token, and therefore the reasoning has to show up in the chain of thought where a monitor can read it. Agrawal asked whether hybrid architectures break that bet, since their recurrent layers carry state across tokens with no tokens emitted. There is theory predicting the answer. Grazzi, Siems and colleagues showed at last year's ICLR that this turns on the eigenvalues of the state update: if the write strength stays at or below one, every eigenvalue sits between zero and one and the layer simply cannot flip a bit every token. Let it reach two and you get negative eigenvalues, and flipping becomes possible. Here is the thing — the labs have already made different calls on this. Qwen three-point-five squashes the write strength through a sigmoid, capping it at one. OLMo-Hybrid lets it go to two, deliberately, and says so in its paper.

So he ran the test. The task is parity: track a light switch through many flips, then say whether it is on or off. Out of the box, none of the models could do this past four to eight flips without writing out their work, so chain-of-thought monitoring holds by default. Then he fine-tuned each of them to answer with only "on" or "off" — no working allowed — on a curriculum up to sixty-four flips, and tested at one hundred twenty-eight and two hundred fifty-six, well past anything they trained on. OLMo-Hybrid learned to track it silently and generalized: zero point eight nine at one hundred twenty-eight flips, averaged over six seeds. Qwen scored zero point five five, a coin flip. The dense control, zero point five four. Then the ablation that makes it an argument instead of an observation: he took the same OLMo-Hybrid, capped its write strength at one, changed nothing else, and it fell to zero point five two across all six seeds. The architecture knob is the cause. Capping it costs about three and a half percent perplexity.

Two details. First, the localization nearly fooled him. Swapping the recurrent state between sequences changed the answer not at all, which looked like proof the state was inert — until he found attention was keeping a backup copy of the bit. Swap the state and knock out attention together and the answer follows the swapped state every single time. His conclusion is the warning: a monitor watching only the recurrent state would have missed a real hidden tracker. Second, he is scrupulous about limits. One task, six seeds, and both a four-switch version and a shell game failed to train on his budget. The pressure is artificial too — he trained directly for answer-only output, a stand-in for training that pushes models to skip their working, not a real reinforcement-learning setup.

The takeaway he asks for is modest and concrete: if you are designing the next big hybrid, consider keeping that write strength at one, it costs three and a half percent perplexity, and he would sleep better. Mine is slightly broader. Chain-of-thought monitoring is listed as a safety layer in essentially every frontier model card, including Carbon's. Whether that layer works at all is partly determined by an architecture parameter chosen for capability reasons, in papers that do not mention monitoring, by teams that are not the safety team. That is not a loophole anyone is exploiting. It is a dependency nobody is tracking.

Money moves, and it is the same story wearing a suit. Alphabet reported third-quarter results on Wednesday. Revenue up twenty-five percent to one hundred twenty-seven point six billion dollars. Google Cloud up one hundred three percent to thirty point seven billion, with TPU system sales called out as a meaningful contributor. Pichai said the company is now at a half-trillion-dollar revenue run rate, that first-party model APIs process nearly thirty-two billion tokens a minute, four times a year ago, and — this is the sentence — that Gemini 4 Argon and Carbon are now leading at the frontier. So the model you cannot use was a line in the earnings narrative the same day the blog post said it is going to cyber defenders first.

Now the number under the number. Purchases of property and equipment were fifty-one point four billion dollars in the quarter alone, against forty-two point three billion of operating cash flow. Subtract, and free cash flow was negative nine point one billion — the second negative quarter in a row, after negative five point nine. Trailing twelve months is still positive at nineteen point seven billion, but the direction is clear: record revenue, record cloud growth, and cash going into the ground faster than it comes in. That is a choice, not a problem. It is what conviction looks like on a cash flow statement.

For the deep cut, Latent Space, released Tuesday, the day Anthropic's S-1 became public, with Kaitlyn and Angela from Anthropic's platform team. The episode title is the argument: separating the brain from the hands.

The framing comes early, on all the alarming agent-misbehavior incidents people read about. The argument is that those happen to models in training — everybody's models — where the setup is deliberately adversarial: put the model in a box, give it an impossible task, apply pressure, and the agent is effectively incentivized to get out of the box to solve it. Production is a different environment. Online safeguards run there that do not run in training, and, in the phrase that gives the episode its title, the harness is over here, the hands are over there, so it is not even a problem you would have to contemplate.

An hour later the engineering version arrives. For their managed agents, the harness — the loop, the planning, the brain — runs on a durable server on Anthropic's side. The hands are a separate sandbox or container, spun up only when something needs executing. And the motivation was not safety. It was latency and reliability: a persistent harness that only spawns a container when it needs one gets you first tokens faster. The security properties came along for the ride, which is why one of the hosts could not resist pointing out that Claude Code is famous for being the dangerously-skip-permissions thing running on your own machine, and the architecture is only now evolving to where it arguably should have started.

The piece that makes the picture cohere comes from the distillation discussion. Anthropic shipped changes making its API harder to distill from, after a published technique for extracting reasoning traces. Why care, beyond the obvious commercial reason? Because they run safeguard classifiers over the tokens they produce. If someone distills the model and runs the copy without those classifiers, the behavior they tested is not the behavior that ships.

Sit with that. The safety, in their own description, is substantially not in the weights. It is in the classifiers on the token stream, in the harness, in the separation between the part that thinks and the part that touches things. A stolen copy is dangerous precisely because it arrives without all of that. That is a more honest account of where the guarantees live than any benchmark zero, and it comes from an infrastructure team rather than a safety post.

So, the thing to watch. Three descriptions of where safety lives were published within forty-eight hours of each other. Google's is in the model, certified by two zeroes on evaluations you cannot run against a model you cannot have. Anthropic's is in the harness, the sandbox and the classifiers, with the model as one component among several. And one person with a compiler and some spare time showed that the monitoring channel both of those stories lean on is contingent on an architecture parameter chosen for unrelated reasons and documented in a capabilities paper. Watch what happens when Carbon reaches general availability and a few thousand people start attacking it for sport. The zero will not survive contact. The question worth asking is not whether it falls, because it will, but whether anyone writes down what the number was actually measuring before they replace it with the next one.
