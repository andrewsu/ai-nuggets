## Script

Here's what the AI world is talking about today.

The through-line, if you squint at everything that moved in the last forty-eight hours, is this: the base model stopped being where the action is. The gains, the danger, and the value have all migrated into the layer wrapped around the frozen weights — the post-training, the scaffolding, the environment you drop the model into. Yesterday I told you intelligence stopped being the scarce input. Today the labs handed us the receipts. Let's get into it.

First up on the Twitter pulse: the big drop of the week, GLM 5.3, from the Chinese lab Z.ai — that's Zhipu, 智谱. It landed Thursday under a tagline that tells you exactly where their head is at: "Built to code. Ready for cyber defense." And the headline is not the benchmark. The headline is what they did not change. GLM 5.3 runs on the exact same base model as GLM 5.2 — the same roughly seven-hundred-forty-three-billion-parameter pretrained brain. They did not scale it. They did not retrain it. Every single capability gain came from 后训练 — post-training — on better, harder, more realistic task environments.

And the gains are not small. On a coding-agent benchmark called DeepSWE, they went from forty-six to sixty-seven. On Terminal-Bench, a test of driving a real command line, they went from under five to twenty-eight — that's not an improvement, that's a phase change. Same brain, roughly a fifty-percent jump in coding, purely from teaching it to operate inside professional-grade work environments — simulated engineering tasks, ML-infrastructure debugging where the reward is a measurable speedup, judge agents checking that a task is even solvable before it becomes training signal. If you've been listening this week, this is the exact thing vLLM's Simon Mo argued was the real 护城河, the real moat — not the data, not the weights, but the gym the model trains in. Z.ai just ran that thesis in public and posted the scoreboard.

But here's the part that made the timeline light up, and it's genuinely unsettling. The cyber capability came out of nowhere. Z.ai says they expected some improvement in finding vulnerabilities — instead the capability, in their words, "continued compounding as training scaled," and the model started, quote, "reasoning across multiple stages of exploitation, forming coherent plans for complete exploitation chains." Not "here's a bug." Rather: here's how to chain three bugs into a full break-in. On one exploit benchmark they more than doubled, from twenty-four to fifty-four. In real-world testing this thing found over two-thousand-four-hundred vulnerabilities across two-hundred-sixty-nine open-source projects, more than a thousand of them rated critical or high severity.

And then Z.ai did the thing nobody expected an open-weights lab to do. They held the weights back. GLM 5.3 is out through their coding product, but the open weights and the API are staged for release around the end of the month, gated behind — their phrase — "safety evaluation and hardening." An open-weights lab, the whole identity of which is "we ship the weights," looked at what fell out of scaled post-training and decided to gatekeep its own model for two weeks. Sit with that. The DeepSeek playbook, the Qwen playbook, the whole Chinese open-weights wave has been "release everything, commoditize the frontier." This is the first time one of them hit a capability that scared them enough to pump the brakes. The offense-defense split we've been tracking all week — one capability, two costumes, depending on who holds the login — Z.ai just internalized it into their release schedule.

Now, the contrarian pairing, because the same forty-eight hours delivered the perfect intellectual foil. A researcher writing under the handle "Out of Distribution" shipped a tool called Torchwright that does the opposite of everything I just described. GLM 5.3 says capability lives in post-training. Torchwright says: what if you skip training entirely? It's a compiler. You write out grade-school long multiplication as a computation graph — the actual algorithm, digit by digit, carry by carry — and Torchwright compiles that logic directly into transformer weights. No gradient descent. No data. No training run at all. You hand it the algorithm, it emits the weights. And the resulting model — built on an ordinary Phi-3 checkpoint — hits one-hundred-percent accuracy across all three million supported problems, up to twelve-digit multiplication. Perfect. Every time.

Now, the author is the first to say this is a toy — nobody needs a transformer that does arithmetic when your phone already does it. But hold the two stories next to each other, because together they bracket a real question. GLM 5.3 says the weights are a vessel you fill through experience, through the environment, through post-training. Torchwright says the weights are just a programmable substrate — a computer you can write to directly, bypassing the learning entirely. So what is a model actually doing when it "learns" multiplication? Is it discovering an algorithm, or is it groping toward one that Torchwright could have just written in? The honest answer is we don't fully know, and that gap — between the capability we train into these things and the capability we could in principle compile in — is exactly the black box the whole field keeps circling. One lab poured compute into an environment and got an emergent exploit-writer it didn't ask for. One researcher wrote the algorithm by hand and got a perfect calculator he fully understands. The distance between those two is the distance between where AI is and where interpretability wishes it were.

Let's talk money moves, and this one is a tell. Situational Awareness — the hedge fund Leopold Aschenbrenner spun up in 2024 off the back of his "compute is destiny" manifesto — just put another four-hundred million dollars into a chip startup called Source Foundry, bringing its total to half a billion. And the timing is the whole story. This fund got wrecked. As AI infrastructure stocks slid this summer, its assets under management reportedly fell from twenty billion to ten billion. At the end of July it sold off the bulk of its public portfolio to Ken Griffin's Citadel — a near-collapse. It held onto two things: its Anthropic shares, and its conviction. And what does a bloodied compute-thesis fund do with its remaining chips? It concentrates them into the deepest physical bottleneck in the entire stack. Source Foundry isn't a model company or an app. Founded by two Stanford researchers, valued around five billion, it's going after ASML — the Dutch giant with a near-monopoly on the extreme-ultraviolet lithography machines that print the most advanced chips on Earth. Aschenbrenner got margin-called by the market and responded by betting harder on the machines that make the machines that make the intelligence. Whatever you think of the guy, that is the compute-is-destiny thesis in its purest, most stubborn form: when everything above you is commoditizing — the models, the apps, the tokens — the durable scarcity is the physical chokepoint, the 算力 supply chain, the lithography. It rhymes with the Hadrian and the power-lease stories we covered earlier this month: capital fleeing the model layer and burrowing into atoms.

And for the deep cut, I want to take you somewhere completely different, because it reframes this whole "AI got superhuman, now what" anxiety in the most useful way I've heard. On Thursday's No Priors, Sarah Guo and Elad Gil sat down with Erik Allebest, the CEO of Chess.com, and the episode is literally titled "What Chess.com teaches us about superhuman capabilities." Here's the argument chain, and it's worth following.

Chess is the one domain where the superhuman-AI future already happened — decades ago. When Deep Blue beat Kasparov in 1997, the confident prediction was that engines would kill chess. Why would humans play a game that machines had solved? And Allebest's point is that the prediction was exactly, measurably wrong — but with a twist in the middle. There was a stretch where the dominant engine, Stockfish, played such flawless, machine-perfect chess that top-level play did start to feel, his word, "boring." Perfect play is sterile. The interesting thing is what pulled it back: the neural-net engines, Leela Chess Zero and its kin, which learned to play in a way that felt more human, more creative, more surprising. And then the real inversion — AI didn't replace the human game, it became the infrastructure underneath it. Game review, puzzles, coaching, personalized training: AI is now the tool that helps humans get better at the thing the AI is already better at. And the result isn't a graveyard. Chess.com has around two-hundred-fifty million registered members and ten million people playing on a given day. A domain that was "solved" by superhuman AI is the healthiest it has ever been, precisely because the superhuman capability got re-cast from opponent to coach.

Ollie's takeaway, and this ties the whole episode in a bow: the fear is always that superhuman AI in your domain makes you obsolete. The chess data says the actual outcome is re-mediation — the human activity reorganizes itself around the machine as scaffolding, and often grows. Which is the same lesson as GLM: the value moved into the layer around the raw capability. For a biomedical AI lab, the model that out-predicts your assays isn't the end of the wet lab — it's the Leela moment, where the tool becomes the coach and the interesting work moves to how you wrap it, prompt it, and put it in front of a scientist.

So here's the one thing to watch. End of this month, when Z.ai's decision comes due — do the GLM 5.3 weights actually ship after their "safety hardening," and in what form? Because that's the first real-world test of an open-weights lab self-gatekeeping an offensive capability. If they release clean, the norm holds. If they carve it up, geofence it, or quietly delay — the way we saw licenses get fenced earlier this month — then the open-weights movement just discovered its own red line, live, on a two-week timer. That's the tell. I'll see you tomorrow.
