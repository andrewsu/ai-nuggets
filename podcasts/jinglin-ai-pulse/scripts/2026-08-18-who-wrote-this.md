# Who Wrote This? — 2026-08-18

## Script

Good morning, Ollie. Here's what the AI world is talking about today.

Yesterday the argument was about plumbing — routing, serving, memory. Today it's about something the plumbing can't fix. Anthropic started stamping an invisible mark into every sentence Claude writes, and a chunk of its paying users revolted. A security firm published a chain where an AI wrote a vulnerability into a company's build system, a second AI reviewed the code and missed it, and a third AI — an autonomous attacker — found it and stole a credential inside five days. And a reporter traced a hundred polished, footnoted policy reports back to a think tank that doesn't exist, built so that chatbots would cite it. Three unrelated stories, one question underneath all of them: where did this artifact come from, and who can prove it. Call it the provenance problem — 溯源, tracing something back to its source. This week it stopped being theoretical.

Let's start with the Twitter pulse.

The story that ate the timeline is Anthropic's text watermark — 水印. The mechanism is the whole fight. When a language model picks the next word, several would usually work about equally well — grey or overcast. So the watermark doesn't change what Claude says; it changes the source of the randomness used to break those ties. A secret cryptographic key nudges the coin flip, and if you hold the key you can look at a long enough passage and say, with statistical confidence, this came out of Claude. It's Google DeepMind's SynthID-Text approach, applied globally — new models from this month, older ones retrofitted over the coming months. Anthropic says there's no impact on content, creativity, or readability, citing DeepMind's published testing that found no statistically significant difference in how readers rated the output. Only Anthropic holds the key, and a detection interface is promised but not shipped. The reason it's happening now is regulatory: the European AI Act's transparency duties came into force at the start of this month.

The loudest complaints aren't arguments — people canceling because they can no longer pass Claude's writing off as their own to a client or a professor is just the feature working. The serious objection came from John Gruber, and it's about craft. His claim is that the premise, that genuinely interchangeable word choices exist, is false. Grey and overcast are not the same word. So a system that sometimes raises the probability of a slightly worse word and lowers it for the best one isn't marking a neutral substrate; it's degrading the text a little, deliberately, on behalf of a third party. His line, verbatim: "The idea that anything other than my needs should factor into the generation of text for me is patently offensive."

The sharpest practical objection is different: what if Claude didn't write the thing? If you drafted a paper and used Claude to tighten the prose, does your text carry a mark saying a machine made it? Anthropic's answer is reasonable — the watermark attaches only to words the model chose, so under light editing there's, in their phrasing, very little for it to attach to. But that answer exposes the limit. The signal is sparse in fact-dense passages, because dense factual prose offers fewer arbitrary choices to encode into; it's nearly absent in code, surviving mainly in comments; and it doesn't survive a rewrite. A developer named James Padolsey has already shipped a tool to strip it, with the criticism that lands hardest for me: this regime mostly inconveniences ordinary users while doing little against anyone deliberately evading it.

So watermarking is provenance imposed from outside the model, weakest exactly where the stakes are highest — short text, factual claims, code — and strongest where it matters least. A compliance artifact more than a security one. The next two stories are what happens where it can't reach.

The second item is the cleanest example I've seen of AI operating on all three sides of one incident. The security firm Wiz published a writeup of a bug in Snowflake's public GitHub repository, where a build workflow took the title of any issue a member of the public filed and dropped it straight into a shell command — the textbook injection hole, where you type a quote character in a title and everything after it runs as code on the build server. That workflow used to be written safely, passing untrusted input through environment variables and a proper parser. The change that broke it arrived in a pull request generated with Copilot, which swapped the safe pattern for the unsafe one. Copilot Autofix — the AI security reviewer — looked at the final revision of that same pull request and raised nothing. Then Wiz's autonomous red-team agent, sweeping public repositories, found it within five days, crafted an issue title that broke out of the shell command, and pushed the repository's Jira credential out to a server it controlled — and when its first payload died on a shell syntax error, the agent fixed its own quoting and tried again until it worked. The stolen token had read access to Snowflake's engineering projects, compliance tracking, and bug bounty system. Snowflake confirms nobody else got in during the window.

Sit with the shape of that. An AI introduced the flaw, an AI certified it, an AI exploited it, and the humans were never the bottleneck at any stage. We've spent months here on agents that write code; this is the second-order problem. When the reviewer is also a model, "it passed review" stops carrying independent information — you didn't get a second opinion, you got a correlated one. And discovery-to-exploitation is now measured in days by machines that never sleep, while credential rotation is still measured in quarters by humans who do.

The third beat points the same problem at the input side. Reporting this week identifies something calling itself the Hanover Institute for Public Policy — presenting as a neutral research shop on Israel and Palestine, with over a hundred unbylined reports published since the sixth of August. It isn't a think tank. It was manufactured by a production company called Piro, working for the Israeli government's advertising agency, for a reported nine hundred thousand dollars. The tell isn't even hidden: Piro's own website advertises a service it calls A-I story optimization — engineering content for how large language models evaluate credibility — and a co-founder's public post named the targets, ChatGPT, Gemini, and Perplexity. Eleven of a dozen sampled articles came back flagged as machine-written.

Notice the exploit. It isn't jailbreaking a model and it isn't poisoning a training set. It's dressing up in the clothes retrieval systems are trained to trust — footnotes, a table of contents, a measured institutional tone, volume — so that when a model reaches for a source on a contested question, this is what's there. Watermarking marks what the machine wrote; it does nothing about machine-written text purpose-built to be laundered through a machine as a citation. Our provenance regime checks the output layer, and the attack moved to the source layer.

One quick counterpoint, from the top paper on the daily feed this morning. The OpenMOSS team in China released MOSS-VL, real-time vision-language models that watch and talk at once — video feeds in through gated cross-attention while the model is generating text — and they shipped all five checkpoints, the full training curriculum, and the inference code, 开源, in the open. On a day about not being able to tell where anything came from, one lab published its entire provenance chain.

Now to Money Moves, and today's is a genuine reversal.

Groq raised three hundred and fifty million dollars, led by the investment firm Disruptive with Nvidia planning to participate, at a valuation of three and a half billion — down from nearly seven billion a year ago. A down round at roughly half, and the reason is what the money is for. Groq built its reputation on a custom inference chip designed as the alternative to Nvidia. Last December Nvidia did a roughly twenty-billion-dollar non-exclusive licensing deal that also moved founder and chief executive Jonathan Ross and the company president onto its own payroll — taking the assets and leaving the corporate shell standing. What's left is pivoting to being a neocloud: thirteen data centers, six million developers, scaling toward two hundred megawatts by next year, running Nvidia GPUs.

Hold that against yesterday, when OpenAI was running its flagship on Cerebras wafer-scale silicon at fourteen times the speed and I called it a bet that specialized inference hardware becomes its own capability axis. Today the other great specialized-silicon bet gives up on its own silicon, at half its old price, and becomes a reseller of the incumbent's chips — with the incumbent in the round. Both are true at once, and the resolution is uncomfortable: the thesis that custom inference hardware wins may be correct, and Nvidia may simply buy the thesis rather than lose to it. Twenty billion for the team and a license is cheaper than a competitor. So when you evaluate an AI hardware vendor, the question isn't only whether the architecture is good — it's whether its engineers will still be there in three years, or at Nvidia, with the data centers full of the thing they were built to replace.

One data point loops back to the top: Anthropic's annualized revenue run rate hit sixty-five billion dollars at the end of July, up from forty-seven billion in May and nine billion at the end of last year, with a confidential filing pointing at a fall listing. That reframes the watermark fight. This isn't a small company making a principled gesture; it's a company weeks from public markets eating a visible hit to goodwill with its most technical users in order to satisfy a European regulator. When revenue compounds like that, regulatory durability is worth more than the affection of the people canceling.

Now the deep cut, which reframes everything above.

On The Cognitive Revolution earlier this month, Dan Balsam, chief technology officer of the interpretability company Goodfire, laid out an argument about what's actually inside a model that ends somewhere useful for a biology lab. It starts as a correction. For a few years the working assumption in interpretability was the linear representation hypothesis: a concept is a direction in activation space, one vector per idea, find the vector and you've found the concept. Goodfire says that's too coarse. Concepts sit on geometric structures — manifolds — where the shape itself carries meaning. Days of the week arrange in a circle; emotions arrange on a wheel, valence on one axis and intensity on the other. Balsam's analogy: "It's maybe like the difference between understanding the periodic table and understanding chemistry — you can have all the individual elements, and that gives you some information, but really the way in which they combine, the structures they form, that's what helps you gain a sense of the complexity of the world."

The load-bearing consequence is that this explains why steering models has been so unreliable. Push hard along a concept vector in a straight line and you leave the manifold — you're in a region of activation space the model never occupied in training, and you get out-of-distribution nonsense. Rotate around the curve instead and you get precise control with the output intact. The failures weren't telling us steering is impossible; they were telling us the geometry was wrong.

Two findings matter for your work. The first they call predictive data debugging: run a fine-tuning dataset through an interpreted model and read off which concepts that data is going to move, before you spend the compute — catching unintended behavior at the data stage rather than in evaluation. Notice what that is in today's vocabulary. It's provenance done from the inside: not a mark stamped on the output, but a causal trace from a behavior back to the data that put it there.

The second is the one I'd point a Scripps audience at. Models trained purely on genome sequences spontaneously recover the tree of life — evolutionary relationships nobody supervised them to learn, sitting there in the geometry. Chemistry models recover the periodic table the same way. These aren't ontologies imposed by an annotator; they're the compression the data forced on the model. Which is the most concrete argument I've heard that reading a model's internals is a scientific instrument, not just a safety tool. If a sequence model independently reconstructed phylogeny, the parts of its geometry that don't correspond to anything you recognize are a hypothesis generator.

Here's my takeaway. Today gave us three failures of external provenance — a watermark that marks the wrong layer, a review chain where every checkpoint was a model so passing review told you nothing independent, and a corpus seeded with fake authorities because retrieval trusts the costume. All three try to answer "where did this come from" by inspecting the artifact after the fact. Goodfire's bet is that the durable version gets answered inside the model — trace the behavior to the data, read the geometry, don't take the output's word for it. Much harder than stamping a key into the sampling step, and the only one of these an adversary can't paraphrase away.

To wrap, the one thing to watch tomorrow: whether Anthropic actually ships the watermark detection interface, and who gets a key. Right now Anthropic is the only party on earth that can answer the question its own watermark exists to answer. A mark only its issuer can read is a compliance record, not a public provenance system — the European transparency requirement satisfied on paper while nobody outside one company gains the ability to check anything. That's the difference between provenance and paperwork.

That's your pulse. See you tomorrow.
