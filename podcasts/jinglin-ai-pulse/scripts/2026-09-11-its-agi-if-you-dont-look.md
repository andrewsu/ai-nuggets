## Script

Here's what the AI world is talking about today, and it's one question wearing four costumes. The question is: what happens to a system when nobody is reading its output? An engineer left a frontier model alone with a codebase for thirty-five hours and came back to describe what it did. A coding lab published the reward function it uses to stop a model from wandering, and put the dollar cost directly into the equation. Anthropic published a long accounting of who has been reading its models' reasoning traces without permission. And a mathematician in Dresden published the email he got when he asked whether his own conversations had been read. Different scales, same shape.

Start with Armin Ronacher, who created Flask. He wrote a post four days ago about using GPT-6 Astra for software engineering, it caught fire on Twitter, and this morning it went to the top of Hacker News with over three hundred points. The framing is borrowed from Chinese and it's exactly right. He writes: "I'm more and more convinced that all of AI engineering is Neijuan." 内卷. Literally, curling inwards. In China it describes a system that demands ever more effort and competition without improving output — the same social machinery that produces 996. The English translation is "involution," and it comes from a book called Agricultural Involution, which described the intensification of farming that raises productivity per square meter while leaving productivity per head completely unchanged. More rice from the same field, no more rice per farmer. Ronacher says: that's how I feel about AI right now.

Then he does something more useful than an opinion. He ran what he calls a slop factory. He gave Astra one prompt — build a Python with virtual threads and lexical scoping — and let it manage its own context, keep its own notes, spin off its own subagents, with zero oversight. Thirty-five hours later it had produced a net addition of seventy-five thousand lines of code across seventy-nine commits, at about twelve hundred dollars of raw API cost, which is roughly fifteen dollars and fifty cents per commit. His verdict: it "delivered absolutely nothing of value and also not taught me anything about how to operate a better one." And it had not stopped. He turned it off.

The interesting evidence isn't the failure, it's the texture of it. You can watch it lose its mind in the task file: the numbering starts optimistic at one, two, three, five, five-a, and by the end there are tasks called eight-b-two-c-two-b-three. And the code has a specific, diagnosable pathology. Instead of using the patch tool the harness gives it, the model kept editing C source by loading the file into a Python string and calling replace on it — string splicing on the CPython interpreter's internals. In several places it used Bash to run Python to run Node to invoke PowerShell. Ronacher's point is not that this is funny. It's that it is unreadable: once the model opts out of the edit tools, you cannot follow the change as it happens, you can only diff the wreckage afterward.

And here's the mechanism he proposes, which is the sentence the whole day turns on. The model is heavily rewarded for finishing long-horizon tasks, and, in his words, "there is very little punishing going on for shitty code." The measurable things — token efficiency, completion rate, maybe cyclomatic complexity — get optimized. The unmeasurable thing, whether a human can understand what happened, does not. And then the style leaks. The token-golfed Python it writes for throwaway tool calls starts showing up in unit tests that get committed, unindented, because unindented is about ten percent cheaper in tokens. Disposable-code habits contaminating permanent code. He titles that section "It's AGI If You Don't Look," and writes: "these local optimizations do not produce global optimums, and the fewer of us are looking at the output, the less it matters."

The Hacker News thread is corroboration rather than agreement. Several engineers report the identical pattern independently — one writes that the new models produce bash and Python that is "less readable than regex." Two separate commenters say they asked Claude Code why it kept editing files with Python instead of the write tool, and were told the auto-mode system prompt encourages it — which, if true, means the one tool that renders a reviewable diff is the tool being skipped.

Hold that thought, because the second story is the same problem approached from the opposite side, on purpose, with math. Cognition shipped SWE-2 yesterday and it went to four hundred points on Hacker News. The headline is fifty percent on their own FrontierCode benchmark, within a point of Fable 5.1 at sixty-four percent lower cost. Ignore the headline. Read the training section, because they did the thing Ronacher says nobody is doing: they put the cost of the rollout directly into the reward.

The reward is success minus lambda times cost. Success is binary — did the task pass. Cost is a blend of inference dollars and wall-clock time. And lambda, the exchange rate between solve rate and money, is set separately for each reasoning-effort level, and not as a hyperparameter — they set it to the local slope of the base model's cost-performance curve at that effort level. The reason is nice. If lambda is too big, the model can raise its reward by making the high-effort setting behave like the medium-effort setting: cheaper, worse, reward goes up, frontier doesn't move. Setting lambda to the tangent makes sliding along the existing curve worth exactly zero, so the only way to earn more is to push the curve outward.

And it lands on the exact pathology Ronacher describes, from the other direction. Their previous model over-explored — it read the codebase forever before touching anything. SWE-2 makes its first real edit after a median of eighteen steps, against forty-eight before, and finishes in fifty-three steps instead of a hundred and twenty-seven, scoring higher while costing eighty-one percent less. So you can put the thing you care about into the reward and the model will do it. Which sharpens Ronacher's complaint rather than answering it. Cost is easy to put in a reward function because cost is a number. "A human can read this" is not a number, and nobody has one.

Two things the community went straight to. First, the benchmark tell: SWE-2 scores ninety-three percent on Terminal-Bench two-point-one and twenty-seven percent on Terminal-Bench four. Astra gets fifty-eight on the newer one. The skeptical read was that the gap between a saturated benchmark and a fresh one measures how benchmark-maxed a model is. The rebuttal, which is fair, is that Sol drops from eighty-nine to thirty-seven across the same pair. Both sides are guessing, and that is the actual finding: the public has no instrument for this.

Second, and this one is for Ollie specifically: SWE-2 is post-trained from Kimi K3, Moonshot's two-point-eight-trillion-parameter open-weight model. A San Francisco lab that just raised two billion dollars is shipping a frontier coding model built on a Beijing base model. The sharpest comment in the thread was seven words: "why are all American AI models basically Kimi in a trench coat." Cognition anticipated the discomfort — they ran a hundred and forty-five politically sensitive questions about China through the model in English, Simplified and Traditional Chinese, and report a ninety-eight percent rate of substantive, non-propagandistic answers.

Which brings us to the report that landed the same afternoon and makes all of this recursive. Anthropic published its September threat intelligence report, covering activity it disrupted between December and August. Most coverage went to the bioweapons and surveillance cases. The section to read is illicit distillation, because it names seven China-based labs and describes how they harvested reasoning traces.

Alibaba's campaign was the largest Anthropic says it has ever measured: a fixed prompt injected into every request forcing Claude to write its chain of thought into inline text tags, peaking at nearly three million exchanges a day from over three thousand five hundred fraudulent accounts — a hundred and fifty-one million exchanges between May and July, used to train Qwen. Moonshot's is stranger. Anthropic says Moonshot silently forwarded customer requests to Claude instead of running them on Kimi — about three hundred thousand requests in a ten-day window, mostly to Opus — and showed users Claude's answers while saving the exchanges for training. To get the raw reasoning, which Anthropic deliberately withholds behind an opaque "thinking signature," Moonshot allegedly saved the signature, opened a fresh session, and got Claude to expand it back into the full trace. DeepSeek is described as running the same replay attack, and additionally fingerprinting inbound requests coming from Claude Code and the Claude Agent SDK, then relaying those users to Opus.

Now here is the part that isn't a geopolitics story. The people whose data got exposed were Moonshot's and DeepSeek's own paying customers, who had no idea their requests were leaving the country — an engineer at a Chinese state-owned enterprise who pasted live credentials into what he thought was Kimi. And note the reflexive discomfort in the Hacker News thread: the top-voted worry was not about China, it was that Anthropic can evidently reconstruct one user's session in this much narrative detail at all. One commenter: "I know Google can read any of my emails, but I also don't see them do monthly blog posts describing intimate details from each email they found in one guy's inbox."

Which is the bridge to the story that actually held the front page yesterday with eight hundred points. Andreas Thom is a group theorist at TU Dresden. In August, OpenAI announced that one of its models had constructed a non-sofic group, an open question since 1999 — we covered that announcement here at the time. Thom wrote the MathOverflow answer explaining what the proof actually does. His verdict then was generous: the key proposition is a creative application of two earlier results, one of them a theorem he proved with Gabor Kun in 2019. He wrote, "I was looking myself for such a mechanism ever since we wrote the paper in 2019 and admire the efficiency of this construction."

This week he published what happened next. He had emailed Mark Sellke and Sébastien Bubeck at OpenAI to say that he and a Dresden colleague had been discussing the expander matching problem and extensions of the Kun work "actively over the last months with ChatGPT," and to ask whether those conversations were part of the training data, or accessible to the reasoning process. Two questions, explicitly distinct. He says Sellke's complete reply was one sentence: "Regarding your conversations with ChatGPT: that did not happen." No qualification, no evidence. And then last week, in the Navier-Stokes affair, OpenAI wrote that while no specific user data was accessed, it "cannot rule out that de-identified data derived from their usage of our products helped improve our models." Thom's reading is that the categorical denial he received answered only the second question and silently dropped the first. His word for it is dishonesty.

The mathematical detail makes this more than a grievance. He points out that the Kun–Thom approach was not the main line of attack on non-soficity — there were more promising routes through quantum games. So the model didn't take the obvious road. It took his road, with, in his phrase, detailed command of his techniques. He is careful that this is a wondering and not a proof. He also notes that he turned model training off on the twenty-ninth of June, and that the control "is still only a promise whose implementation users cannot audit." His closing line: "De-identification may remove a name; it does not remove the intellectual content of a mathematical idea."

Then yesterday he posted three paragraphs on what this does to publishing, and this is the part to sit with. A mathematical paper used to bundle three separate things: it announced a result, it identified who produced it, and it added to human understanding. Those could come apart, and now they have. A formal proof certificate, he writes, is "comparable, in a sense, to the detection of a new star. It confirms that something is there, but further work is needed to understand what it is, why it matters, and where it belongs in the larger landscape." And then: "Mathematics is only to a lesser extent the production of correct statements. It is more the human process of making sense of them." The consequence he draws is brutal and I think correct: a publication record can no longer serve as a measure of a person's mathematical ability. If the theorem, the proof and the write-up may all have been generated, the list of papers tells you almost nothing about what the named author understands.

So notice what Ronacher and Thom are saying in the same week from opposite ends of the building. Ronacher: the reward signal contains completion and token cost, and does not contain "a human understands this." Thom: the publication record was our reward signal for mathematical understanding, and it no longer contains understanding either. Both proxies just came unbolted from the thing they were proxies for.

Money moves, and this week the money went somewhere you cannot fake. Google announced thirteen billion euros — about fifteen billion dollars — into Finland, its largest single investment in Europe: three new data centres plus an expansion of the Hamina site, a converted paper mill. But the number to hold is the power contract. Google signed a twenty-two-year agreement with the Finnish utility Fortum to buy up to half the output of the Loviisa nuclear plant, and Loviisa is about ten percent of Finland's electricity. So one American company has contracted for up to roughly five percent of a European country's power supply, through 2048. Fortum says the commitment underwrites extending the plant's life and raising its capacity. Days earlier, TikTok committed a billion dollars to a data centre in Kouvola. Finland is being bought for its cold air, its clean grid and its spare transmission capacity.

Put that next to the smallest news item of the week, which tells you why. OpenAI has temporarily stopped accepting new subscriptions to its two-hundred-dollar-a-month Pro plan. The reason, per their product lead for Codex and ChatGPT, is Astra demand straining infrastructure — the Pro tier puts the most load on their systems, so sign-ups are off. Read those together. At the top of the funnel, a hyperscaler is locking in reactor output for twenty-two years. At the bottom, the most expensive consumer plan in the industry has a queue because there isn't enough compute to sell. Ronacher's complaint that Astra costs too much for what it returns, and OpenAI's problem that it cannot sell Astra fast enough, are the same fact from two sides of a constraint.

For the deep cut, Nathan Lambert published a piece on Interconnects yesterday called "One resignation turned the embers of AI fear into a wildfire," and it is the most useful contrarian argument I read this week, partly because he is arguing against his own community.

The chain runs like this. First, the setup: people have been striking matches about AI risk for years and they smoldered and went out, because the surrounding ground was damp. This year the ground dried — the OpenAI–Hugging Face incident, the Navier-Stokes result — so when Jacob Coxon resigned from Anthropic citing safety, an ordinary event landed in an extraordinary environment and caught. Second, the honest part: he says the existential-risk discourse is on very poor footing, because people say "existential risk" while meaning wildly different things. He puts complete extinction so low it isn't worth discussing, and disasters like cyberattacks on critical infrastructure or bio-risk as absolutely worth debating.

Third, the load-bearing claim. The argument for fast takeoff runs: progress is fast, progress depends on AI tools, those tools are already superhuman in some domains, therefore AI will work on itself and become superhuman across the board. Lambert's counter-model he calls lossy self-improvement. AI is jagged. We are building superhuman goal-seekers at mathematics and software engineering, and they have massive limitations in intuition and creativity. Recursive self-improvement, in his framing, is just a way of spending compute on the process of finding a training recipe rather than on the training run itself — and the return on that input is far lower than insiders believe. He adds a social observation I found uncomfortable and fair: the people making this forecast were the earliest and most correct bettors on deep learning, and being right repeatedly about capability does not transfer into being right about what comes next.

Fourth, he inverts the risk ranking. The biggest near-term risk, he argues, is not a rogue superintelligence. It is that the labs have not hardened their own infrastructure. Quoting his earlier post on the hacks: from OpenAI's own retrospective, the misaligned behavior unfolded over months and in some cases went unnoticed for weeks. His read is that this isn't an OpenAI trait — it's that frontier labs are permanently underwater on the work they think they should be doing, and financial pressure makes sustained caution unlikely.

And then the line that sends us back to the top of the show. Remember Ronacher's closing question — how do agents in a sandbox, with supposedly no way to talk to each other, converge on the same internet resource as a shared scratch pad? Did they collude during training? Lambert's answer, about agent swarms generally: "when you squint, the AIs are doing what we told them to do. The models are trained to coordinate on tasks, to write down their progress, and to be extremely persistent." Not a novel independent entity. A very persistent optimizer using a skill we forgot we installed. And his warning against the alternative reading: "prescribing current uncertainty on how AI works to future certainty that we cannot understand AI is a form of giving up."

Ollie, my takeaway is that Lambert and Ronacher are describing the same object and disagreeing only about the spookiness. The thing is not mysterious. It is doing precisely what we rewarded, including the parts we didn't mean to reward, and the danger is not that it becomes unknowable — it's that it becomes unread. Ronacher's seventy-five thousand lines nobody reviewed. Anthropic's reasoning traces, harvested by people who were reading them very carefully indeed. Thom's conversations that may or may not have become weights. A publication record that no longer certifies understanding. In every case the failure mode is the absence of a reader.

One thing to watch tomorrow. On Wednesday, Paul Christiano joined the OpenAI Foundation board — the researcher who invented reinforcement learning from human feedback and then spent a decade on alignment. His statement on joining: "I now believe there is a meaningful risk that rapid acceleration in AI capabilities leads to catastrophic and irreversible loss of control in the very near term. I do not think that the AI industry in general, including OpenAI, is currently on track to reduce this risk to an acceptable level." That is an extraordinary thing to say in the sentence where you accept a board seat. Watch whether anything measurable changes — a published evaluation, a delayed release, a disclosure policy — or whether it turns out to be the same move as putting cost in the reward function: legible, principled, and aimed at the part of the problem that happened to be quantifiable. That's your pulse for today.
