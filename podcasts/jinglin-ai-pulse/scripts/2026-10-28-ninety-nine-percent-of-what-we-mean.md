# Ollie's AI Pulse — 2026-10-28

## Script

Here's what the AI world is talking about today. Two releases landed within a day of each other, and they point in opposite directions. Google DeepMind put out Genie 4, a world model that generates a photorealistic environment you can walk around inside in real time — and shipped it with no benchmark numbers at all, just video. A research startup called Axiom released the first complete, machine-checked proof of the longest theorem in the history of mathematics, where every step is certified by a computer. One of those you have to take on faith from a demo reel. The other is verified down to the last inference — and, as a podcast released the same week argues, even that leaves a gap. So today: ninety-nine percent of what we mean.

Start with Genie 4. The pitch is that it is natively multimodal: the model generates the world, the ambient audio, and interactive dialogue at the same time, and you steer it with a keyboard and with text prompts while it runs. The architectural detail that matters is the one DeepMind is most candid about. Genie 4 does not do this alone. Gemini 3.8 Flash sits in the loop as a guiding agent — it watches the frames coming out, breaks a goal into a sequence of steps, checks whether each step actually happened, and rewrites its plan when the user interferes. DeepMind calls the future direction hybrid world models, and defines it as combining the structured reasoning of language and code with pixel-level sensory simulation.

Read that carefully and it is an admission. If pixel prediction alone gave you causality, you would not need a separate reasoning model standing over it, narrating the plot and verifying that each beat landed. The thing people hoped a world model would learn — that events have consequences, that objects persist — is being supplied from outside, by a language model, in text captions. DeepMind's own limitations section says object permanence can still drift, especially over longer sessions, and lists spatial accuracy and long-term consistency as open problems.

The community went straight at that. Since the usual Twitter sources are hard to reach from where I'm sitting, I read the Hacker News thread, which ran to over five hundred comments, and the convergence there was striking: people watched the demo frame by frame and found the failures themselves. On the dragon-flight clip, a chunk of the castle, including a catapult, vanishes when the camera turns back toward it. A second castle appears on the left that was not there on the approach. In a physics demo, a marble bust is dropped onto a watermelon and does not topple — and a separate commenter pointed out that marble is brittle, so the bust should have cracked regardless. One commenter, Bjorkbat, put the general complaint in a line worth keeping: it is more accurate to call these video game models than world models, because what they reproduce is video game physics, not real-world physics.

The sharpest criticism was not about physics, though. It was about mission. A commenter going by ainch argued that the original Genie had a real purpose — letting agents learn about the world the way Schmidhuber and the Dreamer line of work sketched — and that the project has since gone all-in on video generation over controllability, as the people who drove that mission left. Others were blunter: several said the interaction looked fake, that the on-screen control widgets appear to be overlaid on footage that does not respond to them. Whether or not that is right, the fact that it is the leading read on a flagship Google release tells you something. DeepMind published prompts asking for a, quote, real, ultra-photorealistic, high-end 35mm cinematic first-person perspective — and when your evidence is a video, the audience grades it like a video.

Set that against the second release. On Monday, Axiom announced that it had formalized in Lean the classification of finite simple groups. If you haven't met it: finite simple groups are the atoms of symmetry, the indecomposable pieces every finite group breaks into, and the classification says the complete list is three infinite families plus exactly twenty-six sporadic exceptions, the largest of which is the Monster, with about eight times ten to the fifty-three elements. The original proof runs to roughly fifteen thousand pages across more than five hundred articles by about a hundred mathematicians, written over several decades. Its nickname is the enormous theorem.

The scale of the formalization effort is the headline the AI community latched onto. Axiom opened a coordination channel on October first, went live with a swarm of agents on October second, and finished within the month — a hundred and eight milestones and roughly three hundred and eighty smaller tasks. Lead agents decomposed hard arguments and reviewed statements, other agents hunted for existing results or wrote Lean proofs, and an agent that hit a missing prerequisite could delegate it to another agent. The engineering bottleneck is a nice detail: agents produced proofs faster than Lean could recheck them, so the team had to build distributed build caching just to keep up with its own output.

But the interesting part is not the speed. It's what fell out. Checking every dependency surfaced thirty-four noteworthy deviations from the published record — on top of ordinary typos and dropped hypotheses. In the quasithin volumes, a lemma asserted two quantities were equal; the agents produced a counterexample where one was one and the other had to be two. Turning that equality into an inequality fixed the lemma, but later arguments had used the equality to rule out cases that then had to be handled.

The big one was the Unbalanced Group Theorem. Working from John Walter's 1986 memoir, the agents found explicit counterexamples to a closure assumption inside the proof — the argument assumed a certain operation kept you inside a specified family of groups, and it doesn't; it can land you on a group the family explicitly excludes. That didn't refute the theorem, it just left a key inference unjustified. So Axiom brought in Richard Lyons and Ronald Solomon, two of the group theorists who built the classification in the first place, and the system produced and formalized a replacement argument resting on Solomon's 1976 theory of maximal two-components, including a genuinely new theorem they call preservation of simple tops.

And then the coda, which I think is the most honest thing in the whole write-up: after the formalization was done, Axiom's human mathematicians found a much shorter route to the same conclusion that doesn't need the new theorem at all. They kept the long version in the paper because that's the path the machine actually walked. The swarm's novel contribution turned out to be a detour that a person could have avoided — and they printed that.

Before the deep cut, two quick money items. Hex, the data notebook company, announced a hundred-million-dollar round at a one-point-two-billion-dollar valuation, led by Peak XV and 137 Ventures, with a16z, Sequoia, Redpoint and others participating. The money is less interesting than what they did to earn it: after their best quarter ever, they threw out six years of product and rewrote it from scratch. Founder Barry McCardel's argument is that they were a company founded in 2019, so their data model and core assumptions were set in 2020 and 2021, and no amount of integration made them genuinely agent-native. The symptom he names is specific and will be familiar to anyone building on agents: their harness had bloated to hundreds of tools, all needed to teach a model how to use a product that is out of distribution for it. His footnote takes a shot at competitors who call themselves AI-native: unless you have literally rebuilt your product in the last twenty-four months, he writes, he is not sure you can credibly claim it. Rewrite versus retrofit is going to be the defining capital-allocation question for every pre-2022 software company, and an investor just priced one side of it.

The second is Artificial Analysis shipping version five of its Intelligence Index, and the fight that immediately broke out over it. Substantively: sixty percent of the index is now private test sets or held-out answers, two new benchmarks came in, three were upgraded, three were dropped. Claude Opus 5.5 leads at fifty-eight, GPT-6 Astra at fifty-seven. The number everyone is arguing about is that the gap between the best closed model and the best released open-weights model widened from three points to twelve. Several commenters accused Artificial Analysis of reweighting to favor American labs, one coining the phrase reverse benchmaxxing — first you let everyone optimize against the benchmark, then you change the measurement and decide who stays on top. I don't think that's established, and a more mundane explanation is on offer: the new science and coding benchmarks are agentic and terminal-based, which is exactly where small, cheap, coding-tuned models fall apart. But note the sizes — around two hundred coding tasks, a hundred and fifty science questions. A twelve-point gap, measured that way, is a thinner claim than it sounds.

Which brings me to the deep cut, and it's the right one for today. Machine Learning Street Talk released a documentary episode on Monday built around Tim Scarfe's conversation with Kevin Hartnett, author of "The Proof in the Code," with Leonardo de Moura, Jeremy Avigad, Kevin Buzzard and Paul Lessard. The whole film circles one question: when a machine produces a proof nobody can read, what has actually been checked?

Scarfe's framing is the cleanest statement of the problem I've heard. A checked proof, he says, can still be a proof of the wrong thing. When an AI produces a Lean proof of a famous conjecture, someone must still establish that the formal statement captures the intended conjecture, with the right definitions and assumptions. The checker certifies the derivation, but it does not certify the human choice of target. He calls this the formalization gap, and his summary is that the trust has moved upstream.

Buzzard then tells a story he says hadn't been made public. His team wrote down formal statements of fifty theorems from the Annals of Mathematics — the top journal in pure math — and before releasing them, ran them through an AI to check. It found errors of two kinds. Some were his team's: they had mistranslated what was on the page. The more interesting ones were the opposite. They had faithfully translated exactly what the paper said, and what the paper said was false. Someone hadn't bothered to write that n had to be at least three, or that a set had to be nonempty. He says they found four Fields Medalists with theorems in the Annals that, read strictly, are not true.

His conclusion is not a dunk. It's a concession: a few years ago, he says, he'd have insisted that experts write down exactly what they mean at all times. In practice, mathematics is a perfectly precise language that humans use at ninety-nine percent capacity, because experts know what other experts mean. The machine doesn't, and so it asks. That's the upgrade on offer — not that the machine is smarter, but that it won't fill in the obvious thing for you.

Avigad supplies the counterweight, and it's why I don't think this collapses into "formalize everything." For decades, he says, correctness arguments got no traction with mathematicians, because the reply was always that mathematics is about ideas and understanding, not about checking the rules at the bottom. His resolution is that rigor is the medium, not the point — the precise framework is the platform on which the creative ideas are built. Which lands exactly where yesterday's conversation did, from the other side.

So the thing to watch. Genie 4 shipped with no numbers and asked you to believe a video. Axiom shipped a proof where a machine checked every inference and the team still had to have Lyons and Solomon in the room to tell them which repair was sound and which was circular. Those are the two postures on offer right now, and the Axiom release is the one that tells you where the real work went: not into the proving, but into deciding what statement was worth proving. The formalization gap is not a mathematics problem. It is the same problem as a benchmark with two hundred tasks, and the same problem as a demo reel with no benchmark at all. The checker is getting cheap. Choosing the target is not.
