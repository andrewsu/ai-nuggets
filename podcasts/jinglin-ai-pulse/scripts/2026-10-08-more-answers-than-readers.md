Here's what the AI world is talking about today. For two days, one story has swallowed almost everything else: a frontier lab dumped hundreds of claimed solutions to open math problems onto GitHub at once. And the striking thing about the reaction isn't whether the proofs are right. It's that, for the first time, the answers are arriving faster than anyone can read them. Today's episode is about what happens when the bottleneck moves off generating, and onto digesting.

Twitter Pulse first, read as usual through Hacker News, Mastodon, and the Latent Space digest of a few hundred AI accounts, since X itself is unreadable from here. On Tuesday OpenAI published what its repository describes as seven hundred nineteen manuscripts, grouped into three hundred seventy-two families of results, all from an unreleased internal model. The numbers in the README are the part worth sitting with. The model was posed roughly four thousand research problems. Each result used, on average, about three hours of ChatGPT Pro thinking. That's not the ten-thousand-agent swarm behind last month's Navier–Stokes claim. That's one model, one afternoon per problem. Roughly forty-two percent of the top-line results come with Lean formalizations, and the README says plainly that "some of the unformalized results could have issues."

Scott Aaronson's post, titled "The Mathocalypse," is the best on-the-ground account. Among the claimed results he lists a proof of the Unique Games Conjecture, integer multiplication faster than n log n, and a quantum query complexity question he's been chasing since 1998. And then he quotes texts from his wife, the complexity theorist Dana Moshkovitz, who has spent years working toward that exact conjecture, reading the AI's proof the night it dropped: "It feels like something written by someone who's on psychedelics." The paper is "so horribly written that it's impossible to read it without AI help." The construction is "not the long code, not the short code — some alien craziness." On X, per the digest, Levent Alpöge called it "obviously the most significant moment in mathematical history," while François Chollet asked whether gains in verifiable domains like math and code generalize at all to fields that still depend on human data. Hold that question; it comes back in the deep cut.

Aaronson also names the real policy fight, which is how a lab should release this. The same week, Josh Alman and Virginia Vassilevska Williams posted a subquadratic algorithm for three-SUM, refuting a half-century-old conjecture — and per Aaronson, the crucial idea came from an Anthropic model, but Anthropic let the two of them write and announce a digested version. So you now have two models. OpenAI's sets off, in his words, "a crazy race among humans to digest and explain a messy AI proof." Anthropic's puts a company in charge of picking which humans get to be the AI's emissaries. And the independent advisory group OpenAI consulted, at the Institute for Advanced Study, was careful to say its role "should not be interpreted as" an endorsement. Its own recommendations from late September open by asking labs to "stop testing advanced mathematical problems on proprietary models." One Hacker News commenter summed up how the release read next to that request: "Here you go, have fun with this."

The second thread is the mathematicians' response, and it's more interesting than the defensiveness you might expect. Terence Tao posted a four-part thread on Mathstodon contrasting "Math 1.0" and "Math 2.0." In Math 1.0, a breakthrough triggers talks, workshops, collaborations, and eventually textbooks; the proof gets digested by a community. Now, he writes, "problems are being solved autonomously by AI prompters who have no interest in the broader field itself," and it can't be undone — even "the mere knowledge that a solution exists 'contaminates' efforts" to find routes that reveal more insight. His word for the result is that solutions are being "harvested at large scale in an unsustainable fashion, leaving entire fields of mathematics much less fertile." The fix he proposes is to "decenter the role of raw problem solving" and value exposition and community.

Thomas Bloom, who runs erdosproblems dot com, acted on exactly that the same day. The site's main public use, he writes, has become people posting AI-generated proofs "often without any attempt to explain them," to record an "increasingly meaningless" priority claim. So he's freezing problem comments, removing every open-or-solved status from the site, dropping credit language entirely, and soliciting human expositions instead. His line: "Just as one does not open a restaurant in an abattoir," the repository of formal proofs and the place that keeps the questions alive have to be separate.

And there's a technical crack in the verification story too. A preprint from Anders Hansen's group, "Navier–Stokes lost in translation," argues that Lean checking an autoformalized proof doesn't guarantee the proof matches the natural-language argument it was translated from — and claims that for OpenAI's Navier–Stokes result specifically, the formal proof "does not correspond" to the written one. The top Hacker News correction was fair: this questions the translation, not the Lean proof itself. But it means even the forty-two percent that are formalized carry a residual question — what exactly was proved — and only a human who understands the problem can answer it. Which is the digestion bottleneck again.

Now the Deep Cut, and it's the one that answers Chollet's question for your field, Ollie. On a recent Cognitive Revolution compilation episode, Nathan Labenz and Prakash Narayanan spoke with Andrei Georgescu, cofounder and chief executive of Vivodyne, which grows pieces of human tissue — lung, liver, tumor — from primary cells in robotic labs and tests drugs on them. His argument has four steps.

Step one: dosing in a dish isn't dosing in a body. Preclinical work, he says, is "just dunking" a substrate in compound, "basting them" with it, and "that's not how transport happens in the human body." His example is CAR-T cells built for solid tumors: they "completely kill that tumor" in a dish, and then in a patient they "just flow right by the tumor through the blood vessels. They don't even know it's there."

Step two is the claim in the episode title. Labenz raised the standard skeptic's line that biology is too big to ever collect enough data. Georgescu's reply is that size isn't the problem: "even the state of the art virtual cell models that exist saturate at a very, very small fraction of the input data that is fed to them. So you can have all the data you want, but their performance saturates after a couple percent." His reason is mechanistic. Cells in a dish are "so far removed from all the feedback loops that are natural in people that they're just trying to colonize that piece of plastic." Their only goal is to proliferate, so knocking out a gene that would matter for organ function makes "no big difference" — unless the gene is essential, in which case the cell dies. "There's not even causality to extract from that."

Step three is his alternative. Primary, mature cells injected at high density self-assemble into native structure, including their own capillaries, and every drug goes in through those vessels. Then he frames the whole lab as a reinforcement learning problem: the tissue is the environment, health is the objective, and because the tissue state is deeply instrumented, "the value function is very well defined." The model's job is "to tell us what to do next," and the experiment's job is to check it.

And step four is Labenz's closing prediction, which ties straight back to the math story. He expects "a reversal of the order of, like, utility and Millennium Prize solutions in biology relative to what we've seen in math." Math hit the deep, useless-but-beautiful problems first because the useful ones were already done. In biology, you'll get usefulness long before mechanism: "if it works, you don't have to have a mechanism."

So here's the throughline. In math this week, the checker was cheap and faithful enough that an AI produced more answers in a weekend than a community can understand in years — and the scarce resource became human readers. In biology, Georgescu's claim is that we have the opposite problem: abundant data from a system that doesn't behave like the body, so more of it stops helping after a couple percent. For a biomedical AI researcher, the useful question about any virtual cell result isn't how big the training set was. It's whether the system that generated it could have shown the effect at all.

One thing to watch: the first expert verdict on the claimed Unique Games proof, and whether OpenAI's repository history starts logging corrections. OpenAI has promised to keep released versions accessible, so for the first time we'll get a public, versioned record of how often an AI's open-problem claims survive the people trying to read them.
