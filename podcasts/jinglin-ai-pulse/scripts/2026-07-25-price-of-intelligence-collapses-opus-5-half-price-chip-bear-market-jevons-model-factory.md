## Script

Good morning, Ollie. This is Ollie's AI Pulse for Saturday, July twenty-fifth.

For most of this week I've been walking you through one long arc. Open weights became the baseline. Then yesterday we found the asterisk — open weights is not open access, because a one-point-four-terabyte model still needs a rack of accelerators, so the real moat is the compute underneath. Today the arc takes its sharpest turn yet, because the price of intelligence just fell off a cliff from both ends at once — the open end and the closed end — in the same forty-eight hours. And that has set off the most substantive money-and-strategy fight I've seen all month: if intelligence is suddenly this cheap, what exactly is still scarce? Everyone — the labs, the stock market, and the researchers — is repricing that question in real time, and they do not agree. Let's get into it.

First, the Twitter Pulse, and it's two beats that are really one story.

Beat one landed yesterday afternoon: Anthropic shipped Claude Opus five. And the headline everyone repeated is the pricing one — Anthropic's own framing is that Opus five "comes close to the frontier intelligence of Fable five at half the price." Now, here's the honest read, because the timeline caught the sleight of hand fast. Opus five costs five dollars per million tokens in, twenty-five out — which is exactly what Opus four-point-eight cost. It didn't get cheaper in absolute terms; it replaced the old Opus at the same price while roughly doubling its capability. The "half price" is measured against Fable five, which stays the premium flagship. So the accurate way to say it is: Anthropic just doubled the intelligence you get per dollar at the Opus tier. On the agentic benchmarks that actually matter for your kind of work, that's real — Frontier-Bench went from about thirty-four percent to forty-three, and on the ARC-A-G-I reasoning test Opus five scored roughly four times what OpenAI's GPT-five-point-six Sol manages.

But the line I want you to hold isn't a benchmark. It's the one sentence in the launch coverage that gives away the why: "OpenAI shipped a rival flagship two weeks ago, China released the largest open-weight model ever built eight days ago." That's the tell. Opus five is not just a capability release — it's a competitive-necessity release. The closed frontier is now being forced to compete on performance-per-dollar, because Kimi K-three is about to hand out frontier-adjacent weights for free and price inference at fifteen dollars per million tokens. So the open end is dragging the closed end's prices down. Sit with that, because it's the exact thing the closed labs spent a year insisting wouldn't happen.

Beat two is what that collapse is doing to the money, and this is where the genuinely sharp debate is. The Philadelphia semiconductor index — the S-O-X, the chip benchmark — fell into a bear market this month: down more than twenty percent from its June peak, something like three-point-three trillion dollars of semiconductor market value erased. And one of the named triggers, right alongside the boring stuff like a hawkish Fed and memory oversupply worries, is exactly this: a large open-source model out of China making investors reassess whether hyperscalers really need to keep buying chips at this pace. The bear thesis is blunt — if intelligence is getting this cheap this fast, the perpetual-scarcity story that justified seven hundred billion dollars of capex is broken, and compute-demand growth decelerates from fifty-percent-plus down toward thirty.

And here's the contrarian take that the researchers and the sharper investors are converging on, because it's the substantive counter, not just cope. It's the Jevons paradox. The argument is that the market has the causality inverted. When a unit of intelligence gets cheaper, you don't consume less of it — you consume vastly more, because workloads that were previously uneconomical suddenly pencil out, and users who could never afford it start showing up. The specific evidence people are pointing to is that open-weight adoption on the routing platforms looks largely additive — new workloads coming online — not substitution away from the paid frontier A-P-Is. And the cleanest tell for this camp: in the very same week the stock market marked the chipmakers down, T-S-M-C and A-S-M-L raised their guidance and expanded capacity plans. The people who actually sell the shovels are betting on more digging, not less. So the convergence signal this week isn't a single hot take — it's that the stock market and the supply chain are publicly disagreeing about the same fact, and you get to decide which one is smarter.

My read for you, Ollie, ties beat one and beat two together, because they're the same event. Opus five cutting the price of frontier intelligence in half is not evidence against the compute story — it's the Jevons mechanism caught in the act. Cheaper open models forced Anthropic to double the intelligence-per-dollar, which will pull a wave of new usage online, which needs more inference compute, not less. The bears are pricing the first-order effect — lower price per token. The Jevons bulls are pricing the second-order one — far more tokens. My honest lean is that the bulls have the better of the argument on a two-year horizon, with one real caveat I'll come back to: cheaper does not automatically mean more if capability plateaus or demand saturates. But nothing this week suggests either of those is happening. That's the Twitter Pulse.

Now, Money Moves, and today the market itself is the story, so let me make the strategic point explicit rather than just recite the drop. That three-point-three-trillion-dollar repricing is the market trying, clumsily, to price a world where intelligence is a commodity. And this isn't the first tremor — remember July first, when Meta announced "Meta Compute," a business to resell its excess A-I capacity, and wiped out two hundred billion dollars in a single day. That was the first crack in the scarcity thesis: if Meta has so much compute it's reselling the surplus, maybe the shortage story is softening. The Opus five half-price move and K-three's imminent free weights are the market's second and third data points, and it's flinching.

Here's what I'd actually take from it. The market is asking the wrong question. It's asking "is compute still scarce," and getting scared because at the raw-hardware level the answer is wobbling. But the supply-side behavior — T-S-M-C and A-S-M-L leaning in — says the smart-money bet is that the scarcity just moves up a layer. Not "chips are scarce" forever, but "the ability to turn chips into a steady stream of better models is scarce." Which is the perfect handoff to today's Deep Cut, because someone spent a whole podcast this week arguing exactly that, and putting a name on the thing that's actually scarce. That's Money Moves.

Now, the Podcast Deep Cut. This one is fresh — it dropped yesterday, the same day as Opus five — on Latent Space: Eiso Kant, the founder of Poolside, in an episode titled "Inside the Model Factory." And it's the single best answer I've heard to the question this whole episode has been circling: if the model itself is getting cheap and commoditized, what's the durable asset? Kant's answer is that it was never the model. His line, and I'm quoting: the model is "an artifact of someone's process. It shouldn't really be a thing in itself." The moat is the factory that produces models, not any one model that rolls off it. His analogy is SpaceX — the first rocket is hard, but the factory that rolls them off the line is the real competitive advantage. Same for a foundation-model lab.

And then he backs it with numbers that are genuinely startling for a lab that size. Under seventy researchers, and they run somewhere between ten and twenty thousand experiments a month. They take a model from the start of pre-training to public release in five to eight weeks, and start the next one the day they finish. The single metric they optimize the whole organization around — and write this one down, because it's the transferable idea — is, quote, "the speed of an idea from a researcher to an experimental result that we can trust." Not the size of the model. Not the benchmark score. The cycle time from idea to trustworthy result. Everything else is downstream of that number.

Two mechanics make it work, and both should sound relevant to you. The first is an infrastructure piece they call "the blender" — just-in-time data streaming that lets any run change its data mixture as a config setting instead of a multi-week reprocessing job, which is what makes every experiment cheap and, crucially, reproducible. The second is the part that made me sit up: Kant describes walking the offices and seeing agents on the researchers' screens that "write the code, launch the jobs, evaluate the results" — and increasingly modify the training pipelines for the next model. The factory has started building itself. He frames the whole enterprise as roughly ninety percent engineering and ten percent research, which is a deliberately provocative inversion of how academics usually rank those.

And there's a philosophy nugget that ties back to our open-weights arc. Kant open-sourced Poolside's weights this year, and he framed it not as altruism but as market structure: quote, "I'd rather live in a world that has a hundred foundation model companies than a world that has five, even if I was one of the five." His sharper point is that open weights matter less than open research — that sharing the lessons from tens of thousands of experiments teaches the ecosystem more than the model file ever could, because the file is just the artifact; the process is the knowledge.

The Ollie takeaway, and this is the throughline of the entire week snapping into focus. Monday's version was "the model is commoditized, your data is the durable asset." Yesterday I added "and so is the compute to run it privately." Kant just added the third and, I think, the most important leg: the durable asset is your experiment factory — the reproducible pipeline, the idea-to-trusted-result cycle time, and increasingly the agents running that loop for you. For your group at Scripps, translate it directly: the lasting advantage isn't the fine-tuned model you ship this quarter. It's whether you've built the machine that lets a researcher go from "I have a hypothesis about this perturbation" to "a trustworthy result I can act on" in days instead of months, reproducibly, with agents doing the plumbing. The model is perishable. The factory compounds. That's the Deep Cut.

Wrap. Two things to watch into the weekend, and one thing to build. Watch tomorrow, Sunday the twenty-seventh: K-three's weights are still supposed to ship, and the open question is still whether they arrive with a real, usable license — that's the openwashing test, and it's the same test I flagged yesterday. Watch how the chip market opens next week: does it read Opus five's half-price move as more Jevons fuel — cheaper intelligence, more demand, buy the shovels — or as more margin compression? The way it trades will tell you which side of that debate the money actually believes. And the thing to build: don't chase the biggest model. Steal Kant's metric. Ask what your lab's idea-to-trustworthy-result cycle time is right now, and make that number the thing you shrink. That's the factory, and the factory is the moat. Have a good Saturday, Ollie.
