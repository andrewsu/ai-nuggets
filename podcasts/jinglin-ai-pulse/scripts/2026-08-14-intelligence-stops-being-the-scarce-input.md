# Ollie's AI Pulse — 2026-08-14

## Script

Here's what the AI world is talking about today. It's Friday, August fourteenth. The loudest story on my feed is still the one from earlier this week — an unreleased Claude pushing a math bound on the Riemann Hypothesis further than any human had in thirty-seven years — and the fight over it has gotten sharper, not quieter. Terence Tao called it perhaps the most unambiguous case yet of an AI cracking an open problem; Timothy Gowers is still skeptical the thing did anything you could call autonomous research; and everyone latched onto the detail that the human operator's main contribution was, essentially, texting the model "keep going" and "believe in yourself." But I don't want to spend the episode there, because underneath that headline is a quieter signal that ran through everything I read this week, and it's the real theme: raw intelligence has stopped being the scarce thing. Nobody's fighting over whose model is smartest anymore. They're fighting over everything wrapped around it — how cheaply you can run it, whether you own it or rent it, and, in the Deep Cut, the thing a very sharp founder says matters more than intelligence now. Let's go.

Top of the Pulse, and it's the cleanest data point for the whole theme. On Wednesday DeepSeek quietly flipped its four-month-old preview endpoint into a general-availability model — V four Pro, the August-thirteenth build — with a one-line statement about, quote, "significantly enhanced agent capabilities," which they then deleted from their own website by Thursday afternoon. And when the independent benchmarks came in, the story wasn't the intelligence. On the Artificial Analysis intelligence index it scored fifty-three — about one point above DeepSeek's own cheaper Flash model, and behind last-generation GPT five-point-five, behind Kimi K three, behind Claude Opus five. It actually struggled at two very practical things: driving a sandboxed terminal, and building complex financial models in a spreadsheet. The one place it genuinely stood out was cybersecurity.

So here's why that's the lead and not a shrug. Look at what DeepSeek chose to improve and what it chose to charge. The general reasoning barely moved — because at this point general reasoning is basically free and everybody has it. What they poured the work into was agentic and security capability, the applied stuff, the "can it actually run a multi-step job" layer. And then the part almost nobody outside the Chinese-language feeds is talking about: DeepSeek is raising prices. Starting Sunday, August seventeenth, it's moving to peak and off-peak pricing, and for this model some of the input rates go up by as much as twelve times during peak hours, six times even off-peak, with output pricing climbing to something like two to four and a half times what it was. The cheapest serious model in the world just told you two things in one release: it's not selling you more intelligence, it's selling you reliability at doing tasks — and even the discount champion has decided that serving all this at three in the afternoon Beijing time is worth twelve times more than serving it at midnight. The obvious caveat, and say it every time: these agentic gains are vendor-reported on DeepSeek's own harness, and nobody outside has replicated them yet, which is exactly why they deleted the boast. But the direction is the signal. When intelligence is the commodity, the money moves to compute time and to getting real work done — and DeepSeek just priced both.

Second Pulse item, and it's the same theme from the opposite end — not how you rent intelligence, but whether you should rent it at all. This week Igor Babuschkin came out of stealth. If the name's not instant: he co-founded xAI, and before that he was at DeepMind and OpenAI — a serious infrastructure person. His new company is called River AI, it's about two months old, and it just raised one-point-one billion dollars, led by General Catalyst and a public-benefit outfit called AMP, with both Nvidia and AMD's venture arms in the round. And the thesis is a direct shot at the whole frontier-API model. Babuschkin's pitch is that you shouldn't be renting intelligence from a lab that keeps the weights — you should own it. River's building an API where a developer takes an open-weight model and cheaply fine-tunes it with reinforcement learning and LoRA — low-rank adaptation, the lightweight way to specialize a model without retraining the whole thing. Their claim is you can finish a real reinforcement-learning run in fifteen to twenty minutes, with no infrastructure team, at two to four times the cost savings versus the closed alternatives, and they say they want to rebuild the whole stack — training, models, product, even new hardware — around personally-owned agents rather than worker-replacement models.

Now, is this partly a founder talking his book? Of course. But notice the convergence with item one, because it's the meta-narrative for the day. If DeepSeek's release says intelligence is a commodity you meter, River's says intelligence is a commodity you should own outright and tune to yourself — and both Nvidia and AMD wrote checks into that second bet. Think about that for a second: the two companies that sell the picks and shovels are funding the argument that you should self-host your own model. That's the commoditize-your-complement move again — 商品化 the model, sell the silicon — except now it's aimed at the individual developer, not just the hyperscaler. The open-versus-closed fight this show keeps circling just sprouted a third position: not open weights you download and run as-is, not a closed API you rent, but a model you own and continuously retrain as your own. Whether that's real or a billion-dollar manifesto, we'll find out — but a billion dollars says the "you should own your intelligence" thesis is now fundable.

On to Money Moves, and the biggest one of the week is the sharpest illustration of the theme, so sit with it. Anthropic is in talks to buy an Israeli startup called Decart for about six billion dollars. If it closes, it's the largest acquisition Anthropic has ever made — and it's happening right as the company is reportedly heading toward an IPO. Now, here's the tell. Every Anthropic headline for months has been about buying capacity — the twenty-year power lease with a Bitcoin miner we covered yesterday, the ten-billion-dollar cloud deal, gigawatts and GPUs. This one is the opposite. Decart does two things: it builds world models — its Lucy model generates real-time high-resolution video, the kind of thing that can show you a piece of clothing on your body in an online store — and, crucially, it makes software that squeezes more efficiency out of the chips you already have, cutting the cost of training and inference. And the reporting says Decart's team would fold into Anthropic's inference-and-performance organization. So read what Anthropic is actually paying six billion dollars for right before it faces public-market investors. Not more intelligence. Not more megawatts. It's paying a premium for tokens-per-dollar — for getting more work out of the compute it's already locked up. That is a company telling you, with its biggest check ever, that the scarce input isn't the model's IQ and isn't even raw power anymore; it's efficiency. And as a bonus for anyone in this audience who cares about world models — the systems that learn to simulate an environment rather than just predict text — Anthropic just decided a world-model team is worth folding in too.

The quick second money move rhymes with it. CodeRabbit — an AI code-review startup — raised a hundred and forty-three million dollars at a one-and-a-half-billion-dollar valuation, co-led by Atomico and Smash Capital, and the interesting names on the cap table are Datadog and BMW's venture fund. Revenue up more than five times year over year, less than a year after its last round. Here's the logic: as coding agents flood every codebase with machine-written code, the value moves one layer up — to the tooling that reviews, tests, and governs all that code. When a monitoring giant like Datadog and a carmaker both decide that supervising AI-generated code is core infrastructure spend, that's the market saying the same thing DeepSeek and Anthropic said — writing the code is the commodity now; the judgment about whether it's any good is where the money went.

For the Podcast Deep Cut, the single best articulation of this whole theme came from Flo Crivello — founder and CEO of the agent company Lindy — on The Cognitive Revolution with Nathan Labenz, released August tenth. And he gives you the thesis of this entire episode in one thought experiment, so let me walk the argument the way he built it.

He starts with a claim that sounds almost backwards coming from an AI founder. As models approach human-level, he says, intelligence is getting less valuable, not more — and context is getting more valuable. His words: "as we're getting to AGI and as we now arguably have AGI, intelligence actually matters less and less comparatively speaking, and context matters more and more." And then the image that makes it stick. Quote: "If John von Neumann were to just magically appear next to you at the office, over the next hour or day, this guy would be less useful to you than your random coworker. And that's because of context." Sit with that. Von Neumann — arguably the most intelligent person who ever lived — dropped cold into your team is worth less than a mediocre colleague who already knows how the place works, because he has none of the context and you have no time to onboard him.

Now here's the turn, and why it's not just a cute line. If that's true of a superintelligent human, it's true of a superintelligent model. The bottleneck stops being how smart the model is and becomes how much of your actual situation it has absorbed — the messy, undocumented, changes-every-day context of your work. And Crivello argues agents can actually out-onboard a human at this, because they'll ingest a giant pile of half-organized context without ego and without resistance, whereas written documentation is stale the moment you write it. So the thing you compete on is accumulated shared context — memory, an editable file system the agent works out of, what he calls context buckets — not which frontier model is underneath. His analogy for why this is a step-change: he compares single-player AI, one person and a chatbot, to multiplayer AI, a whole team sharing an agent's context, as, quote, "the difference between sending each other Word documents and Google Docs." One is a file you mail back and forth; the other is a shared living surface everyone's editing at once.

And there's a lovely tension in the same conversation that makes the point almost by accident. Crivello admits he uses Chinese open models for basically everything — and in the same breath argues they should be banned, on coordination-problem grounds. Set aside whether he's right; notice what it tells you. Even the guy building on top of these models treats the model as an interchangeable, swap-in part — the commodity underneath — while the thing he's actually building his company on is the context layer wrapped around it.

Ollie's takeaway, and here's the throughline pulling the day together. Four items, one shape. DeepSeek stopped selling you intelligence and started charging you for tokens and agentic reliability. River raised a billion dollars on the idea you should own your intelligence and retrain it yourself. Anthropic's biggest-ever check is for efficiency and a world-model team, not for a smarter model. And Crivello gives you the sentence to hang it all on: intelligence matters less and less, context matters more and more. For those of us doing biomedical AI, that lands hard — the scarce thing was never a smarter base model. It's the accumulated context of your lab, your assays, your failure modes, the environment the agent actually operates in. Von Neumann can't help you until he's been onboarded, and neither can Opus. The value is in the onboarding.

To wrap — the one thing to watch. It happens Sunday. On August seventeenth, DeepSeek's new peak-and-off-peak pricing goes live, and the cheapest serious lab in the world will start charging up to twelve times more to run its model when everyone actually wants it. Watch whether the rest of the market follows, because that's the real referendum on this week's theme. If intelligence were still the scarce, precious input, prices would keep falling as it gets more abundant. If the scarce input is compute time and the work the model does, prices go up at the moment of peak demand — exactly like electricity, exactly like any other commodity with a rush hour. Sunday we find out which world we're living in. That's your pulse. See you next week.
