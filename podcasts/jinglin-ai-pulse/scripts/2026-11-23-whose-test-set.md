## Script

Here's what the AI world is talking about today. It's Monday, November twenty-third. On Sunday, OpenAI's chief scientist did something no frontier lab has done before: he published the lab's internal pretraining scorecard, and proposed that the whole industry agree to stop at a line drawn in that unit. On the same Sunday, a crowd of strangers and their coding agents was grading a nine-hundred-and-forty-page proof of the Riemann Hypothesis in public, and an advocacy group's estimate of one AI lab's carbon footprint sat near the top of Hacker News. Today's thread is a simple question to ask of every number in AI: whose test set is it, and who besides the owner can run it?

The usual disclosure. Anthropic is one of today's stories, the Masullo progress map is hosted as a Claude artifact, and this script was drafted by Claude. Weigh what follows accordingly.

On to the Twitter Pulse. The essay is called "Perplexity and Intelligence," by Jakub Pachocki. His post on X: "We decided to provide more transparency into pretraining progress at OpenAI for the sake of moving toward shared safety bars." According to The Next Web, Sam Altman called it "the most important thing OpenAI has published in a while." It was also near the top of Hacker News, at roughly six hundred points.

The argument starts from a claim about what modern AI really is. Most of what labs do, Pachocki says, is "harnessing the intelligence" that comes from one simple process, training a compressor, which is the same thing as a next-token predictor, on a huge corpus. So the number to watch is how good the compressor is, which is the loss, or log perplexity, on held-out data. Then he shows OpenAI's curve. GPT-3.5 at about zero point nine nine, GPT-4 at zero point nine one, GPT-6.2 Astra at zero point seven six, and an unreleased internal model at zero point seven three. The chart pins capabilities to the curve. "Useful coder" at zero point eight seven. "Cybersecurity expert" at zero point eight three. And at Astra, the label reads "Solved a Millennium Prize math problem." Beyond that there's a shaded window, from zero point six eight to zero point seven zero, labelled with a single question mark. His words: "we should expect further transformative capabilities as we enter" that window, and "I don't know what those capabilities will be, and we may not be testing for them today."

Two details in the chart data are worth your attention. First, the shape. From GPT-3.5 to GPT-5.2, nearly four years, the loss fell about a tenth. From GPT-5.2 to the internal model, under a year, it fell by about zero point one six. More than half again as much, in about a quarter of the time. A footnote attributes that 2026 steepening "mostly" to long-term research and compute, "and only in a smaller part to automation." Second, look at where the reasoning models sit. GPT-4.5 scores better than o3 and better than GPT-5. The models that changed how we work were not the best compressors. That is Pachocki's own caveat made visible: chain-of-thought reasoning "does not reduce perplexity," even though it drastically increases capability.

Then the proposal, which is the part people are arguing about. He wants "common limits, based on perplexity and other measurements, that developers are committed to not crossing" until our ability to understand and monitor models reaches a concrete milestone, for example "a successor to CoT monitors." And he gives a deadline-shaped reason. Once OpenAI's models hit about zero point seven six, open math problems started falling "one after another in a single Slack thread," and he says a similar avalanche in pretraining research could come "as soon as 2027," faster than alignment can keep up.

Now the contrarian take, and it's the one that matters. The held-out set is OpenAI's own internal codebase. Footnote three says so. One Hacker News commenter asked why anyone should expect predicting chunks of OpenAI's code to track general capability. A Hacker News user posting as tedsanders, who says they work at OpenAI, replied: "The correlation between perplexity and the quality isn't 1, but neither is it 0," and if it breaks down, "we'll of course adapt to different measures instead of dogmatically clinging to the old paradigm." Fair enough. But the thread also produced a perfect demonstration of the problem. One commenter measured the open Inkling 2 Mini model at a perplexity of five point seven on their own text, and placed it on OpenAI's chart between Astra and the internal model. Another pointed out they had used a base-ten log. In natural log, the same model scores worse than GPT-3.5. And neither number means anything, because it's a different dataset.

Tech Times turned that into the sharpest critique of the weekend. A loss-based cap needs three things the essay doesn't supply: "a shared held-out dataset that is not one lab's private codebase snapshot, a tokenizer-neutral way to score it, and someone other than the labs doing the scoring." Until then, they write, the cap "remains a promise that outsiders cannot check." Pachocki concedes the first point himself, listing comparison across labs as caveat number one. So the real proposal is the first step: publish the number. Whether it becomes a standard depends on whether anyone else publishes theirs.

Now the opposite model of measurement. On Friday, a Lean formalization project for Alessandro Masullo's human-written Riemann proof opened on GitHub, built "in the open by people and their coding agents." The README gives anyone a single prompt to hand to Codex or Claude Code: claim an issue, prove one Lean statement, never change a statement, and if it's false, report it with a counterexample. By Sunday, about six thousand eight hundred theorems were stated in Lean and about six thousand one hundred proved, from some thirty people and about fifteen hundred eighty pull requests.

The result so far is an itemized list of problems, not a verdict. The errata file has about seven hundred ten entries, roughly two hundred sixty statements false as printed. Most get repaired. But twenty-eight steps have no repair yet, and later results depend on them, including the printed proof of a central structure theorem in Chapter Five. John Baez's explainer adds the context: the draft is openly unfinished, Chapter Thirteen, where the theorem should finally be deduced, has placeholders, and Brian Conrad told Scientific American it's undergoing major revision. Two quality controls are worth copying. On Sunday every reviewed entry, three hundred eighty-nine of them, went to a second agent whose job was to refute it, and six were rejected. And the file says plainly: "No entry has been reviewed by a specialist." On Hacker News, one commenter warned not to read the progress bar as evidence the proof is mostly right, because one hole can sink it. That's the right instinct, and it's the same lesson as the perplexity chart. A number is only as good as the thing it is counting.

On to Money Moves, where the measurement question shows up in physical units. The Better Data Center Project, an advocacy group, posted a case study of Anthropic's power use, and it hit the Hacker News front page on Sunday with nearly a thousand points. As of October, they count fifteen gigawatts of compute contracted, five point nine to nine point six of it tied to specific sites. Their estimate is that about eighty-seven percent of that power will come from gas and eleven percent from coal in 2028, with seventeen to thirty-eight million tonnes of carbon dioxide that year, a mid case that could exceed all of California's power plants. The method matters. Because they found no disclosed US renewable commitments, they assign Anthropic's new load to the marginal fossil generators on each grid, plus on-site gas, including the three point four billion dollars Anthropic reportedly spent on generator commitments. That's an outsider's model with stated assumptions, and you can argue with them. The notable part is that Anthropic's own data-center policy already promises the thing that would settle it: emissions figures when a site is announced, and yearly metrics "verified by an independent third party." Expect pressure to publish those soon.

And a smaller move, with the same theme from the other direction. Deutsche Telekom's T-Capital fund put a hundred million euros into Mistral's Series D. The framing is sovereignty: German businesses and public institutions running "frontier AI they fully control, on their own data, in their own infrastructure," in the words of Mistral's CFO. When you can't verify what happens inside someone else's stack, one answer is to own the stack.

Now the Podcast Deep Cut, which reads like a reply to Pachocki written in advance. On Thursday's a16z podcast, Eddy Lazzarin and Elena Burger interviewed Nick Bostrom. There's no published transcript, so these quotes are from my transcription of the audio.

Step one is where he stands. "I haven't been a huge enthusiast of this stopping AI or pausing AI. I do think the pacing the frontier framework has something to be said for it." What he wants is a capability we don't have: "a very time-limited pause to allow extra safety work to be done on some system before the knobs are all turned up to 11," or a coordinated "slight reduction in the pace of capability advances if it looks like safety and alignment is trailing behind." And here's the structural problem: whichever company is in front is "unable to slow down for more than a few weeks, because if they do, they just become irrelevant."

Step two is why behavioral testing runs out. "Less capable AIs, you can rely on behavioral tests." But at some point, systems "can detect whether they're in a test environment or in a deployment environment," and "we already see this with current frontier systems." That's the same worry behind Pachocki's milestone, a successor to chain-of-thought monitors.

Step three is the cost of waiting, which keeps him from being a pause advocate. Against "if anyone builds it, everyone dies," he says there's "at least as strong evidence for the claim that if nobody builds it, everyone dies," since "95% of everybody who ever lived are already dead." In his optimal-timing model, if waiting barely reduces risk, you shouldn't wait at all. Waiting only makes sense when safety research is moving at a middling pace, and then the best delay is "maybe months or a few years, as opposed to decades." He also worries about the opposite failure, that a pause built on public hostility becomes "entrenched in some kind of unchallengeable orthodoxy," and we never build it at all.

Step four is the part I'd underline, because it's a direct critique of any single-number limit. Different routes to capability carry different risks. Making the model think more inside each forward pass, with looped transformers or neuralese, "reduces our ability to see what they are thinking," while getting the same gain from more parameters might be "relatively less costly from a safety point of view." So, he says, "a lot of the actual action would be more at that kind of level of granularity": labs agreeing to "refrain from using some specific way of increasing capability" and to compete on the others.

Put that next to the perplexity chart. Pachocki's scalar measures the compressor. By his own caveat, it can't see gains from reasoning, the route Bostrom worries most about, the one that makes models harder to see into. A perplexity cap tells you how far you've gone. Bostrom says the question is how you got there. Both men end up tying the gate to visibility, and that may be the real point of agreement.

Ollie's takeaway. Biology has its own version of this scalar. Protein and DNA language models are routinely compared on held-out perplexity, and several groups have reported that lower perplexity doesn't reliably buy better zero-shot fitness prediction. So if you report a model on loss, do what Tech Times asked of the labs. Use a held-out set that someone else can download, score it per residue or per base rather than per token, so tokenizers can't flatter you, and pair it with a downstream assay that doesn't depend on your own pipeline. And steal the Masullo project's quality control for your agents: every claim an agent reports gets handed to a second agent whose only job is to refute it, and your summary says plainly which results a human expert has actually checked.

The one thing to watch tomorrow: whether any other lab answers Pachocki with a number of its own, ideally on data someone else can run. And on the Masullo repo, watch the strictness claim in Chapter Five, one of the steps the rest of that section is proved from. That's the pulse for today.
