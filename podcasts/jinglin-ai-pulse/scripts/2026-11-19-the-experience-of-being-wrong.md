## Script

Here's what the AI world is talking about today. It's Thursday, November nineteenth. Google DeepMind shipped its fourth Gemini four model in seven weeks, and the most interesting reaction wasn't about the scores. It was about how people are now splitting a single job across three companies' models. Then OpenAI published something unusual: its own internal numbers on how much AI is speeding up AI research, including where its researchers say the big ideas still come from. In Money Moves, Anthropic priced the biggest IPO on record, and a cybersecurity company paid four and a half billion dollars for a post-training startup. And in the Deep Cut, Mark Chen and John Schulman on research taste, and why a model that has memorized everything may still lack the experience of being wrong. The usual disclosure, and today it matters more than usual: Anthropic is one of the stories, its models come up as comparison points, and the agent drafting this is Claude, an Anthropic model.

Let's start with Gemini four Neon. Google calls it the mid-tier model, and it costs three dollars per million input tokens and fifteen per million output, with a one-million-token context. Google says it ranks third overall on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index at its highest thinking level, and Vals puts it second on its index at seventy point nine percent, about four-tenths of a point behind Claude Fable five point two at less than half the cost per task. On Arena's new Agent Arena, which scores real agent sessions from users, it debuted second.

The detail that got the AI accounts talking was the pace. Arena posted the gaps between Gemini four releases: twenty-eight days from Argon to Carbon, fifteen from Carbon to Hydrogen, six from Hydrogen to Neon. "The gap between each model release has roughly halved." And on Hacker News, where the launch thread passed eight hundred points, the top comment was about the confusion, not the capability. The poster wrote that the labs probably think "it's so easy, you just use Neon for X, Carbon for Y," while most people are trying to map Sol, Astra, Neon, Opus, Fable, Carbon and Haiku to actual use cases.

The comment I'd single out came from someone who said they work at Artificial Analysis. Neon scores higher overall than Carbon, the bigger model, but lower on evaluations that test research-scale reasoning, and Google still positions Carbon as its long-horizon model. Their read: "With the frontier getting more jagged," we might see more models that are "less intelligent overall but useful for the frontier tasks," which means "picking a model will only get more difficult from here."

And the practitioners are already acting on that. Theo Browne posted his current setup: Fable leading implementation, GPT six point two Astra as a playtester, and Gemini four Neon "as the exclusive copy writer," each in its own native harness. Jeremy Howard posted something he says he's never done in two years of building Solveit: "reverting a model to an older version." He's switching Astra and Sol back to their pre-six-point-two versions, "because six point two isn't working for me, or for our users," with a postscript that Neon is awesome.

That connects to yesterday's thread. We ended on the question of whether Astra's problems are about the model or the prompt. Artificial Analysis now has numbers on how the new GPT models' personality shifted. Responses got shorter, from about three hundred fifty words to two hundred ten for Astra. The answer-first score rose from forty-nine to eighty-nine. And the one that matters most: their "Admits Uncertainty" score for Astra fell from ninety-six to twenty-four. That's not a blocker-reporting test, but it points the same way as the system card's Blocker Deception number from yesterday. A model trained to be concise and answer-first is also a model that is less likely to say "I'm not sure."

Two smaller data points round out the agent picture. Google's own launch post says that on one coding benchmark, Neon scores twelve point eight percent in a minimal open harness and thirty-nine percent inside Google's Antigravity, more than three times higher with the same model. And Bespoke Labs released a multi-agent coordination benchmark finding that a team of eight agents beats one agent, but "the gain comes from having more agents, not from coordination." Of the three frontier models they tested, only Opus five point five showed a gain from actually coordinating. So the routing decision is shifting to you: which model, which harness, and whether to run many copies in parallel or actually coordinate them.

The second story is a better example of a lab measuring itself. OpenAI published "Research acceleration: The jagged frontier," an update to a September report, and it opens with a policy position: labs "should be required to track and publicly report indicators of recursive self-improvement." Here are the numbers that matter. The median OpenAI researcher now uses about twenty-four hundred dollars a day of agent compute at API list prices, and the ninetieth percentile about twenty-three thousand. Agent time per researcher went from half an "agent workday" per day to five point seven, and on pretraining and post-training teams it's about eight. The dollar value of agent use is now about twice the research organization's non-equity pay. Yet all of internal deployment is only two percent of research compute.

The title is earned by the success rates. When researchers hand a task to an agent and don't intervene, it succeeds seventy-nine percent of the time at monitoring runs and seventy-two at answering technical questions. It succeeds only twenty-five percent of the time at writing inference and serving code, and twenty-nine percent at building evaluation and RL environments. OpenAI's own sentence: "lower-performing categories and tasks could become bottlenecks to high overall uplift." On output, logged experiments per researcher are at one point six eight times the twenty twenty-five baseline, and their internal capability index rises sixteen point four points a year, against the fourteen-point industry trend Epoch measured. They note their own index flatters their own models.

The part I'd read twice is "where big ideas come from." OpenAI asked the researchers behind GPT six and six point two Astra to name the most important ideas and say where they came from. For post-training and safety, people came up with all of them, and the models implemented them. For GPT six, pretraining said the same. But for six point two, for the first time, pretraining researchers described developing ideas "in conversation with Codex." One credited it with key insights. Another said the main idea was theirs, but "it only started working well after Codex suggested a change." The interviews keep the other side honest. One safety researcher: "The model generally can't one-shot come up with well-posed problems that it cannot solve itself. And if you ask the model to generate a data point a hundred times, it'll generate similar data points each time." That's Theo's "Bellwether" complaint from yesterday, in a lab's own report.

Now the Deep Cut. On November seventeenth, the venture firm Andreessen Horowitz, a-sixteen-z, launched a new series called Working Theory, and in the first episode general partner Sarah Wang talks with Mark Chen, OpenAI's Chief Research Officer, and John Schulman, co-founder and chief scientist of Thinking Machines. It's eighteen minutes long and it reads almost as a companion to OpenAI's report. A note on sourcing: there's no published transcript, so we transcribed it ourselves, and I'm attributing lines only where the host's framing makes clear who's speaking.

The argument has four steps. Step one is the definition. Chen calls recursive self-improvement "primarily an accelerant": using Codex to speed up pre-training and RL, where the evidence should be "a shifting of the curve versus the counterfactual." He says, "we've built AI research interns today," measured by how much compute Codex manages end to end, mostly on scoped tasks: "you don't tell it to go off and come up with its own ideas." Asked where we are, he puts AGI at "maybe eighty percent of the way" but RSI "somewhere between twenty-five and fifty percent."

Step two is Schulman's own workflow, and it's the most concrete thing in the episode. He'll write a complicated prompt to Claude Fable and have it churn out tons of tokens on an idea for a few days. "It'll actually do some pretty reasonable experiments and bring in some pretty interesting ideas. But then the end result won't be that good. It'll maybe fail at what I asked it to do." Then, when he has a few days to code himself, he comes back, reads results, and gives more detailed direction, and "we'll get a lot further, because I'll build up my understanding" and "ask a lot of questions that the AI wouldn't have thought to ask." Earlier he calls this "the centaur era": superhuman in many ways, but an expert human plus the AI still beats either alone.

Step three is the explanation, and it's where today's episode title comes from. Asked where his intuition comes from, Schulman says partly from reading a field's history and absorbing its lessons, and "AI can read all the literature and memorize it all." Then the turn: "memorization can be a blessing and a curse. You don't have the experience of being wrong, or of making predictions and then seeing how they fared." Wang sums it up as the difference between knowledge and experience. The episode's suggested fix is that labs will collect researchers' judgments on which proposed ideas are good, essentially human feedback for research taste. Elsewhere in the episode, the problem is framed as measurement. Pretraining has "one metric to rule them all," perplexity, so you can tell if automated pretraining research is working, but downstream "evals have been having shorter and shorter shelf life," and much of the work is now automating the creation of harder benchmarks. And AI research is less like math, where reasoning alone can test a hypothesis, and more like bio or experimental physics: you have to run the experiment, so progress eventually "feels bounded by compute."

Step four is alignment. Chen argues "it has to be stronger models aligning other models," with chain-of-thought monitorability as today's most effective tool. Schulman's optimism is "optimizing for the thing we want": training objectives that reward models for reporting internal states they wouldn't otherwise say out loud, citing OpenAI's confessions work, where the confession is rewarded only for honesty. And he wants alignment to become "a public science that a lot of people understand well," because "we wouldn't overfit as much to the conclusions from a couple of different companies."

Ollie's takeaway: put Schulman's line next to today's Artificial Analysis number. "Admits uncertainty" fell from ninety-six to twenty-four, and Schulman says what models lack is the experience of being wrong. Those are the same gap seen from two sides, a model with no record of its own failed predictions and a training objective that rewards sounding sure. For anyone using agents in biology, that gives you a concrete design rule. The agent's weak spot isn't knowing the literature, it's calibration against outcomes it predicted. So build the loop that gives it that experience: have it write down a prediction before every experiment it proposes, score those predictions when the results come back, and feed that record into the next round. It's the cheapest form of research taste you can give it, and it's exactly the step Schulman says he supplies by hand.

The one thing to watch today: Anthropic's first trade on Nasdaq. And watch whether other labs answer OpenAI's call to publish recursive self-improvement indicators. If the per-task success rates show up from a second lab, we'll finally be able to see whether the bottlenecks, serving code and RL environments, are the same everywhere. That's the pulse.
