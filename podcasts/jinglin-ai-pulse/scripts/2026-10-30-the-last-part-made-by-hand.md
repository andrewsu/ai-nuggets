## Script

Here's what the AI world is talking about today. A speech company published a scaling study arguing that the tokenizer — the one piece of the language model pipeline nobody learns — is costing you most of a compute generation, and then showed the same architecture discovering codon boundaries in a human gene it had never seen. Mistral released the weights of a trillion-parameter model and, in the same post, said out loud that it is still adjusting the guardrails. Stack Overflow fell below a thousand questions a month. And on a podcast, three RAND researchers argued that the United States currently has no superintelligence strategy and is therefore running one by accident. The thread running through all of it: what happens to the parts of a system that were made by hand.

Start with the research, because it is the best thing I read this week. Cartesia published part one of a series called "From Tokens to Bytes." Their framing line is the one to remember: tokenization remains one of the last parts of the language modeling pipeline that is designed by hand rather than learned. Everything else — features, representations, even the optimizer schedule, in some labs — got absorbed into training years ago. Byte pair encoding did not. You still freeze a vocabulary on some corpus before the model sees a single gradient, and the model lives inside that decision forever.

Their alternative is the hierarchical network, which they introduced last year. Instead of chopping bytes into fixed tokens up front, the model compresses bytes into chunks inside itself, dynamically, as part of end-to-end training. What makes this paper different from the original is that they did the boring, expensive, correct thing: they built a proper compute-optimal scaling ladder for a normal tokenized transformer, tuned it honestly, even retrained a byte pair encoder on their own corpus so the baseline would be stronger than an off-the-shelf one, and then swapped in a hierarchical network matched on parameters, on floating point operations, and on compression ratio. Same data, same bytes, same architecture in the main trunk. The only thing that changed was whether the chunking was learned or handed down.

The headline is a compute multiplier of one point two eight on held-out pretraining loss. That is roughly the size of Kimi's Delta Attention gain, so it is a real architectural win and not a rounding error, and it comes from the part of the stack people treat as plumbing. But the number that matters more is what happens when you stop measuring in-distribution loss. On Paloma it is one point eight three. On OLMES it is two point zero six. The further you move from the training distribution, the bigger the advantage gets, which is exactly the signature you would expect if learned chunking is building something more semantic than frequency statistics.

And then code. Across the fifteen programming languages in the training mix, the median multiplier is two point zero four. On ten languages that were not in the mix at all — OCaml, Prolog, Lean, Fortran — the median rises to three point one, with Prolog at five point four. The explanation they offer is that code has, effectively, an infinite vocabulary. Variable names, whitespace conventions, symbol soup. A fixed merge table trained mostly on English prose has no good way to represent that, and the deeper point is that the shortfall never shows up in your loss curve, because the loss curve is computed in the same tokens that are failing you.

The wrinkle I appreciated: the hierarchical network's code advantage does not come from spending more compute on code. It compresses code *more* than byte pair encoding does — SQL at one point four times, Go at one point two six — so it is modeling code better while spending less on it. The authors are honest that this makes clean comparison almost impossible. Byte pair encoding compresses English more and code less; hierarchical networks do the reverse. You cannot simultaneously match data and match compute per source. They flag it as an open problem rather than papering over it, which is more than most architecture posts manage.

Scaled up, they trained a two-stage byte-level model against SmolLM3, Hugging Face's three-billion-parameter transformer with a fully published recipe, controlling for floating point operations per byte to within two percent. The hierarchical network reaches SmolLM3's final average benchmark score after about one trillion tokens. SmolLM3 needed about eleven. Note the shape of the architecture that gets there: the outer encoder and decoder stages eat forty-three percent of the compute, against eight percent for embeddings and the output head in the tokenized model. They are paying a lot to learn the chunking, and still coming out ten times ahead.

Now the part for you specifically, Ollie. Part four applies the same machinery to DNA, at nucleotide resolution — a vocabulary of four. Against a transformer baseline, the compute multiplier is between five point eight and seven point one across three held-out sets. Six to seven times. And the qualitative result is the one worth sitting with: the model was trained only on next-nucleotide prediction over prokaryotic genomes, and when you show it a human histone gene it has never seen, its learned chunk boundaries line up with codon boundaries inside the coding region, with most chunks running one to three codons, and then go long and unstructured across the promoter and the termination hairpin where there is no reading frame to find. Nobody told it about codons. Nobody told it where the gene starts.

The strategic claim they end on is that bytes are not special; every domain has primitive units — pixels, waveforms, nucleotides — and most of them have no sensible hand-built tokenizer at all. For biology that reframing is not academic. The field has spent years arguing about how to tokenize sequence data, and the honest answer may be that the argument was downstream of a limitation nobody had to accept.

Second item, briefly, because it is the loudest story of the week by engagement. Mistral released the open weights of Mistral Large 4 — they call it Le Chonk — a trillion-parameter multimodal model, and Hacker News put it at fourteen hundred points. The interesting number is not a leaderboard position, it is the efficiency: on the Artificial Analysis Coding Agent Index it scores forty-eight, effectively tied with GLM-five-point-three, while using forty-seven percent fewer tokens per task. On Terminal-Bench four point zero it gained thirteen points over its own preview while cutting output tokens by thirty percent. Three weeks of additional reinforcement learning, they say, with no sign of saturation. Priced at seventy cents per million input and two dollars ten output, under a modified MIT license whose one real condition is that model-as-a-service providers need a separate commercial agreement.

Read the post carefully, though, and the most interesting paragraph is not a benchmark. They report that on the Cyber Index they score fifty-one, which is genuinely not the top of the open field — two other models score higher — and they report a refusal rate of ninety-six point six percent on malicious requests, which is. And then they write that having watched live traffic since the preview, they see potential for real-world uplift, and are modifying their guardrails accordingly, and will keep doing so. That sentence sits in a post announcing that the weights are downloadable. Guardrails that get updated over months are a hand-maintained layer on top of a thing you just distributed permanently. Mistral clearly knows this; they published the sentence anyway, which I read as candour rather than carelessness. But it is the open-weights governance problem stated plainly by a lab that is choosing to have it, and it is a useful contrast to the model-stays-home posture we talked about yesterday.

Third, quickly, because it is the other thing everyone was arguing about. A Stack Exchange data query showing Stack Overflow below one thousand questions a month hit seven hundred eighty-eight points and seven hundred comments. The reflexive reading is that chatbots killed it. The thread did not settle there, and the correction is worth carrying: the question peak was 2013, and the decline started long before any of this. One commenter's framing was that the golden age was the period when the backlog of important questions was being worked off; once that was done, project-specific traffic moved to GitHub issues, and what language models actually replaced was the residual. Someone raised the obvious worry — if nobody asks, what trains the next model — and the rebuttal was sharper than the worry: models with tool calling pull documentation and source directly now, less efficiently than a canonical answer but without needing one to exist. Set that beside Martin Fowler's post the same day on what he calls LLM Voice, where his advice is not to let a model write your first draft because the voice survives editing. Both are about a hand-made commons, and both are better arguments than the ones usually made for them.

Money moves. FuriosaAI, the Korean inference-chip company, closed a six hundred twenty million dollar Series D on Wednesday at a two point nine billion dollar post-money valuation — roughly four times its last round. The composition is the story. Korea's National Growth Fund put in two hundred eighty million, making a sovereign vehicle the single largest investor; existing shareholders added one hundred twenty-five million; and the entire international contingent, including Cisco Investments and an Abu Dhabi holding company, totalled one hundred ten million. Korea's export-import bank made its first direct startup investment ever. This is a state deciding it wants domestic inference silicon, and writing the cheque to prove it.

The product thesis is narrow on purpose. Their RNGD accelerator is a one-hundred-eighty-watt air-cooled card already in mass production since January, running Samsung SDS's commercial inference service, LG's EXAONE deployments, and live search summarization at Upstage. Their performance claim is deliberately relative and unverified — comparable throughput to a GPU drawing at least twice the power. Volume is about twenty thousand cards this year and more than forty thousand next, which is a rounding error against Nvidia and is also not the point. The point is that inference at a known wattage inside your own borders is now a thing governments buy. The third-generation part is being built with Broadcom, which tells you where the real leverage sits.

For the deep cut, the Cognitive Revolution published a conversation on Wednesday with Joel Predd, Ben Boudreaux and Matt Chessen from RAND's Center for the Geopolitics of AGI, on their new paper, "Maintaining Freedom of Action."

Their first move is a taxonomy, and it is the cleanest I have seen. Seven archetypal strategies for the path to superintelligence: acceleration, dominance, co-development, preparedness for coexistence, moratorium, deterrence, and continuity of society. Each is a coherent position held by serious people, and each has a distinct win condition. Then five pivot factors that determine which one is right: Is danger close? Is coexistence feasible? Is restraint feasible? Is a decisive strategic advantage actually reachable? Is suppression feasible? Answer those five and your strategy falls out almost mechanically. That is the contribution — most AI strategy arguments are people with different unstated answers to those five questions talking past each other.

The argument chain then goes like this. There is no consensus on the five questions. There is no evidence that could produce consensus on the timeline the decisions demand. Picking wrong forecloses options — commit to dominance or deterrence and you burn the trust you would need to pivot to cooperation; commit to acceleration and you never build the verification and evaluation capacity the other six require. And then Chessen's line, which is the one that stuck with me: the United States has no declared superintelligence strategy, and so, quote, we may be running an acceleration strategy by default. Doing nothing is not neutral. It is a choice with a name already in the taxonomy.

Predd adds the game theory, from a colleague's paper. Model the race as a prisoner's dilemma and the equilibrium is not cooperation and not a clean race — it is mutual sabotage, both sides degrading each other's progress, even though cooperation pays more collectively. The one thing that breaks that equilibrium into a workable dominance strategy is what they call a wonder weapon: a capability that discontinuously shifts the balance and lets you hold a rival's program at risk. He is careful about how rare those are. Military innovation almost always diffuses through doctrine and force structure over years; the canonical exception is nuclear weapons. So the dominance strategy is coherent, but it is conditional on AI birthing a capability of a kind history produces about once a century.

Their reading of the nuclear era is the sharpest part. We did not have one nuclear strategy. We sprinted — that was the Manhattan Project. We held a roughly four-year monopoly and used it. People proposed something like a moratorium and it was not adopted. We later signed verifiable agreements with the rival, and later still ran something like suppression against proliferation, with limited success. Read forward, that looks like a single doctrine. Read honestly, it was a sequence of different strategies as the facts arrived. Freedom of action is the claim that the sequence was the strategy, and that you should fund the capabilities each stage needs before you know which stage you are in.

The obvious objection is cost, and they take it. A peer reviewer told Predd that a strategy without a price tag is just a vision. His answer is that every archetype except acceleration implies a society-level reorientation on the scale of the early nuclear era, so the distance from today's status quo to any of them dwarfs the distance between them. They are now pricing a hundred-and-six-item security architecture catalogue to put an actual number on it. Until that lands, the insurance-premium argument is doing a lot of work, and they know it.

My takeaway, and the reason this is the deep cut rather than a news item: this is the rare strategy paper whose output is a decision procedure rather than a position. You can disagree with every one of their recommendations and still use the five questions to locate yourself, and to locate the person you are arguing with. That is worth more than another manifesto.

So, the thing to watch. Four stories, one shape. Cartesia's result says the hand-built tokenizer was quietly costing a compute generation, and that the cost was invisible because it was measured in the units the tokenizer defined. Mistral's post says the guardrails are hand-maintained and will keep being revised, on weights that are already out. Stack Overflow is a hand-built commons draining out, with an argument running about whether anything needs to replace it. And RAND's point is that if you never explicitly choose a strategy, the default is not the absence of one. Watch for part two of the Cartesia series, which they say covers out-of-distribution transfer in depth — because if the two-times gap on downstream evaluations holds up under that analysis, the question stops being whether anyone replaces tokenization and becomes which lab does it first at frontier scale.
