Here's what the AI world is talking about today. Yesterday afternoon, about ninety minutes apart, the two largest frontier labs in the world both shipped a new model. Between them they took more than three thousand points and sixteen hundred comments on Hacker News in a single evening. And here's the thing worth noticing before anything else: neither company led with the claim that its model was smarter. Both led with the claim that it was cheaper. One developer on Twitter asked the question that I think is the right one to sit with all day — what does it mean that both labs' headline feature is cheaper tokens?

So let's take the price seriously, and then let's check it.

Anthropic went first. Claude Opus five-point-five, the first model in a new five-point-five family. The pitch in the opening sentence is that it performs at the level of Fable five-point-one on most work and costs forty percent less to run than Opus five. The list prices are four dollars per million input tokens and twenty dollars per million output, down from five and twenty-five — that's twenty percent off the sticker. The bigger move is in cache reads, which went from fifty cents per million to twenty cents, sixty percent off, and cache reads are where most of the money goes in agentic and coding work. Output is more than thirty percent faster.

OpenAI answered within hours with G.P.T. six Sol and G.P.T. six Luna. These are not new frontier models — Astra stays on top — they're cheaper models trained with similar methods. Sol goes from four dollars in and twenty out to two dollars in and ten out. Luna goes from twenty cents and a dollar-twenty to ten cents and fifty cents. Both fifty percent off. The whole announcement is structured as a cost-per-task argument: Sol at extra-high effort beating Opus five at max effort on a business-workflow benchmark at nine percent of the cost per task, Luna beating the previous generation's midsize model at one tenth the cost.

Now the receipts, because this is where the day gets interesting.

Artificial Analysis ran Opus five-point-five through their Intelligence Index and published a decomposition that I want to walk through slowly, because it's the most useful number of the week. At max effort, Opus five-point-five costs five dollars and ninety-eight cents per index task. Opus five, at max effort, cost five dollars and eighty-six cents. Those are the same number. So where did the forty percent go? Their breakdown has three steps. Step one: the new model uses substantially more tokens per task — enough that, holding price constant, cost per task would have gone up about eighty percent, to ten dollars fifty-one. Step two: the twenty percent cut in base token price pulls that down to eight dollars forty-one. Step three: the cheaper cache reads pull it the rest of the way, to five ninety-eight. The price cut is real. It's just being spent on the model's own increased token appetite. Vals independently flagged the same thing — that the lower sticker price is partly offset by higher usage, especially on coding, which is exactly where the model posts its biggest gains.

The forty percent claim isn't false. It applies at default settings, at medium effort. At the top of the dial, on that index, the per-task saving over the previous generation disappears.

The mirror image shows up on the OpenAI side. Artificial Analysis has G.P.T. six Sol at max effort scoring fifty-seven on their Coding Agent Index, two points up from its predecessor, at roughly half the cost — clean win, sits on the frontier. But Luna at max effort scores forty-one, which is two points *down* from G.P.T. five-point-six Luna, with lower scores on two of the component evals, at about sixty percent lower cost per task. That's a capability regression purchased with a discount. It may well be the right trade at that price point — at ten cents per million input tokens Luna is essentially free for high-volume work — but it is worth saying plainly that in this generation, newer did not mean better across the board.

And then there's an anomaly in Anthropic's own numbers that the community pulled out within hours. On one agentic coding chart, extra-high effort costs about two-point-eight times what medium effort costs, and scores three-point-two points *lower*. One researcher called it the most bizarre benchmark result he'd seen and advised people to just stay on medium. The best explanation offered came from a researcher who noticed the same pattern in the previous Opus generation: that particular benchmark penalizes unnecessary changes, and more reasoning effort produces more scope creep. The model thinks harder, does more, and gets marked down for the extra. The dial that's supposed to buy you quality is, on this measurement, buying you a bill.

One more caveat, then the point. The author of one of the programming benchmarks Anthropic cited pointed out that the near-perfect solve rate comes from a one-hundred-sixty-six out of two-hundred subset, which likely excludes the hardest programs, and that there's a metric mismatch — Anthropic reports average test pass rate, the benchmark reports full task completion, and a partial solve typically passes sixty to seventy percent of tests.

Here's what makes all of this more than scorekeeping. Anthropic wrote the caveat themselves, in their own announcement, one line under their own headline table. Their words: at these levels of capability, benchmark margins have become a less reliable guide to real-world differences, and in their own use, the gap between Opus five-point-five and Fable five-point-one is narrower than the scores suggest. That is a frontier lab publishing a table and then telling you, in the same breath, not to read too much into it. Hold onto that sentence. We're coming back to it.

The second convergence yesterday is the one I'd actually bet on mattering more in six months. Both labs made prose quality a headline feature. Anthropic devoted a whole section to it: the most important information up front, less jargon, fewer idiosyncratic phrases, follows the writing rules you give it. One early tester's line, quoted in the announcement, was "it writes the way I do." OpenAI's version, in almost the same words: more clarity, less jargon, fewer odd turns of phrase, fewer low-value details, slightly shorter answers without losing substance. An Anthropic researcher's summary on Twitter was four words — we fixed the writing.

Two competitors, the same afternoon, independently deciding that the thing to advertise is legibility. And Anthropic makes an argument for it I haven't seen a lab make this directly: that easier-to-follow output is easier to *check*, and that this is a safety benefit and not just an ergonomic one. If you're going to run an agent unattended across your codebase for nine hours, your ability to read what it says it did is a control surface. That reframes writing quality from a taste preference into part of the oversight stack. The Latent Space team, who generate a daily A.I. newsletter with these models, said the difference was night and day and migrated immediately.

The last thread from the launch discourse is about pacing. Anthropic's second sentence notes that this is their first release since they publicly called for pacing the frontier, and the most-engaged comment on Hacker News made the obvious observation: the first line invokes pacing, and everything after it is a detailed demonstration of not pacing. The defense several people offered is that neither of yesterday's releases is a new top-tier model, so these were efficiency releases, which is arguably what pacing looks like in practice. I think that's weaker than it sounds. If the frontier is defined by what a given amount of money can buy, then cutting the price of near-frontier capability by half is a frontier move, whatever the version number says.

Which brings us to money, and to the other half of this economy.

Snorkel A.I. raised a three-hundred-fifty-million-dollar Series E at a three-and-a-half-billion-dollar valuation, led by Insight Partners and S-thirty-two. That's nearly triple the one-point-three billion it was worth seventeen months ago. Annualized revenue run rate is now three hundred seventy-five million dollars, an eighteenfold increase in twelve months.

What Snorkel sells is training datasets and simulated reinforcement learning environments. Yesterday on this show we spent the lead segment on Xiaomi open-sourcing roughly seven thousand R.L. environments alongside its weights, and on the argument — from Nathan Lambert's remarks to Congress, and from Thom Wolf — that high-quality environments are now the strategically scarce input, the thing labs started actually buying this summer. Today we have the price tag on that thesis. Eighteen-x revenue growth in a year, in a company whose entire product is the substrate models train against.

One number in the reporting deserves care, because it's the kind of thing that gets repeated wrong. TechCrunch notes that the comparable data companies — Mercor at two billion gross, Handshake at one billion, Micro-one at five hundred million — pay roughly sixty to seventy percent of top-line straight through to the domain specialists doing the work, so net revenue is dramatically lower than the headline. Snorkel's argument is that its number is cleaner, because it sells environments and finished datasets rather than brokering human labor, so expert payments land in cost of goods sold. That distinction is worth tracking, because it's the difference between a software margin and a staffing-agency margin, and right now the market is pricing all of these companies as if they're the former.

Now the deep cut, and it's the best thing I read all week.

Latent Space published an episode yesterday with John Platt of Google. If you've ever called a calibration function in scikit-learn, you've used Platt scaling. He also invented sequential minimal optimization, the algorithm that made support vector machines trainable. He has two asteroids named after him and an Academy Award, and he took Feynman's physics-of-computation class in nineteen eighty-two. His colleague's running joke is that his career has been defined by being twenty years early to the next big thing.

What he's building now is called Empirical Research Assistance, published in Nature. The premise is a reframing worth stealing: an enormous fraction of scientific work reduces to what he calls a scoreable task. You have a problem, you can write down a measure of success, and the job is to find code that maximizes that score. The origin idea was an auto-Kaggle — a system that could solve any Kaggle problem you throw at it.

The mechanism is conceptually simple and that's the interesting part. Gemini keeps a tree of past experiments, where each node is a notebook. At each iteration an Upper Confidence Bound rule picks which notebooks look most promising to mutate — optimistic rather than greedy, so sometimes the fifth-best notebook gets picked. Gemini proposes about ten mutations for each one. History is shared across branches, so different leaves learn from each other. It is, structurally, a cousin of Monte Carlo tree search, and evolutionary program search has existed since the seventies. What changed is the proposal distribution. Platt's team saw a step change between Gemini two-point-oh and two-point-five where the whole thing went from not working to working great. The search algorithm was never the bottleneck. Knowing where to look was. His description of the result: it's almost like having a hyper-eager grad student who doesn't sleep.

The results are not toys. In the Nature paper it hits expert-level performance across genomics, public health, satellite imagery, neuroscience prediction, time-series forecasting, and mathematics. There are now eight manuscripts applying it to open problems. Its epidemiological forecasts of U.S. hospital admissions sit at or near the top of the C.D.C.'s public leaderboards for flu, COVID and R.S.V. And on contrails — those aircraft ice trails that account for about one percent of human-induced warming — his team had been stuck for over two years on a counterfactual problem, modeling the reflected-sunlight half of the effect. The system found a simple model with confounders they hadn't considered.

Then the interviewers asked him the right question. If you've got an optimization god, how do you avoid fooling yourself? And his answer is the reason I'm putting this next to the benchmark story.

His point is that the system gives you predictive models, and it is entirely on the scientist to make sure they're descriptive. He tells on his own company to make it concrete: Google ran a Kaggle competition on contrail detection, and part of the winning solution was that entrants noticed a half-pixel error in the labels — whether the origin sits at the corner or the center of a pixel. Excellent for winning fifteen thousand dollars. Useless for contrails. As he puts it: people themselves will act like these L.L.M.s and try to reward hack. It goes back to Goodhart's law — any metric that becomes a target is no longer good as a metric. And on the system itself: it's a power tool, it can slice your fingers off.

So circle back. A frontier lab published a benchmark table yesterday and then told you in the next paragraph that benchmark margins no longer track real-world differences. A benchmark author pointed out that a near-perfect score came from a subset with the hardest cases removed. An effort dial that costs three times more returns a lower score, because the metric punishes the extra work it buys. And an ML researcher with forty years of perspective says the failure mode isn't the model, it's that we optimize against scores, and we are the easiest people to fool.

Platt's practical advice, for what it's worth, is deliberately unglamorous. Always fit a linear regression first — he says it three times. Develop deep domain expertise, because there's no other route to taste. Do things the old-fashioned way sometimes: you could drive up the mountain or you could hike up it, and it's okay, maybe even fun, to occasionally hike. And the line I'd pin above a lab bench: there's a strong impetus in the world to optimize and squeeze everything out, but you lose something when you hyper-optimize. It's overfit.

Ollie, the takeaway for your work is direct. The scoreable-task framing is a genuinely useful filter for biomedical A.I. — ask of any problem on your list whether you can write down the score, because if you can, this class of system is now a credible way to attack it, and genomics and neuroscience prediction are already in the validated set. But the same paragraph that makes that exciting is the one that tells you the score is the risk. Every hour you don't spend on the search, you should spend on whether the thing you're maximizing is the thing you want.

One thing to watch tomorrow. Sonnet five-point-five and Haiku five-point-five are promised in the coming weeks, and Anthropic's own framing is that they'll carry the same efficiency gains. If the pattern from yesterday holds — headline price down, token consumption up — then the number that actually matters isn't on either announcement page. Watch for the independent per-task cost, at default effort, from a shop that isn't selling you the model.
