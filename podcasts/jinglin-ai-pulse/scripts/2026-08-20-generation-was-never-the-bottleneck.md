## Script

Here's what the AI world is talking about today. A team shipped an open-weight model family whose whole pitch is that it writes its own training problems — a closed self-improvement loop, MIT licensed, three hundred ninety-seven billion parameters at the top end, matching Claude Opus four-point-eight on Terminal-Bench. And two days earlier, Terence Tao published the text of his ICM lecture arguing that generating the answer was never the hard part of mathematics, and that the field spent a century optimizing a proxy for something it never bothered to write down. Those two documents are in direct conversation, and almost nobody is reading them side by side. Let's do that.

Start with the model. Ornith one-point-five comes from DeepReinforce, who shipped Ornith one-point-zero in June, and the interesting thing is not the benchmark line — it's what they automated. One-point-zero did self-scaffolding: the model wrote its own harness for a task somebody else gave it. One-point-five closes the loop. The model proposes the task, generates a task-specific scaffold to evaluate it, and produces the solution rollouts used for reinforcement learning — and all three stages are optimized jointly, reward flowing back through the whole thing at once, using GRPO.

Be precise about what "self-improvement" means here, because the phrase is doing a lot of work. This is a training-time data flywheel, not a claim that the model you download edits its own weights while you use it. What's actually novel is the reward design, because they had to solve a problem that only exists once the proposer and the solver are the same network. The task reward multiplies three signals. Validity is a hard gate — the scaffold has to execute, and the evaluation has to match what the task claims to test, so a malformed task earns exactly zero. Then frontier difficulty, measured from empirical success rates on the model's own rollouts, with reward peaking around a zero-point-two success rate. That's the elegant bit: as the model gets better, the tasks it used to be rewarded for proposing stop paying, so the curriculum advances on its own, without a human deciding what's hard this week. And third, novelty — semantic distance from prior tasks — deliberately kept subordinate to the first two.

The harness gets its own reward triple: does it align with the task, is the reward signal faithful, and — this is the tell — is it resistant to reward hacking. They wrote an anti-reward-hacking term into the objective, because when the model grades its own homework the shortest path to a high score is a broken grader. On their SWE-Bench runs they strip Git history and disable network access for the same reason. That is an honest response to a real failure mode, and it tells you more about where this technique sits than any leaderboard row.

The numbers, briefly. The flagship hits eighty-six point one on Terminal-Bench two-point-one against Opus four-point-eight's eighty-five, and fifty-six on DeepSWE against Opus's fifty-nine, clearing GLM five-point-two and DeepSeek V-four Flash on both, averaged over five runs. Below it, a thirty-five billion mixture-of-experts activating three billion parameters per token, and a nine-billion dense model with a mobile build.

And here is the deflation, which the Hacker News thread found immediately and which I think is the actual story. Ornith one-point-five is post-trained on Qwen three-point-five and Gemma four checkpoints. The self-improvement loop is not building a foundation model — it is a post-training procedure applied on top of somebody else's base. Commenters also noted the comparison table quietly omits the newer Qwen three-point-eight twenty-seven-billion model, and posted numbers putting that Qwen ahead on SWE-bench Pro and substantially ahead on DeepSWE. One comment captured the mood: "Another day another startup claiming some vague version of RSI to try to close their round." The practical verdict was warmer, though — one user reported the thirty-five billion model was "on par with Qwen3.8 27B at a much higher speed" on real scraping work.

So hold that thought — a system that automates the generation of problems, sitting on a base it did not build — and turn to Tao.

The essay is twelve pages, submitted to the ICM proceedings, and the move it makes is a refusal. Tao declines to argue about whether AI can do research mathematics — he conditions on it. Assume the tools arrive and work at reasonable cost. Then he asks the question he thinks is genuinely orthogonal to capability: what are the goals and values of mathematical research, and have we ever written them down?

His answer decomposes problem-solving into five stages, not one. Generation of a proof. Verification. Exposition. Publication through refereeing. And canonicalization — the slow absorption of a result into textbooks, into the shape of the field. Generation is the stage AI is good at, and he gives the receipts: the First Proof project's second batch, evaluated in late May — ten research-level problems never posted online, contributed by working mathematicians, four AI systems under controlled conditions, and seven of the ten got at least one passing grade, meaning essentially flawless or needing only minor revision, at tens to hundreds of dollars per problem. Verification he treats as increasingly solved infrastructure: Lean, Rocq, HOL mean correctness no longer rests on author reputation.

It's the last three stages where the argument gets uncomfortable, and there is one observation I have not been able to stop thinking about. On exposition, he distinguishes artificial friction in a piece of writing — typos, disorganization, awkward phrasing — from natural friction, the places where a human author labored because the mathematics itself is hard right there. That roughness is a signal. It tells a reader where to slow down. His line: "An excessively AI-polished proof may sand away both the 'artificial' friction and the 'natural' friction, leaving a text that is easy to read and hard to learn from." If you have ever read a perfectly fluent model explanation and come away with nothing, you have felt this. He is naming a mechanism.

On publication he predicts proof indigestion — 消化不良 — a volunteer refereeing system buried under machine-generated submissions, and he explicitly rejects automated filtering as a substitute for human judgment. And on canonicalization he lands the load-bearing claim: "The very success of AI tools in mathematics depends crucially on the canonical theories that human mathematicians have painstakingly built and rebuilt over the centuries." The models eat the canon. The canon is made by the slowest, least automatable, least rewarded activity in the field.

Then the structural diagnosis, which is where this stops being about mathematics. Tao quotes Thurston: "We are not trying to meet some abstract production quota of definitions, theorems and proofs. The measure of our success is whether what we do enables people to understand and think more clearly and effectively about math." The community's real value is understanding. But understanding is hard to measure, so for a century the field used a proxy that correlated beautifully — solve hard problems first. Goodhart's Law says a proxy that becomes a target stops measuring the thing. AI, plus an industry whose entire incentive structure is benchmarks, is the machine that severs that correlation. He frames it as mathematics' second foundational crisis: the crisis of nineteen-hundred to nineteen-thirty forced the field to make truth explicit, and the AI era is forcing it to make values explicit. His concrete asks, drawing on June's Leiden Declaration, are unglamorous — disclose tool use, keep credit and responsibility with humans, say so explicitly when you cannot identify a source, reward being first less and invest more in exposition, refereeing and canonicalization. Plus a personal standard I would love to see borrowed by every field: if authors cannot give a clear, expert-level talk on their own result, correct and properly attributed, it should not be published.

Put the two together and the convergence is sharp. Ornith automated task generation and immediately discovered its binding constraint was the grader. Tao, from a completely different direction, says the same thing about a whole discipline: once generation is cheap, everything downstream of it becomes the bottleneck, and those stages are exactly the ones nobody gets credit for.

Now, money moves — and the pattern this week is that capital is not buying models, it is buying control surfaces.

SpaceX closed its sixty billion dollar all-stock acquisition of Anysphere, the maker of Cursor, in mid-August — folded into a new SpaceX AI division, and by most counts the largest startup acquisition on record. Cursor gets SpaceX compute, including Colossus. Then on the nineteenth, Bloomberg reported SpaceX had also tried to buy Cognition. CEO Scott Wu denied it — not for sale, no talks — though notably he did not deny the second half of the report, that the two are discussing Cognition using SpaceX compute. Cognition raised a billion in late May at twenty-five billion post-money and is reportedly in early talks at forty.

Read that alongside Stripe. Stripe bought OpenRouter for seven and a half billion, up from a one-point-three billion valuation three months earlier. The founders joked about "the singularity" in a leaked investor letter. TechCrunch's read is duller and more convincing: this is expense management for AI. Stripe's business has always been money coming in; token spend is the fastest-growing line of money going out, and OpenRouter sits on it. PitchBook's Franco Granda framed it as gaining "some degree of power over suppliers such as the frontier labs themselves, as well as hyperscalers." Eighty-eight percent of the Forbes AI fifty already run on Stripe.

And this morning Binance launched Agent OS, which lets AI agents analyze markets and place trades on your behalf — MCP support, so ChatGPT, Claude Code or Cursor can drive it, plus a payment facilitator API and x402 transaction verification. The architecture is the part worth noticing. The reasoning happens entirely outside Binance, on your machine or in your chosen app, so Binance has essentially no visibility into why any trade was placed. Control lives in sub-accounts: withdrawals off by default, per-permission grants, optional per-trade approval, and no exchange-imposed cap — your sub-account balance is the cap. Binance VP Jeff Li said they put the control at the account level to protect users' funds. Kraken, Coinbase and OKX have shipped comparable things, so the whole category has converged on pushing agent oversight down to the individual user, in a domain that is irreversible by design and where prompt injection is a live attack.

Three deals, one thesis. When an MIT-licensed model matches Opus four-point-eight, the weights are not where the rent is. The rent is at the surface where an agent touches something real — your editor, your token bill, your account balance.

Which brings me to the Podcast Deep Cut, the episode that names the discomfort under all of this. Flo Crivello, founder of Lindy, was on The Cognitive Revolution with Nathan Labenz on the tenth, launching Lindy Teammate.

His technical framing first, because it is the best articulation I have heard of why context beats capability right now: if John von Neumann magically appeared next to you at the office, over the next hour or day he would be less useful to you than your random coworker, because he has no organizational context. So Lindy's engineering is almost entirely a memory problem — onboarding a twenty-person team burns three to five million tokens crawling Slack, Notion and Docs into a knowledge graph, and the bottleneck is the Slack API, not the model. A background memory agent wakes every fifteen minutes and learns emergently to ignore log channels and restructure files to cut retrieval hops. It all lives in an inspectable, Git-backed file system — he is bearish on RAG and bullish on text, because you can open the memory file and write "this is what I never want you to remember."

But the second thread is the one that matters. Lindy runs on Chinese open weights. "Everything is DeepSeek right now" — DeepSeek Flash is free, roughly Sonnet four-point-six quality, and on his numbers about a hundred times cheaper; even eating a two-x penalty on third-party caching he is fifty-x ahead. That is the difference between a thousand-dollar bill and fifty thousand. He puts the frontier gap at three to six months and calls open models a required part of the stack for anyone seriously operating agents today.

And then he argues they should be banned. Four links. Distillation — 蒸馏 — is IP theft: training from scratch costs billions in human-data investment, copying the artifact costs hundreds of millions, and he runs the analogy through pharma, where you get very cheap drugs and then no new drugs ever. Censorship: he calls them "the greatest instrument of foreign propaganda on American soil ever," and demonstrated it live by asking his own product about Tiananmen and getting a refusal. Agentic reach: these models now run chunks of the American economy, increasingly unsupervised. And as a self-described libertarian, he thinks protecting domestic labs is legitimate national-security policy.

What makes it worth your time is that he states the contradiction himself: "I'm speaking against my own interests here, my company is economically dependent on those very cheap models. But I'm also in a coordination problem, because I can't not adopt these models while they're out there, since my competitors are going to do it." That is a clean statement of a collective action failure from someone standing inside it.

Labenz pushes back on three fronts and it does not resolve. Consent hypocrisy — the entire frontier was built on mass appropriation of human knowledge without consent, including China's digitized heritage, so the terms-of-service line looks arbitrary. Crivello concedes revenue protection is his weakest argument: "the poor trillionaires, leave them alone… that's fair." Second, most American companies route Chinese weights through US inference providers, so rug-pull risk is largely handled. Third, why not audits and insurance instead of a ban the rest of the world will simply ignore. Crivello's counter-offer is an FAA for AI, certifying sanitized retrained models as fit for American use, and he immediately identifies why it will not happen: "sanitizing and retraining these models is a public good nobody has a private incentive to supply."

Ollie's takeaway. Every item today is the same shape. Ornith automated generation and hit the wall at verification. Tao says mathematics automated generation and will hit the wall at digestion and canonicalization. Stripe, SpaceX and Binance are buying the layer where generated output touches consequences. And Crivello is a founder whose cost structure depends on generation being nearly free, who cannot name a mechanism that makes provenance and sanitization anyone's job. Cheap generation is not the achievement. It is the thing that reveals which institutions you never built.

Watch two things tomorrow. Whether anyone reproduces Ornith's self-improvement loop on a base model they actually trained themselves — that is the experiment separating a post-training trick from a real flywheel. And whether the first Binance Agent OS prompt-injection incident shows up, because the architecture guarantees that when it does, nobody at the exchange will be able to explain what the agent was thinking.
