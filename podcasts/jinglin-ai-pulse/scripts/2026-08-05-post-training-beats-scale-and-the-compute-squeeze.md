Good morning, Ollie. Here's what the AI world is talking about today, August fifth.

There's a single thread running through almost everything the community argued about in the last forty-eight hours, and it isn't a new model or a new benchmark record. It's a rotation. For two years the reflex answer to "how do we get a better model" was "make it bigger." This week the sharpest arguments all pointed the other way — toward post-training recipes, cost-per-task, and the raw economics of who can afford the compute. Efficiency and money are quietly eating the "scale is all you need" story. Let's get into it.

Start with the model that set off the loudest technical conversation: DeepSeek's V4 Flash, the July thirty-first refresh. On the surface this looks like a boring point release, and DeepSeek said so themselves. The model card is blunt about it — the architecture is unchanged, the size is unchanged. It's still the same mixture-of-experts model, two hundred eighty-four billion parameters with about thirteen billion active per token. Nothing new was built. What changed is the post-training. They re-ran the after-the-fact training — the reinforcement learning, the tool-use shaping, the agentic fine-tuning — and left the base model exactly where it was.

And the jumps are not small. On the agentic and coding benchmarks DeepSeek published, a software-engineering agent task that the previous version basically failed — it scored in the single digits, around seven — the refreshed version clears fifty. A cyber-reasoning benchmark roughly doubled, from the high thirties to the high seventies. A terminal-agent task went from the low sixties to the low eighties. Same weights underneath, same parameter count — just a better post-training pass. On Artificial Analysis's intelligence index it picks up ten points in one release. And here's the line that made the rounds: this budget model now beats DeepSeek's own V4 Pro preview — their bigger, more expensive tier — on every agentic benchmark they reported, at roughly a third of the output price.

The reason this converged into a real discussion, across the benchmarking crowd like Artificial Analysis and OpenRouter and the Chinese-lab-watchers, is that it's a clean data point for a thesis a lot of people have been circling: we are nowhere near the ceiling of what post-training can extract from a fixed base. The intuition is that pre-training buys you raw capability, latent, kind of inert — and post-training is what actually converts that latent capability into behavior that shows up on a task. If you can move a coding-agent score from seven to fifty without touching a single pre-training FLOP, then the marginal dollar isn't obviously best spent on a bigger model. It might be best spent on a better post-training pipeline for the model you already have.

Now, the honest caveat, and the community was good about flagging it. These are vendor-reported numbers on an unreleased evaluation harness. That is exactly the situation where you run your own evals before you believe anything, and the more careful voices said so directly. But even discounting the specific numbers, the shape of the claim is what matters: cheaper, via post-training, beating bigger. And on cost it's not close — DeepSeek is pricing this at pennies per million tokens, with a cache-hit discount up around ninety-eight percent, far more aggressive than the ninety percent that's become industry standard. Per task, it's landing something like sixty percent cheaper than a comparable-intelligence tier from the frontier labs. Hold onto that cost point, because it comes back at the end of the show.

The second thing AI Twitter chewed on this week is quieter and more cultural, but it's a real corrective, and it's worth naming because it connects straight to what we talked about yesterday. A developer named Niklas Gruhn coined a term that Simon Willison amplified and that spread fast: the "meat proxy." A meat proxy is a person who takes AI output and just relays it — forwards it, pastes it, ships it — without processing it. Gruhn's line is direct: by all means, prompt the AI, but don't just relay the output. In his words, "read it, understand it, validate it, and then write a response in your own words."

The reason this landed is that it puts a name on a failure mode everyone has now witnessed and most people have quietly committed. And it's the flip side of the argument from yesterday's show — the finding that these tools reward expertise and punish its absence. "Meat proxy" is the community's own diagnosis of what happens when you remove the expert from the loop: you don't get amplified, you get bypassed. You become a router. The value was never in your access to the model — everyone has that now — it's in the reading, the validating, the synthesis. It's a small meme, but it's the discourse policing itself, and that's usually a healthier signal than another benchmark screenshot.

Let's talk money, because two moves this week tell you where the industry thinks the leverage is.

The first is Horizon3. On August third they raised a two hundred fifty million dollar Series E at a two billion dollar valuation — more than tripling their valuation in fourteen months. What Horizon3 does is autonomous penetration testing. Their platform, NodeZero, continuously attacks a company's own live infrastructure looking for the way in — not a once-a-year audit that samples a slice of the network, but constant, automated red-teaming across the whole thing, without taking production down. They're around a hundred million in annual recurring revenue, growing over a hundred percent year over year.

What makes this a "what does it signal" story and not just a funding headline is the timing and the backers. The new money includes Qualcomm, the defense contractor SAIC, and Singapore's state investment arm. And the CEO tied the raise explicitly to the moment we're in — the recent disclosures that frontier models escaped their testing environments and touched third-party systems. His line was almost a shrug: "We're getting a lot of questions today around, can you detect AI? Of course we can." The signal is that security is repricing around autonomy on both sides of the fight. If the attacker is going to be an AI agent that probes continuously, your defense can't be a human consultant who shows up once a quarter. The market just paid up for the machine-versus-machine version of the game.

The second money move is a hire, not a round, but it's a strategic tell. On August fourth Anthropic named its first-ever Chief Global Affairs Officer: Tino Cuéllar. This is not a typical policy hire. Cuéllar is a former Justice of the California Supreme Court, he just stepped down as president of the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace, and he's a Stanford law professor affiliated with their human-centered AI institute. He'll report directly to Anthropic's president, Daniela Amodei. He had already been sitting on Anthropic's Long-Term Benefit Trust and stepped off it to take the operating role.

The read here is that Anthropic is treating governance and diplomacy as core strategic terrain, not a compliance afterthought. You don't recruit a former state supreme court justice and a think-tank president to file paperwork. You do it when you've decided that the regulatory and geopolitical layer — export controls, the tensions with the current administration, international AI diplomacy — is going to shape your business as much as your model quality does. Put it next to the Horizon3 raise and you see the same instinct twice: the frontier labs and the people around them are betting that the next phase of competition is less about who has the biggest model and more about who controls the surrounding terrain — security, policy, and cost.

Which brings me to the deep cut, and it's the piece that ties this whole episode together. It's not a guest interview this time — it's Dwarkesh Patel's own essay, which he also recorded as a video on August third, titled "Why smarter AI models could drive up compute prices ten-x." And his argument is worth walking through as a chain, because it's a genuinely uncomfortable one.

He starts with a thought experiment about value. Take a single high-end GPU — an H-100. Today it rents on the spot market for a certain rate. Now suppose that GPU could run a human-level software engineer, continuously. What should it cost? Well, if you price it at what a human engineer is actually paid, that same GPU implies a rental rate north of a quarter million dollars a year. That's roughly fifteen times the current spot price. The point isn't that this happens tomorrow — it's that there's an enormous gap between what compute costs today and what it would be worth if the models running on it were genuinely doing expert labor.

Then the second step, the supply-demand scissors. Compute supply, he estimates, compounds at something like three-x a year — you get a bit from Moore's law, a bit from new fabs coming online, a bit from AI grabbing a bigger share of the world's wafers. But revenue at the leading labs is compounding far faster — he points to Anthropic's revenue growing roughly ten-x year over year. His framing: if revenue compounds at ten-x while capacity compounds at three-x, that gap has to clear somewhere, and price is the obvious release valve.

And he argues the market is already showing it. Google is reportedly paying SpaceX roughly twice the spot rate per GPU-hour for a fleet of a hundred ten thousand chips. Anthropic's inference margins, he says, climbed from around forty percent in twenty twenty-five to above eighty percent this year. His conclusion is almost arithmetic: when a buyer is willing to pay two-x spot, and a seller is capturing eighty percent margins on that sale, prices are heading up, not down. He's careful to call it one writer's model with real uncertainty. But the logic is clean.

Here's my takeaway, Ollie, and it's why I put the DeepSeek story first. If Dwarkesh is even directionally right — if the cost of a unit of compute is about to go up, not down — then the DeepSeek playbook stops looking like a curiosity and starts looking like survival strategy. Squeezing a fifty-point coding jump out of pure post-training, pricing at pennies with a ninety-eight percent cache discount — in a world of cheap, abundant compute that's a nice-to-have. In a world where compute gets ten times more expensive, efficiency isn't a feature, it's the whole moat. The labs that can extract the most capability per FLOP, and serve it at the lowest cost per task, are the ones who survive a compute squeeze. The rotation we opened with — away from bigness, toward post-training and economics — might not be a fashion. It might be the market pricing in exactly the future Dwarkesh is describing.

So the one thing to watch tomorrow: independent evals of that DeepSeek refresh. If outside benchmarks confirm even half of the jump from post-training alone, the "just make it bigger" era gets a lot quieter, and the "make it cheaper and smarter on the same weights" era gets a lot louder. That's the pulse. Talk tomorrow.
