## Script

Here's what the AI world is talking about today. Two things landed within hours of each other, from groups that don't coordinate, and together they carve one question into two halves. The question is whether agents can do science. One group answered by measuring it, and got thirty percent. The other answered by shipping a driver layer for the lab bench. And behind both, a blog post that went to six hundred points on Hacker News argues the opposite — that the small cheap models have arrived and most work is already solved. Those claims are not in conflict. Together they're a map of where the frontier actually sits.

Start with the measurement. Terminal-Bench-Science shipped its first release yesterday, led by researchers at Stanford with the Laude Institute and the team behind Terminal-Bench. The premise is in the announcement's first line: scientists, not model developers or data vendors, should set the bar for scientific capability in AI. Seventy tasks across the life, physical, Earth, mathematical, and engineering sciences, drawn from researchers' own workflows — statistical inference, simulation, theorem proving, image reconstruction, inverse problems, sensor calibration — each graded on a concrete artifact with reproducible tests.

The headline number is that Claude Opus 5, running in Claude Code, resolves thirty percent. GPT five point six Sol with Codex is second at twenty-two point four, Fable 5 third at twenty-one point four, and Opus four point eight — one generation back — sits at ten and a half. Then the floor: Kimi K3 and Grok four point six under ten percent, and GLM five point three the strongest open model at eight point one. Three independent trials per task across all seventy, so this isn't a single-run artifact.

But the number I'd actually keep is buried in the methodology. Of nine hundred twenty task proposals, four hundred sixty-four were approved, three hundred eighty-six pull requests were opened — and seventy tasks made the release. That's the honest measure of how hard it is to write a scientific task that is simultaneously well-specified enough to grade automatically, scientifically real, and not already solved by a frontier agent. Roughly one proposal in thirteen survived. The bottleneck in benchmarking science isn't compute or annotation budget. It's specification.

And the calibration is explicit, which I appreciate. Terminal-Bench-Science separates systems about as well as Terminal-Bench three point zero does, but pushes every model's score down more than ten percentage points — a gap that was designed in, because tasks were tuned during review to beat the newest frontier models. So the thirty percent is not a claim that science is twice as hard as software engineering. It's a claim about where they chose to put the difficulty knob, and one they reset every release.

The sharpest pushback on Hacker News was about grading, and it's the right worry. One commenter noted that models sometimes simplify a task rather than implement it as specified, and asked whether the tests check correctness or just check that something ran. Another was blunter: we don't need more science-shaped slop clogging the peer review pipeline. Fair critique of the genre, though it lands less hard here than on most science benchmarks, precisely because artifacts are graded against reproducible task-specific tests rather than judged by a model.

Now the other half. Anthropic opened a research preview of the Model Hardware Standard, and if you work in a wet lab this is the more consequential of the two announcements. The problem it addresses is boring and universal: instruments don't talk to each other. Every microscope, liquid handler, plate reader, and robot arm ships with a proprietary interface in a different language, and integrating them takes weeks to months of bespoke glue code. The standard reduces devices to simple read and write primitives, so a temperature is a readable variable and a set-point is a writable one, over a shared memory dictionary — an idea that came out of HHMI Janelia — that lets instruments and programs see each other's state at memory speed. Agents reach it through the Model Context Protocol, a command line, or a code API. It's model-agnostic, and Anthropic says it will open-source it after the preview.

The partner results are what make this concrete rather than aspirational. In the Baker and Pinglay labs at the University of Washington, a PhD student connected six instruments in under a week including the time writing drivers, against previous attempts he describes as weeks of evaluating platforms, chasing vendor support, and finally giving up. His bottleneck before was a PCR machine that handled one plate at a time and needed a plate change every ninety minutes — which, in his words, is how he sometimes ended up moving plates at four in the morning instead of sleeping. At Carnegie Mellon, serial dilution dose-response experiments ran about three times faster across a liquid handler, plate reader, robot arm, and cameras spread over three computers with incompatible interfaces. And there the agent evaluated its own curve, found the fit too poor to accept — R-squared under nine tenths, driven by saturation at the top of the concentration range — and independently decided to discard the plate and rerun with the top concentration compressed from two hundred micrograms per milliliter to one hundred. The second run came back above zero point nine eight, with no human input at any step. They also induced six fault conditions, including a rotated plate and an active emergency stop, and the interlocks blocked every one before anything moved.

But the QuEra Computing result is the one I'd think hardest about. QuEra builds neutral-atom quantum computers, and their lasers have to hold an ultra-precise frequency lock. When the lock breaks, someone has to recover it. Before the standard existed, a team of four — a laser systems engineer, a software engineer, an algorithms specialist, and a tester — spent several months writing a bespoke recovery script that mimicked what a human does at the bench, step for step. It worked about fifty-eight percent of the time and took around a hundred fifty seconds per attempt. With it, they ran four Claude instances as four roles — one proposing a hypothesis, one editing the script, one running it against the live laser and logging every step, one reading the logbook and deciding what to change next. That loop ran hundreds of times, unattended, overnight. By morning: about six seconds, ninety-six percent. The final validated script recovered the lock in six hundred ninety-five of seven hundred trials, ninety-nine point three percent, with the hardest disturbances taking ten to fourteen seconds against five to ten minutes for a human.

Here's the part that matters intellectually, and it's easy to skim past. The output of that overnight agent loop is a deterministic, fully inspectable script that runs in production with no AI in the loop at all. The agent wasn't the controller. The agent was the search process that found the controller. That distinction is the whole argument for why this is deployable in a regulated or safety-critical setting, and it's a template worth stealing: point the loop at the live apparatus, let it write a program, then ship the program and retire the loop.

The limitations are stated plainly and they're the ones you'd predict. Claude learned the physical world from text and images, so its spatial reasoning still needs expert oversight — Anthropic's own example is Genentech researchers having to teach Claude that errors from foaming in a protein sample were physical failures, not software bugs. And it doesn't work at all with hardware that has no programming interface, which is a lot of hardware.

Hacker News was more skeptical than the press, on one specific point: you need permission to read the specification. The top comment was simply that you shouldn't need permission to read a standard. Others noted the pattern from the Model Context Protocol — ship something underbaked, win on first-mover distribution rather than technical merit — and raised the obvious concern that Anthropic gets to train against a protocol nobody else has seen. Worth holding onto, given that AWS, Danaher, QIAGEN, Tecan, Doosan, Universal Robots, and Hugging Face's LeRobot are all already building support.

Now put that against the third story, which argues the reverse. Calvin French-Owen, who co-founded Segment and spent last year at OpenAI, published a short post called "Small Models Have Arrived," and it went to six hundred thirty-four points. His claim is economic, not technical. Running complicated research threads on GPT five point six Luna, he finds it hard to run up a bill — searching thousands of emails costs tens of cents. His personal eval is building a daily personalized news site: about a dollar a run on the previous Sonnet-class generation, and about ten cents on Luna. A dollar a run is why, he argues, we haven't seen more consumer AI companies — token costs broke the old playbook of cheap website, viral growth, ads marketplace.

Then he borrows a frame from his Segment co-founder Peter Reinhardt that I think is genuinely useful. There are two kinds of work. There's the IQ one-eighty work, where some mad-scientist type produces a solution you'd never have thought of. And there's the token spewer work — being ultra responsive, pushing the ball forward across dozens of fronts, hopping on calls, nudging people, blocking and tackling. Reinhardt runs multiple companies and estimates about ninety-five percent of his own work is the second kind. French-Owen's conclusion: frontier demand keeps compounding, but the fast-cheap-good-enough tier is about to take off, and the missing pieces are harnesses, prompt-injection safety, roles, and permissions — not intelligence.

And here's the convergence, which nobody involved arranged. French-Owen carves out an exception in one clause: he expects frontier demand to keep compounding especially for fields requiring novel breakthroughs or discovery — engineering, hard science, model training. Terminal-Bench-Science is the first rigorous measurement of exactly that carve-out. And on it, GPT five point six Luna — the model his entire post is about — finishes last, at three point three percent, against Opus 5's thirty. Two independent sources, one qualitative and one quantitative, agreeing on where the seam is. The small models arrived for the token-spewer work. They have not arrived anywhere near the bench.

To the money, where the day's big number closed. Nvidia has agreed to buy Hugging Face for twelve point nine billion dollars. The Information broke it, CNBC picked it up with the same figure. Yesterday this was talks; today it's a deal. The context that makes it interesting: late last year Hugging Face turned down a five hundred million dollar Nvidia investment at a seven billion valuation, explicitly because it didn't want a dominant shareholder that could sway its decisions. Nine months later it sold outright, to the same buyer, at nearly twice that valuation.

The Hacker News thread ran to nearly nineteen hundred points and split cleanly. Skeptics did the arithmetic: at roughly a hundred fifty million in annual revenue, twelve point nine billion is something like eighty-six years of revenue. Defenders pointed at Microsoft buying GitHub, where the multiple looked equally absurd and the real asset turned out to be the developer graph, not the revenue line. My read is that Nvidia is buying neither revenue nor technology. It's buying the default distribution point for open weights, at a moment when the open-weight frontier is Chinese — GLM, Qwen, Kimi — and every one of those models becoming easy to run is a GPU sale. The question the thread was actually asking is whether the hub stays neutral. Nvidia's incentive says it does, because proliferation sells hardware. But incentives change, and a hub with a hardware vendor's thumb on it is a different object than a hub without one.

One smaller move before the deep cut. Barret Zoph is now vice president of research at Google, bringing reinforcement learning and post-training expertise to Gemini — his fourth institution in under two years, after OpenAI, Thinking Machines, and OpenAI again. The signal isn't the drama. It's that post-training talent, arguably the scarcest skill in the industry, now moves faster than product cycles do, which is a poor foundation for anyone claiming a durable capability moat.

Which brings us to the deep cut, and it's the piece that ties the whole day together. Dylan Patel of SemiAnalysis was on the Dwarkesh Podcast on Monday, and the episode is titled with his conclusion: Anthropic and OpenAI will have most of the world's compute by 2028. It's worth following the argument link by link, because the conclusion sounds like hype and the chain doesn't.

It starts with unit economics. Compute costs roughly ten to fifteen million dollars per megawatt. In the GPT-4 era, serving a model on Hopper generated negative gross margin — you spent more on the megawatt than you made from it. That has inverted. Patel says that serving current-generation models, revenue per megawatt has gone as high as fifty million dollars in Anthropic's case. His framing of what that unlocks: if I spend ten bucks on inference capacity, I generate fifty bucks of revenue, and then I can turn around and incrementally spend all of that profit on training.

Link two: if you can turn a megawatt into five times its cost, you can outbid anyone else for the next megawatt. So the labs win the auction by default. This year, OpenAI went from two gigawatts to above five, Anthropic from under two to above five — a three-to-four-times increase, absorbing about thirty percent of all compute added worldwide. Next year, on contracts already signed, he puts the two of them at forty to fifty percent of new compute.

Link three is the one people miss, and it's why the conclusion is stronger than the percentages suggest. A watt deployed this year does far more work than a watt deployed two years ago — GB three hundreds, TPU v seven, Trainium three, three to five times the performance per watt of the prior generation. So the labs aren't just taking half the new watts; they're taking half of the best new watts. Patel calls it a ladder: the share multiplies against the efficiency multiplier. His line: by the time you're towards the end of 2028, if this trend continues, and I see nothing that's stopping it, you've got them just controlling most of the usable flops in the world on their own.

And then the implication he actually cares about, which is not an infrastructure point. We're moving fast, he says, into a regime where most people, in terms of work output, are concentrated within two labs. If those systems are misaligned, then most of the world is misaligned, because most of the world's minds are there. And even if they're perfectly aligned, very few organizations hold very much influence. His summary of the whole dynamic, and I think this is the quote from the episode: every force is screeching towards centralization, and that's scary as hell. Dwarkesh doesn't resolve it. He closes by naming it as an open intellectual project — what is a vision of a decentralized, broadly empowered future after AGI that takes these economies of scale seriously — because the alternative he sees is choosing which single source of centralization you prefer, two corporations or one government.

The strongest counterargument came from a commenter on the episode page, and the distinction is worth keeping. Compute centralizing and advantage centralizing are not the same claim, and the second is the one worth arguing about. If two labs hold most of the training capacity in 2028, the scarce thing stops being capacity and becomes judgment about what to point it at — and judgment sits in a lot more places than two. Which is precisely why today's other two stories matter more than they look. Terminal-Bench-Science hands the judgment about what counts as scientific capability to practicing scientists, through an open pull request process, calibrated against the frontier every release. And the Model Hardware Standard, if it really gets open-sourced, hands the physical apparatus to whoever has the instruments — not to whoever has the megawatts. Neither one decentralizes compute. Both of them decentralize the question of what compute should be aimed at.

One thing to watch. Terminal-Bench-Science version zero point two takes task proposals until October fifth, and its explicit ask is for workflows frontier agents should be able to do but cannot yet. If you have a computational workflow in your own work that Opus 5 fails on, that is now a contributable artifact with a review process and a leaderboard attached — a better use of a bad model day than a complaint. And watch whether the hardware specification actually goes public, and how fast, because a standard for driving lab instruments that only one lab is allowed to read is a very different thing from a standard.
