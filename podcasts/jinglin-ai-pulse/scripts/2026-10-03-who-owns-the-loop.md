Here's what the AI world is talking about today. The most upvoted post on Hacker News in two days wasn't a model. It was version one-point-oh of a piece of software whose whole job is to sit between you and a model. In the same forty-eight hours, DeepSeek shipped one of those too, Figma locked one out, Apple announced it's tightening the operating system's permissions because of them, and a team from Meta and the University of Washington published a paper arguing you shouldn't need one at all. The thing they're all fighting over has a boring name. The harness. And the question underneath today's episode is simple: who owns the loop?

Start with the vote count. Pi 1.0, from Earendil, sixteen hundred and fifty-nine points, nearly six hundred comments. Pi made its name as the minimalist coding agent, the one that said no to almost everything. Version one adds Codemode, deferred tool loading, mid-conversation system messages, and, quite specifically, cache warming for Anthropic models. The demo video is the giveaway: Pi writes itself an extension that plans with Claude Opus, implements with GPT, and uses a decision model to judge when to switch from one to the other. The harness isn't wrapping a model anymore. It's routing across three of them.

The best exchange in the thread came when a commenter asked, reasonably, what Pi's standard for "proven" even is, given that MCP has been around for nearly two years and only now made it in. Armin Ronacher answered for Earendil, and he called his own answer "pretty disappointing." Quote: "we look at what the models are doing. They are trained on their respective harnesses and we're not here to fight their behavior." He added that Codex uses code-mode internally for parallel tool calls, so supporting code-mode was a given.

Sit with that for a second, because it inverts the usual picture. We tend to think the harness is the user's layer, the part you customize. Ronacher is saying the most independent harness in the ecosystem is downstream of choices made in someone else's post-training run. Further down, a commenter asked the question everyone has: how much does the harness actually matter? Someone replied that the differences people attribute to Claude versus Codex are often more the harness than the model. The asker came back with the honest version: what is the harness doing that's not the model? That's the right question, and today it got three different answers.

The first answer is commercial: the harness is the product. DeepSeek Harness went to public preview this week, open source, MIT license, built on an everything-is-a-plugin architecture, with a Creator mode where you ask it in chat to write a new plugin, and it writes, installs and verifies it. The Hacker News reaction split predictably on data sovereignty and default-on telemetry in the desktop build. The sharper debate was one commenter predicting harnesses will shrink as models get better until they're at the level of vanilla Pi or not even that, and another replying that, on the contrary, this will be the next battlefield. When the lab with the strongest open-weights track record ships its own front end, that's a bet on the second view.

The second answer is the one that should make you uneasy: the harness is an identity. Figma's remote MCP server, the only one that gives agents edit access to Figma files, now admits only allowlisted clients. Claude Code, Codex and a handful of others. Pi gets a 403. And here's the detail that tells you what kind of control this is. A developer who built a workaround documented that Figma's registration endpoint checks exactly one thing, the client name string. Send "Claude Code" in parentheses "figma" and you get a 200. Anything else, 403. So the workaround package simply tells Figma it's Claude Code, and the consent screen says so. The top comment called whitelisting "against the spirit of MCP." The honest counter came right under it: "Open MCP basically kills your product in my opinion, you can't charge for any feature the LLM can do itself." And the reply to that: whitelisting just accelerates your obsolescence. Figma has its own agent, billed in Figma credits. The gate isn't about safety. It's about whose loop gets to touch the canvas.

The third answer comes from the platform owner, and it is about safety. Apple posted a two-paragraph developer notice titled "Updates to Full Disk Access in macOS." Full Disk Access, Apple writes, "largely sidesteps these controls in order to allow backup apps to function properly," and some developers are using it in ways that expose "files, mail, messages, and even browsing history" without users' full understanding. New controls are coming so that granting it requires "very explicit user action." And the line that matters: "As AI agents become increasingly capable and autonomous, the risks associated with this level of access will grow substantially." TechCrunch notes this landed days after a columnist reported Meta's Muse agent on Mac knew the contents of their private messages, which Meta disputes. That's the operating system vendor announcing that the harness is now its problem too.

Now the counter-thesis, and it's the most intellectually interesting item of the week. "Context Language Models," from Rulin Shao, Luke Zettlemoyer, Pang Wei Koh and colleagues at the University of Washington and Meta Superintelligence Labs, with Nathan Lambert among the authors. The announcement tweet called it the Bitter Lesson for context management, and its last bullet was three words: no harness.

The mechanism is almost embarrassingly simple, which is why I like it. Every harness today decides for the model what to remember: compact at a length threshold, summarize every turn, offload to a tool. Earlier work gradually let the model decide when to compact, or what to retrieve, but always from a menu a human wrote. The CLM move is to delete the menu. Mirror the model's live context into a file, give the model write access with bash, and sync every edit back into what it sees on the next turn. The context stops being a log that only grows and becomes an artifact the model curates.

And the model did things nobody designed. On an Erdős optimization problem it built a compact orchestrator state block — budget used, slots busy, dead ends, next steps — and edited it in place while running subagents. On a deep-research benchmark it wrote little loops that collapsed old irrelevant search results down to one line each. It even invented a new chat role, alongside the standard roles, for its own internal notes. Zero-shot, on existing models, it beat the strongest harness baseline on that deep-research benchmark by eleven point four percent with about a fifth less compute, and on a twelve-hour software-optimization benchmark it scored five percent higher with fifty-nine percent less compute.

The number I'd actually carry around is a smaller one. When they trained a nine-billion-parameter Qwen model with reinforcement learning to manage its own context, accuracy went from twenty-eight point eight to forty-two point five. But against a Codex-style summary harness trained with the same recipe, the margin was zero point four points. The win was mostly efficiency: thirty-eight point eight percent fewer flops. So the honest read isn't that the hand-written harness was leaving accuracy on the table. It's that once you train, the model finds a cheaper route to the same place. Hacker News found both costs immediately. One commenter worried about attention budget: do you want your agent solving its own memory crisis, or solving the task? Another pointed out the practical killer: if the model keeps editing the middle of its own prefix, your cache hit rate collapses, and you can't run this efficiently on a commercial API. The authors know; they built a serving trick that reuses cached states past the edit point and report thirty-five percent less server-side compute. But notice where that leaves us. Removing the harness from the client means putting more of it into the server.

For your world, Ollie, this is the paper to read if you run long agentic literature or analysis jobs. The emergent tracker behavior is exactly what a lab-notebook agent should do, and the ContextBench diagnostic separates context management from reasoning, which is the confound in most agent evaluations.

To the money, where a similar story played out in fast-forward. Two weeks ago TypeSafe's Jev created the decision-model category, small models that return calibrated choices instead of text. This week Amazon open-sourced Strands Decider, a two-billion-parameter version that started as a distinguished engineer's homebrew project, and Cloudflare released Clef, open weights, Apache license, built on frozen Qwen backbones with low-rank adapters and a routing head. On the benchmark table Cloudflare built from Jev's own decision index, a Clef model wins seven of ten rows; Jev keeps the benchmark for deciding when to call a tool, and on TypeSafe's own workflow suite it still wins agent-trace observability. The top question on Hacker News was how so many people built these within weeks. One answer: "It's not a 'new paradigm', it's a low-hanging fruit that's been lying around for years." Another, less kind: if everyone else can spin up your product in under a month, there probably wasn't much product there.

TypeSafe's CEO, Diogo Almeida, told TechCrunch: "I get that people think it's a gold rush, but they might be underestimating the difficulty of making the models actually smart." He might be right; Amazon's Marc Brooker made the same point from the other side, saying the hard part is pushing accuracy and calibration without degrading what makes it general purpose. But read Cloudflare's post to the end and you see where the money actually is. The model is the free sample. The product is a pipeline: route your AI traffic through their gateway, and it becomes a dataset; generate rollouts on their GPUs; score them in their sandboxes; train with a new Trainer service; redeploy on their edge. The most useful comment in the thread said Jev-like models aren't much use unless you can fine-tune them, and Cloudflare is offering the fine-tuning loop on traffic it already sees. The weights are commoditized in a fortnight. The labeled-decision flywheel is not.

Which brings me to the deep cut, and it ties everything together. On Latent Space yesterday, swyx and Vibhu sat down with Alex Zhang, the MIT PhD student behind Recursive Language Models and KernelBench. Swyx teed up the harness question, and Zhang refused the framing.

"To be honest, I think all of them are the same. Most of the design decisions or, like, the design choices around these harnesses are the same." Claude Code, Codex, Pi, Oh My Pi. His definition is the cleanest I've heard: "a harness is a very opinionated program over how you want a language model to be form fit over a problem." And the dominant opinion, the one everybody trains around, he calls "trajectory as a prompt": keep the whole rollout as the model's context, subagents or not. Once models are good enough, he says, "the only benefits between these different harnesses is just cost, for the most part."

Then the turn. The harness shouldn't be judged on convenience; it should be judged on generalization. "An RLM is basically just a harness design where the only tool in the harness is code," with the option for the model to call itself as a subagent, and the context living in a code environment, which could be a file system. Two tasks that look different to a regular agent produce wildly different trajectories, but under this design they decompose into the same program, because each subagent is solving an easier local piece. His phrase for the result: "every individual language model call over the course of this task is in distribution, even if the entire task itself is out of distribution." Hence his thesis: "we should rethink what the role of the harness is, because harnesses can actually greatly increase the generalization capability of your model."

Third step, and this is the one I'd underline. He argues that pre-training scaling laws only hold because architecture choices are stable right now — change the architecture and the power law looks very different — "And it's like the same thing with harnesses." We've measured scaling for one harness shape. Nobody has run the experiment for others, and he says it needs teams dedicated to post-training at scale on different harness designs.

Then the endgame. "What we think of as a language model, like the thing that we query, might actually be like a swarm or like a scaffold or like some Weird harness design that scales very well, but the user just doesn't see it." Swyx priced the extreme case: OpenAI's agent swarm run, ten thousand agents in eighty-eight hours, a hundred and thirty billion output tokens, roughly forty million dollars at public pricing. Zhang's reaction: "Surprisingly, actually, like, less than I thought." And then the self-check: "I'm. Fairly certain that, like, 95% of the swarm is entirely useless," or in his words, "You're just burning tokens."

Here's the takeaway I'd hold, Ollie. Put Zhang next to the CLM paper and they look like opponents — one says design better harnesses, the other says no harness — but the CLM authors cite his work and draw the line precisely: recursive models decide what to read into context; context language models decide how the live context itself is kept. Both are moving the same decision from a human-written loop into something the model learns. Zhang's version moves it into a code environment; the CLM version moves it into the weights. And that's exactly why the commercial fights got loud this week. Ronacher says the harness must follow what the models were trained on. Figma is gating its canvas on a string that names a harness. Apple is gating the disk. If Zhang is right that the next scaling curve is in harness design, then the companies fighting over who owns the loop aren't fighting over a user interface. They're fighting over the next training target.

The one thing to watch: Apple's "very explicit user action" doesn't have a spec yet. When the actual Full Disk Access flow ships, look at whether it distinguishes a backup app from an agent, and whether an agent can ask for the permission mid-task or only at install. That design will decide how much of the agent loop on a Mac lives with the developer, and how much lives with Apple.
