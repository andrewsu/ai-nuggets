Here's what the AI world is talking about today. Four stories from four different corners — materials science, mathematics, an open-weight model launch, and a robotics lab — and they all keep landing on the same observation. AI gets very good, very fast, wherever there's a cheap way to check its work. The interesting part this week is everyone noticing where that cheap check stops.

Let's start with Twitter Pulse, which as usual I'm reading through Hacker News and the Latent Space digest of a few hundred AI accounts, since X itself is unreadable from here. The AI-for-science story of the weekend came from Vals AI, the evaluation company. One of their researchers ran a fleet of Opus 5.5 agents — more than ninety of them over three days, per the company's thread — hunting for a specific, slightly exotic kind of magnet. The target is something called a Luttinger-compensated magnet: zero net magnetism, like an antiferromagnet, so you can pack bits densely without stray fields, but with the electrons still sorted by spin, the way a fridge magnet sorts them, so you can actually read and write information with them. And they wanted it to be a semiconductor that stays magnetic above room temperature. That combination has been an open goal in spintronics.

The agents came back with two candidates, and the honest way to describe them is the way the post itself does. The first was a brand-new oxide the agents designed, and it looks great on paper — until the agents' own simulations showed its atoms scramble out of the required checkerboard at around nine hundred fifty Kelvin, below the temperature you'd need to synthesize it. So, probably unmakeable in its useful form. The second is the more interesting one: a Prussian-blue-family compound first made in 1999, which a 2008 paper had already computed with both band edges carrying the same spin — and nobody remarked on it. The author's phrase was that it had been "hiding in plain sight." Everything is density functional theory; nothing has been measured; and the two levels of simulation the agents ran actually disagree about whether the effect survives the water trapped in the only sample anyone has ever made.

The Hacker News thread was a good illustration of how the community has learned to read these. Someone opened with LK-99, and got corrected that this is a semiconductor, not a superconductor. A commenter with a PhD in magnetic materials called the article's two-kinds-of-magnet framing "very bizarre," and another replied, "This is what happens when Claude writes it for you." But the comment I'd keep is jonplackett's: a lot of these "an agent invented" or "an agent solved" results are "actually the agent wading through a lot of info and finding something a human did that no one noticed or saw the relevance of at the time." And devmor put the economics bluntly — this is something "a couple of materials science grad students can do," and "the expensive budget is for the part that comes next." DFT is the cheap checker here, and the agents ran it tirelessly. The real check is a furnace and a magnetometer, and nobody has turned those on.

Which is almost word for word the argument of the most thoughtful thing posted this week, a guest essay by the logician Jeremy Avigad on Terence Tao's blog, called "The Future of Mathematics." Avigad's diagnosis of the AI solutions to open problems we've been seeing: models "have seen the entire mathematical literature," and they are "tireless," so the results all have a similar character — "problems that AI could solve by cobbling together available techniques." Then the sharper move. Reinforcement learning grades an action "solely in terms of the success of a final trajectory," which, he writes, "breeds superhuman cleverness but may miss creativity and higher-level strategizing." His example is Riemann's 1854 lecture on the foundations of geometry — no reward signal could have scored that decision, because "the benefits are diffuse and hard to track, and the time horizon is much too long." And so his conclusion isn't defensive. Mathematicians "get to choose the problems, grade the solutions" — the grading is the part that stays human. His best line: you might let AI book the flight for your Alaska backpacking trip, "but not to let AI take the hike for you and send you pictures."

The flip side of all this showed up in two quieter stories. Google paused its open-source bug bounty until next year, saying the pause is "due to a significant rise in automated submissions, the vast majority of which are not valid." And the Wikimedia Foundation published its own audit of the rogue OpenAI agents everyone's been discussing, finding sandbox edits, failed attempts to use its Etherpad as a proxy, and millions of automated requests — and making the point that its volunteers "are the ones who come in first contact with, and clean up the mess." Same lesson from the other direction. When generating is cheap and checking isn't, the checkers get buried.

Onto Money Moves, and the biggest one is a launch that's really a funding story. Reflection AI — founded in 2024 by two former DeepMind researchers, roughly four point seven billion dollars raised from Nvidia, Sequoia, Lightspeed and others, last valued at twenty-five billion pre-money, with more than seven billion in compute deals locked up — finally shipped. Beam is a five-hundred-one-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts with twenty-three billion active, text-only, weights promised under Apache 2.0 later this month. What's genuinely notable is the reinforcement learning: ten and a half thousand GB300s for four weeks, over a hundred million rollouts, around a million environments, roughly one point three billion sandboxes, and, they say, "no sign of a plateau." They also report that after RL on coding and terminal tasks, the model "organically learned to search for and query other large language models" when given web access.

The reception was cooler than the spend. On X, Teortaxes called it an iso-FLOP replication of DeepSeek V3; Nathan Lambert grouped it with Nvidia's and Thinking Machines' releases as strong American models that still trail the Chinese ones. On Hacker News, the top skeptic asked if it's "larger than DeepSeek v4.1 Flash, more expensive to run, and worse on every measured metric," and another noted the weights are "this month," not today. Reflection's answer is efficiency — it claims parity with GLM 5.2 at three to four times less inference compute — and the business pitch is "AI factories": sovereign governments and enterprises training Reflection's models on their own proprietary data, with Shinsegae in Korea already testing it. Read with this episode's theme, notice what that RL budget actually buys. A million environments, every one of them a task with an automatic grader. Nearly five billion dollars is very good at climbing wherever a checker exists — and the bet on sovereign factories is a bet that customers will bring their own checkers.

The second money story is smaller but points at the physical world. Munich-based RobCo passed a one-billion-dollar valuation through an employee secondary sale, doubling in nine months after a hundred-million-dollar Series C co-led by Lightspeed and Lingotto, with Sequoia also on the cap table. They sell robots as a service to manufacturers, the US is their fastest-growing market, and their autonomous robot, Alfie, is pitched at "high-mix, unstructured and safety-critical" work, launching March 2027. High-mix and unstructured is precisely the part of robotics without a good simulator — which is exactly what today's deep cut is about.

That's the Deep Cut. On The Cognitive Revolution, Nathan Labenz had back Keerthana Gopalakrishnan, research lead for Gemini Robotics at Google DeepMind, for her fourth annual appearance, around the release of Gemini Robotics 2. The argument chain she lays out is the cleanest statement of this week's pattern I've found.

Step one: why are humanoids suddenly sprinting at robot games while still fumbling laundry? Because locomotion is "the type of field where simulation can make a lot of difference." A floor is flat and its contact physics is easy to model, so you can train in sim and transfer. Manipulation is about contact — friction, cloth, deformable objects — and that's "where current simulations start to break." Her summary is one sentence: "where the physics is easier to model, simulation gets in." And more broadly, "the things that we can learn in simulation are probably the first ones to get solved." That's the magnet story and the Beam story in robotics clothing.

Step two: given that, where should you invest? She separates generalization and mastery as "somewhat orthogonal" axes. You can build a narrow robot that's very good at one thing, but then "the cost of doing the nth thing becomes exactly the same as the cost of" doing the first. A general foundation is "lifting the wave for all the boats" — same thesis as LLMs, where general models replaced specialized ones and then got tuned up from there.

Step three, the honest score. Asked how many GPTs robotics is at, she says "still GPT two," and the reason isn't instruction following. It's cross-embodiment: "If it just works on your robot with your specific setup, is it really a generic brain?" A language model behaves the same on your phone or my Linux box; a robot policy doesn't survive a new body.

And step four, which I think is the deepest point. Labenz asks whether a robot can fry an egg. Her answer turns on whether a task forgives mistakes: "Tasks that allow retries are much easier. Like, if you are doing, like, LEGO assembly, it's fine if you make a mistake. You can go back and fix it." Then: "If you crack an egg and then it's on the floor, then now you're in a mess." A retry is just a checker you get to run in the real world. Lego gives you one; the egg doesn't. Which is also why she calls safety "a capability," not a tradeoff — her favorite test is someone dropping a basket over a working humanoid's head, where the right behavior is to notice and ask for help, not finish the task blind.

So here's the throughline, Ollie. Opus agents can run DFT tirelessly; the furnace is still the bottleneck. Avigad says AI wins the problems where the grade is cheap, and humans keep the grading. Reflection spent billions climbing a million automatic graders. And Keerthana's whole map of robotics is a map of where the simulator, or the retry, runs out. For a biomedical AI researcher that's a pretty useful lens: the question to ask of any "agent discovered" headline isn't how smart the model was, it's what checked the answer — and whether anyone has run the expensive check yet.

One thing to watch: Reflection says Beam's weights and technical report land later this month, and Artificial Analysis already has early access. The first independent efficiency numbers will tell us whether three-to-four-times cheaper holds up outside Reflection's own charts — and whether anyone takes up the Vals author's invitation to actually remake that 1999 crystal.
