## Script

Here's what the AI world is talking about today, and for once there is no ambiguity. Two stories held the top of every feed for eighteen hours, and one of them is the most consequential thing that has happened to the practice of research mathematics in a long time.

The headline is that OpenAI says an internal model resolved the Navier–Stokes Millennium Prize problem. The story is not the proof. The story is a four-page statement from an NYU professor about how the proof came to exist, and a mathematician in Los Angeles quietly building the general theory of what just happened to his field.

Three documents, and reading them in the wrong order gives you the wrong story.

Document one went up Monday night. Tristan Buckmaster at Courant and Levent Alpöge, who works at Anthropic, released finite-time blowup with a smooth forcing term for three equations — incompressible porous media, Boussinesq, and three-dimensional incompressible Euler — all formalized in Lean. Buckmaster is careful about credit in a way worth pausing on. The program isn't theirs. It belongs to Diego Córdoba and Luis Martínez-Zoroa, who spent years building forced-blowup constructions with rough forcing; what Buckmaster and Alpöge did was push it to smooth forcing and to Euler with heavy help from language models. In his words, in view of this body of work, he believes Martínez-Zoroa deserves a Fields Medal.

Terence Tao read it and explained the mechanism, which is genuinely beautiful. You iteratively add small, localized, high-frequency corrections to an existing solution, except almost no feedback runs from the high frequencies back down into the low ones. Instead the low-frequency components exponentially amplify the high-frequency ones, which take over the dynamics at exactly the right moment before blowup so the next iteration can proceed. For Boussinesq that collapses to a remarkably simple ordinary differential equation, wrapped in seventy-six pages of technical work.

The timeline is what to hold onto. For most of a year progress was slow. Then on the fifteenth of August they got smooth-forcing blowup for Boussinesq and Euler. Buckmaster says the first machine-generated proof Alpöge sent him was the most horrendous he has ever read. They verified it in Lean on the twenty-second, and have been working around the clock since to understand their own proof and make it readable. He apologizes for the writeups and calls the Euler one, quoting, AI slop. He had planned to say on release that the results are not the important thing — the important thing is that a mathematician and a language model can now do all of this in a month, and that this is a Deep Blue and Kasparov moment for how we train students, assign credit, referee, and decide what is worth one human life's attention.

He never got to say that, because of document two.

On the third of September, with a rumor circulating that Anthropic had solved a major open problem, and with Alpöge tipped off that word of their progress had reached OpenAI, Buckmaster emailed a prominent mathematician there to explain this was a personal collaboration, not an institutional one, and that he pays for the tools out of his own research funds. Three days later he was asked onto a call that same afternoon. Sébastien Bubeck joined. Alpöge was not on it.

On that call he was told an internal OpenAI model had produced a proof of finite-time blowup for forced Navier–Stokes, about a hundred pages, which he has not seen. He was shown a prompt and told the model had simply been given the problem statement. Over the course of the call, as team members fed Bubeck corrections over internal chat, it emerged that an entire team had been working on it, that easier problems including Euler were tried first, that even the prompt he'd been shown had itself been written by prompting Codex, and that an enormous amount of compute had been used. He asked when the first prompt was sent. Eventually it was agreed: within the past few days, after word of their work had reached OpenAI. He asked whether the model had been trained on, or had access to, their Codex sessions — into which they had been putting every draft for the whole project. He was told the model did not look up user data. He asked again, about training, and did not get an answer.

Two proposals followed. One, they post Euler and OpenAI posts Navier–Stokes the next day. Two, Buckmaster alone writes up the Navier–Stokes result acknowledging OpenAI's model — and Bubeck, per Buckmaster, twice said he wanted Alpöge removed from authorship because Alpöge works at Anthropic. Buckmaster declined both and said he would go public. He reports the reply: why would you ruin your career? He answered that he is an academic and asked why going public would ruin it. The reply he reports is, if you don't want me to be nice, then I don't have to be nice.

Bubeck has called the allegations false and inflammatory. His fuller account says Alpöge reached out unprompted first, that the career remark was made in frustration and retracted immediately on the same call, that he never asked to strip Alpöge of authorship of Alpöge's own work — only that it would be simpler if Alpöge weren't an Anthropic employee, in the context of Buckmaster leading a rewrite of OpenAI's proof — and that he himself received a barrage of unfounded accusations and a threat to go to the press. He maintains neither the researchers nor the agents saw the other team's work before public release.

Document three is OpenAI's post, and read after the other two it becomes a remarkable artifact, because it confirms nearly all the load-bearing facts. Since the twenty-eighth of August they have been training an internal model significantly more capable than GPT-6 Astra. On the first of September they heard rumors two Millennium problems had been resolved, and launched an effort in response. They ran coordinating agent groups with a cached internet and code execution, prompting different groups with different variants of the problem statement. The group that got Navier–Stokes involved on the order of ten thousand concurrent agents. Across all problems, four point nine million messages and about three hundred billion output tokens — at Astra's published output price, somewhere between fifteen and twenty-two million dollars of tokens, and nobody outside OpenAI knows what the internal model actually costs. And then this sentence, which is theirs: while unlikely, we cannot rule out that de-identified data derived from their usage of our products helped improve our models.

Now the technical caveat almost nobody is stating clearly. Fefferman's official Clay statement offers four statements and asks for a proof of any one of them, explicitly — his phrase — to give reasonable leeway to solvers while retaining the heart of the problem. In statements A and B, the ones asserting smoothness, the forcing term is required to be identically zero. In C and D, the breakdown statements, you are allowed a smooth forcing function. OpenAI says it established C and also D. By the official rules, that resolves the Millennium problem, and OpenAI says it does not intend to claim the prize. But C is not the logical negation of A. You can drive a fluid to infinite speed by pushing on it forever and still not know whether an unforced fluid stays smooth. The question everyone actually cares about is untouched. A leeway clause written in the year 2000 to help human solvers turned out to be the door a ten-thousand-agent swarm walked through.

Which brings us to the second thing the community is talking about — same person, three days running.

On the fifth of September Tao posted an eight-part counterfactual that I think is the sharpest writing about AI and science this year. He takes the real history of bounded gaps between primes. Goldston, Pintz and Yıldırım get a conditional bound in 2005. Zhang in 2013. Polymath8 grinds it to four thousand six hundred eighty. Maynard, then a graduate student, invents a different method that November and drops it to six hundred; by April 2014 it's two hundred forty-six, and the Maynard sieve becomes a textbook technique with applications everywhere. Then he reruns the tape with 2026-style benchmark-chasing AI companies present in 2005. Late August, multiple companies race to release unconditional versions. Within a month, millions of dollars of compute, the bound stabilizes in the low triple digits, and the problem is declared saturated. Nobody digests the output — no preprint, no paper, nothing taught in a classroom. Around 2010 one of the few people left in the area sifts the hundreds of machine-generated pages and teases out the idea we call the Maynard sieve, and keeps it private, because publishing an insight now triggers another frenzy. In 2012 they fail at twin primes and abandon it unpublished. In 2013, Zhang is still an adjunct lecturer. Maynard switches fields.

The punchline is that in that timeline there is more numerical progress on the bound by 2026 than in ours. The metric goes up. The field is gone.

And the trigger was real: Stadlmann improved two hundred forty-six to two hundred forty on the thirty-first of August, and on the third and fourth of September various AI companies raced to announce their own improvements on social media. Two days later Tao went back to his own Equational Theories Project — twenty-two million implications in universal algebra — and pointed at a note buried in section fourteen of the report. There were cases where an implication stumped the humans, forced them to invent new techniques for constructing infinite counterexamples, and was later found by an automated prover to have a small finite counterexample. If the machine had gone first, the technique would not exist. His line: for the first time in the history of mathematics, open problems in pure mathematics are being mined as a non-renewable resource.

Monday night he generalized it, and the analogy is what traveled. A region can suffer a critical shortage of drinking water while surrounded by an ocean. You can generate infinite open problems — the ten-to-the-ten-to-the-tenth digit of pi — and almost none are worth attention. Knowing which ones are requires reading the difficulty landscape of a field, and every tool that lowers difficulty also flattens that landscape. Normally a tool compensates by extending your reach and creating a new frontier. What's different now, he argues, is that there's no visible boundary between AI-feasible and AI-hard — partly because the technology moves so fast, and partly, his words, because of the refusal of AI companies to disclose their negative results or reveal the process by which they got their solutions. So the scarce resource is no longer the solution. It's the identification of a promising problem. Quoting him: even the rumor of someone working on a problem can now trigger a massive amount of AI-powered effort to flatten it before the original research project has time to reach its full potential.

That sentence is the whole week. The rumor was the prompt.

His conclusion isn't prohibition, which he thinks is infeasible. It's standards — designate classes of problems where a raw solution, with no accompanying analysis of why the problem was hard, has negligible or even negative value. The way a modern food bank stopped accepting anything technically edible and published standards instead. And the cleanest version, from the fifth: rather than competing to be first to announce a solution to an open problem, compete to be first to announce a new mathematical insight.

The best pushback in his replies came from a commenter arguing that flattening the difficulty landscape isn't the same as flattening the mathematical landscape — the construction is full of objects nobody has studied yet, and calling this a consumed problem confuses mathematical worth with the privilege of being the solver. The one that lands hardest came from an early-career mathematician: publication inflation plus radical uncertainty about how work will be evaluated is already pushing people out before tenure, and a generational gap does more damage than any flattened problem.

Quickly, the money, because it rhymes. Cognition raised two billion dollars Monday at a forty-eight billion valuation, up from twenty-six billion in May, with run-rate revenue going from four hundred ninety-two million to roughly nine hundred million over those four months. Andreessen Horowitz and Accel led as new investors, Nvidia among the thirty-plus firms that piled in. Scott Wu's framing is the interesting part: Cognition is an independent agent lab that can choose and combine whichever models suit the work. In a week where the two biggest labs are fighting over authorship of what their models produced, there's a company worth forty-eight billion dollars whose entire pitch is that it doesn't matter which model did it. The agent layer is repricing faster than the model layer.

Now the deep cut, and it's the best thing I read this week precisely because it was recorded before any of this happened.

Two weeks ago Latent Space published an hour-and-twenty-three-minute conversation with Anima Anandkumar, Bren Professor at Caltech. The title is her thesis: we have foundation models for language, not for physics. The argument is tight. Continuous physical systems — weather, fusion, fluid and heat flow — are large, chaotic and fundamentally multi-scale, and they resist the scaling recipe twice over. First, the data isn't there; open datasets in these domains run to tens or hundreds of thousands of examples. Second, and this is the part that should stop you, the resolution physics demands blows up sequence length. Her words: if each dimension is even a few hundred grid points, which is where industrial scale starts, you're talking hundreds of billions to even a trillion context length, so forget ever having a transformer for anything of this scale — all of the world's compute will not be enough.

Her answer isn't more tokens, it's structure. Neural operators, where you stop modeling a grid and model a function evolving across scales, so you can build in physical priors. The canonical demonstration is weather. The Earth is a sphere; run a model on a rectangular grid and it blows up fast; move to spherical harmonics, the natural basis for the problem, and it stays stable long enough to roll out months instead of days. That's the spherical Fourier neural operator behind FourCastNet 3. In fusion, a few thousand samples predict plasma disruptions a million times faster than simulation. Her summary of the method: all of the things that work with deep learning, let's take them, but make them a bit more principled.

Here's why I'm putting this next to the Navier–Stokes story. On the seventh of September — the day before OpenAI's announcement — Anandkumar's group posted evidence that the Euler equations admit a self-similar finite-time blowup on all of three-dimensional space, with no boundary and no forcing. Hou and Chen got Euler blowup with a boundary in 2022, because a boundary confines the singularity and makes the analysis tractable. This has neither. And the method is exactly what she described on the podcast. Start with a physics-informed neural network, whose standard failure mode on this problem is collapsing to a trivial solution, and nudge it into interesting regions with carefully chosen constraints. Then refine that into a spline representation so you can get certified tight bounds — an approximate solution is useless, you need bounds for the stability analysis.

Their own broader-implications paragraph draws the line explicitly: the central computational ingredient is physics-informed optimization, with the governing equations built into the objective, and language models lack that physical grounding. Tao, independently, called their paper relatively AI-light compared with the other recent results, restricted largely to secondary tasks like literature review and Lean formalization — and also said the rigorous stability demonstration is still lacking and may require enormous further effort. So it's evidence, not a proof.

But look at what one week did to one equation. Two people and a year, using several commercial models, got forced blowup for Euler and Boussinesq with a Lean certificate and a proof they describe as slop and are still trying to understand. One lab, ten thousand agents, eighty-eight hours and three hundred billion tokens, got the forced Navier–Stokes statement the official rules happen to accept. And one academic group with a physics-informed network and no language models in the loop got a candidate for the unforced problem — the one everybody actually wants — with certified bounds and an honest statement that the hard part isn't done.

Ollie, the takeaway that transfers to your work is Anandkumar's, and it isn't that language models are weak. It's that they have no grounding in the object of study, so the only lever they offer is more of them. Where the structure of your domain is known — spherical geometry, conservation laws, the constraints of a physical system, the geometry of a molecule — encoding it beats scaling it, and it beats it by orders of magnitude in compute. The unforced candidate came from the group that spent a decade building the inductive bias. The forced result came from the group that spent twenty million dollars of tokens in a weekend. Both are real. Only one is repeatable on your budget.

One coda that makes the week stranger. Late Tuesday night an Anthropic researcher named Jacob Coxon, previously at OpenAI, resigned publicly, saying both companies are gambling with our lives and racing straight to self-improving superintelligence, and that the people building AI earnestly believe it could kill us all by the end of the decade. Evan Hubinger, Anthropic's staff lead on alignment, backed him publicly without quitting, putting the odds above ten percent within the decade and saying there's no plan yet for the superintelligence case. Seven hundred thirty-five comments on Hacker News, and almost none of the argument is about the substance. It's about whether to believe anything a frontier lab employee says while their employer heads toward an IPO. That's the hidden cost of this week's other story. When a lab's public account and a first-hand account of the same events don't match, every subsequent claim gets read as positioning. Even the warnings.

The one thing to watch tomorrow. OpenAI has published a claim, a Lean formalization and a process description — but not the hundred-page proof and not the prompts, and Tao's central complaint is precisely about undisclosed process. So watch whether the proof and the prompt log go up, and watch whether anyone outside OpenAI reads those hundred pages and reports what's inside. Because the two endings to this week are very different. Either the mathematics community digests this construction and extracts something teachable, which is Tao's whole ask — or the artifact sits there verified and unread, and the next person with a promising idea about a hard problem quietly decides not to tell anybody.
