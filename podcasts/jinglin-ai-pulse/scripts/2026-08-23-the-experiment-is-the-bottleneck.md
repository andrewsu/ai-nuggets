## Script

Here's what the AI world is talking about today. Somebody finally ran the recursive self-improvement experiment at a scale where the answer means something, and the answer is not the one either camp wanted.

Prime Intellect put eighteen frontier models on the same open research problem, gave each of them eight H-two-hundreds and up to eight days of fully autonomous wall clock, and let them hill-climb. A hundred fifty-three runs. Everything published — traces, scratchpads, reasoning streams, monitor reports, the harness. And the verdict splits. Not one of the eighteen models produced a genuinely new method; every winning ingredient was already in the literature. And yet the spread between best and worst was enormous, and it came from somewhere nobody was really looking.

The task is the nano-G-P-T speedrun, which is a lovely choice because it has a scoreboard and almost no room to hide. You train a hundred twenty-four million parameter transformer and count how many steps it takes to reach a validation loss of three point two eight. The tuned baseline the agents start from takes three thousand two hundred ninety steps. The best human record, in an open pull request, is two thousand six hundred. So the whole gap is six hundred ninety steps, and the agents have to find all of it themselves. To claim a record, a model runs its recipe eight times on seeds it cannot touch, and a frozen verifier accepts only if the eight-run mean clears the bar with a margin that makes passing on luck roughly one in a thousand. The sandbox has no internet at all — which is deliberate, because in earlier experiments Prime Intellect gave agents a paper-search tool and found they over-anchored on existing pull requests. Cutting off the paper search made them slightly more creative. Sit with that one for a second.

Now the results. Claude Fable five, in Claude Code, closed eighty-one point seven percent of the gap to the human record — two thousand seven hundred twenty-six steps, over eight point seven days and eight hundred eleven experiments. Opus five closed fifty-three point six. Kimi K-three, from Moonshot, closed fifty-two point two and beat GPT-five-point-six Sol at thirty-five point nine — and Sol did that while burning two point nine billion tokens and twenty-eight thousand tool calls, an order of magnitude more than Fable spent. The bottom of the table is Kimi K-two-point-seven at seven point two percent. Same task, same node, same verifier. One model recovers four-fifths of the human progress and another recovers a fourteenth of it.

So where does the gap live? Prime Intellect's answer is the most useful thing in the writeup: the models all find the same ideas. What separates them is how they run experiments. Read that again if you work in a lab, because it is the opposite of the story we have been telling ourselves about research automation. Ideation was never the scarce input. Experimental hygiene was.

Three specifics. First, noise modeling. The rulebook handed every agent an estimate of the speedrun's run-to-run noise, and Prime Intellect admits the number they supplied was slightly too large. Sixty-two of about a hundred runs went and measured the noise themselves instead of trusting it, and those runs are concentrated at the top of the table. Forty-two went further and discovered something the rulebook never mentioned, on purpose: rerunning the exact same recipe on the exact same seed still moves the loss, because GPU kernels are not deterministic. That within-seed noise is much smaller than seed-to-seed noise — so a model that finds it can compare two recipes on a shared seed and resolve differences a normal screen cannot, at the same cost. Several models rebuilt their entire screening protocol around a fact about their own hardware that nobody told them.

Second, what a negative result actually licenses. A failed test tells you about one recipe in one context. Weak models did not get this. They killed whole families of ideas on a single seed, treated their own crashes as evidence the idea was bad, and threw away small gains that did not clear the bar alone — Grok four-point-five lost row normalization twice, not because it does not work, but because of its own scaling bugs. Strong models tested borderline results on three seeds and paid for eight only when their noise model said it was worth it. And critically, they went back: after every merge they re-ablated the whole stack and dropped what had stopped helping, and they revisited old negatives when the recipe changed, because something that did nothing before might matter now. Opus five reopened a beta-two question under a new recipe and it became a record. When Fable ran out of single knobs, it started testing pairs that were individually worse but jointly better — one late re-probe was worth thirty-one steps.

Third, and this is the one I'd put in front of every graduate student: sometimes the right research decision is not to spend the GPU run. DeepSeek V-four-Pro screened candidate preconditioner update rules on a small synthetic gradient process on CPU, watched its own diagnostic come back impossible, revised it, caught its own normalization erasing the quantity it was measuring, and killed the branch before ever touching a training run.

Now the honesty, and Prime Intellect deserves credit for printing it. This benchmark has a lot of variance. Two runs of the same model in the same harness land about fifty-four steps apart at twenty-four agent-hours — and at twenty-four hours Fable is at three thousand ten, Opus at three thousand forty-five, Sol Pro at three thousand one hundred. That top-of-table ordering is inside the noise band. What is emphatically outside it is the multi-day result. So read tiers, not a leaderboard, and notice the tiers only separate when you let the runs go long. On Hacker News a METR co-author added the caveats you'd want: possible contamination from models having trained on this very baseline, and several runs that had not plateaued when they ended, so none of these are ceilings. For calibration, Intology's nano-G-P-T Bench, at about two point seven days per agent, found agents recovering less than ten percent of five months of human progress. Give them eight days and one recovers eighty percent. Horizon length is doing enormous work here.

Second thing the community is chewing on, and it is the same fact from the opposite end. A post titled "Why your local LLM feels dumber than it is" hit the Hacker News front page today at three hundred forty-four points. The author, posting as thr3e on the Level1Techs forums, did the experiment almost nobody does: hold the weights fixed and vary only the serving stack.

One model, Qwen three-point-six twenty-seven-B, official sixteen-bit checkpoint, one Blackwell card, eager execution, CUDA graphs off. The prompt is the part I like — roughly a hundred thousand tokens captured from a real agentic workstream with real tool calls, chosen because it appears in no benchmark and no calibration set. Nobody could have benchmaxed it.

Change only the attention backend, among FlashAttention two, FlashInfer and Triton, and for the first several thousand tokens all three agree on every next token. Then, deep in the prompt, they start disagreeing — in clusters, tied to content, not smoothly with length. And the control matters: rerunning the same backend gave logits that were bit-for-bit identical, so the divergence is purely the matrix multiplies. Notice how that sits against the speedrun finding. Nondeterminism is not a law of GPUs; it is a property of a configuration. Prime Intellect's agents found it in multi-GPU training and weaponized it. This author engineered it away and could then attribute divergence exactly.

Then it gets practical. Quantize only the key-value cache and leave the weights alone, and enough top tokens flip during a tool call that sixteen-bit is fine, eight-bit eventually recovers, and four-bit never does. Then a five-way weight-quantization bakeoff whose ranking is not the one on the model cards: a community eight-bit release beat the first-party FP-eight, and Nvidia's own NVFP-four came dead last, around fifty percent top-one token flips by eighty-eight thousand tokens of context. Both NVFP-four and the four-bit AWQ build failed to close their tool calls and botched a Cisco command — the correct call was "show arp" and they ran "show run." Same weights. Same benchmark scores on paper. And the best correction in the thread was that most of the time when a local model feels dumb it is not the quant at all, it is the chat template, because repackagers drop the template metadata and your runtime silently falls back to a default. Same disease. There is a 复现 problem — fùxiàn, reproducibility — under every local eval number anyone reports, and it stays invisible until an agent tries to do something with consequences.

One more, quickly, because it landed yesterday at two hundred six points: the Model Context Protocol maintainers published a new roadmap, and two of its five priorities read as admissions. Agentic messaging primitives, because request-response was never the right shape for long-running work. And progressive tool discovery, because tool catalogs are now too large to put in a context window. The harness is a variable — the same Kimi K-three scored differently under two of them in the speedrun — and the protocol layer has started treating it like one.

To the money, where the signal this week is which constraint people are paying to escape. Starcloud raised two hundred fifty million dollars at a two point three billion dollar post-money valuation, five months after a hundred seventy million, led by Manhattan West Ventures with twenty-five million from Nvidia, plus Cisco, Benchmark and EQT. They put AI inference on satellites. But read what the money is for: the chief executive, Philip Johnston, says the primary cost driver has become securing launch capacity, that they need to book an enormous amount of launch against an eventual eighty-eight-thousand-spacecraft constellation, and that failing to book SpaceX capacity in twenty twenty-nine would be challenging — because Falcon nine retires by twenty twenty-eight and New Glenn, Vulcan and Neutron are not yet at volume. Watch the chain: the binding constraint went from chips, to power, and now for one company it is rocket seats.

The counterpoint is small and tells you about the application layer. Rillet raised a hundred million at a one billion dollar valuation, led by Iconiq with Sequoia and Andreessen — AI-native accounting aimed at ripping out NetSuite and Oracle, six hundred customers, revenue doubled last quarter. The detail is the process: the chief executive presented growth numbers at a routine board meeting, existing investors initiated afterward, and it closed in forty-eight hours. They were not raising.

For the deep cut, Latent Space published a long conversation on Friday with Joon Sung Park, now co-founder and chief executive of Simile, previously first author on the Smallville generative agents paper. It's framed as simulation being the new scaling law, but the argument that matters is about data, and it lands directly on everything above.

Park sorts behavioral data into three buckets: interviews, which give you texture; observational data like transactions, which give you base rates of what people do; and third, the one he calls most important, data that describes the causal mechanism — the why. In practice that means randomized controlled trials, and his reason is the line I'll be repeating. The world is our ground truth, but it happens once. A setup where everything is equal except one variable almost never occurs naturally, which is exactly why that data is both scarce and load-bearing. And he ties it to what customers want: decision-makers do not want to hear that sales will fall in two quarters, because the only response to a prediction is "wow, that sucks." They want to know what to change, and that is unanswerable from correlation at any scale of correlation.

Then the evaluation, which is the part a working scientist should steal. He brought over a thousand representatively sampled Americans into a virtual lab, spent two hours each collecting interview and behavioral data, sent them away for two weeks, and brought them back for a battery — behavioral economics games, Big Five, the General Social Survey, published randomized trials. The twins predicted how their source individuals would act. The reported figure is eighty-five percent, eighty-six in the current version of the paper, but the denominator is what makes it a real number. It is not raw accuracy. It is accuracy relative to how consistently the same human reproduces their own answers two weeks later. He built the noise floor into the metric — exactly what the top-tier speedrun models did on their own, and exactly what local eval numbers never do.

And then the uncomfortable finding. Frontier models are bad at this because of what they were optimized for. In Park's account, the labs collect data from professional programmers and scientists to build super rational, objective reasoning machines. Simile does not care about any of that: what they are trying to create, in his words, are models that are as dumb as I am — so that if I make some mistakes, the model has to make the same mistake. On general population questions frontier models come in around fifty to sixty percent by his measure; on the niche populations customers care about, down to twenty or thirty. Rationality is a liability when the thing you are modeling is not rational.

The last step closes the loop. To post-train on causal mechanisms they went to the Open Science Framework — the preregistration platform that exists because of the replication crisis, where you commit to your hypothesis before running the study so that a p-value under nought point nought five means what it claims. Tens of thousands of registered, professionally designed experiments. The infrastructure built to stop humans fooling themselves about their own results became the training corpus for models that predict human behavior. And his stated ambition is that within a few years one simulation will cost as much as training a foundation model — and that if one could resolve climate coordination, he would raise the money to run it today.

Ollie's takeaway is that these are one claim from three directions. The scarce input in AI research in twenty twenty-six is not ideas — eighteen frontier models generated the same ideas and none generated a new one. The scarce input is a trustworthy experiment. Prime Intellect's tiering is a measurement of noise modeling and re-ablation discipline wearing a capability eval's clothes. The serving-stack post shows your own numbers are a property of a software stack you never audited. And Park's method says that when the counterfactual does not exist in nature, you either pay to run the trial or build something that manufactures it — and either way you report against the noise floor, not against zero. For a lab that is one concrete habit: before you compare two conditions, spend the cheap run measuring how far apart two identical conditions land. The models that did that clustered at the top of the table.

Two things to watch. Prime Intellect says they are adding seeds and harnesses, so the near question is whether Fable's eighty-one point seven percent survives a second seed and whether the tier boundaries move when the variance shrinks. And the one I'd watch harder: all of this happened with the internet switched off and creativity going up. If the next iteration turns retrieval back on and the models get worse at research again, that is not a harness detail. That is a finding about what our agents are actually doing when we hand them the literature.
