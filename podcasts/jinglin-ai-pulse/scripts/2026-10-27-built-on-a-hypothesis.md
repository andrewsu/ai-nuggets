# Ollie's AI Pulse — 2026-10-27

## Script

Here's what the AI world is talking about today. Last episode ended on one thing to watch, Anthropic's public S-1, and on Monday it landed. Within hours, OpenAI posted something that pushed it off the top of the technical conversation: a claimed disproof of the Exponential Time Hypothesis, one of the load-bearing assumptions of theoretical computer science. Once you look closely, the two stories have the same shape. Each one is about a structure built on a hypothesis. One is hundreds of papers that took a conjecture for granted. The other is hundreds of billions of dollars of compute bought on the assumption that demand keeps compounding. In between, a dermatologist tests a quieter assumption, that general-purpose models carry over to specialist tasks. So today: built on a hypothesis.

Start with the math. OpenAI announced three results at once. In harmonic analysis, its models produced a proof of Sogge's local smoothing conjecture in every dimension, which would settle the Kakeya conjecture and Stein's restriction conjecture in all dimensions too. For context, Hong Wang and Joshua Zahl proved Kakeya in three dimensions only last year. In physics, it claims a rigorous existence proof for quantum chromodynamics and a unified completion of the Standard Model, with pure Yang–Mills existence, part of a Clay Millennium Prize problem, as a sub-result. And in computer science, a paper titled simply "The Exponential Time Hypothesis is False."

The release process changed, and Hacker News noticed. Three weeks ago we covered seven hundred nineteen manuscripts dumped at once, and the withdrawals that followed. This time OpenAI recruited outside "shepherds" and let each choose its own release strategy. The math result went out raw. The physics group, led by David Gross with Arthur Jaffe, Michael Douglas, Igor Klebanov and Nikita Nekrasov, chose to hold back the AI-produced artifacts and write the result up themselves. And the computer-science group is led by Ryan Williams of MIT and by Russell Impagliazzo and Ramamohan Paturi of UC San Diego. If those last two names ring a bell, they should. Impagliazzo and Paturi are the authors of the 2001 paper that defined the Exponential Time Hypothesis. The people who proposed the conjecture are now shepherding its refutation. Their conditions were that all the raw data go public and that OpenAI fund a conference on what this means for the field. OpenAI says it seeks no academic credit, and that the proof was formalized in Lean the day after the model found it.

So what is actually claimed? The hypothesis said that deciding whether a three-SAT formula has a satisfying assignment takes time exponential in the number of variables, two to the order of n, in the worst case. The paper gives a deterministic algorithm that does something harder than deciding. It counts every satisfying assignment, in time roughly two to the n divided by the square root of log n, using only polynomial memory. That's subexponential, but only barely. The intuition is pretty. Write the count as a sum, over every assignment, of a product of little polynomials, one per clause, each equal to one when its clause is satisfied. If you could split the variables into groups so that no clause touched two groups, you could count each group on its own and multiply, which is enormously cheaper. Real formulas don't split like that. So the algorithm introduces an extra parameter. At zero, every clause sees its original variables. At other chosen values, the groups only talk to each other through a small set of shared linear summaries, so the sum separates. Compute it at those easier points, then interpolate back to zero to recover the exact count.

The thread got calibrated fast. Many readers jumped to P versus NP, and several commenters, quoting the paper itself, pulled them back: a subexponential bound doesn't survive general polynomial reductions, so this does not make all of NP subexponential. A self-described SAT-solving expert, bsubs, said there are no immediate applications, because the results are "galactic." The real damage is downstream. The paper says it refutes not just ETH but its stronger cousins, including the Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis, and a whole fine-grained complexity literature used those as premises. Some of that is close to home for you. The quadratic lower bounds for longest common subsequence and dynamic time warping, close relatives of sequence alignment, were proved assuming the strong hypothesis. The paper notes that some of those bounds, including longest common subsequence, also follow from a weaker conjecture about orthogonal vectors that is still standing. One commenter asked whether this means self-attention can now run in less than quadratic time, since that lower bound was also conditional on the strong hypothesis. The correct reply was no: "the hypothesis implies quadratic" does not mean "no hypothesis implies faster." Losing the premise doesn't hand you the algorithm.

And the second-order discussion was about which fields fall next. One commenter asked whether the run of results in formally checkable fields reflects a real limitation in the scruffier sciences. The best reply was practical: the search harnesses rely on cheap correctness checks to throw out wrong ideas, and in biology the check is a wet-lab experiment that adds days per iteration. That's exactly the argument Alex Rives made in last week's deep cut.

Second story, and it's a biomedical one. A dermatologist ran a small, transparent experiment that reached the Hacker News front page this weekend. They took two hundred biopsy-proven lesions from the public MILK-ten-k dataset, each with a clinical photo and a dermoscopy image, and asked GPT-six Astra and Claude Fable five-point-one to pick one of six diagnoses. As a baseline, they fine-tuned Inception v3, a ten-year-old convolutional network, for about twenty minutes on sixty-eight thousand public images, with every photo of the test lesions and patients removed. The CNN named the right diagnosis for a hundred twenty-five of two hundred. The frontier models got eighty-four and eighty-five.

The detail that changes the take is the triage numbers. The language models were cautious almost to the point of uselessness. They kept ninety-seven and ninety-six of the hundred lesions that shouldn't be discharged, but they also discharged only thirty-two and twenty-two of the hundred harmless ones. The CNN, at its preset threshold, discharged seventy-six harmless lesions and also eighteen of thirty melanomas. Moved to the language models' level of caution, it kept ninety-eight or ninety-nine of the dangerous ones. The language models also failed in a telling way: each had a favorite answer. GPT-six Astra called seventy-five lesions basal cell carcinoma when only twenty-five were, including fourteen of the thirty melanomas. The author is upfront about the caveats. Thirty melanomas give wide error bars, and the dataset has been public since 2025, which could only have helped the language models.

The author explained the surprise in the thread: "I had assumed the general image interpretation abilities would transfer." That's the hypothesis being tested, and it didn't hold up. The top comment pointed out the practical lesson: "Just use the LLM to make a thing that's actually good at what you need it to do." Another commenter, who said they built the model currently leading the skin-lesion leaderboard, added a more interesting data point. Open medical image-language models like Google's MedSigLIP don't break into that leaderboard's top ten even when people adapt them. Their read was that a shared image-and-text representation helps with explaining and communicating a diagnosis, but not with making it.

Now the money. Anthropic filed its S-1 on Monday, to list on Nasdaq under the ticker A-N-T. The revenue line is the headline: forty-one million dollars in 2023, three hundred eighty-six million in 2024, four-point-six billion in 2025, and sixteen-point-three billion in just the first half of this year, up from one-point-two billion a year earlier. The second quarter alone was eleven-point-six billion, with about a billion dollars of operating income. The filing doesn't print a third-quarter number, but it says third-quarter revenue was eighty-six times the hundred ninety-seven million of late 2024, which works out to roughly seventeen billion. The eighty-billion-dollar net loss for the half is almost entirely an accounting mark: seventy-two billion of it is the rising fair value of old convertible notes as the company's valuation went up.

The number the filing itself leads with is efficiency: second-quarter revenue up thirteen hundred sixty-eight percent year over year, while compute and infrastructure spending grew three hundred percent. SemiAnalysis called it "likely the most efficient scaling of a business in the history of capitalism." Then look at what that efficiency has been used to justify. At least a hundred ten billion dollars committed to Amazon through 2036, non-cancelable. Up to a hundred twenty and a half billion for NVIDIA compute from SpaceX's AI subsidiary, the company formerly known as xAI, at about three point one billion dollars a month once fully delivered. Forty-five billion to Microsoft, and roughly forty-five and forty-one billion more to Nscale and Lambda. The top Hacker News comment, from trjordan, put the whole bet in one line: "Everything comes back to whether the demand is really there."

Two smaller disclosures are worth knowing. On governance, a company holding the seven co-founders gets fifty-point-one percent of the vote with no economic rights, and the Long-Term Benefit Trust elects a majority of the board, so public shareholders will own the economics and very little of the control. And in the risk factors, Anthropic says it recently delayed pre-training a new frontier base model by more than a month to harden its security against weight exfiltration. The investment case is a hypothesis about demand. The governance is a hypothesis about who should be trusted with the result.

Which brings us to the deep cut, and it fits today's lead almost too well. On Monday, Lex Fridman released what he called an emergency episode on AI and mathematics, with three mathematicians: Ankur Moitra, a theoretical computer scientist at MIT, Alex Kontorovich, a number theorist at Rutgers, and Steven Brunton, a fluid-dynamics researcher at the University of Washington. It was recorded after the earlier wave of AI claims on the Unique Games Conjecture and Navier–Stokes, but it never mentions the Exponential Time Hypothesis, which went public the same day the episode did. So listen to Moitra describing satisfiability: "we think that it takes exponential time, not that much faster than enumerating all possible assignments and checking them." Within hours, that was the claim on the table.

The argument worth keeping is Kontorovich's, and it comes in steps. First he proposes what he calls the solved axiom: suppose any problem a human could solve with AI's help, the AI could also solve alone. He doesn't believe it, but he assumes it to see what's left. Chess is his warning. Human-plus-engine teams lasted about ten seconds, he says, before the human's job became, in his words, "Yes, accept the first move." Second, even in that world, he argues pure mathematics isn't destroyed, because "the value of pure mathematics is that it lives in the heads of human beings." Third, and this is the sharpest line of the episode: "until five seconds ago, there was a near perfect correlation between the ability to solve new deep theorems and what we actually want as human mathematicians." What they actually wanted was understanding. Solving was the proxy, and the proxy has just come apart from the goal.

Moitra adds two things. One is a warning not to anthropomorphize. Looking through OpenAI's solutions, he says, "they just don't look like a human has solved those problems." The other turns human weakness into an argument. Because he can't hold that many papers in his head at once, "in order for me to reach that same finish line, maybe I have to develop more generalizable and abstractable ways of getting there." Limited working memory forces compression, and compression is what carries over to the next problem. Kontorovich adds a correction to the "convex hull" dismissal we've discussed on this show before. Borrowing from Tim Gowers, he calls it the "semigroup generated by" known moves: all the ways known moves can be chained together, which is a far bigger space than the word "hull" suggests. And Moitra tells a story that sticks. Hours before the Unique Games release, he asked Subhash Khot, who proposed that conjecture, whether he'd read a positive proof if one came out. Khot said, "No."

Here's the takeaway for you, Ollie. The ETH paper went through Lean, so its truth may get settled quickly. What Lean can't certify is the thing Kontorovich says mathematicians actually wanted, and that's why the shepherds exist. Biomedical AI has the same split, just less visibly. A model can be right about a lesion, a perturbation or a binding site without containing any abstraction you can carry to the next problem, and today's skin-cancer test is a reminder that being right on average isn't even guaranteed. When you design agents for science, ask them for the compression, not just the answer. Moitra's point is that the abstraction exists because someone with limited working memory needed it, and that need is still yours.

One thing to watch tomorrow: the first public statement from the Impagliazzo, Paturi and Williams group. When the people who wrote down a hypothesis say whether they accept its refutation, that's the verdict the field will act on. On the business side, Anthropic's listing is reportedly expected around November ninth.
