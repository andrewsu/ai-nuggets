Here's what the AI world is talking about today. Google shipped a frontier model that almost nobody can use. The loudest post on Hacker News yesterday was a coding agent explaining why it reversed its most famous design decision. A blog post with four hundred and thirty comments argued that the Western price war is being paid for by a Chinese lab's published architecture. And on the biggest AI podcast of the week, the man who runs computer use at OpenAI and the man who runs its API spent an hour answering a question about intelligence with answers about plumbing. Four unrelated stories, one question underneath all of them — and it isn't how smart the model is. It's what the model keeps.

Start with Argon. Google announced Gemini 4 Argon last night: one thousand three hundred and seventy-nine points, eight hundred and eighty-seven comments. The headline claims are real. Seventy-seven point nine percent on the hardest long-horizon software engineering benchmark, against roughly seventy-four for both Opus five-point-five and GPT-6 Astra. First on the Vals Index, which weights finance, coding, legal and tax work by each sector's contribution to U.S. GDP. By one count, first place on thirteen of nineteen published benchmarks.

And the internal results are the part you can't benchmax. In Google's open-source video decoding library, Argon agents replaced thirty-two thousand lines of hand-written vector code by running profile-guided experiments and reading the compiler's output, producing plain safe Rust that the compiler vectorizes on its own: two point seven times faster than the Rust port it started from, identical output. That's an agent doing what senior performance engineers do.

Now three places the headline doesn't survive contact with measurement. First, the flagship number. Google says it raised the output limit to an industry-leading one million tokens, up from sixty-four thousand. Fifteen-fold. Except Vals lists maximum output at two hundred and sixty-two thousand, and Artificial Analysis only reached a million through a new API feature called Long Decode Continuation, which pauses a long response and resumes it across calls. So the industry first isn't a bigger decode. It's a resume primitive. The limit didn't get raised — it got moved out of the model and into the API.

Second, the price. Two dollars per million input tokens, ten per million output, cached input at ninety-five percent off. But footnote one of Google's own post says that after the introductory period expires, four dollars per million input and twenty per million output will apply — no end date announced. Artificial Analysis measured one dollar ninety-nine per task at the discount, and three dollars ninety-eight at list price, which is worse than Astra's three dollars twenty-six. Their own note is the one to keep: the savings come from price, not efficiency. Argon averages sixty-two thousand output tokens per task against Astra's twenty-seven thousand. And under the pricing comment on Hacker News, hours before any of that was published, someone wrote: "watch it somehow use twenty times more tokens tho." The measurement was already in. Though in fairness, Vals measured Argon using about a quarter of Sonnet five-point-five's output tokens on their own index — different comparator, different suite, opposite conclusion. Which is the lesson. Tokens per task is not a model property; it's the model crossed with your workload.

Third, and this is the number I'd actually carry around. On Artificial Analysis's knowledge-reliability benchmark, Argon hallucinates on fifteen percent of items against Astra's fifty-one. But accuracy is fifty percent against Astra's sixty-three. That benchmark rewards correct answers, penalizes hallucinations, and applies no penalty for refusing — so what you're looking at is a model deliberately trained to abstain. Google bought a two-thirds cut in confabulation with thirteen points of coverage. For literature triage that's an obviously good trade. For an open-ended research question it may not be. Nobody led with that number, and it's the one that changes what you'd use the thing for.

What Hacker News actually spent eight hundred comments on wasn't safety or benchmarks. It was availability — and not for the model that just launched. Paying Workspace and Enterprise customers reported being stuck on Gemini three-point-six while free Gmail accounts get three-point-eight. One described a renewal that came in fifty percent higher because, quote, Gemini is included now, think of all the added value, and still ended up paying extra for Claude. The company that just posted state of the art on thirteen of nineteen benchmarks cannot get its previous generation to customers who are already paying.

Which brings me to the most intellectually interesting thing on the internet this week. A post called "The AI Race Just Got Awkward" — three hundred and eighty-five points, four hundred and thirty comments. Its argument: the sharp cache-read price cuts across the recent Western releases are the fingerprint of Western labs adopting cache compression that DeepSeek published for free, and nobody said thank you.

The architecture half is verifiable, so I went to the primary. DeepSeek's technical report from mid-September: five hundred and fifty-two billion parameters, million-token context, forty-five trillion training tokens, open checkpoints. Their framing of the problem is this entire episode's thesis, stated by the lab itself — the widespread adoption of long-horizon agents has made model workloads increasingly input-heavy, and the compute, storage and bandwidth demands of the cache are, their words, the primary bottleneck to further lowering deployment costs. Not reasoning. Not parameters. The cache.

So they went after it. An encoder-decoder design that activates sixteen billion parameters per token when generating but only eight billion when reading the prompt. Cross-layer cache reuse plus four-bit quantization, getting the live cache down to eight hundred and ninety bytes per token. And the intuition is lovely: layers get one of three roles. Some compute the global cache and pick which sparse positions to attend to. Some borrow that cache but run a cheap scorer to pick fresh positions. Some borrow both and skip scoring entirely — a per-layer decision about what to recompute versus what to just keep. Across the published lineage, the cache footprint per million tokens falls from three hundred and eighty-nine gigabytes to eight hundred and ninety megabytes. A factor of four hundred and thirty-seven.

Meanwhile the price side: cache reads on Anthropic's Opus went from fifty cents to twenty per million, a sixty percent cut. OpenAI's Sol line went from fifty cents to ten. Eighty percent.

The causal link between those halves is the author's inference, not a documented fact, and the thread pushed back well. One commenter: neither lab has said that's what they used, and cache prices falling proves nothing on its own. Others offered the deflationary read — the labs may have had these optimizations all along and been holding the margin, the way cloud providers price bandwidth. But the sharpest comment answered the author's own closing question, which was a genuine "I have no clue why they'd give this away." The answer: this class of optimization needs matching support in the inference software, so open serving stacks have to be updated to run these models at all. It was never the kind of advance you could keep secret. Publishing wasn't altruism — it was the only way to ship. And notice where Argon landed: cached input, ninety-five percent off. Everyone is competing on the cache now.

The third story made me sure this was the day's real theme. Six hundred and forty-two points for a post titled "You Said No MCP!" — Armin Ronacher writing for Earendil about their coding agent Pi. Pi's founding thesis, from Mario Zechner's original essay, was that existing harnesses inject hidden context, change behavior between releases, and give you limited visibility into what the model actually saw. It shipped with no MCP, no subagents, no plan mode, no background bash. Forty-five thousand GitHub stars for saying no.

MCP is now in the core, and the reason isn't a change of principle. It's context economics. What changed, in his words, is that they did a lot of work in recent months to let Pi work with new models that allow deferred tool loading, mid-conversation system messages and reasoning level changes. Deferred tool loading is the enabling primitive: you can now have a tool available without paying for its description in the context window.

The second piece is a thing called Codemode, and the distinction he draws is the systems thinking this discourse usually lacks. When a harness executes a tool it can run where bash runs, or where the harness loop runs — and the trust levels are completely different. The loop is trusted; the sandbox where tools execute is not. Codemode runs on the trusted side, a JavaScript sandbox letting the agent compose tool calls in any order, with state living in the session transcript instead of the filesystem. His example: ask Pi to find the twenty most frustrated commenters on the issue tracker, and it wires together the project-tracker server and a decision model — in his phrase, without wasting any context at all.

He's clear about what's still broken, and the diagnosis is a context diagnosis: most servers are still built for harnesses that just dump tools into the context, then optimize token efficiency by returning text instead of data. And my favorite moment in the thread came when someone suggested just asking the agent to explain the pull request. Ronacher: "I don't think it's a good idea to put your clanker to a PR and then try to explain it. That's because you are then reading a derivative work of a derivative work instead of going to the source." On a show that exists to summarize things, I'll take the hit.

Two research items bracket this, both posted yesterday. One, at fifty-eight upvotes on Hugging Face's daily papers from a largely NVIDIA-affiliated group, is called Mid-Harness, and it asks the exact question those podcast hosts kept asking — is it the model or the harness? Their answer is neither. It's the boundary. They sample several candidate shell actions and verify them before forwarding one for execution, leaving generator and harness untouched. With a nine-billion-parameter generator, more sampling buys almost nothing under weak verification; put a frontier model in as the verifier and first-attempt success on a terminal benchmark goes from fifty percent to sixty-eight with eight sampled actions. Same generator throughout. All the gain came from choosing better among things the model could already say.

The other is obscure — a single-author preprint with one upvote — but it has the number that makes this episode concrete. Serving is stateless across requests: ask a second question about a document and the model recomputes that document's attention state from the first token. Across seven real-world datasets, the author measured that ninety-eight point seven percent of prompt tokens were text the model had already read. Take the rest of the claims with the usual preprint salt, but that figure is the argument in one number. Almost everything we pay a frontier model to do is re-reading.

To the money, because it's the same story at the balance-sheet layer. Bloomberg reports OpenAI is in early talks to raise at least thirty billion dollars at around one point four trillion — a sixty-four percent step up in six months, since March was a hundred and twenty-two billion at eight hundred and fifty-two billion post-money, and that round was supposed to be the last before an IPO. The IPO has slipped to 2027, and Altman's stated reason is worth quoting exactly: "I think it is unacceptable to be taking like a ten percent chance of killing everybody by the end of the decade." Run-rate revenue hit about forty billion in August, up roughly seventy percent since July.

Set that beside yesterday's leaked Anthropic prospectus with its five hundred and eighteen billion in committed compute. Both leaders are raising enormous sums against compute obligations while cutting cache-read prices sixty to eighty percent, and those two facts only coexist because of the efficiency work — whose published form came from a lab that gave it away because it couldn't ship it any other way. The cheapest input to the Western AI buildout this year was free. And as a reality check on where revenue actually lives: the other notable round was EliseAI, three hundred and fifty million at a four billion valuation, for automating administrative work in housing and healthcare. Not a frontier model. Paperwork.

Which brings me to the deep cut, and it closes the loop exactly. In June, Dwarkesh Patel posted a framing question that annoyed a lot of people: why has progress on computer use been so slow, when computer use is so clearly verifiable? His own answer was that verifiability isn't enough — a domain also has to be grindable. Yesterday, Latent Space put that to Ari Weinstein, who cofounded Sky, was acquired into OpenAI, and now leads product and engineering for computer use. His answer: "they said that a few months ago, I think, and I hope they have a different perspective now because Computer Use is, like, one hundred and eighty degrees different than it was."

Then three mechanisms, and watch how none of them is "the model got smarter at computers." The first is recovery: "before they could, like, reliably start tasks, but then they would run into problems, and now they're really good at debugging. They're really good at trying again, introspecting what is and isn't working."

The second is batching through code. Expand the tool calls in Codex, he says, and "it's not just doing one action at a time. It's actually writing JavaScript code that it executes, that the computer executes to perform sometimes many actions at once." Which is precisely Codemode, arrived at independently by a different team for a different product.

The third is representation, and asked for his aha moments he named it twice. The old loop was screenshot, try something, realize you need to scroll, screenshot again. "With accessibility and other, and direct access to the DOM and other things like that, now the language model can actually see, like, an entire page or an entire application." His framing of why is my favorite thing in the episode: screen reader technology, built for humans with accessibility needs, "is also really helpful for LLMs to be able to use computers." And on the feature that packages a running app's full state for the model, he's blunt about where the difficulty lives: you dump everything out, but, quote, "also making it token-efficient, doing it efficiently. There's, like, a bit of an art to it." Representation, not reasoning.

Then the part that tells you the bottleneck has moved. Asked where this goes next, he says the team is now "increasingly bottlenecked by just, like, the speed of doing an operation" — a non-trivial amount of time in their own benchmarks is spent waiting for the website to load. And independently, the same day, a researcher doing hands-on testing reported that OpenAI's fast inference tier generates about eight times quicker while end-to-end agent tasks only speed up two to four times, because tool latency dominates. The product lead's stated bottleneck, confirmed from outside, inside twenty-four hours.

The second half of that episode is Nikunj Handa, who leads product for OpenAI's API team, and it's the same story in API form. Async function calling exists because, quote, "tool calls take so long that you don't have to, like, pause the model's execution while the tool is running." And then caching, which he calls a main area of focus: "really go deep on caching, particularly with these, like, personal agents that are, you know, like, basically, like, a single thread that just goes on and on forever." They now guarantee cache hits within thirty minutes, and have launched a twelve-hour guarantee for one customer. There's pre-warming, where you pay the cache-write fee up front for a prompt you know is coming. His advice is to build apps that are, in his phrase, very cache aware. Swyx then makes the pivot that names the whole problem: caching is great, "but at the end of the day, you're still bumping up against a million-token context." Which is compaction — and the new techniques they're working on include, quote, file-based systems that they're experimenting with.

Ollie, here's the takeaway I'd hold. Dwarkesh asked a question about learning and got, from two OpenAI leads in one episode, a complete answer about systems: recovery loops, batching actions into code, richer page representations, async tools, cache guarantees, pre-warming, file-based compaction. His grindability instinct was right that there's a second axis beyond verifiability. He just put it in the wrong place. It isn't a property of the domain. It's a property of the harness — and the harness is now where the research is. Google moved its output limit into an API feature. DeepSeek moved its cost structure into per-layer decisions about what to recompute. Earendil moved tool definitions out of the context window entirely. OpenAI is selling a twelve-hour cache guarantee. And a terminal-agent paper got eighteen points of success out of a frozen model by verifying actions before executing them. None of that is a smarter model. All of it is better bookkeeping about what to keep.

The one thing to watch is a date that doesn't exist yet. When Argon reaches paid API customers and Ultra subscribers, two numbers will tell you whether thirteen-of-nineteen is a product or a press release: whether the introductory price holds past its unannounced expiry, and what the output tokens per task look like on your workload rather than on someone's index. Multiply those and you have your real cost. The benchmark table isn't in that equation anywhere.
