## Script

Here's what the AI world is talking about today. It's Sunday, November twenty-second. Two stories dominated the weekend, and they turn out to be the same story told twice. In one, a proof that no human has read was passed through a chain of independent checkers until the mathematical community said it had, quote, "full confidence." In the other, a security researcher sent two million prompts through the AI inference market with booby-trapped secrets inside, and found that at almost every hop, someone could read them, and some did. Today's thread is the chain of custody between the person who produces something and the person who has to trust it.

The usual disclosure, and it matters again today. The proof in the lead was found with Claude by a mathematician at Anthropic, the MIT checks I'll describe were run with Claude Code, and this script was drafted by Claude. Weigh what follows accordingly.

On to the Twitter Pulse. Yesterday we ended by asking whether the Lean build of Levent Alpöge's Riemann proof would finish cleanly. It did, and faster than almost anyone expected. On Saturday the Lean and Mathlib communities, endorsed by the Lean Focused Research Organization and the Mathlib Initiative, posted a joint statement. Quote: "We have full confidence that Alpöge's proof of the Riemann Hypothesis is correct." And then: "We hereby certify that both the statement and the proof of the Riemann Hypothesis are correct." It was the top story on Hacker News, past a thousand points.

What's interesting for us is less the verdict than how they got there, because it's a template for trusting something you cannot read. The statement checks four things. First, the statement of the Riemann Hypothesis is the one already sitting in Mathlib, vetted by experts long before this proof existed, so nobody had to trust Alpöge's phrasing. Second, the code compiles, and dozens of people reproduced the same build. Third, the community wrote a separate file that imports only Mathlib and states the theorem, and two independent tools confirmed that what Alpöge proved is exactly that statement. Fourth, the proof itself was checked by several separate checkers, written in different programming languages, and two of them come with their own formal proof that they cannot accept a false proof.

The detail that I think deserves the most attention is this one: the checks "ran on an inert export of the proof, out of reach of hypothetical malicious code in the Lean proof sources." Someone who works at the Lean research organization explained why on Hacker News. Building a Lean project can execute arbitrary code, so a hostile proof could in principle tamper with the machine checking it. So you split the work. You build the untrusted proof in a sandbox, export it as plain data, and then let the checkers read the data. The thing being judged never touches the judge.

Andrew Sutherland at MIT posted the receipts. All one hundred seventy-five thousand modules compile. The exported proof is about sixty-one gigabytes. An independent kernel written in Rust accepted it in under half an hour on eighty threads. A rebuild on a separate machine, with no network and a read-only source tree, reproduced the export byte for byte. And there's a lovely piece of forensics: one compiled file came out differently on different runs. Sutherland traced it to a race condition in how Lean names a variable when two theorems are elaborated in parallel, a bug already fixed in a newer version, and showed it cannot affect the proof. That is what custody looks like when people take it seriously.

Two more things on his page change how to read the headline. First, the scope. "GRH" here means Dirichlet L-functions. The broader families of L-functions are not among the statements checked. Second, and this is the clever part, he anticipated the obvious objection: what if Mathlib's definition of the zeta function is subtly wrong? So he combined the proof with an independent formalization of Robin's criterion and derived two inequalities about sums of divisors of whole numbers. No zeta function, no hypotheses, just arithmetic anyone can state. If the definitions were off, those consequences would be the place it shows.

The dissent on Hacker News was substantive. One commenter pointed out that a fake refutation of the Collatz conjecture once got through one of these checkers, and that none of the four criteria rest on the human expert who was in the loop. Another put the remaining risk as a misaligned system deliberately exploiting a Lean bug, and called it unlikely but not zero. The reply from the Lean side: since then, Lean ships formally verified checkers, so an exploit would have to defeat the correctness proofs of the checkers themselves, which is a much smaller target. The human proof from Alessandro Masullo is in a different place. The same Lean staffer said efforts to mechanize it are underway on the Lean chat, with "nothing to report yet."

And for the quieter version of the same shift, read Mitchell Taylor's guest post on Kevin Buzzard's blog, also from Saturday. He's an analyst, by his own account the worst student in his Lean course, and he says roughly ten hours of class was enough to check that his papers were formalized correctly. His rule now: "I personally prefer to Lean verify any major claim produced by an LLM before I commit too much of my time to understanding the argument." Verify first, then invest in understanding. That's a working mathematician's habit, not a headline.

Now the inverse story. On Thursday, HD Moore, the chief research officer at the security firm Dragos, published the results of a five-month experiment. He sent nearly two million prompts to AI services, each seeded with secrets that existed nowhere else: real AWS keys tied to empty accounts, passwords, private URLs, references to git repos. Then he watched what got used. His summary: "What we found revealed security breaches, prompt theft, and widespread failures to protect customer data."

The method is the part to steal. Each secret went into exactly one prompt to one provider, so any use of it points back to a specific hop. To find the hops, his team built what amounts to traceroute for inference, using tiny broken requests whose nested error messages reveal every router in the chain. They traced about two thousand routes. Several hundred contained a system they couldn't identify, and a handful sent data to a company that wasn't on the advertised path at all.

The findings come in three kinds. Attackers had compromised three hosting providers; in the worst case, every request in certain windows in late October was stolen, including requests rejected before a model ever ran, and the stolen keys were used for tens of thousands of AWS calls, at one point with an LLM-driven penetration-testing agent. Second, sellers on an open marketplace simply listed models at the lowest price, turned on logging, and waited. Third, and this is where the weekend's news came from, a Chinese lab's systems fetched secret URLs from more than half the prompts sent to its models, including about two-thirds of requests flagged zero data retention, and the same scraper fired when the prompts went through US resellers of that lab's models. Moore's line: "Zero Data Retention is supposed to mean something. In practice, these prompts were retained, shared with other companies, and read by people."

Then the dominoes. On Friday night OpenRouter emailed customers. Nate Berkopec on X: "Just got the email from Openrouter and now the story is confirmed." According to Ars Technica, the email named MiniMax and three resellers. On Sunday OpenRouter posted that it had off-boarded three providers and pulled one hundred twenty-five endpoints from its zero-retention list. And Together AI disclosed a separate incident with a detail you couldn't make up: an AI coding agent committed a credential to a public GitHub repo, the credential opened a private repository of stored prompt samples, and an attacker downloaded them and went hunting for keys. Five of the keys they tested were Dragos canaries.

Put the two stories side by side. The math community ran its checks on an inert copy, separated the untrusted thing from the judge, and demanded independent reimplementations. The inference market decrypts your prompt at every hop and asks you to trust a label.

On to Money Moves, and it follows directly. On Friday, OpenAI updated its usage policies so that model routers and AI gateways now need OpenAI's "review and approval before offering that access." Semafor's read is that this is about who can reach OpenAI models at all, including from China, since distillation campaigns have run through intermediaries that obscure the end user. The same day OpenAI published a post on adversarial distillation. Its weekly enforcement actions have ranged from thousands to tens of thousands of accounts, with the rate rising sharply over the summer. It also reported that the success rate of attacks extracting a model's hidden reasoning fell from fifty-four percent on GPT-6 Astra to zero point two percent on GPT-6.2 Astra, and it found that training on exact reasoning traces transfers more cyber capability than training on the summaries users see.

What this signals: the router layer was the cheap, neutral middle of the AI economy, the thing that let one account reach hundreds of models from three continents. Within forty-eight hours it was squeezed from both sides, by a frontier lab that wants to know who's on the other end, and by evidence that the middle leaks. Expect distribution to look more like contracts and less like a marketplace, and expect "who handled my prompt" to become a line item that enterprises pay for.

Now the Podcast Deep Cut. On Saturday's Cognitive Revolution, Nathan Labenz talked with Ben Buchanan, the first White House special advisor for AI, and Tantum Collins, who ran technology and national security on Biden's National Security Council after years at DeepMind. Their new book is called The Bitter Struggle. Here's the argument chain, quoted from the published transcript.

Step one is the premise, borrowed from Kennedy's Rice University speech, which hung in Biden's private study. Buchanan paraphrases it: "We don't know whether space will be good or ill for humanity, but only if we have preeminence can we help decide."

Step two is the mechanism. Because of the allied "compute supply chain choke hold," Buchanan says, the US could "deny China computing power, build a lead over them without being unduly accelerationist ourselves." Step three is the purpose: "the phrase we used to use is spend that lead on safety."

Step four is the scorecard. Despite what he calls poor enforcement and partial reversals, he estimates "something like a hundred to one advantage over China in producing computing power and probably something like a fifteen to one advantage over access to it." And notice what the gap between those two numbers is made of: "if we close the remote access loophole and stop the smuggling," he says, fifteen to one "could probably get closer to a hundred to one." In other words, the leak in the lead isn't the chips. It's access through intermediaries. That's the same layer that OpenAI just put behind an approval gate, and the same layer the Dragos traces mapped.

Step five is where it lands. Collins says what they pushed for in government wasn't fixed rules but "insight and agility," meaning government knowing "what is happening within the labs." And for any international deal, you'd need "some kind of verification scheme," so that each side "can trust the other is enforcing," and there, he says, there is "a long way to go on the technical front." Buchanan's bottom line: "we've got to slow down the United States to make sure we are spending that lead well."

Here's what I'd take from it. Their whole strategy assumes you can verify things about compute and conduct across a boundary you don't control. This weekend showed what that looks like when it works, in mathematics, and what it looks like when it doesn't, in inference. Lean can certify a sixty-one gigabyte proof between strangers. Nobody can yet certify where a prompt went after it left your laptop.

Ollie's takeaway, concretely. If you send unpublished results, patient-derived data, or credentials through a model API, treat every router as a hop that can read it. Go first-party under a contract, pin your providers instead of accepting a default pool, and treat zero data retention as a claim to test, not a guarantee. And borrow the Dragos method: seed your own pipelines with a canary, a fake key or a unique URL that should never be touched, and watch it. On the other side, borrow the Lean method for your agents' results. Keep the thing being judged away from the judge, check with independent tools, and test a downstream consequence that doesn't depend on your own definitions, the way Robin's inequality did for zeta. In biology, that's the orthogonal assay.

The one thing to watch tomorrow: the single-threaded Lean kernel replay on Sutherland's page is still running, so look for the last check to go green, and for any sign of progress on formalizing Masullo's proof. And watch the provider list on OpenRouter's incident page. Dragos says it's working with about a dozen providers, and so far only three have posted. That's the pulse for today.
