## Script

Here's what the AI world is talking about today. DeepSeek open-sourced the inference engine behind its production service, and buried in the Chinese-language write-up was a number nobody in this industry usually says out loud: how much money one accelerator earns in an hour. That one disclosure set the tone for the whole day. Revenue per card-hour, cache retention measured in days, and a draft IPO prospectus putting another lab's compute bill in writing. Today is mostly about what a GPU-hour is actually worth.

Start with the release. DeepInfer is the framework that serves DeepSeek V4.1 Flash in production, Apache 2.0, and it runs on two hardware targets: NVIDIA's Blackwell generation, and Huawei's Ascend 950. That second line is the one Hacker News fixated on, one day after the U.S. government ordered DeepSeek out of federal civilian systems. The thread hit over two thousand two hundred points, the biggest story on the site in the last two days.

But the code is honestly the less interesting half. The engineering post DeepSeek put on Zhihu, in Chinese, is where the substance is, and swyx put it bluntly on X: "deepseek posted way more alpha on their zhihu page, if you only read twitter you are reading the laowai approved stuff." The line everyone quoted reads, in the original, 单一集群的服务吞吐是 155 万亿 Tokens/天，整体收入是每卡每小时 38 美金（按谷价）. A single cluster serves a hundred fifty-five trillion tokens a day, and overall revenue is thirty-eight dollars per card per hour, at off-peak prices.

That's the number the whole conversation ran on. Renting a current Blackwell-class card runs very roughly five to ten dollars an hour. Yuchen Jin did the arithmetic: even assuming seven dollars an hour, that's about eighty-two percent gross margin. One analyst on X, zephyr, first posted a margin range topping out near ninety-seven percent, then publicly corrected it, because their idealized model implied about forty-nine dollars an hour and DeepSeek actually reports thirty-eight. Their revised range was eighty-four to ninety-six percent. Tim Dettmers went further, writing that DeepSeek's inference system is "on par with OpenAIs stack and better what Anthropic is running (at least for their user size)."

Now the part that makes those margins make sense, and the part worth carrying away even if you never touch an inference server. The headline is mostly cache. According to the production statistics DeepSeek posted alongside the article, about ninety-six percent of input tokens are cache hits. So the hundred fifty-five trillion is not a hundred fifty-five trillion freshly computed tokens. It's mostly reused context, billed at a discount and served off disk.

And the disk is the real design story. DeepSeek keeps the attention cache for prefixes on its own distributed file system, about a hundred pebibytes of SSD, with a retention of three days for agent traffic and seven days for chatbot traffic. Because that cache lives in shared storage rather than on any one machine, any prefill worker can pick up any conversation. They don't route requests back to the machine that saw them last; they pool everything and balance purely on estimated compute. The post even says why chatbots get the longer window: chat traffic has a fatter long tail, where a small number of cached prefixes get read an enormous number of times, and cutting retention to three days costs them half a point to a full point of hit rate.

There's a neat trick for the model's sliding-window layers, too. Instead of storing that short-lived state, they throw it away and, on a miss, recompute only the last hundred twenty-eight tokens, with attention clipped to that segment. It's an approximation, but it turns the cost of a miss into a constant, and DeepSeek says it roughly halves total prefill compute.

Two things happened within hours that make this a convergence story rather than one company's flex. First, OpenAI's developer account announced twelve-hour prompt caching in the API, pitched explicitly at persistent agents that reuse context throughout the day, with the thirty-minute default still available and the two mixable in a single request. DeepSeek's stated design goal is seventy-two hours. The labs are now competing on how long they'll remember your prefix.

Second, the contrarian take, and it came from the people DeepSeek built on. DeepInfer's own README says it was originally built primarily on vLLM. Overnight, the vLLM team posted, quote, "vLLM is all you need to reproduce DeepSeek's performance," reporting decode throughput within six percent of DeepInfer's published numbers under DeepSeek's own configuration, and about thirty-nine dollars a GPU-hour in revenue at DeepSeek's off-peak pricing on an agentic workload. Two caveats keep that honest. It's decode parity, not a reproduction of the whole service. And it's a competing engine team grading itself, though with public recipes. What's missing from both is the hard part: DeepSeek says plainly that its cache management and load balancers are too entangled with internal infrastructure to release. Su Jin Yan, an inference engineer, made the same point in Chinese, that the essence, 精华, is in the Zhihu article, not the repo. The paper is the gift. The repo is a sample.

So the convergent read from X, Hacker News and the vLLM team is this. The model was never the moat for cheap inference. The cache, the scheduler and the storage fleet are, and DeepSeek just published the blueprint while keeping the building.

The second thread is smaller but closer to home for a biomedical AI lab. Artificial Analysis launched independent leaderboards for HealthBench and HealthBench Professional, OpenAI's benchmarks that grade replies to realistic health conversations against physician-written rubrics. The interesting result isn't who's first. It's how much the ranking depends on one scoring choice. Artificial Analysis reports both the raw rubric score and the length-adjusted score OpenAI uses, which penalizes replies over two thousand characters and boosts shorter ones.

On the Professional set, the clinician conversations, Claude Opus 5.5 has the highest raw score of any model, seventy-one point four percent, but it averages around five thousand nine hundred characters per answer, and after adjustment it drops to sixth at fifty-nine point nine. GPT-6 Sol is the only model averaging under two thousand characters, and the only one whose score goes up. On general HealthBench the same tradeoff shows up axis by axis: Sol writes the shortest answers and has the best communication score, eighty-one percent, and the worst completeness, thirty-four. Sonnet 5.5 leads completeness at sixty-three.

None of that is a scandal. Rambling medical answers really are worse for a patient. But it means the leaderboard is partly a vote on a length threshold someone chose. Add that the benchmark is OpenAI's, the graders are OpenAI models, and OpenAI models make up the entire cost-versus-score frontier on the Professional set, and you have a good reason to read these numbers as "useful, not neutral." And the same DeepSeek V4.1 Flash from the first story scores sixty point nine on general HealthBench at half a cent per task, about a point behind the leader at a sixteenth of the cost.

Money moves. The day's biggest number came out of a draft IPO prospectus. The Wall Street Journal reviewed Anthropic's draft filing, as relayed by Implicator and others: sixteen point three billion dollars of revenue in the first half of this year, up from one point one billion a year earlier, and compute and infrastructure costs of nearly nine billion, up from two point three. The headline net loss is eighty point seven billion, but seventy-two billion of that is an accounting charge from the rising value of convertible notes, not cash out the door. Strip it out and the remainder is eight point seven billion. Hacker News split predictably between "it's a con" and "that's a nothing burger," and the more useful commenters landed in between: the charge is real dilution for shareholders, even if it's not operating burn. The number that should hold your attention is the forward one. Around four hundred fifty billion dollars of cloud agreements over seven to ten years, mostly noncancelable.

Put that next to DeepSeek's thirty-eight dollars a card-hour and you can see the debate the industry is actually having. One side says serving is wildly profitable if your cache and scheduler are good enough. The other is signing decade-long, take-or-pay compute contracts on the assumption that the margin will still be there when the bill comes due. Both can be true at once, but only if the price of a GPU-hour doesn't move much. Hold that thought for the deep cut.

Two smaller moves worth a line each. OpenAI acquired Orchestra Bio, a platform where people and AI agents coordinate drug programs, folding it into Rosalind, its life sciences line. The example in OpenAI's own post is telling: a failed manufacturing batch delaying a trial start, with agents mapping which timelines it touches. That's not discovery; it's program management. The bet is that the money in AI for pharma is in operations, not only in molecules. And Bob McGrew, OpenAI's former research chief, launched Arda with a hundred sixty million dollar Series A at a one and a half billion dollar valuation, after a year building manufacturing-line software with Anduril as its design partner.

For the deep cut, Latent Space's conversation with Evan Conrad, CEO of SF Compute, recorded at AI Engineer New York and released yesterday. It's thirty-six minutes, and it happens to answer the question today's DeepSeek numbers raise. One caveat: the published transcript is automated, so I'm quoting it as written.

Conrad's company runs a market for GPU time, where you can buy capacity starting an hour from now or three years out. His argument runs in four steps.

First, compute is not a commodity yet. Generations, memory, interconnect domains all matter, so instead of forcing everything into one standard unit, his order book lets a buyer say something like, I'll take any Blackwell under six dollars an hour, while a buyer with a hyper-specific need pays more.

Second, the price curve isn't a simple slope. Asked whether future compute trades above or below spot, he said they see a hump, and explained it with milk: "if I have milk and it is about to expire, I will lower the price on it." Capacity that's about to go unused has to clear at any price, so it's cheap. Further out, it rises. Further still, it can fall again, because people would rather have compute now.

Third, here's the sentence that ties straight back to DeepSeek. "Right now if you took a B300 and you trying to run inference on it, you can make forty dollars an hour or so, selling that as inference. Any model basically." At fifty percent utilization, he says, call it twenty-five. "Meanwhile, you can buy B300s at like five to six dollars an hour. One of these numbers has to change." That's the same gap DeepSeek just documented from the other side, an independent market operator saying it out loud the day before DeepSeek put its own number in writing.

Fourth, and this is the part that reaches the Anthropic story, who can run that market? His answer is that NVIDIA, the neoclouds, the hyperscalers and the inference companies all structurally can't, because they're on one side of the trade. "You have to kill your business in order to make this. That's why nobody does it." And a neutral market matters because lenders need a trustworthy forward curve to finance clusters against. He's wary of cash-settled compute indices built on thin data, and he brings up LIBOR, the interbank rate that was manipulated, as the cautionary tale. His position is that cash-settled derivatives are fine, but they should be derived from prices where someone actually delivered the GPUs.

Here's the takeaway for you. The forty-dollar inference-hour against the six-dollar rental hour is the gap every lab's financing assumes will persist, and both Conrad and the DeepSeek post say it's mostly a function of software you can now read: caching, scheduling, utilization. If open stacks like DeepInfer and vLLM close that software gap for everyone, the most likely number to change is the forty. Practically, for anyone building research agents, design them so the long shared context sits at the front of the prompt and stays stable. That's exactly what the providers are now competing to keep cached for twelve hours, three days, or a week, and cached input is where the price has room to fall.

The one thing to watch tomorrow: whether anyone independent publishes an Ascend number from DeepInfer. Support is confirmed. Comparable efficiency on Huawei silicon isn't, and that's the figure that would turn a software release into a hardware story. That's the pulse for today.
