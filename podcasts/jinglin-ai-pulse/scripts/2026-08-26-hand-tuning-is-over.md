## Script

Here's what the AI world is talking about today. Two stories dominated, and on the surface they have nothing to do with each other. One is a custom silicon disclosure. The other is a pile of papers about agent harnesses. Underneath they're the same story, and it's the one this show has been circling all week: the artifacts we used to hand-tune are turning into the outputs of optimization loops. Today you can watch it happen to a chip and to a harness on the same day.

Start with the chip. OpenAI took its Jalapeño accelerator to Hot Chips at Stanford and gave the closest look yet at what it is, and SemiAnalysis published a deep dive that sat at the top of Hacker News all day with nearly five hundred points.

Briefly, the numbers. Thirteen point four petaflops of four-bit matrix compute, two hundred sixteen gibibytes of Samsung HBM4 at fifteen point four terabytes a second, seven hundred watts a package, on TSMC's three-nanometer-performance node. Against Nvidia's Blackwell parts at matched operating points on the open-weights GPT model, OpenAI claims one point nine times the peak mixed tokens per second per kilowatt, one point seven times lower end-to-end latency, and fifty-three point seven times the throughput at the previous best token-to-token latency. Richard Ho, who runs OpenAI's chip group, named the GB300 as the reference part.

One design choice matters more than any of that. The GPU industry has converged on splitting inference into separate hardware pools, one for compute-bound prefill and one for bandwidth-bound decode. OpenAI refused, building a single balanced chip that handles every phase and gates the blocks that idle, because a fungible fleet shifts capacity between latency-sensitive requests and throughput batches while a fixed split strands hardware. And they optimized for performance per watt rather than per dollar, because they're limited by datacenter power, not by budget. That tells you more about this industry than any benchmark.

But the part that matters for anyone who doesn't build chips is how it got built. Concept to tape-out in roughly nine months, with major changes landing up to the day of the register-transfer-level freeze — they call it continuous convergence, which is a polite way of saying they never froze a spec. They wrote their own kernel language, Gluon, on top of Triton, and then pointed an internal Codex variant at it. That variant writes functional kernels autonomously, and OpenAI says its optimized attention and mixture-of-experts kernels run one point five to one point eight times faster than the expert-written versions. More than a two-times throughput gain in under two weeks between builds, and eight-way to thirty-two-way tensor parallel, a full rack, in eight days.

Which is why SemiAnalysis wrote the sentence everyone quoted: the CUDA moat is potentially dead given how fast OpenAI can bring up new models on their silicon. Sit with the recursion there. GPT five point six Sol, running on Nvidia GPUs, helped design and program the chip that undercuts Nvidia's software lock-in. Fifteen years of CUDA accumulation partially neutralized not by a better ecosystem but by an agent that regenerates the kernels on demand.

Now the caveats, which the coverage mostly buried. Every number came from OpenAI. All of it is single-turn, eight thousand tokens in and one thousand out, the easiest workload in the world to tune for, with no runs at all on the long-context multi-turn agentic suite — exactly where the industry is heading. Jalapeño's figures use single-token prediction while Nvidia's use speculative multi-token prediction, worth another three to five times. And the honest comparison isn't Blackwell, it's Vera Rubin, also on HBM4, where Jalapeño lands merely comparable on performance per dollar.

Second thing the community was chewing on lands within hours of this recording. Alibaba's Qwen team is open-sourcing Qwen three point eight Flash Next at eleven tonight Beijing time. The ModelScope teaser page briefly exposed the specs before being edited down, and enough people captured it that the numbers are well-attested if not confirmed: a hundred twenty-five billion total parameters, about six billion active per token, and then the interesting part — a separate fifty-one-billion-parameter n-gram embedding block that works as a lookup table rather than as ordinary weights. Plus gated delta-net hybrid layers, what they call Qwen Sparse Attention, 稀疏注意力, xī shū zhù yì lì, and a claim of roughly one-ninth the training cost of Qwen three point seven Plus at comparable capability.

The framing matters more than the specs. Qwen is explicit that this is not a flagship — it's an architectural preview of Qwen 4, shipped early so inference runtimes can prepare, which means releasing the architecture before the model that justifies it and treating the ecosystem's readiness as the deliverable. The sharpest question in the Hacker News thread was about that fifty-one-billion table: must it live in fast memory, or can it stream from storage? Nobody knows, and the answer decides whether this is runnable on a workstation. That's an infrastructure question, not a capability question.

Now the third thread, where today's shape becomes clear. Yesterday's discourse settled on the claim that half the agent is the harness. Today the Hugging Face daily papers page carries three separate harness-optimization papers, and not one of them argues about whether the harness matters. They're all automating it.

The one that led, at forty-five upvotes, is AutoSaddler, out of POSTECH, KAIST, Southern University of Science and Technology, and Microsoft. It treats the harness as code and harness improvement as an offline learning problem with mini-batches. Each iteration runs the current harness on a batch of tasks, diagnoses the failed traces against the harness source, generates a structured patch across three layers — prompts, tools, and runtime middleware — verifies it on the same batch, then checks whether the gain generalizes on a held-out dev set, and files the lesson in a graph of harness versions that the next iteration mines to recombine parts from earlier lineages. Nine points on GAIA2, about nine and a half on SWE-Bench Pro, ten on Terminal-Bench two, forty percent to fifty, where it beats the hand-tuned expert harness by two and a half.

But the results aren't the interesting part. The ablations are. Strip out generalization-aware selection — keep the automated diagnosis and patching, just stop checking whether patches generalize before you keep them — and performance drops from sixty-two percent to fifty point six. The base, unoptimized harness scores fifty-three. Read that again. Automated harness optimization without a held-out check makes your agent worse than not optimizing at all. And the mechanism is precise: fix rates are essentially identical either way. The entire gap is regressions. With the check, the regression rate trends down over iterations; without it, up. At one iteration the ablated system added a new progress-message tool and rewired the hook on the existing, widely-used message tool to force traffic to it. Regressions on previously-passing tasks jumped from eight percent to twenty-two.

The second ablation is just as good. Remove the patch taxonomy and let the agent edit the harness freely, and ninety-one and a half percent of its patches collapse into text edits — prompt wording, tool docstrings. Meanwhile the patch types with the highest acceptance rates are precisely the ones it stops generating: adding a new tool, eighty-three percent accepted; changing the agent loop, seventy-one; changing infrastructure, sixty-seven. Unconstrained, those are four percent of what it tries. Forced into a structured space, over twenty-five. An agent given freedom to improve its own scaffolding spends that freedom rewriting prompts, the cheapest and least durable thing available to it — and their numbers say code-level patches fix just as much while causing half the regressions, eight percent versus seventeen.

Two more from the same page, quickly. Recuris splits agent memory in two — working memory that tracks progress and picks skills, experiential memory that stores capabilities — with a meta-agent turning failure evidence into verification-gated edits to the skill store. It improves thirty-five of thirty-seven model-benchmark pairs, and the gains grow with horizon: plus thirty-two points on the longest tasks. And CAFE, out of Fudan, argues a self-improving search agent needs a critic that evolves alongside it, since improving only the agent or only the critic plateaus while alternating doesn't.

Here's the detail that convinced me this is a phase change rather than three coincidences. There's a footnote in AutoSaddler's related-work section listing roughly ten contemporaneous papers on automatic harness optimization that appeared while they were finishing theirs — observability-driven evolution, weakness-guided repair, Bayesian configuration search, retrospective self-supervised optimization, joint optimization of harness and weights. Ten independent groups converging inside one review cycle. That footnote is the meta-narrative. Six months ago the harness was a craft skill you bragged about. Today it's a search space with baselines.

Now the deep cut, and it's the best counterweight I've found to everything above. On Friday, Latent Space published a conversation with Joon Sung Park, who now runs a company called Simile and whose earlier work you know as the generative-agents paper. The claim is in the title: simulation is the new scaling law.

Follow the argument, because it's tighter than the slogan. Premise one: frontier models are optimized to be objective reasoning machines, and that optimization actively damages their ability to reproduce human behavior. Park's line is the most memorable thing in the episode — the models that we're trying to create are models that are as dumb as I am, so if I make some mistakes, the model has to make the same mistake. A model trained to be right cannot simulate a person who is wrong in the specific way people are wrong.

Premise two is the measurement. Frontier models predicting human survey and behavioral-game responses land between fifty and sixty percent on general populations, dropping as low as twenty on niche ones. Park's digital twins reproduce a person's behaviors and attitudes about eighty-five percent as accurately as that same person reproduces their own answers on a retest. That denominator is the sharp part: the ceiling isn't a hundred percent, it's human self-consistency.

Premise three turns methodological. Getting there took three kinds of data, and only the third is unusual: long-form interviews, behavioral traces, and then causal data — randomized controlled trials, harvested from the Open Science Framework, recording not what people did but why an intervention changed what they did. Post-training on that trial data is what moved the numbers. So the missing ingredient wasn't scale or architecture. It was experiments. The dark knowledge of humanity, in his phrase, only exists where somebody ran a trial. Hence the reframe: simulation isn't predicting the future, it's shaping it — what a decision-maker wants isn't a forecast but a counterintuitive intervention that works, which is a search over a simulator, not a prediction from a model.

My takeaway, and the reason I put this next to the harness papers. Every headline today is an optimization loop replacing hand-tuning: Codex writing kernels faster than the humans who wrote the compiler, ten groups automating the harness, an evolutionary graph beating an expert-tuned agent. Every one of those loops runs on a verifiable signal — does the kernel go faster, did the task pass. Park is describing the domain where that signal doesn't exist and can't be scraped, and his answer is that you have to go generate it, one controlled experiment at a time. Which is the same answer Xaira's people gave on the same podcast a month ago about biology: causal models need causal data. Two different fields, same bottleneck.

One thing to watch tomorrow. Qwen's weights land tonight, and the question isn't the benchmark scores — it's whether that fifty-one-billion-parameter n-gram table has to sit in fast memory. If it does, this is a datacenter architecture, and the open-weights local-inference story quietly gets worse even as the models get better. If it can stream from disk, Alibaba has found a way to buy capability with storage instead of VRAM, and that changes what a workstation can run. Same question as the whole episode, one layer down: the frontier keeps moving into the plumbing, and the plumbing is where nobody's looking.
