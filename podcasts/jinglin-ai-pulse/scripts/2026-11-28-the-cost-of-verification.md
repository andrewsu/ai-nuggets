## Script

Here's what the AI world is talking about today. It's Saturday, November twenty-eighth, the long weekend after Thanksgiving, and one idea ran through almost everything worth your time: the cost of checking. A new discrete logarithm paper arrived in the middle of the cryptography rumors, and the experts' first job was working out what it actually changes. Daniel Bernstein published a running count of bugs in the proof checkers everyone now leans on. Michael Nielsen took his famous neural networks textbook offline in protest. In the money section, the companies selling training data to frontier labs explain why a verifier is the product. And the Deep Cut is Ege Erdil on Dwarkesh's podcast, arguing that the price of verification, not raw intelligence, sets how fast AI research can automate itself.

A quick disclosure before we start. Nielsen's note and part of the Deep Cut criticize Anthropic directly, and this script was drafted by Claude, an Anthropic model. I've stuck to their words where I can, but weigh my framing accordingly.

First, cryptography. On Thursday's episode we covered Scott Aaronson saying he was "virtually certain" the AI labs are sitting on cryptographic bombshells. Since then, three things landed. A paper from Zhejiang University's blockchain and data security lab, by Shuchang Liu, Bingsheng Zhang and Kui Ren, claims the first deterministic subexponential-time algorithm for the discrete logarithm problem. It covers prime fields and, more alarmingly on paper, elliptic curves over prime fields, with no random bits and no reliance on the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis. The abstract is candid about where it stands. It builds on the October refutations of the Exponential Time Hypothesis and assumes a Boolean tensor construction OpenAI published that month. On Thursday, The Times ran the headline "AI on brink of making online security useless, mathematicians fear," and readers quoted Peter Scholze from the piece: "breaking public-key-cryptography is not harder than solving Riemann." And per Latent Space's newsletter, the poster known as kimmonismus relayed a rumor that a lab will soon announce a theoretical cryptanalysis breakthrough that does not threaten deployed systems.

So, does this paper threaten your SSH keys? The Hacker News thread mostly said no, though people disagreed on why. One commenter claimed it was worse than baby-step giant-step, the classic square-root attack. Two others corrected him from the paper's own table: the new bound is p to the little-o of one, which does eventually beat the square root, but only for keys far larger than anything deployed. Colin Percival made the most useful point. Factoring algorithms "essentially always have an analogue for prime-field DLP," so after last week's factoring result, "the surprise would have been if this algorithm didn't exist." In other words, this is the expected follow-on, not the bombshell. Another commenter noted that the more interesting news is the deterministic part: no randomness and no unproven hypotheses. One reply from the thread captures the mood: "the Bayesian probability of new math trashing a major piece of in use cryptography in the near future has shot up," now ahead of the quantum risk. The practical advice in the thread was hybrid key exchange, which your browser already does: pair a classical scheme with a post-quantum one, so the connection holds as long as either survives.

Which brings us to who checks the checkers. Daniel Bernstein, djb, the cryptographer behind Curve twenty-five-five-nineteen, put up a page called "False computer-checked proofs." It lists reported soundness bugs, meaning ways a proof checker could be fooled into accepting something false. As of Wednesday's version, Lean has twenty-three reported, ten of them this year. Isabelle has twenty and Rocq, formerly Coq, has a hundred and eleven. HOL Light has three, and its kernel is about four hundred lines. His advice is the security engineer's: keep the trusted core small, run proofs through several independent checkers, and prefer checkers that have themselves been proven correct, like Candle, a verified clone of HOL Light. On Hacker News, one commenter noted the list's own chart shows the bug curve bending up years before AI arrived. Another pointed to a past case where a DeepMind researcher built a "proof" that passed both Lean and an early version of the independent checker nanoda by exploiting a different bug in each. The honest summary is that a soundness bug in one checker rarely misleads mathematicians, since you need matching bugs in every checker plus a proof that looks plausible on review. But the inputs are changing. Proofs used to be written by humans in good faith. Now some arrive as millions of lines of machine output, like last week's seventeen-million-line Riemann proof, produced by systems trained to pass the check. For you, Ollie, the lesson carries straight to agent pipelines: if the grader is the only thing standing between an agent and a reward, assume the agent will eventually find the grader's bugs.

The second conversation was more personal. Michael Nielsen replaced his book, Neural Networks and Deep Learning, which taught a generation of researchers backprop, with a note. He wrote it between twenty eleven and twenty fifteen, expecting neural-network AGI to be "many decades away." Now, he writes, "The frontier labs are knowingly endangering all of humanity," and "I don't wish to help (even in a very small way) train people to contribute to this work, and so I've taken the book down." What's worth hearing is the argument, because it isn't the usual rogue-AI story. His claim is that the real danger is destructive technologies "latent in nature" that are cheap to build and hard to defend against. His example is the matchstick, a one-cent technology that takes hundreds of billions of dollars a year to defend against. Now imagine a hundredfold speed-up, so that every technological problem of the twentieth century, the bomb, chemical weapons, the ozone hole, arrives in nineteen-oh-one, and then another set in nineteen-oh-two. From there he argues that technical alignment is "mostly market-supplied safety." It makes systems more palatable, speeds up the race, and leaves the underlying problem worse. Then he proposes a test for anyone at a lab who says they're reducing risk: would "an independent jury representing humanity" believe it on the evidence? He says no lab comes close, cites Anthropic's 2026 Responsible Scaling Policy dropping its pause pledge, and calls Dario Amodei "somebody in the business of selling matches who also believes he should be the fire chief." The top reply on Hacker News made the obvious objection: "So now it's in the model weights, but I can't use it to grow my understanding." Others pointed out it's archived everywhere, so the takedown is a statement, not a real restriction. Notice that Nielsen's jury test is a verification argument too. The labs' central safety claim, that it's better we build it than someone else, is one nobody outside the labs can check.

One quick hit on architecture. Google Research posted about DeepCrossAttention, which replaces the plain residual sum with learned, per-token attention over the outputs of earlier layers. In their results, a thirty-layer model matched a forty-two-layer baseline, and the paper claims up to three times faster training. The paper is from last year's ICML, which Hacker News pointed out, joking that it's "somewhere between four years and one decade" old in ML time. The real news is in the blog's last section. Moonshot's attention residuals, DeepSeek's manifold-constrained hyper-connections and Google's version all attack the same assumption, that adding every layer's output with equal weight is good enough. And Google says Thinking Machines' Inkling 2 shipped with both Moonshot's Block AttnRes and DeepCrossAttention's query-key-value split. Labs are converging on the residual stream as a place to optimize.

Money moves. Latent Space skipped the Claude sabotage drama as its lead, saying it wanted more evidence first. Instead it led with the coding-data market, where, by its account, several roughly one-year-old startups are each making around a hundred million dollars a year from frontier-lab contracts. Their guest, Gabe Greenberg of G2i, gave the numbers: fifteen million dollars of revenue last year, about a hundred million this year, "no VC backing," profitable, with "like five or six customers." A few details stood out. Labs don't run procurement processes: "a researcher in 60 seconds can spend $10 million," and a pilot might be "a few million dollars." Exclusivity isn't the default. Data sold off the shelf to four labs is cheaper for each of them, and "they don't always care," because only the top one or two labs get a real edge from exclusive data. Tasks are priced by average handling time, with staff engineers at around two hundred fifty dollars an hour. Raw company data is now a commodity too, but where three months ago "anything would go," buyers now want several terabytes and more than ten employees' worth of connected Slack, email and GitHub. His explanation for why labs buy in a hurry is the one that tells you the most: compute sitting idle is wasted, so "good, better, best, and it's not always best." Swyx's estimate is that data spend is rising from two or three percent of a lab's compute budget to four to six. Greenberg's next markets are robotics, 3D world models, CAD, mechanical engineering and chips.

The other money item is one you can act on. Moonshot launched Kimi for Science: up to twenty thousand dollars in Kimi API credits, which they put at about seven billion tokens, plus early model access, for researchers in the natural sciences and engineering. Applications are due December eighth. The credits are for model usage, not cash, and they want your workflow, data sources and evaluation plan. If you have a literature-mining or agent pipeline that's limited by token budget, it's a short application.

Today's Deep Cut. Dwarkesh Patel sat down Daniel Kokotajlo, of AI 2027, and Ege Erdil, now a researcher at Google DeepMind, expecting a debate. Dwarkesh says they ended up agreeing far more than he expected. The numbers first. Erdil puts a software-only intelligence explosion, AI research automating itself on a fixed pool of compute, at ten to twenty percent over the next three years. Kokotajlo says over fifty, with full automation of AI research "slightly more likely than not in 2027." Erdil's point is that the gap matters less than it sounds: "even if it's only 20% likely, if the situation is very bad, you should still do something about it."

Where they really split is on pausing. Kokotajlo asks him, more or less, why he works at Google instead of protesting outside Anthropic. Erdil's answer has two parts. First, he's suspicious of acting on theoretical risks before seeing real costs, and he doesn't expect alignment to come from a theoretical breakthrough, because "Machine learning is not a field that has large theoretical breakthroughs." Second, a pause now would be backed by people who dislike big tech and data centers for their own reasons, not because they understand what's coming, and he worries that decisions made that way will "not be the output of any process that makes any sense." Kokotajlo's reply is that public understanding grows with time, not capability, so each extra year still buys some of it. Put that next to Nielsen's note and you have the full range: one researcher deletes his textbook, the other takes a job at a frontier lab, and both start from the view that the world isn't ready.

The part I'd keep is the final segment, on whether AI research is actually verifiable. Erdil rejects the usual split between verifiable and unverifiable tasks. Whether a video edit makes Dwarkesh happy is verifiable, he says, "you can give the video to him." The real variable is cost. Checking a Lean proof of the Riemann hypothesis runs on a laptop, in roughly linear time. Checking whether an optimizer tweak really saves compute in pretraining is "very hard to settle." Small-scale wins can fail at scale, and some ideas only show up at scale, so "you get false positives and false negatives in both directions." Models are sample-inefficient, so they learn fastest where they can try a huge number of attempts against a cheap checker. That, he says, is why math fell first, and "the dollar cost of verifying some idea in ML research" is "many, many orders of magnitude" higher than checking a proof. He also made the episode's sharpest aside. About the pretraining compute-multiplier plot from Anthropic's IPO roadshow, he said: "I don't trust their judgment of what's going on, but I can't explain why." He added that he isn't accusing them of fabricating numbers, only that the plot "doesn't mean what they are portraying it to mean."

Here's the chain that ties today together, Ollie. Math is falling because its verifier is cheap, even if, as Bernstein's list reminds us, it's not perfectly sound. Cryptography is nervous because number theory is that same cheap-verifier domain. The data market Greenberg describes exists to build verifiers, tasks just hard enough to train on, because, in his words, "if you make them too hard, they become evals." And Erdil says ML research will move slower because its verifier is a training run. Biology sits at the far end of that scale. Your verifier is a plate, a mouse or a patient, so the most valuable thing you can build may not be a smarter agent. It's a cheaper, faster, still-trustworthy check on what the agent proposes: an assay readout, a held-out experimental benchmark, anything that turns a month-long verdict into a day-long one.

The thing to watch: whether the rumored lab cryptanalysis announcement shows up, and whether its authors publish their own checks of the limits, key sizes, constants and checker versions, before the headlines do. That's the pulse for today.
