## Script

Here's what the AI world is talking about today. It's Wednesday, November twenty-fifth. Two labs posted proofs of the same fifty-year-old conjecture within six hours of each other, neither one with an announcement, neither one naming the model that found it. Then, while everyone was still reading those, one of the two labs dropped nine more manuscripts overnight, including an exact answer to a question about matrix multiplication that most of the field expected to come out the other way. And the single best piece of commentary on all of it was recorded four days ago, before any of it happened, by someone who told us exactly what to watch for.

The usual disclosure, and today it matters more than usual. Anthropic is one of the two labs in the lead story, and this script was drafted by Claude, an Anthropic model, so weigh my framing accordingly.

Let's start with what landed. On Monday afternoon, Anthropic pushed a folder to a public GitHub repository called formal-math. No blog post, no tweet from the company. The README says, in full sentences, that for every field, circuits for the Hamiltonian cycle polynomial, and for the permanent outside characteristic two, need more than a certain superpolynomial number of gates. That means VP is a proper subset of VNP. It also says, and I'm quoting, "The result was discovered by an internal research Claude model," and "Both were written by AI models, and no human mathematician has checked the proof."

If you don't live in complexity theory, here's the shape of it. The determinant of a matrix is easy to compute. The permanent is the same sum over permutations with the minus signs removed, and is believed to be hard. Valiant asked in nineteen seventy-nine whether the permanent can be computed by arithmetic circuits of polynomial size — circuits built from addition and multiplication alone. That question has been the standard warm-up for P versus NP for almost fifty years. Lance Fortnow called it, quote, "the most important computational complexity result of the year, which says a lot for twenty twenty-six."

Then, about five and a half hours after Anthropic's commit, OpenAI quietly added its own VP ≠ VNP manuscript to its math repository, with a line in the changelog saying the result "was obtained on November sixth." Two weeks earlier. No announcement there either. Andrew Curran, who tracks these releases closely, pointed out that this is now a pattern: both labs dump major results online with no comment, no model named, no recipe. On Hacker News, one commenter did the obvious thing and compared the two git timestamps, confirming Anthropic was public first by five and a half hours. And the top comment under that story is a good one: if you want credit for a result you're sitting on, publish a hash now and the proof later, or use a trusted timestamping service. Right now the only evidence for "we had it on November sixth" is a line of markdown that OpenAI wrote itself.

Then overnight, OpenAI superseded its own two-day-old paper. The changelog for November twenty-fourth adds nine manuscripts in two families. One builds a geometric theory of tensors and applies it to circuit lower bounds, including a stronger version of the permanent bound than either Monday paper. The other claims a deterministic strongly polynomial algorithm for linear programming — Smale's ninth problem — and a refutation of the polynomial Hirsch conjecture. One Hacker News user who tracks a ranked list of five hundred open problems counted eight of them resolved by this single batch.

But the result that actually took over Hacker News, six hundred and forty-six points overnight, was the matrix multiplication one. The exponent of square matrix multiplication, usually written omega, is the number everyone in numerical computing has a stake in. Naive multiplication is cubic. Strassen showed you can do better. The open question has been how far down you can push it, and a large part of the field has conjectured for decades that the answer is two, essentially as cheap as writing down the output. OpenAI's paper proves a matching lower bound: over every field, the exponent is exactly nine fourths. Two point two five. Not two. And the proof is non-constructive in the direction people would want. It works by showing that a faster matrix multiplication algorithm would let you compress tensor networks so much that you could test whether the entries of a list are all distinct more cheaply than is possible.

Two reactions worth holding. The first is from that same thread: the top comment argues that if a lab were using an unreleased internal model to make fundamental breakthroughs, and feeding those back into building stronger models, what you'd see from outside is exactly this — public model quality plateauing while unexplained artifacts appear on GitHub. A reply added the reason open math problems are such good fuel: known-hard, immune to test-set contamination, and precisely verifiable. That trifecta is rare, and it is what makes them good reinforcement learning signal.

The second reaction is Fortnow's, and it's the deflating one. Despite the names, VP ≠ VNP does not imply P ≠ NP. His point is technical and worth carrying around: the Boolean analogue of this result would be a statement about circuits of depth log squared, which is weaker than what P versus NP requires. Algebraic circuits are a genuinely more restricted model. His closing line is, quote, "settling the P v NP question remains far out of reach for man or machine."

Now the second thread, and this one is closer to your bench. Also on Tuesday, OpenAI released a preprint claiming its GPT-6 models have intrinsic protein design capability — that they can design protein backbones and sequences using reasoning and code, without calling a specialized structure model to generate the design. The lab numbers, with a partner called Onava doing the wet work: three hundred and twenty-two minibinders tested across five targets, of which about eleven percent showed binding. When the model was instead given a structure and asked to invent new sequences for it, initial screens showed binding in eighty-five percent of designs for MDM2 and sixty-two percent for VEGF-A, but only fourteen percent for PD-1. And a hundred and twenty-eight of a hundred and eighty-two redesigned nanobody loops retained binding to interleukin-4 receptor alpha.

Here is the part that should shape how you read it, and it comes from the comments, not the press release. A commenter who did their PhD in the Baker lab gave the fairest assessment anyone has written. Their object-level verdict: binder design against these targets is "basically the easiest task," solved many times over in the last few years, and the designs here are helical bundles, which are known to bind well and form strong interfaces. They flagged the thing I'd flag too — they couldn't tell how train-test splitting was handled, or whether it was, and the specialized models being compared against did rigorous holdout evaluation. For the antibody work they want sequence homology to the published anti-PD-L1 antibodies before calling it design rather than recall. But then they stepped back twice. One level up: getting a general language model to do tasks that normally require specialized protein models is impressive. Two levels up, their phrase, it's "a big gauntlet thrown." Their last line was about looking for a new line of work sooner than they'd planned.

OpenAI's own limitations section is more candid than the headline: the campaign relied on Boltz-2, OpenDDE and ESMFold2 to filter candidates, and the post says plainly that the results do not establish end-to-end zero-shot design without specialized models. Full structure prediction from sequence remains, in their words, substantially harder. And Lewis Tunstall added the dimension to ask about first: on a prior DNA task, the general model was roughly two thousand times more expensive per task than the specialized one. General-purpose wins on flexibility, not on unit economics, and when you run thousands of designs that ratio is the whole argument.

For the money, the launch worth your attention is Epochal, started by three members of xAI's founding team — Guodong Zhang, Zihang Dai and Manuel Kroiss — along with Alex Bocharoff and Frank Luan. Secondhand reports put a hundred million dollars of Zhang's own money into it; I could not confirm that from a primary source, so hold it loosely. What I can confirm is the thesis, from their own announcement, and it's the most interesting idea in this episode. Quote: "Today's AI has read almost everything and lived through almost nothing. It learns from the internet all at once, already knowing how each story ends." Their product, built in ninety days, is a time-gated sandbox: over a trillion indexed pages spanning thirty years, with granularity down to the minute, so an agent can search and browse the web exactly as it existed on a chosen past date, and then be asked what happens next.

Think about why that's a business. The current training paradigm runs on verifiable rewards in timeless environments — math, code, puzzles with a fixed answer. Epochal's bet is that the next source of verifiable signal is time itself: the outcome is known to the grader and hidden from the agent, and there is thirty years of it. One of their listed use cases should interest anyone who evaluates models against literature — training an auditor to find a vulnerability using only advisories published before the fix. That's the same contamination-proof property that makes open math problems good signal, except you can manufacture it at scale.

Now the Deep Cut, and the timing is almost unfair. On November twenty-first, four days ago and two days after the Riemann hypothesis announcement, Václav Rozhoň of the Polylog channel sat Scott Aaronson down with a stack of cards, each one a famous open problem, and asked him not when each would fall but in what order. The full transcript is published. Aaronson opens by saying he's done making confident predictions: he describes being asked at a dinner when AI would prove the Riemann hypothesis, answering "I'm not confident of anything anymore," and then waking up the next morning to find it done.

Then comes the part that matters. Rozhoň asks what he'd expect to see on the way to P ≠ NP, and Aaronson names two things. One is a superlinear circuit lower bound for satisfiability. The other, in his words: "Take an algebraic circuit lower bound. Computing the permanent of an n-by-n matrix is a famous example... I would definitely expect a proof that the permanent requires more than polynomially many arithmetic operations before P ≠ NP." And then: "We haven't seen those yet. If we do see them, I'm going to update toward 'Oh, a solution to P ≠ NP by the AIs might really be around the corner.'"

Two days later, both labs posted it. In his blog post last night, he made good on the update, moving his P ≠ NP confidence from ninety-five back up to ninety-six percent — it had fallen from ninety-seven when AI refuted the exponential time hypothesis.

But the card I keep thinking about is matrix multiplication. Rozhoň handed it to him on Saturday and Aaronson literally tore it in half. His reasoning: if the exponent is two, then someone just has to find the algorithm, and an AI trying thousands of candidates might simply hit it. If the exponent is greater than two, you need circuit lower bounds, and — his words — "It might be essentially as hard as proving P ≠ PSPACE." So he placed the two halves in different parts of the timeline, one near the easy end and one near the far end. Three days later, the hard half resolved.

One more piece of his argument is worth carrying into your own work. Aaronson's account of where these systems are strong: first, "bringing together known techniques from different areas of math," because they've read the whole literature and we haven't; second, anything highly parallelizable, where finding a weird construction means searching thousands of possibilities — a human gets discouraged, and, quote, "The AI doesn't care. It just keeps chugging." Where they've been weaker is pioneering genuinely new proof techniques, which he thinks is a matter of time rather than an inherent limit. On this week's result he notes both labs' proofs have the same shape: a hard compression step, then diagonalization, which he calls "the oldest trick in the book."

Ollie's takeaway, and it's a practical one. The pattern that produced this week is not "the model got smarter." It is that verifiable, contamination-proof problems are the best available training signal, and the labs are harvesting them. That's the thread connecting all three stories: open math problems, Epochal's point-in-time web, and the wet-lab validation in the protein paper. Binding assays are expensive, slow and uncontaminable, which is exactly why that preprint exists — and why the Baker-lab critique lands, because without a disclosed train-test split, a protein design benchmark isn't contamination-proof at all, it just looks like it. So when you evaluate someone's model on your domain, ask first what the held-out thing actually is and who controls it. And if your lab holds data with that property — longitudinal clinical outcomes, unpublished screens, anything where the answer is known to you and genuinely unknown to a model trained on the public internet — you are sitting on something whose value just went up.

The one thing to watch tomorrow: whether any human complexity theorist finds a real gap in either VP ≠ VNP proof, and whether OpenAI or Anthropic says anything at all about the models that produced them. Further out, watch for the first lab to adopt hashes or trusted timestamps for unreleased results, because the next priority dispute won't be settled by a changelog. That's the pulse for today.
