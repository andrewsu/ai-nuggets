Here's what the AI world is talking about today. Over the weekend, one question kept coming up in different forms: as these systems pull ahead of the people using them, who's doing the checking? Andrej Karpathy says the gap between what frontier AI can do and what most people think it can do is getting wider, and that human comprehension is starting to fall behind. Sakana put out a self-improvement loop where the model grades its own work and trains on what it liked. Satya Nadella published the opposite principle: no model should ever verify its own work. And on the Cognitive Revolution, Nathan Labenz reported that a frontier-lab executive told him there's probably a level of intelligence we just shouldn't go past.

Twitter Pulse first. And for once, it really is Twitter. We found a route that lets us read posts on X directly, so today's quotes come from the posts themselves, not from summaries.

The biggest post of the weekend is Karpathy's, from Sunday: about thirty-two thousand likes, nearly four million views, and four hundred fifty points with six hundred fifty comments on Hacker News. He quotes his own post from April, which said two groups of people were talking past each other about AI, and says the gap is now "more a sharp funnel." He gives his own napkin math for four tiers. About six billion people have barely touched an LLM. One to two billion are casual free-tier users who see, in his words, "derpy chatbots." Around twenty million professionals in math and code see first-hand that projects that used to take weeks or months "can now be completed by agents with a prompt." And about five thousand people have access to frontier systems inside the labs, where it looks like "swarms of thousands of agents collaborating over weeks." Then the line that matters: "human review and comprehension are starting to fall behind."

What's new compared with April is the explanation for why the funnel exists. He gives three causes. First, the payoff scales with how big and ambitious the problem is, so if you ask for a paragraph, you never see what the system can do. Second, capability is jagged. It peaks in work that's "digital, verifiable and economically valuable," because it comes from reinforcement learning on environments chosen by revenue potential. Third is simply access: free tier, paid tier, internal.

The sharpest pushback on Hacker News went after the twenty million. rico735 said the twenty million don't actually agree; the real split is between people who still read the code and people "who don't review code and are confident that the AI has made no mistakes." tripleee argued that the cases that really work are ports and translations, where the original code already serves as a spec the agent can check itself against. And usernomdeguerre pointed out that Karpathy's own second cause undercuts his framing: if the gains land only where a machine can check the output, then most people are in the lower tiers because most of their work can't be checked that way, not because they haven't caught up.

A blog post that also hit the front page Sunday makes rico735's point concrete. Piotr Sarnacki looked at DHH's agent-written rewrites of the Campfire chat app in Rust, Elixir, and Go. His central claim is "if your prompt is not specific enough, many decisions are a coin flip." Under heavy load, it delivered about one percent of new-message notifications. Sarnacki traced that to one setting: a broadcast buffer of two hundred fifty-six. Raising it got delivery from about fourteen percent to about ninety at a hundred requests a second, but the worst-case delivery delay went from eleven seconds to over two minutes, which he argues is worse. Nobody chose that trade-off; the agent did. His conclusion: "you still need to know what you're doing."

The second topic is the one I'd spend your attention on, because the two documents are each other's opposite. On Sunday, Sakana's MASS project page reached Hacker News. The paper is from Sakana with Berkeley's Somayeh Sojoudi and Matei Zaharia. The idea is homogeneous recursive self-improvement: one model plays three roles. As the executor, it does a research task with a team of subagents. As the optimizer, it rewrites the team's workflow. As the evaluator, it judges which result was better. The best self-judged runs become fine-tuning data, and then the improved model takes on all three roles again. Their reasoning is that at the frontier, human experts can't supervise fast enough, so "the model itself may then inevitably be the most capable supervisor available."

There are real findings here. They started from a twenty-seven-billion-parameter Qwen model and ran two rounds. On twelve synthetic research tasks in finance, robotics, and pharmacy, the external judges preferred the round-two model over the base model about seventy percent of the time. The most interesting result is that they trained only on task execution, yet the optimizer and evaluator got better too. The model's agreement with outside judges rose from seventy-three to ninety-three percent. Another surprise: when they upgraded only the evaluator to a stronger outside model, it barely helped. The bottleneck was the optimizer, the part that decides what to change.

Now the fine print, which matters for how far the "recursive self-improvement" label stretches. The headline public-benchmark gain is score per output token, one point two to one point six times the base model, which partly measures getting more efficient. On ScienceAgentBench, actual success rose from about twenty-nine percent to thirty-two. On MLR-Bench, the model finished a paper in fifty of sixty runs instead of thirty-one, but the average score of the papers it did finish went down slightly. Coding benchmarks went slightly backwards. And the twelve training tasks were written by GPT-5.5. On Hacker News, Agentlien found it "very suspicious that a paper on RSI would stop after just two rounds," and cootsnuck said "the code they released doesn't match what's in the paper."

Here's the contrast. On Saturday morning, Satya Nadella posted a long X article titled "Models as Insider Risks in the Super Intelligence Era." His first principle is model diversity: "No one model should become the sole dependency for an important outcome or be responsible for verifying its own work." The next one goes further: "Validation must be independent of the intelligence being validated." He also warns against the obvious workaround, models checking models, because you "end up with an opaque model inside an opaque orchestration layer, watched by another opaque model. Essentially nested black boxes." His closing line: "The most trustworthy Super Intelligence system will not be the one with the model we trust most. It will be the one that enables us to trust the model the least."

What nobody on either thread pointed out is that MASS already follows Nadella's rule where it counts. Every number Sakana reports as progress comes from GPT-5.5 and Claude Opus 4.8 acting as judges outside the loop. The loop grades itself. The paper doesn't trust that grade, so it brings in other models to measure progress.

A quick third item, on the human side of the funnel. A team including Berkeley's Brian Christian presented the peer-reviewed version of its persistence study at the Conference on Language Modeling this week. Across three randomized trials with twelve hundred twenty-two online participants, people who had a chatbot alongside them while solving fraction problems, or SAT-style reading questions, did better while the tool was there. When it was taken away, they got more questions wrong and skipped more of them than people who never had the chatbot at all. Christian put it this way: "This is not a story about fractions and SAT problems." Put that next to Karpathy's funnel and the question becomes whether the people supervising these systems are still building the skills they need to supervise them.

Money Moves. Briefly, Manus has closed the round we reported as talks last month: more than five hundred million dollars, led by Boyu Capital and IDG, its first raise since China forced the Meta deal to unwind. Its new Cue app, which gives personal agents their own email, phone number, and wallet, is a bet on closing Karpathy's gap from below.

The more telling money number came from a podcast. On the same Cognitive Revolution episode, Thomas Somers, co-founder of the chip company Positron, said his company's token spend briefly "eclipsed human salaries," peaking above a hundred thousand dollars a day, before it came down as they optimized and as Opus 5.5 did many of their tasks at about a quarter of the price. His rule is to run no real development work on anything but the best model: "I don't care if it's a tenth of the cost." Then swyx described his Kill My SaaS bounty: ten thousand dollars to anyone who can replace a software subscription his company pays over forty thousand a year for. It worked, and his old-school events team switched over. His catch matches the Sarnacki post: "evals are the problem," because people spent no thought on their entries and checking them took all the effort. His line: "if you haven't actually tried to completely vibe code a SaaS that you use, you don't understand how models are still very bad at all this." Put together: budgets are moving from salaries and subscriptions to tokens, and the cost that remains is checking the output.

Now the Deep Cut, from the same October 8 episode. Nathan Labenz reported back from The Curve, a conference held last weekend at Lighthaven in Berkeley. The event ran under Chatham House rules, so this is his secondhand account of what founders, executives, and top researchers at frontier companies said, without names. Quotes are from the show's published transcript. The argument has four steps.

Step one: the biggest divide is inside versus outside the labs. Labenz said the room had converged on the idea that "it does look like the AIs are gonna get really powerful," but "probably the biggest divide in terms of perspective is really just inside the frontier companies, outside the frontier companies." In his words, "Everybody knows that they're living in the future." That's Karpathy's five thousand, described from the room they were in.

Step two: one senior lab executive told him pretraining "continues to deliver," and maybe even better than the scaling laws predict, because of synthetic data. The model's own reasoning tokens get turned back into pretraining data, which Labenz called "one way in which recursive self improvement is happening." The same executive believes the latest models already have the research taste needed for paradigm-level breakthroughs, but that it's "an elicitation problem," and "this is why you need 10,000 agents" to crack something like a Millennium Prize problem.

Step three is the line in the episode title. The executive said "there likely is ... a level of intelligence that we just shouldn't go past." Labenz called it the first time he'd heard that from a frontier-lab leader. He followed up with what he expected to be a straw man: would you sign on to a cap of something like ten to the twenty-seven FLOPs for the next pretraining run? "He basically said, yeah. I think that could be reasonable. The end." Labenz added his own caveat right away: with so much compute going into synthetic data, "you can definitely play a sort of shell game of hide the compute."

Step four explains why the race stays close. The two or three leading labs say everyone else keeps up partly through a "distillation conduit": RL-environment vendors use Claude to build training environments and sell them to competing labs. On the safety side, labs are now telling models to hack their own RL environments so the holes can be patched, and they're seeing what Labenz called "a proto scaling law": the cleaner the environments, the less the models cheat later. The labs expect safety to be the bottleneck, but they think they can ship the next one or two generations. After that, in Labenz's paraphrase, "all bets are off." One person told him that within a year, "it will not be possible to do the very best science without AIs playing a big role in it."

Here's the takeaway, Ollie. Every story today runs into the same limit: generating work is getting cheap, and supervising it isn't. For biomedical agents, three habits follow. First, take Nadella's rule literally: never let the model that ran an analysis also be the one that judges it. Even Sakana, which built its whole loop on self-judgment, brought in other models to measure its results. Second, the claims you can most safely hand to an agent are the ones a machine can check. Everywhere else, the human still has to understand the output, and per Berkeley, that ability fades quickly if you stop using it. Third, when someone from inside the funnel says the view is different from there, it's worth noticing that what one of them asked for was a cap.
