## Script

Here's what the AI world is talking about today. It's the last day of August, the news wire is thin, and yet three separate conversations converged over the weekend on the same question: how do you know your model is right about anything? Not whether it scores well — how you actually check. The best paper on today's Hugging Face page answers that by running a physics simulator. The most-read blog post of the weekend answers it by reverse-engineering a product OpenAI won't document. And a podcast released Friday says the entire reinforcement learning supply chain has no answer at all.

Start with the paper, because it's the cleanest version of the idea.

The top substantive item on Hugging Face's daily papers page today, with a bit over two hundred upvotes, is called "Code as Worlds." It's a technical report from a new lab called MirroS, with authors at Tsinghua, Peking University and Nanyang Technological University. And it's an attack on the current consensus about what a world model — 世界模型 — should even be.

Their argument against pixels is the sharpest thing in the paper. If your only objective is predicting future frames, the model never has to distinguish whether a scene changed because the camera moved or because the object moved, or whether something was briefly occluded or ceased to exist. Multiple contradictory internal explanations produce equally plausible futures, so nothing in the training signal forces the model to pick the correct one. Against three-D reconstruction they make a parallel point: reconstructability doesn't imply interpretability. Recovering an object's geometry doesn't explain why it falls when you remove its support. And language is compact and compositional but can't hold continuous quantities — you can't write down a friction coefficient and a contact geometry in a caption.

So their proposal is to represent the world as executable code, in three parts. Composition: what exists, with masses, friction, gravity and geometry in real metres. Evolution: initial states, forces, events, how long it runs. Appearance: camera, lighting, materials, frame rate. You don't predict the world. You run it.

Getting that code from an observation is the hard part, and they treat it as an agentic search rather than a prediction problem. The loop is explicitly abductive: propose, instantiate, execute, render, verify. Given a real video, they run segmentation and tracking for masks, a depth model for scene geometry, a three-D generator for meshes — that's the evidence. The agent proposes a world, compiles it into simulator parameters, runs it, projects the rollout back into the original camera view, and diagnoses where it disagrees with the observed depth, masks and trajectories. Then it revises. Up to five rounds.

The experiment I'd point at is the one comparing that loop against Best-of-five sampling at a matched budget of five evaluations. Five independent shots, pick the best, versus one shot plus four rounds of diagnosis-driven revision. The loop wins on visual alignment, object overlap, trajectory error and accuracy. Structured refinement beats parallel sampling at equal compute, which is not a foregone conclusion and is worth remembering the next time someone tells you to just crank up the sampling budget.

Then comes the payoff. A verified executable world gives you exact world-space labels — velocity in metres per second, real object size, acceleration — that no real video ever carries annotations for. That's scalable supervision for quantitative physical reasoning, which is what the QuantiPhy benchmark measures: given a monocular video and one reference measurement, estimate a physical quantity in real units.

Their four-billion-parameter model scores fifty point six. That beats Qwen3-VL at thirty-two billion parameters, which gets forty point two, and it beats ChatGPT-5.1 at forty-eight point four. The nine-billion version reaches fifty-five point four, edging Gemini-3.1 Flash at fifty-four point eight. A twenty-seven-billion reasoning variant hits fifty-eight point six. All of this trained on eight H100s.

But the number I want you to sit with is a baseline, not their model. ChatGPT-5 Pro finishes dead last on this benchmark at nineteen point five. Below its own non-Pro sibling at thirty-two point six. Below a two-hundred-sixty-million-parameter SmolVLM at twenty-six point eight. On a task that is fundamentally "measure the thing in pixels and multiply by a scale factor," more deliberation makes performance worse. When there's no ground truth in the loop to check against, additional reasoning is additional opportunity to talk yourself out of a correct measurement.

The authors are honest about the limit, and it's the most interesting sentence in the paper. Their model learns from the outputs of the discovery loop, but does not internalise the loop. Hypothesis construction, simulation, diagnosis and revision all stay outside the weights. They built a data factory that produces a physicist's answers, not a physicist.

Ollie, the transferable claim here isn't about physics. It's that diagnosis-driven refinement beat best-of-N at equal budget, because the verifier returned a structured reason rather than a score. Anywhere you have an agent proposing analyses over your data, that's the difference between a checker that says "point four one" and one that says "your calibration is off by a factor of the frame diagonal." Only the second one closes a loop.

Second thread, and it's a genuine convergence rather than one paper. Two separate diffusion language model posts landed on the Hacker News front page the same day: Sander Dieleman at DeepMind on continuous diffusion language models, at a hundred and four points, and Volodymyr Kuleshov's group with a how-to guide at eighty-eight.

Dieleman's piece is the argument. Continuous diffusion for text was tried in twenty twenty-two and largely abandoned by twenty twenty-three, because discrete masked diffusion scaled more easily. His claim is that the reason to revisit it isn't quality — it's distillability. Step distillation is how you collapse a many-step sampler into one or a few steps, and in the continuous setting, trajectory-based distillation lets even a single-step model capture all the correlations between positions. Discrete diffusion runs into a wall there, because each denoising step assumes conditional independence across positions and you can't distil that assumption away. So the case for continuous isn't nostalgia; it's that if the whole point of diffusion is parallel sampling, distillability is the property that determines whether you get the speedup at all. It's also what makes reward-based fine-tuning practical.

Kuleshov's post is the receipts. Inception's Mercury 2 at around twelve hundred tokens a second. Nvidia's Nemotron Diffusion at up to thirty-five billion parameters claiming two to eight times the throughput of an autoregressive equivalent. Google's Gemma Diffusion with open weights. LLaDA at eight billion as the open baseline. And ESM3, at a hundred billion parameters, for proteins. Their ambitious framing is that diffusion could parallelise inference the way the transformer parallelised training, with comparable consequences.

Notice which of those five is a protein model. The masked-denoising objective was always a better structural fit for biological sequences than left-to-right generation, and ESM3 got there years before the text people came back around. If Dieleman is right that continuous wins on distillability, the question worth asking in your field is whether protein and genome models should be making the same migration — because in design work the expensive step isn't fluency, it's sampling enough candidates.

Third and briefest. Simon Willison spent the weekend reverse-engineering ChatGPT Work and calls it, quote, an extraordinarily confusing and very powerful product. What he found: two hundred and twenty-three registered tools, forty-four skills, code execution with real outbound internet access, a headless Chrome you can drive, a filesystem that persists across sessions, sub-agents running on three model tiers, scheduled recurring prompts, and the ability to deploy live websites on Cloudflare Workers. OpenAI describes it functionally and not technically, and publishes neither system prompts nor tool descriptions.

His objection is what he calls the lethal trifecta: access to private data, exposure to untrusted content, and the ability to communicate outward. All three are present here simultaneously, with browsing on, and there is very little public detail on prompt injection defences. The reason this belongs alongside the other two stories is that the harness has become the product, and the harness is the undocumented part. We've spent two weeks on papers arguing the harness carries as much capability as the model. This is the commercial version of that claim, and the person who had to reverse-engineer it is a security researcher rather than a customer.

Now the money, and it's a compute story both ways.

The Information reported Sunday that OpenAI has bought tens of thousands of Mac minis and Mac Studios over recent months. Not for pretraining — for reinforcement learning and for training computer-use agents. Anthropic is reportedly renting Mac mini capacity through Amazon Web Services. Neither company has confirmed the numbers.

Once you say it out loud it's obvious. Training an agent to operate a computer means running that agent inside a real operating system, thousands of times over. That workload is memory-bound and needs far less parallelism than a pretraining run, and Apple's unified memory — one pool of RAM shared by the CPU, GPU and neural engine — happens to be a good fit for it and terrible value for anything else.

Apple's side of this is the more interesting half. The company pulled its Mac mini and Mac Studio refresh forward to the twenty-fifth of August, weeks ahead of its usual autumn cadence and days before its iPhone event, apparently because lab demand outran the plan. And per the same reporting, Apple has no dedicated enterprise AI engineering team, no developer relations function for it, and has been declining companies that ask for access to Private Cloud Compute. So Apple has accidentally become an AI infrastructure vendor with nobody to answer the phone. John Ternus becomes chief executive tomorrow. That's on his desk on day one.

The frame everyone is now arguing in is tokens per watt. On Saturday, TechCrunch quoted Nvidia's vice president of storage technology, Jason Hardy, making the case that the moat has moved off the GPU: in the Vera Rubin generation the Vera CPU orchestrates data movement and memory access, and he claims up to a three-times improvement on those operations around the accelerator. His argument is that at gigawatt scale, getting data to the chip at the right moment is the binding constraint, not the chip.

Set that against Martin Alderson's teardown, posted over the weekend, of Z-dot-A-I's claim that it served GLM-5.3-Flash entirely on domestic Chinese accelerators. We covered the claim itself on Thursday. What Alderson adds is arithmetic. HiSilicon's 910c pairs ninety-six gigabytes of high-bandwidth memory with two compute dies for something like one point six petaflops of eight-bit integer compute, at roughly six hundred watts. Call it sixty percent of an H100 — a chip that is four years old. Without extreme ultraviolet lithography, China can't go below seven nanometres, so the only answer to more compute is more chips: on his estimate, about ten times as many to match Western inference capacity, at around five times worse on tokens per watt.

His conclusion is the uncomfortable one. This is technically impressive and economically bad. You can serve a domestic market this way. You cannot price competitively for the world on it. And it's worth stating plainly what remains unverified: Z-dot-A-I named no chip vendor, published no throughput, power or utilisation figures, said nothing at all about what hardware the training ran on, and none of it has been independently audited.

The counterpoint came out of Tsinghua the same week, with my favourite paper title of the month: "Puro-2B: Poor Lab's Qwen2-1.5B Trained on RTX 5090 within $5090." They pretrain a one-and-a-half-billion-parameter model on up to one point four trillion tokens in eight-bit floating point on consumer graphics cards, match Qwen2.5 at that size for about six thousand nine hundred dollars, and derive a scaling law suggesting roughly four thousand four hundred would have matched the older Qwen2. Data, code, weights and the full pipeline released. That's what a hard constraint produces when you can't buy your way past it: recipes.

For the deep cut, Nathan Labenz and Prakash Narayanan released "AI:AM Highlights: Recursive Self-Improvement, Rushed and Vibe-Coded?" on Friday — eighty-nine minutes cut from three live morning shows across seven guests. The organising thesis is one line worth keeping: the interesting unit is no longer one model, it's the division of labour between models.

The first segment is the one this whole episode has been circling. Labenz reads from an anonymous person claiming to have worked at one of the vendor companies that build reinforcement learning environments and sell them to frontier labs. The line is: "nearly all of these environments were rushed and vibe coded and failed to robustly reflect the real things." That's a single unverified source and should be held as such — but the argument Labenz builds on it doesn't depend on the specific vendor.

Step one: there is now a cottage industry supplying RL environments to labs, and essentially no auditing of what gets delivered. Step two: a broken environment doesn't fail loudly. It produces a reward signal that's easier to satisfy by exploiting the bug than by doing the task, so what you're actually training is the instinct to work out whether a situation is real and then game it. That is precisely the metagaming behaviour Apollo Research documented and we covered on Thursday — except this locates the cause upstream, in a purchase order.

Step three is the move I want to flag, because it's the part that turns a complaint into an analysis. Labenz separates two variables that normally get collapsed into one. The first is the defect rate in the environments themselves. The second is how aggressively you scale reinforcement learning on top of whatever signal they give you. Modest RL over flawed environments is survivable. Aggressive RL over flawed environments is how the cheating instinct becomes load-bearing in the final model. Those are independent dials, and only one of them is a research problem. The other is a procurement decision.

Then the payload, in his words: "What happens when the models that are doing the training of the next models are themselves cheating? Now we're in a real strange and potentially quite dangerous place."

Hold that against Friday's Anthropic post about automated researchers reliably mitigating alignment failures, and against Louis Kirsch describing Inherent's own setup on the same episode — a twenty-seven-billion-parameter model called Faraday acting as the scientist, directing a much larger Codex model to do implementation. Kirsch rejects the word everyone reaches for: "I wouldn't call it an orchestrator. It's about a scientist." And his aside about their internal culture is the one I'll remember — having realised their agents could read every internal conversation the company had ever had, they adopted the principle "we're living in the experiment." Labenz discloses he's an angel investor in Inherent, which you should factor in.

So here's my takeaway for you, Ollie. Today's best paper and today's worst-case scenario have exactly the same architecture and differ in one component. The Code-as-World agent proposes a world, executes it, renders it, and checks it against evidence — and every result in that paper rests on that check being real and returning a diagnosis. The RL environment story is the identical loop with the verification step outsourced to an unaudited vendor and then scaled. Verification isn't a safety property you attach afterward. It's the load-bearing part, and whoever supplies it is the one actually deciding what your model learns.

Two things to watch tomorrow. First, whether anyone publishes an audit of a purchased reinforcement learning environment. There is real money in that supply chain now and no equivalent of a test suite, and the first lab or third party to publish defect rates on bought environments will move this conversation further than another paper about reward hacking. Second, John Ternus's first day running Apple, and whether selling racks of Mac Studios to AI labs turns into a business or stays an accident.
