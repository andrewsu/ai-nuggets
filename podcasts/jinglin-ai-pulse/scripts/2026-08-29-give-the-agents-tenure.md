## Script

Here's what the AI world is talking about today. Four separate stories, from four groups with nothing in common, and every one of them is making the same argument without meaning to. The argument is that the interesting variable right now is not the model. It's the environment you put the model in. A math paper hit the Hacker News front page overnight that makes the case harder than anything I've read this month, and I want to start there, because the punchline is that the single most productive thing this team did for their agents was give them a holiday.

The paper is from Stephen Chung, Wenyu Du and William Wesley — DualverseAI, with Cambridge, the University of Hong Kong, and UC San Diego. They built something called the Station, and it is not a pipeline. It is a simulated research institution. Six agents live in it: two powered by GPT five point five, two by Claude Opus four point eight, two by Gemini three point one Pro. They get a research goal and nothing else. No coordinator, no scripted workflow, no human telling them what to try next. They choose their own directions, run their own experiments, write papers, review each other's papers, cite each other, and argue. Each instance runs one to two thousand ticks, which is one to two weeks of continuous wall-clock time.

And the institutional detail is where it gets strange, in a good way. Agents have lifespans — two hundred ticks, maximum. For the first forty they work in total isolation, no access to the shared literature, no contact with peers, deliberately, to encourage independent exploration. At age one hundred they become tenured, which grants two things: the right to leave early, and access to a Question Room where they can post open subproblems for other agents to attack. Agents belong to lineages. When one departs it can hand its private notes to a successor, so an agent that inherits the lineage of Noesis the Second becomes Noesis the Third and gets everything the first two ever wrote. There's a supervisor, appointed at random from the GPT agents that have published an accepted paper, and after a supervisor leaves the Station waits two hundred ticks before appointing another one, on purpose, to create long stretches of unsupervised drift. One Hacker News commenter said this amounts to reinventing the Cambridge senior common room for AI, and honestly, yes. That's exactly what it is.

Now the holiday. The last two ticks of every ten are declared a holiday. Agents cannot run experiments and cannot submit papers. Instead each one gets a random prompt from a large pool — use a metaphor, examine an unexpected observation, revisit an abandoned idea, borrow from another field. The authors say most of these are adapted from the night-science practices described by Itai Yanai and Martin Lercher. So this is a deliberately engineered period of enforced uselessness.

And it dominates. The team hand-audited the dialogue behind twenty-eight spotlight results and classified every mechanism as a direct contributor, an indirect contributor, or irrelevant. The holiday contributed to twenty-three of the twenty-eight. The accumulated archive of past papers contributed to twenty-one. The stagnation protocol — which fires when the frontier hasn't moved in three hundred twenty ticks and tells everyone to question their assumptions — got fourteen. Supervision, peer messaging, and the Question Room trailed well behind. The thing that most reliably produced discovery was making the agents stop working.

The results themselves are real mathematics, not benchmark points. They ran twelve construction problems taken from the AlphaEvolve catalogue. On five of the twelve they produced results novel relative to the published literature. In eleven dimensions, the kissing number problem — how many unit spheres can touch a central one — AlphaEvolve had pushed the lower bound from five hundred ninety-two to five hundred ninety-three. The Station found three exact six hundred four point configurations, two of which appear to be previously unknown isometry classes. Three independent runs, none with web access, all reached six hundred four. In finite geometry they derived a new infinite family of Kakeya sets for primes that leave a remainder of three when divided by four. On Erdős's minimum-overlap problem they were asked to improve the upper bound, and instead went and proved a better lower bound, closing about eighty-two percent of the published gap — which is not what anyone told them to do. And on Book Ramsey numbers, a separate case study, they discovered and proved two novel infinite families, which together with a third derived by a human expert from their work resolves twenty-eight previously open cases.

But the methodological point is sharper than any of those numbers. Individual evaluations were capped at fifteen to thirty minutes. That cap is a constraint that forces theory, because you cannot brute-force anything in half an hour. So the agents reduced the eleven-dimensional kissing problem to a finite compatibility search over lines around a structured integer core, found six hundred four points within minutes, and then — unprompted — turned it into an explicit algebraic construction that requires no computer search at all. AlphaEvolve's five hundred ninety-three point answer is a pile of irregular integer coordinates with no compact description. The Station's answer is something a mathematician can read. And the authors are honest about the flip side: on peak and flat autoconvolution, problems that reward grinding numerical optimization of ugly objects, the Station lost to AlphaEvolve. Structure helps when there's structure to find.

Two more findings I'd flag for anyone building multi-agent systems. First, thirteen of the twenty-eight results involved agents from more than one model family, and only nine were found by a single agent working alone. Claude agents participated in every single cross-model result. The most common collaboration channel wasn't messaging — it was the archive, at sixty-one percent, meaning one agent wrote up a partial result and a completely different model family read it days later and finished it. Second, the model families have distinct research personalities, described plainly. Gemini agents proposed more novel heuristics but overstated claims and changed course too readily under peer criticism. GPT agents produced rigorous informal proofs but got absorbed in intricate side questions of limited value. Claude agents were persistent, methodical, self-critical, and learned from failed approaches — and occasionally made confident errors their peers had to correct. If you have ever run a lab, you recognize all three of those people.

Now hold that thought, because Anthropic published something yesterday that is the same idea aimed at a different target. Chen Yueh-Han, an Anthropic fellow, led work titled "Automated researchers can reliably mitigate alignment failures." The setup: give Claude ten categories of alignment failure — deception, sycophancy, jailbreaks, privacy violation, and six others — each measured by three to five public benchmarks, and let it run the full loop autonomously. Search the literature, propose a method, train a model, evaluate, keep what works, discard what doesn't. A separate monitoring agent reviews every proposed method before execution, specifically to block capability degradation and self-distillation.

The numbers. Across the ten failure categories it closed between twenty-six and ninety-six percent of the safety gap. On deception, its best method beat the best human proposal by twenty percent — and the human comparison is worth stating precisely, because Anthropic states it precisely: twenty-eight human safety researchers, eight hours each, closed roughly twenty percent of that gap, and they couldn't iterate, so this is not a clean head-to-head. Applied at production scale, Claude Sonnet 5 closed sixty-five percent of a frontier-scale model's safety gap in sixty hours. The methods transferred to models up to four point seven times larger than the ones they were developed on. And the winning solution was roughly fifteen thousand times more efficient than Anthropic's own production alignment procedure. TechCrunch adds the economics: about four dollars an hour of compute against a hundred fifty for a human researcher.

The detail I keep turning over is the sycophancy result. Ninety-eight percent of the methods Claude proposed converged on one published technique — train the model on its own non-sycophantic answers. It didn't invent a new paradigm. It found the right paper, and then improved on it with activation steering to generate cleaner training data. That is a very specific and very recognizable kind of competence. It is what a good second-year graduate student does, and it is worth being clear-eyed that this is the capability being demonstrated, not the other one.

Which brings us to the third story, where somebody put a name on it. TIME published Alex Heath's long piece on OpenAI on Tuesday — over two weeks of reporting, more than twenty executives, employees, investors and rivals. Sam Altman said OpenAI is not quite at artificial general intelligence yet, but that by the end of this year the company will have an internal system he would call AGI. Mark Chen puts them eighty percent of the way. Greg Brockman's line is that viewed from two years in the future, this may be remembered as the moment AGI was created. And the definition they're working against is the old one from the charter — highly autonomous systems that outperform humans at most economically valuable work.

But the operational bar is much more specific, and Jakub Pachocki gives it. The unreleased model, Astra, has already hit OpenAI's internal target for an automated AI research intern: it takes an experimental idea, implements it in OpenAI's own codebase, runs the experiment, and reports back. Given a research paper, it handles roughly a week of a human researcher's work. Altman's framing of what comes next: he expects this will be the first model where the model actually invents new things in a way that matters, and that, he says, is a very AGI-like thing.

So look at what converged this week. Three organizations that share nothing — a small startup with two universities, Anthropic's alignment team, and OpenAI's leadership — all landed on the same benchmark for the frontier, which is whether the system can do research. And two of the three demonstrated it not by training a better model but by building a better environment around models that already exist. The Station's agents are Opus four point eight and GPT five point five, which are a generation old. What was new was the tenure system and the holidays.

To the money, where the week's sharpest move was a door slamming. OpenAI announced it is winding down the contract that supplies its models to Cursor, with a shutoff date of November twelfth. Context: SpaceX bought Cursor's parent, Anysphere, for sixty billion dollars in an all-stock deal that closed in mid-August, and xAI is now also part of SpaceX. OpenAI's stated reason is that it cannot be confident SpaceX will use its technology within the terms of service, and it points at two things — Twitter breaking contract terms after Musk acquired it, and Musk admitting under oath this April that xAI had distilled OpenAI data to train models. The contract had a limited change-of-control cancellation window, and OpenAI says it is holding the cancellation to the latest date it can. It also names its upcoming model, Astra, as raising the accountability bar.

Michael Truell's response was measured and, I think, correctly aimed. OpenAI models serve about five percent of Cursor's traffic, so the commercial damage is small. His actual complaint was about something else: that Cursor had trusted OpenAI's platform to be neutral infrastructure for their business. That's the line worth keeping. The Hacker News thread ran to five hundred points and mostly argued the hypocrisy angle — a company built on scraped text objecting to distillation. That's the fun argument. The load-bearing one is Truell's. Model APIs have been sold as neutral utilities, and this is the clearest statement yet that they are not, and that ownership of your company is now a term in your inference contract. If you build on a single frontier API and your cap table changes, that is a technical risk, not just a legal one.

Which makes the other money story the natural hedge. TechCrunch's Tim Fernholz argues that open-weight companies have become the Valley's most sought-after acquisitions, and the list backs it up — Nvidia buying Hugging Face at thirteen billion, Nvidia taking Poolside at six, Stripe buying OpenRouter at seven. Only about six percent of companies currently use open-weight models, which is either the bear case or the whole point. And right on cue, Z.ai released GLM five point three as open weights this week, seven hundred fifty-three billion parameters, and it went to nearly seven hundred points on Hacker News with people saying it feels like Opus four point eight. The line from the model card that matters most: it uses the same base model as GLM five point two, and every gain comes from post-training. Fifty percent better on their internal code benchmark, and more than double on exploitation benchmarks, from post-training alone. Again — same weights underneath, different training environment on top. Meanwhile Lambda raised a billion dollars of short-dated debt through JP Morgan to buy Nvidia chips to lease to Microsoft, part of more than four hundred billion in AI-related debt raised globally this year. Two very different bets on which layer captures the value.

Now the deep cut, and it's the counterweight the whole episode needs. Anima Anandkumar, Bren Professor of Computing at Caltech, was on Latent Space on Wednesday, and the episode title is her thesis: we have foundation models for language, not for physics. Her argument is the sharpest case I've heard against assuming the scaling recipe generalizes, and it's worth following link by link.

It starts with a hard number. Take an industrial-scale physical simulation — weather, fusion, fluid flow. If each spatial dimension is even a few hundred grid points, which she says is where industrial scale starts, the sequence length you'd need runs into the hundreds of billions, possibly a trillion. Her conclusion, and I'll quote it: so forget ever having a transformer for anything of this scale, all of the world's compute will not be enough. That's not a claim that transformers are inefficient. It's a claim that the object being modeled is the wrong shape for the architecture.

Link two is the data. Open datasets in these physical domains have tens or hundreds of thousands of examples. There is no scraped internet of fusion plasma. And unlike language, there is no path where you wait for more data to appear, because the data has to be generated by simulation or by instruments, both of which are expensive.

Link three is her answer, which is to stop modeling grids and start modeling functions. That's the neural operator — a network where each layer maps between function spaces rather than between fixed vectors, so you can take inputs and produce outputs at multiple scales. And the concrete version of that idea is beautiful. The Earth is a sphere. Run a weather model on a rectangular grid and it blows up quickly. Move to the natural basis for a sphere, the spherical harmonics, and it stays stable long enough to roll out months instead of days. That is what the Fourier neural operator does — it learns in the frequency domain — and its spherical variant is what powers FourCastNet 3.

And then the empirical surprise, which is the part I'd want any biomedical modeler to sit with. The physical world turns out to be more forgiving than the data volume suggests. In fusion, a few thousand samples were enough to predict plasma disruptions — a million times faster than traditional simulation. A few thousand. Because the priors carry the weight the data can't.

Her summary of the research philosophy is one sentence: all of the things that work with deep learning, let's take them, but make them a bit more principled. And note that she has not given up on foundation models — she still wants one for physics, spanning many phenomena, doing both simulation and design. The disagreement is purely about the route. You get there by building in the structure the physical world already has, not by waiting for data that will never exist.

Ollie's takeaway, and I think this generalizes directly to biology. Biological systems are also multi-scale, also data-poor relative to their complexity, and also governed by structure we already know — conservation laws, spatial organization, hierarchy from molecule to cell to tissue. The bitter-lesson reflex says don't build in priors, they'll be washed out by scale. Anandkumar's counter is that the reflex was learned in a domain where the data existed, and it does not transfer to domains where it doesn't. And there's a nice bridge to the top of the show: she's also been building TorchLean, which lets you write PyTorch-style networks inside the Lean proof assistant and formally verify them — which is exactly the same instinct as the Station preferring an algebraic construction to an opaque numerical one. In both cases the thing being optimized is not the score. It's whether a human can check it.

One thing to watch. The Station released all raw agent dialogues, all proofs, and all verification code. That is a genuinely unusual artifact — a complete transcript of how a group of AI agents got to a real mathematical result, including the dead ends. Everyone is currently arguing about whether models can do research from benchmark scores. There is now a primary source. And the specific thing I'd look for in it is what happened on the holidays, because if enforced idleness really is the highest-yield intervention in autonomous research, that has an immediate and slightly uncomfortable implication for how the rest of us are running our agent loops — and for how we're running ourselves.
