## Script

Here's what the AI world is talking about today. Two arguments ran in parallel this week, and almost nobody noticed they were the same argument. In one, engineers spent three days debating what happens to a person who stops doing the work themselves. In the other, the market spent two days deciding it no longer believes the numbers behind the AI build-out. Both are questions about what you lose when you hand something off — and the best thing I read all week answers both of them at once, in a conversation about whether you can separate a mind's reasoning from the facts it happens to know.

Start with the engineers, because the discourse this week was unusually good.

On Thursday, Rachel Laycock, the CTO of Thoughtworks, published a short piece asking why good developers are so wary of agentic development. Her framing is what makes it worth your time. The easy answer, she says — and she calls it the lazy answer — is that they love writing code, or they're scared for their jobs. She doesn't buy it, because the people being cautious are the same people who invented continuous integration and automated testing and continuous delivery in the first place. These are early adopters by temperament. So what are they actually protecting?

Her answer is one sentence: built isn't the same as working in production. And underneath that sits a bigger question she states plainly — how much can you trust a system when nobody has experience of building it this way? Nearly everything running in production today was built the old way, by hand, by people. Everything built from now on is built the new way, and nobody has lived with that yet.

Then she gives you the data point, which is what elevates this above an opinion piece. Giles Alexander, Thoughtworks' regional CTO for Europe, ran a week-long experiment: senior engineers, infrastructure consultants, and product people building an airline application with no human writing any code. All of it by agents. Three things came out of it.

First, where the time went. The team estimated about two-thirds of their time at the whiteboard — working out the domain, drawing boundaries, deciding what happens next — and about one-third on what Laycock calls the harness: the tests, contracts, architecture checks, and pipeline that give agents feedback. Almost none of it at a keyboard writing logic. Second, and I love this detail, the product person ended up committing more feature code than anyone else on the team. Third, the uncomfortable one: the application ran, and the build was frequently red. It worked on their machine. A build that's red all the time stops meaning anything, which, as Laycock points out, is precisely the uncertainty continuous integration was invented to remove, twenty-five years ago. And it isn't just their experiment — she cites DORA's 2025 research finding that AI adoption now correlates with higher delivery throughput and lower delivery stability. Faster, and less reliable. Exactly the pair continuous delivery was built to break apart.

Same day, same site, Martin Fowler published something that looks unrelated and isn't. It's called "LLM Voice," and it's about the growing feeling he gets, reading a post, that he's reading machine output. His tell is wonderful: it's not the em-dashes, he says, it's the load-bearing tapestries. He's skeptical of detector tools, partly on accuracy, but mostly because they miss the point — the reaction to LLM voice is a human one, a sense of being in an uncanny valley of prose, and by the time you're reaching for a tool it's already too late. His advice is narrow and strict: don't have the model write your draft. Even edited, the voice is very hard to expel. Fill the prose yourself.

These two pieces are the same piece. Laycock is protecting the judgment experienced engineers developed by doing the work. Fowler is protecting the writing skill you only get by practising. Both are saying the output might be fine, and the capacity that produced it is the thing at risk.

Which is exactly why Hacker News spent Friday resurfacing an eight-month-old blog post. A SigNoz piece titled "AI Isn't Replacing SREs, It's Deskilling Them" — published back in February — hit five hundred ninety-three points on Friday. It's built on Lisanne Bainbridge's 1983 paper, "Ironies of Automation," whose finding is that the more you automate a process, the more critical the human becomes in the rare moments automation fails, and the less practice they get, so the worse they are at exactly those interventions. Automation designed to remove humans from the loop leaves them with the worst possible job: long passive monitoring punctuated by rare, high-stakes crises they're unprepared for.

The modern evidence the post assembles is the part worth carrying around. In medicine, endoscopists using AI assistance for polyp detection saw their unassisted detection rates fall from twenty-eight percent to twenty-two percent after a period of AI use. In aviation, the effect on manual flying got serious enough that the FAA now mandates manual flying time as a countermeasure — which is also the post's proposed remedy for engineering: deliberate inefficiency, designating some incidents a human handles even though the AI could. And the author names a distinction I hadn't had a word for. Deskilling means you had a capability and lost it. Never-skilling means you never built it, because by the time you arrived the machine was already doing it.

The thread pushed back. One commenter with fifteen years' experience said, flatly, no — day-to-day coding lost its allure years ago, it's a means to an end, and AI arrived at the right time for him.

And the sharpest comment on the Laycock thread took the premise and ran straight off the cliff with it. If humans are no longer reading the model's output and internalizing the codebase, the commenter asked, what is source code even for? The assistant might as well take a natural-language prompt and hand you a binary that passes the test suite. I don't think that's where this goes, but it's the right reductio: source code is a human-legible intermediate representation, and its value is entirely contingent on someone reading it.

The counterprogramming came from Cisco, where Jeetu Patel told Fortune he's given a twenty-seven-year-old engineer unlimited tokens to refactor ninety-eight percent of the company's legacy code. The quote is, "you can go nuts." Hold that next to Laycock's red build and you have the whole spread: one organization slowing down to ask whether it can trust what the agents produced, another handing a two-person team the keys to a four-hundred-billion-dollar company's codebase.

Now the money, because the week ended badly and the reason is more interesting than the number.

Thursday and Friday were the worst two days the AI trade has had in months. Intel fell twenty-eight percent on Friday, shedding more than a hundred thirty billion dollars, after its finance chief said the company doesn't expect to bring in more cash than it spends this year and guided fourth-quarter revenue below expectations — while simultaneously ramping AI-related equipment spending. Alphabet dropped twelve percent on Thursday, more than five hundred billion dollars of market value. Reddit fell twenty-nine percent, Roblox eighteen. The Nasdaq closed out October with its worst month since March 2025 — though it's worth saying out loud that it's still up nearly eight percent on the year.

But look at why Alphabet fell, because it's the most interesting fact of the week. Its AI summaries — the thing it now puts at the top of search results — are cutting into its own search advertising revenue. The AI is working, and that's the problem. Reddit fell for the mirror-image reason: Google's AI search is eating the referral traffic Reddit depends on. Meanwhile Alphabet expects to spend up to two hundred five billion dollars this year, nearly all AI-related; Amazon has committed two hundred twenty billion; and five hyperscalers together have committed around eight hundred billion for 2026. That's the shape of it: enormous certain costs against revenue that is, at minimum, rearranging itself.

Underneath the equity story is a credit story, and it got concrete on Friday. The Financial Times reported, and TechCrunch summarized, that Blackstone — the anchor investor in the sixty-three-billion-dollar deal financing Anthropic's chips — has told investors in that deal it now expects Anthropic to receive a high junk rating after its IPO. Blackstone had previously told them the opposite: investment grade before 2027. That's a forecast, not a rating, but it's a reversal by the party with the most exposure, and a junk rating makes every future dollar more expensive. And overnight, the Wall Street Journal's headline — I'll flag that the article itself is paywalled and I read only the headline — says OpenAI's latest round came in at less than half its thirty-billion-dollar target.

Which is why I want to replay something Evan Conrad of SF Compute said nine days ago, before any of this. His claim: the bottleneck in compute and in AI generally is not power and not chips, it's entirely credit. The mechanism is almost comically simple. A lender who finances a GPU cluster has no idea how to underwrite the collateral, because if the lab stops paying and the cloud stops paying, the lender repossesses a supercomputer and has no idea what to do with it or what it'll be worth. So it can't bet on the asset; it has to bet on the customer's credit. And, in Conrad's words, every AI lab pretty much does not have good credit — they're all new companies. His prediction, made October twenty-first: customers increasingly asked to prepay contracts in full, and consolidation, because labs that can't write that check will be forced to sell to someone whose credit rating can. Nine days later: a junk forecast for the second-largest lab, and a round that reportedly missed by half.

Washington noticed. On Thursday, Progressive Caucus chair Greg Casar and Financial Services ranking member Maxine Waters introduced a resolution with twelve cosponsors on one narrow point: no bailout. The findings are the interesting part. AI-related firms hold trillions of dollars in debt, much of it raised in opaque and unregulated private credit markets, and the market's price-to-earnings ratio is nearly as high as at the peak of the 1990s tech bubble. The resolution opposes rescuing insolvent AI firms, opposes emergency lending instead of ordinary bankruptcy, and opposes golden parachutes — it specifically calls for clawing back mansions, planes and yachts from executives whose conduct sank their firms. It's endorsed by the American Federation of Teachers, Americans for Financial Reform, and, interestingly, the Future of Life Institute. It will not get a vote. What it is, is a marker — the first time Congress has written down, in advance, that this build-out is financed in a way that might require someone to decide who eats the loss.

One honest counterweight, from the Hacker News thread on the OpenAI story. A commenter pulled the receipts: six separate moments over three years — a GPT-4.5 launch, a Claude Code rate limit, a GPT-5 disappointment, a DeepSeek release, an AMD chip deal — where the top comment confidently called the bubble's pop. Every one was wrong. That's not an argument the balance sheets are fine. It's an argument for epistemic hygiene about your own priors.

For the deep cut, back to that same week's Cognitive Revolution — specifically to Zvi Mowshowitz, who gave the best argument I've heard this year against an idea most of this field treats as obviously correct. One caveat: the published transcript is automated and mangles a few words, so I've repaired the obvious errors and I'm flagging that I did.

The idea is the cognitive core, which Karpathy named. It goes like this: there is some very small amount of machinery that constitutes reasoning. It's small because the human brain itself runs on remarkably little core code. So the right architecture is a tiny network holding that core, which then loads information into context, looks things up as needed, and queries outside systems. Small model on your phone, all the knowledge elsewhere.

Zvi's position has two parts, and the order matters. First, he concedes the premise almost entirely. He thinks it's probably true that a cognitive core exists in theory — some small set of logical gates that is the core of how a brain works, how a language model functions. Two hundred lines of code gets you a primitive network that does interesting things. He grants all of that.

Then he says the conclusion doesn't follow, and here's the chain. You do not look up facts one at a time as you need them. What you actually have is, in his phrasing, millions of facts that gave you a set of intuitions and heuristics — and those intuitions are what let you figure out which five facts to go find, and how to get from point A to point B. The facts don't sit beside the reasoning. They constitute the equipment that does the reasoning. We all know what it feels like to go into a domain where we don't know the details: you constantly have to ask what that acronym was, what that word means. A model beside you helps enormously. But you still have to know enough to know what to ask. That's what he means by "big model smell" — the richness that lets a system pull up things that feel very different from each other and notice they connect.

He even grants the economics against himself. There's a roughly fixed marginal cost to memorizing one more fact, and a decreasing marginal benefit, because the most important facts come first and later ones duplicate. So in principle there's a break-even point. His observation is that as compute gets cheaper and algorithms improve, that break-even point keeps going up — current frontier models know a ridiculous number of things, and he says he wouldn't have it any other way.

Then comes the part I haven't stopped thinking about, which he offers as spitballing. If you did build a pure cognitive core, it contains no information about what to do or not do, because that's not reasoning machinery, that's content. So take an uncontroversial example — don't steal. That's a fact. A value. By definition, stripping down to the absolute minimum excludes it. You'd load not-stealing into context when you needed it. Which means you could load stealing instead. Or neutrality. His conclusion, and I'm quoting: the true cognitive core is completely amoral. It's completely indifferent. It simply processes information. And so if you have that, it becomes very important to choose what to do with it.

Here's my takeaway, Ollie, and it's why I built the episode around it. Zvi is making the deskilling argument, run on silicon. The SRE post says a human who stops exercising the routine loses the intuition that handles the exceptional. Zvi says a model stripped to pure reasoning loses the accumulated knowledge that tells it which question to ask. Laycock says engineers are protecting judgment that only came from building things by hand. Fowler says don't let the machine write the draft, because the practice is the point. Four independent people, in one week, landed on the same structural claim: judgment is not a module you can factor out and keep. It's a residue of having done the work. If that's right, it has a consequence for how you set up your own bench work — hand off the tasks that were never teaching you anything, and keep the ones that felt hard.

For tomorrow, one thing to watch. The credit argument is now testable. We have a junk forecast and a reportedly undersubscribed round; what would confirm Conrad is a specific term sheet — a compute contract with full prepayment, or a smaller lab selling itself to a buyer with an investment-grade balance sheet rather than raising again. If one of those shows up in the next few weeks, then the bottleneck in this industry stopped being chips some time ago, and nobody updated the story.
